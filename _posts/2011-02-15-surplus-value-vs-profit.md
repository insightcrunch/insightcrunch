Surplus value and profit are treated as two names for one thing in almost every casual discussion of Marx, and that single habit of speech quietly wrecks nearly everything built on top of it. A reader who assumes the words are interchangeable cannot follow why Marx needs three volumes to reach profit, cannot see why the rate of profit behaves differently from the rate of exploitation, and cannot make sense of interest, rent, or commercial earnings except as separate mysteries with separate sources. The confusion is understandable. Both terms point at a gain. Both are measured in money once accounts are drawn up. A firm that extracts a large surplus usually reports a healthy profit, so in ordinary experience the two magnitudes rise and fall together often enough to feel identical. Marx's whole construction depends on refusing that feeling. Surplus value names something produced, in a specific place, by a specific activity. Profit names something received, through a specific distribution, by a specific claimant. Production and reception are related, but they are not the same event, and the distance between them is where Volume Three of Capital does its work.

![Surplus value vs profit, the denominator rule and the three ratios in Marx - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-80.webp)

The stakes of the distinction are easiest to feel through the puzzles it dissolves. A merchant house earns a solid return while employing no one who makes anything. A bank collects interest year after year without a factory floor anywhere in its operations. A landlord draws rent from land nobody in the transaction created. A manufacturer, meanwhile, can organise exhausting shifts, pay wages far below the value the workforce adds, and still report a loss because prices moved against the firm or interest charges swallowed the gain. If profit and surplus value were synonyms, each of those cases would be a contradiction. A theory that equates them has to say the bank creates value out of paper, the merchant creates value out of shelving, and the loss-making manufacturer exploited nobody despite the unpaid hours on the timesheet. Marx's answer is that one pool of surplus value, produced in productive workplaces across the economy, is divided among many hands, and profit is the name for a share of that pool as it arrives in a particular set of accounts. Some recipients produced none of the pool themselves. Some producers receive less than they produced, or nothing, or less than nothing once debts are settled. That is the comparison this article works through, with the formulas stated in prose, one numerical example carried from beginning to end, and a verdict on which term answers which question.

## Surplus value and profit in plain terms

Begin with production, because Marx begins there and because the order matters. A capitalist advances money to buy two kinds of things: means of production, such as machinery, buildings, fuel, and raw materials, and labour power, the capacity to work, purchased from workers for a wage. The first outlay Marx calls constant capital, written as the letter c, because in his analysis its value passes into the product unchanged in size, transferred bit by bit as equipment wears or all at once as materials are consumed. The second outlay he calls variable capital, written as v, because it does not merely transfer. Labour power in use is labour, and labour adds new value to the product, more new value, under normal conditions, than the wage that bought the capacity. The worker must work part of the day to replace the equivalent of the wage, the value of labour power itself, which is set by the cost of keeping the worker and the worker's household alive and fit to return. Marx calls that portion necessary labour. The remainder of the day is surplus labour, and the new value created during it is surplus value, written as s. Nothing in this part of the account depends on cheating, underpayment below the going value of labour power, or sharp practice in buying and selling. Labour power is assumed purchased at its full value. The surplus arises because the use of the commodity bought lasts longer than the labour required to reproduce its price.

Profit enters the story later, and it enters wearing a different relationship to the outlay. When the finished commodities are sold, the capitalist compares the proceeds not with the wage bill alone but with everything advanced: buildings and machines and materials and wages together, the total capital, c plus v. The excess of proceeds over that total outlay, as it presents itself in the firm's own reckoning, is profit. Two shifts have happened at once. The surplus has been related to a much larger denominator, and it has begun to look like a yield generated by the whole advance rather than a product of the living labour within it. Marx treats that appearance as systematic rather than accidental. Cost price, the sum the capitalist actually lays out, lumps constant and variable portions into one undifferentiated expense, and since the gain is computed over the lump, the gain seems to spring from the lump. Volume One had isolated labour as the source of new value by holding the rest of the advance analytically still. Volume Three shows what the same surplus looks like from inside the counting house, where no such isolation survives and where profit, not surplus value, is the visible magnitude that guides decisions.

### What is the difference between surplus value and profit?

Surplus value is the new value produced by labour beyond the value of labour power. Profit is the form in which a firm receives its share of the economy-wide pool of surplus value, measured against total capital advanced. Profit is therefore a distributed appearance of surplus value.

That short answer carries the whole article in miniature, and each clause deserves pressure. Produced by labour: surplus value has a birthplace, the working day, and a measure tied to that birthplace, the ratio of surplus labour to necessary labour. A share of a pool: profit has an address rather than a birthplace, and its size depends on competitive and institutional channels that move value between firms and sectors after production has finished. Measured against total capital advanced: the yardstick changes, and changing the yardstick changes every comparison drawn from it. A distributed appearance: profit is not an illusion or a bookkeeping trick, since firms really receive it and really fail without it, but its form misdescribes its origin the way a shadow accurately reports an object's outline while saying nothing about what the object is made of. Readers who want the production side developed at full length, from the purchase of labour power through the division of the working day, will find it in the [surplus value complete guide](/2010/11/01/surplus-value-complete-guide/), which this comparison takes as its starting point rather than repeating.

A further plain-terms clarification prevents a second confusion that often replaces the first. Surplus value is a magnitude of value, an amount, before it is a ratio. A firm or an economy produces some quantity of it in a period, expressed in money terms once commodities sell. Profit, likewise, is first an amount received. The famous contrast between the two concepts becomes sharpest when each amount is turned into a rate, because rates require denominators, and the denominators differ. Comparing amounts alone can mislead in a third way: the amount of profit a single firm books need not equal the amount of surplus value its own workforce produced, even though the totals across the whole economy are, in Marx's construction, two views of one mass. Keeping amount, rate, source, and recipient separate in the mind is most of the discipline this topic demands. The sections that follow take each separation in turn, and the worked example threads through all of them so the reader can watch a single set of figures change meaning as the question asked of it changes.

## The denominator rule

The denominator rule is the namable claim this article advances, and it can be stated in one sentence: surplus value and profit differ first of all in what they are measured against, and almost every confusion between them dissolves once the reader asks what sits under the line. A ratio is a fraction. The numerator tells you how much of something there is. The denominator tells you what that amount is being compared with, and therefore what question the ratio answers. Divide surplus value by the wage bill and you learn how intensively labour is being exploited, how much unpaid labour stands behind each unit of paid labour. Divide the same surplus by the entire advance, wages and equipment and premises and stock together, and you learn something altogether different: the return the owner obtains on the whole sum committed. Neither answer is wrong. They answer different questions, and trouble starts only when a figure computed for one question is quoted as though it settled the other. A return on total outlay of a given size can conceal fierce exploitation inside a capital-intensive plant, and a modest return can coexist with gentle exploitation in a workshop where nearly all costs are wages. The denominator rule is a reading habit before it is a doctrine. Whenever a percentage appears in an argument about Marx, ask what sits under the line.

Why does the shift of denominator matter so much that Marx builds a volume around it? Because the shift is also a shift in what the number appears to describe. A ratio against variable capital points back at the labour process every time it is read. Its denominator is people, or rather the price of their capacity to work, and a rise in the ratio means the working day is being divided more unequally between the portion that replaces wages and the portion that accrues to the employer. A ratio against total capital points at a portfolio. Its denominator is a sum of money embodied in heterogeneous things, and a movement in it invites talk of efficiency, asset use, market conditions, and managerial skill. Nothing in the second ratio gestures toward the working day at all. An investor comparing two industries by their rates of profit sees steel and software as rival placements for funds, and the comparison works perfectly well for placing funds, while registering nothing about how long anyone worked or how the day was divided in either industry. Marx's claim is that capitalist accounting is not a neutral camera pointed at production. Its most basic category, profit on total capital, frames the gain in a way that structurally omits the gain's source. The denominator rule names the exact spot where the omission happens: under the line, where variable capital disappears into a total.

Pedagogically, the rule earns its keep because it converts a memorised contrast into a procedure. Students often learn that surplus value belongs to Volume One and profit to Volume Three as a fact about the table of contents, then promptly blur the two again under exam pressure. The procedure survives pressure better than the fact. Given any statement involving a rate, the student reconstructs the fraction, identifies numerator and denominator from the wording, and only then decides which concept is in play. Given a firm's glossy claim about returns, the journalist performs the same reconstruction and notices that the figure, whatever its size, is silent about exploitation in either direction. Given a polemic announcing that profits are high and therefore workers must be ground down, or that profits are thin and therefore nobody is exploited, the reader spots the missing step, since neither inference crosses from one denominator to the other without additional evidence about composition and the working day. The rule also explains why the two rates can move in opposite directions, a possibility that sounds paradoxical until the fractions are written side by side, and which the worked example below makes concrete with unremarkable arithmetic.

There is a temptation, having learned the rule, to treat the rate of profit as simply the rate of surplus value diluted, as though profit were surplus value viewed through frosted glass and nothing more. That understates the second transformation Marx describes. The numerator changes character as well as the denominator, because the surplus an individual firm ends up booking is not necessarily the surplus its own workers produced. Competition redistributes. Prices of production, formed around an average rate of profit across the economy, systematically transfer value from labour-intensive branches to capital-intensive ones, so that each capital of a given size tends to draw a similar return regardless of how much living labour it sets in motion. The denominator rule governs the first and most teachable difference, the one a reader can verify with a pencil. Redistribution governs the second, and it is why the article insists on the pool image from the outset. Surplus value is produced firm by firm, in particular working days. Profit is allocated pool by pool, through prices, interest, rent, and commercial margins, and only the aggregate identities hold with any strictness. Confusing the two levels, firm and economy, is the third great source of muddle in this territory, after synonymy and denominator blindness.

## The three formulas stated in prose

Marx's notation is spare, and each formula in the trio should be read aloud as a sentence before it is used as a calculation. The first ratio, the rate of surplus value, also called the rate of exploitation, is surplus value divided by variable capital: s over v. Read as a sentence, it says that for every unit of value laid out on labour power, labour creates a certain additional amount beyond replacing that outlay. It is a ratio between two flows of new value, the portion of the day's added value that returns to the worker as the wage equivalent and the portion retained. Because both terms arise within the same working day, the ratio can equally be expressed in hours, as surplus labour divided by necessary labour, and Marx moves between the value form and the time form depending on whether the argument concerns money magnitudes or the lived division of the day. No constant capital appears in this fraction, not because machinery is unimportant to production, but because machinery adds no new value in this framework. It transfers value it already embodies. Including it in a measure of exploitation would be like judging how hard a crew rows by weighing the boat.

Profit's rate is computed against a wider base: surplus value divided by total capital advanced, s over the sum of c and v. Read as a sentence, it says that the whole advance, every pound or dollar committed to plant, materials, and wages alike, yields a certain percentage over the period. This is the ratio a capitalist actually watches, the one that decides whether funds stay in a line of business or migrate elsewhere, and Marx never denies its practical sovereignty. His point concerns what it measures. Since constant capital sits in the denominator while contributing nothing to the numerator beyond transferring its own value, the ratio mechanically shrinks as production grows more equipment-heavy, even when the treatment of labour is unchanged or worsening. It is therefore a compound signal, mixing the intensity of exploitation with the technical composition of the process, and no reader can decompose the mixture from the headline figure alone. Two firms reporting identical profit rates may stand at opposite poles of exploitation, a possibility the worked example turns into arithmetic shortly.

The organic composition of capital is constant capital divided by variable capital: c over v. Read as a sentence, it expresses how much dead, embodied labour in means of production is set in motion by each unit of living labour power purchased. Marx is careful, in the chapters where the notion does its heavy work, to distinguish the technical composition, the physical ratio of machines and materials to workers, from the value composition, the same relation expressed in prices, and he reserves the term organic composition for the value composition insofar as it mirrors the technical one. A rising organic composition is the fingerprint of mechanisation: each worker tends a larger array of equipment, handles a greater mass of material, and produces a larger physical output, while the wage outlay per unit of total advance falls. The third ratio matters to this article because it is the hinge between the other two. Algebraically, the rate of profit equals the rate of surplus value divided by one plus the organic composition, once numerator and denominator are scaled by variable capital. A reader who grasps that single rearrangement holds the master key to the comparison, since it shows the rate of profit falling behind the rate of surplus value by a factor that grows with mechanisation itself.

A fourth magnitude completes the table even though it is not a rate in the same family: the profit share, meaning the portion of the total new value, or in broader presentations the portion of the product's value, that accrues as surplus rather than returning as wages. In the notation of the example it can be written as s over the sum of v and s, the surplus measured against the new value created in the period. Where the rate of surplus value compares surplus with wages as a ratio of part to part, the share compares surplus with the whole of new value as a ratio of part to whole. The two move together but never coincide, and confusing them produces a familiar numerical slip in classrooms, where a rate of surplus value of one hundred percent, surplus equal to wages, gets misreported as a share of one hundred percent, when the share is in fact one half. Shares dominate public discussion because national accounts and company reports favour them, and Marxian ratios dominate seminar discussion because the theory is built from them. Fluency in this subject means converting between the families without mixing them, and the denominator rule applies with equal force to each conversion.

## One worked example, carried through the whole argument

Abstract fractions persuade slowly, so this article fixes a single set of figures and refuses to change it. Imagine a manufacturing firm, call it the example firm, operating for one production period with figures chosen for arithmetical kindness rather than realism. The owners advance 400 in constant capital: machinery wear, premises, energy, components, and raw materials consumed during the period. They advance 100 in variable capital, the wage bill for the workforce over the same period. Total capital advanced is therefore 500. The workforce, in the course of its labour, transfers the 400 of constant value into the product and adds 200 of new value. Of that new value, 100 replaces the wage advance, corresponding to the necessary labour portion of the aggregated working days, and 100 remains as surplus value, corresponding to the surplus labour portion. The finished output consequently carries a value of 600: four hundred of transferred value, one hundred replacing wages, one hundred of surplus. Nothing exotic has been assumed. The working day across the firm is divided evenly between necessary and surplus labour, an assumption any reader may vary later without disturbing the method.

Now interrogate the same six hundred with each formula in turn. Dividing the surplus of 100 by variable capital of 100 gives the rate of surplus value, one hundred percent. In time language, the workforce labours as long for the employer as it labours to reproduce its own wages: for every hour that replaces the wage, a further hour accrues unpaid to the firm. Against the total capital of 500, the same surplus yields a rate of profit of twenty percent. The organic composition is constant capital of 400 divided by variable capital of 100, a ratio of four to one; expressed as shares of the advance, eighty percent of the outlay sits in means of production and twenty percent in wages. The profit share of new value is the surplus of 100 divided by the new value of 200, which gives one half. Four numbers describe one firm in one period: one hundred percent, twenty percent, four to one, one half. None contradicts another. Each answers a different question, and a summary that quotes any one of them as though it were the firm's single true percentage has already begun to mislead, however accurate the arithmetic behind the quotation.

Watch what each number is good for. The one hundred percent speaks to the labour relation. It would feature in an account of the working day, of bargaining over hours and intensity, of how much of the value workers create returns to them. The twenty percent speaks to the placement of funds. It is the figure compared against rival employments of 500 elsewhere in the economy, against the interest the owners could earn by lending the sum instead, against the return demanded before anyone bothers to organise production at all. The four to one speaks to technique: how mechanised the process is, how much equipment each wage unit marshals, how exposed the firm is to machinery prices and how little of its cost structure moves when wages are bargained. The one half speaks to distribution within the newly created value, the split of the period's net product between the workforce and the claimants on surplus. A complete description needs all four. A polemical description usually needs only one, selected for effect, and the denominator rule exists to catch the selection in the act.

### How does the worked example keep its numbers consistent?

Every later variation in this article changes one element of the original advance at a time and recomputes all four ratios from the altered figures, so the surplus, the wage bill, the constant outlay, and the total always reconcile to the same value of output. Consistency is a discipline of never quoting a rate whose components have silently changed.

Hold the example firm in mind as the variations begin, because the variations are where intuition usually breaks. First variation: suppose the owners mechanise, raising constant capital from 400 to 900 while the wage bill stays at 100 and the division of the working day stays exactly as before. The workforce still adds 200 of new value, split evenly, so surplus value remains 100 and the rate of surplus value remains one hundred percent. Total capital advanced, however, is now 1,000, and the rate of profit falls to ten percent. Exploitation, in Marx's technical sense, has not eased at all. The return on the advance has halved. A commentator watching only the profit rate would announce a gentler, less lucrative capitalism. A commentator watching only the rate of surplus value would report no change in the labour relation whatsoever. Both would be describing the same firm, and the gap between their reports is precisely the gap between the two concepts. Second variation: return to constant capital of 400, but let bargaining or legislation shorten surplus labour so the workforce adds 150 of new value, of which 100 replaces wages and 50 remains as surplus. Exploitation's rate drops to fifty percent. Profit's rate drops to ten percent as well, 50 over the restored total of 500. Identical profit rates, ten percent in both variations, arise from opposite stories: unchanged exploitation inside a heavier plant, and halved exploitation inside the original one. No inspection of the ten percent figure alone can tell the stories apart. That is not a defect of arithmetic. It is the article's central lesson wearing numbers.

## The three-ratio table

Formulas on a page invite a particular kind of false confidence, the feeling that because a ratio can be computed it has been understood. The three-ratio table below is built to interrupt that feeling. It places the rate of surplus value, the rate of profit, the organic composition, and the profit share side by side, and for each it records the formula, what the ratio genuinely measures, what forces move it, what it conceals from a casual reader, and the everyday business expression with which it is most often confused. Read across a row and the concept firms up. Read down the concealment column and the article's warning assembles itself: every one of these magnitudes hides something, and what the rate of profit hides is labour. The table also doubles as the article's reference anchor. A reader who returns months later needing only the distinctions, rather than the argument, can recover them from this grid alone, and a teacher can set the grid as a checking device against which students test any percentage quoted in a textbook, a newspaper, or an examination question.

| Ratio | Formula | What it measures | What moves it | What it conceals | Everyday term it is confused with |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rate of surplus value | Surplus value divided by variable capital | The division of the working day between necessary and surplus labour | Hours, intensity, and the cost of reproducing labour power | The size of the total advance and the weight of machinery | Wage share or labour cost percentage |
| Rate of profit | Surplus value divided by constant plus variable capital | The return obtained on the whole sum advanced | Exploitation, organic composition, turnover, and prices of production | The labour source of the gain, blended into a yield on funds | Return on investment or return on capital employed |
| Organic composition | Constant capital divided by variable capital | How much means of production each unit of labour power sets in motion | Mechanisation, scale, and the relative prices of equipment and labour | Whether exploitation is rising or falling behind the technical change | Capital intensity or automation level |
| Profit share | Surplus value divided by variable plus surplus value | The portion of newly created value accruing as surplus rather than wages | Bargaining strength, productivity in wage goods, and taxation of incomes | The denominator difference between a part to part ratio and a part to whole share | Profit margin on sales |

Two features of the grid repay a slower look. The confusion column is not a list of insults aimed at business language. Return on investment, margin on sales, and capital intensity are respectable measures with jobs to do, and an accountant who used the rate of surplus value to price a loan would be making an error symmetric to the activist who uses return on investment to grade exploitation. The grid's point is that the vocabularies answer to different institutions: one to the labour process and its division, the other to the allocation of funds among rival uses. Translation between them is possible, and this article performs it repeatedly, but translation is work, and the work consists exactly in tracking denominators and redistribution. The second feature is the asymmetry in the concealment column. What the surplus value ratio conceals is scale, which is a limitation of scope. What the profit ratio conceals is origin, which is a limitation of kind, because origin is what the theory of surplus value is about. Marx's charge against ordinary bookkeeping is not that its figures are fabricated. The charge is that its most natural figure, profit on total capital, is constructed so that the one question his theory asks cannot be posed within it.

### Why does adding constant capital lower the second ratio?

Constant capital enters the rate of profit underneath the line while adding nothing to the surplus above it, since equipment and materials transfer their existing value rather than creating new value. Enlarging that part of the advance therefore stretches the same gain across a larger base, and the percentage falls even though production and exploitation proceed exactly as before.

With the grid in place, the relationship among the first three rows can be stated as a small piece of algebra that every reader should perform once by hand. Take the rate of profit, surplus over total capital, and divide both numerator and denominator by variable capital. The numerator becomes the rate of surplus value. The denominator becomes the organic composition plus one. Hence the rate of profit equals the rate of surplus value divided by one plus the organic composition. Test the identity on the example firm: a rate of surplus value of one hundred percent, divided by one plus four, yields twenty percent, matching the direct computation. Test it on the mechanised variation: one hundred percent divided by one plus nine yields ten percent, again matching. The identity is trivial as mathematics and anything but trivial as interpretation. It says the rate of profit is the rate of exploitation filtered through the technical structure of production. Raise exploitation and the filter passes more through. Raise the organic composition and the filter thickens, passing less through at any given level of exploitation. Arguments about profitability that never mention either term of the identity are, from a Marxian standpoint, arguments about a shadow's length conducted without reference to the object or the light.

## Why the rate of profit sits below the rate of surplus value

Given positive constant capital, the rate of profit must fall short of the rate of surplus value, and the reason is embarrassingly plain once the fractions are side by side. Both ratios share the same numerator. The denominators differ, and the profit denominator is larger by exactly the constant capital advanced. A part divided by wages alone will always exceed the same part divided by wages plus equipment, premises, and materials, provided those additions are worth anything at all. There is no empirical discovery here and no tendency law yet, only the geometry of fractions. Yet generations of readers have sensed a paradox in the claim that exploitation can stand at one hundred percent while profit stands at twenty, as though one of the figures must be exaggerated. The paradox dissolves when the bases are restored to view. One hundred percent of the wage bill is twenty percent of the total advance in the example firm, because the wage bill is one fifth of the advance. Percentages without bases are noise, and the two rates are percentages of different bases reporting on different relations.

The equality case sharpens the point by showing when the gap would vanish. If constant capital were zero, if production required no tools, no materials, no premises, nothing but labour hired and set to work on what nature provides for free, then total capital would equal variable capital and the two rates would coincide. Something approaching that limit appears in casual day labour paid by the task, where the worker may even supply simple implements, and it is no accident that exploitation in such arrangements feels unusually transparent: the employer's gain visibly comes out of the day because there is barely any advance for it to hide behind. As production develops, the opposite movement dominates. Tools multiply into machine systems, materials arrive through longer supply chains, buildings and infrastructure swell, and the constant portion climbs. The gap between the rates is therefore not a constant of nature but a historical variable, widening as the organic composition rises. A reader comparing centuries by profit rates alone would systematically understate exploitation more severely in the more mechanised period, because the denominator grows precisely with the development of the productive forces.

A subtlety about turnover belongs here, because it prevents an overcorrection. Capital advanced and capital consumed within a period are not the same sum. A durable machine is advanced in full when purchased but transfers its value in slices across many periods, while wages and materials turn over completely in each cycle, and some firms rotate their whole advance several times within a year. Marx handles the complication by measuring the annual rate of profit against the capital advanced, with surplus accumulated across the year's turnovers in the numerator. Faster turnover can thus lift the annual rate of profit without any change in the rate of surplus value per cycle, a channel through which commercial skill, logistics, and credit arrangements genuinely affect profitability. None of this rescues the synonym view. Turnover changes how often the advance returns bearing surplus; it does not change what surplus is or where it originates. The denominator rule survives the refinement untouched, since the comparison remains between a gain and the totality of funds committed to obtaining it, against a ratio that sets the same gain against wages alone.

Where does the apparently missing portion go, the difference between one hundred percent and twenty that prompts the question in the first place? Nothing has gone missing, and insisting on that answer is part of the article's discipline. The surplus is 100 in both computations. Only the measuring rod changed. Still, the question points beyond itself toward a genuine disappearance act that does occur elsewhere in the system: the surplus produced in one firm does not all remain with that firm, and the surplus produced across the economy is carved among industrial profit, commercial profit, interest, and rent before any individual balance sheet settles. Readers often fuse the two matters, imagining that the fall from one hundred to twenty represents leakage to banks and landlords. It does not. The fall is measurement. The carving is distribution, and it would operate even if every rate were quoted against wages. Keeping measurement and distribution apart, while seeing how each generates its own brand of confusion, is the analytical spine of everything that follows.

## The organic composition, the hinge between the ratios

Of the three ratios, the organic composition attracts the least attention from beginners and repays the most. It records, in value terms, the changing physical relationship between workers and the apparatus they operate. A handloom weaver confronts a frame and a stock of yarn. A worker in an automated plant confronts a line whose embodied value dwarfs a lifetime of wages, monitoring flows of material that no unaided crew could move. Between those poles lies the history of mechanisation, and the organic composition is its index. Marx distinguishes, as noted earlier, between the technical composition, counted in physical units, and the value composition, counted in money, because the two can drift apart when the prices of machines or wage goods change. Productivity growth in machine making cheapens equipment, which can hold the value composition down even as the technical composition climbs. The organic composition names the value ratio specifically insofar as it reflects the technical one, a qualification that matters when readers try to measure the concept rather than merely wield it.

The hinge function follows from the identity already derived. Because the rate of profit equals the rate of surplus value divided by one plus the organic composition, any sustained rise in composition presses the profit rate downward unless exploitation intensifies enough to compensate. That sentence, stated once here and deliberately not developed into a full crisis argument, is the bridge from this comparison to the falling rate controversy that other articles in the series own. The [tendency rate of profit to fall](/2013/10/15/tendency-rate-of-profit-to-fall/) article takes up whether the pressure operates as a law, what countertendencies Marx himself catalogued, and how the dispute has run among economists. For the purposes of the present distinction, only the mechanics matter: composition is the variable that lets exploitation and profitability part company over long stretches of development. An economy can squeeze the working day harder, cheapen wage goods, and raise the rate of surplus value substantially, while its average profit rate stagnates or sinks because each unit of labour is yoked to an ever larger mass of constant capital. Observers fixated on profit rates then pronounce exploitation a spent force at the very moment the ratio against wages says otherwise, and the inversion warning attached to this article exists largely to block that pronouncement.

Composition also disciplines comparisons between industries at a single moment. Sectors differ enormously in how much constant capital a worker sets in motion: compare a refinery, where a small crew shepherds rivers of material through towers of steel, with a cleaning contractor, where the advance is nearly all wages and the equipment fits in a van. At a broadly similar rate of surplus value, the refinery's own surplus, measured against its vast advance, would yield a miserable rate of profit, while the contractor's would yield a spectacular one. Left to themselves, such disparities would empty refineries of investment and flood cleaning with entrants. Competition answers by repricing outputs, and the repricing is the transformation into prices of production, signposted here and treated fully in the [transformation problem explained](/2016/04/15/transformation-problem-explained/) article. Under prices of production, capitals of equal size draw roughly equal profit, an average rate struck across the economy, which means the capital-intensive sector sells above value and the labour-intensive sector sells below value. Value migrates between sectors through the price system. The individual firm's profit thus reflects its size and the average rate far more than its own workers' surplus, a result that should startle anyone still holding the synonym view, and which the next section builds out with the example figures extended across three capitals.

## Three capitals, one average rate

Extend the example across an economy of three capitals, each advancing 500, each exploiting labour at the same rate of one hundred percent, differing only in composition. The first is the example firm itself: constant capital 400, variable capital 100, surplus value 100, output value 600, and an own rate of profit of twenty percent. The second is a labour-intensive workshop: constant capital 250, variable capital 250, surplus value 250, output value 750, and an own rate of profit of fifty percent, since half its advance returns as gain. The third is a heavily mechanised plant: constant capital 450, variable capital 50, surplus value 50, output value 550, and an own rate of profit of ten percent. Aggregate the three: total capital 1,500, total surplus value 400, and an average rate of profit of 400 over 1,500, roughly twenty six point seven percent. Under prices of production, each capital of 500 tends to receive that average rate on its advance, which is to say a profit of about 133, regardless of the surplus its own workforce produced. The workshop, which produced 250, surrenders more than 100 of it through prices below value. The mechanised plant, which produced 50, draws more than 80 beyond its own production through prices above value. The example firm produced 100 and receives about 133, gaining modestly in the redistribution.

Sit with the strangeness of that outcome, because it is the redistribution point in its purest form. Profit, firm by firm, has detached from production, firm by firm. The most exploitative workplace in surplus terms, the workshop whose workers generate two and a half times the mechanised plant's surplus, ends the process with the same profit as its neighbours per unit of capital. Its owner experiences no reward proportionate to the unpaid labour organised under that roof, and its workers experience exploitation that the firm's accounts, after redistribution, do nothing to reveal. Meanwhile the mechanised plant's owner collects a profit largely manufactured by other people's employees in other sectors. Marx draws a moral about appearances from the mechanism: because profit arrives scaled to capital size, capital itself seems productive, each sum breeding its percentage the way a tree bears fruit by nature. The equalisation that creates the appearance is a social process involving every capital in the economy, yet it presents itself to each owner as a property of that owner's own funds. Few inversions in social life are as complete, or as consequential for how gains are justified in ordinary speech, where returns are credited to the wisdom of placing money rather than to the division of working days across a whole economy.

Why does competition enforce the equalisation rather than leaving each capital with its own wildly varying rate? Funds migrate. A sector persistently returning fifty percent on advances attracts entry; supply expands; prices soften until the return sinks toward the average. A sector stuck at ten percent sheds investment; capacity tightens; prices firm until the return recovers. Barriers, patents, regulation, risk, and sheer sluggishness keep real economies far from perfect equality, and Marx never claims the tendency completes itself in any given year. The tendency is enough for the conceptual point. Profit rates are formed socially, across capitals, through rivalry, and therefore the profit booked by a firm is a claim ticket whose value depends on the whole field of capitals, not a thermometer inserted into its own labour process. Readers tracing where the ratios lead when the average rate itself comes under pressure will find the wider canvas in the [marxist crisis theory guide](/2013/10/01/marxist-crisis-theory-guide/), where profitability, accumulation, and breakdown theories are mapped together.

### What changes when the same surplus is divided four ways?

Nothing about production changes at all, and that is the lesson. Division happens after value has been created, as rival claimants assert titles over a pool that already exists: the functioning owner retains a portion, lenders take a portion for the use of money, merchants take a portion for realising sales, and landowners take a portion for access to ground.

## How surplus value is divided: enterprise profit, interest, rent, and commercial gain

Return to the single pool of 100 generated by the example firm, and now follow it out of the firm's hands. In the stylised division used throughout this article, the 100 is claimed four ways: 40 remains with the functioning industrialist as profit of enterprise, 25 goes to a money lender as interest, 15 is taken by merchants as commercial profit, and 20 is paid to a landowner as ground rent. The shares sum exactly to the surplus produced, which is the discipline the pool image imposes: division reallocates, it does not multiply. Each claimant, however, experiences the receipt differently, and each receipt grows its own ideology of origin. Interest arrives as though money itself had laboured. Rent arrives as though the land had yielded a crop of cash alongside the crop of grain. Commercial profit arrives as though buying and selling had created the margin. Profit of enterprise arrives, most seductively of all, as though it were a wage for the work of supervision and the reward of entrepreneurial flair. Marx's Parts Four to Six of Volume Three can be read as a long campaign against those four appearances, conducted by tracing each revenue back to the single source in unpaid labour time.

Interest deserves the fullest treatment, because Marx regards interest-bearing capital as the most fetishised form in the whole system, the point where the concealment perfected by profit reaches completion. When money is lent, it leaves as a sum and returns enlarged, and the interval between departure and return need contain no visible production at all. The lender need never see a factory, a worker, or a commodity. From the lender's standpoint, increase adheres to money as such, a property like weight or extension. Marx unpacks the transaction into its hidden steps: the borrowed funds function as capital in someone else's enterprise, workers produce surplus value within that enterprise, and the gross profit is then split between the functioning capitalist and the lender according to a rate of interest formed by competition between borrowers and lenders and by custom, law, and central bank practice. Interest is therefore a portion of profit, and behind profit a portion of surplus value, wearing the costume of a natural yield. The split also creates the category of profit of enterprise as a residue, what remains after interest is deducted, and the residue promptly misdescribes itself. Set beside interest, which looks like the fruit of mere ownership, profit of enterprise comes to look like the fruit of activity, a salary for management. Marx notes the irony: the same gross profit is split into a part attributed to owning capital and a part attributed to working with it, and the second attribution flatters the functioning owner into imagining a fundamental difference from the wage labour performed a few offices away.

Rent follows a parallel logic with its own twist. Land, unlike machinery, was not produced by labour at all in the case of virgin soil, and its price is not a value but a capitalised claim on future rent, an anticipated stream of surplus portions discounted into a lump. Ground rent arises, in Marx's account, because landed property can block capital's free entry into agriculture and mining, allowing landowners to appropriate a slice of surplus value that would otherwise enter the general equalisation. Differential rent flows from unequal fertility and location, as prices set by the worst land in cultivation leave extra gains on better land, which the owner captures. Absolute rent, in Marx's construction, flows from the monopoly of landed property itself combined with a lower organic composition in agriculture. The details have been disputed for generations, including by sympathetic critics, and the article's discipline is to hold the structural claim separately from the disputed machinery: whatever the precise channel, rent in this framework is a deduction from surplus value produced elsewhere in the system, not a second fountain of value springing from soil. The landowner produces nothing, organises nothing, and lends nothing, yet receives a fifth of the example firm's surplus in the stylised division, purely by holding title to a condition of production that others must use.

Commercial profit completes the quartet and answers a puzzle that troubles many first readers: if merchants merely buy and sell, and buying and selling create no value, why do merchants earn profit at rates comparable to industrialists? Marx's answer leans on the pool. Selling is necessary labour of a social sort, the work of realising value, converting commodities back into money, keeping accounts, moving goods, staffing shops. Much of that work is unproductive in his technical sense, producing no new value, yet it consumes real resources and must be paid for. Industrial capital, left to sell its own output, would tie up funds and time in circulation. By handing goods to merchants at a discount from final price, industrial capital buys speed and frees its own advance, and the merchant's margin is the share of total surplus value surrendered to cover circulation costs and yield the merchant an average return on the capital employed in shops, warehouses, and wages of commercial staff. The discount mechanism means commercial profit is carved from surplus before industrial profit settles, not conjured in the act of exchange. Shop assistants and warehouse staff occupy a poignant position in the scheme: their labour enables the realisation of surplus value and their employer's claim on it, and Marx treats their own unpaid hours as reducing circulation costs and thereby swelling the pool available for division, even though their work creates no value directly. The [surplus value in modern firms](/2011/01/15/surplus-value-modern-firms/) article works through the productive and unproductive distinction against software, retail, and platform cases, where the classification bites hardest.

## Why profit appears to spring from the whole advance

Marx has a name for the mental operation the accounts perform: within cost price, the distinction between constant and variable portions disappears, and with it disappears the only clue to where the increment originates. The capitalist lays out 500 and recovers 600. As a practical person, the capitalist sees outlay and return, two money sums with a difference between them, and naturally attributes the difference to the outlay as a whole, the way a gardener attributes growth to the whole garden. Nothing in the transaction singles out the 100 spent on wages as the portion that expanded. The machinery visibly participates, materials visibly transform, premises visibly shelter the process, and the manager's own exertions are the most visible of all. Labour, meanwhile, has been paid and discharged, its contribution folded into commodities and forgotten. The resulting picture, in which capital as such appears fertile, is what Marx calls an inversion: the relation between people, the division of their working day, shows up as a relation between things, a yield inhering in sums of money and equipment. He insists the inversion is not a lie told by apologists but a genuine appearance generated by the process itself, which is why intelligent people who run businesses sincerely experience profit as the reward of capital and organisation. Sincerity, in his analysis, is part of the phenomenon to be explained, not evidence against the explanation.

The inversion warning attached to this article generalises the point into a reading rule for profit talk everywhere. Whenever profit is described as generated by assets, created by a brand, earned by a balance sheet, or produced by money working, a grammatical subject has been substituted for the human activity the theory points to. Assets do not labour. Brands do not labour, however much labour built and maintains them. Money in a vault at midnight adds nothing to the world's stock of value between dusk and dawn. The warning cuts in the opposite direction as well, against a lazy Marxian mirror image in which every receipt of profit is treated as proof that the recipient personally extracted that amount from identifiable workers in a particular building. The pool and the equalisation stand between production and receipt precisely to forbid that shortcut. A pension fund receiving dividends, a shareholder collecting a distribution, a landlord banking rent: each draws on surplus value in aggregate, through chains of claims, and honesty about the chains is what separates analysis from denunciation by association. The inversion, properly understood, is a doctrine about appearances and their mechanisms, not a licence to assign individual guilt by cash flow.

Volume Three's opening parts perform the inversion slowly, and their sequence is worth following because each step removes one more signpost. First, surplus value is related to cost price, yielding profit as the excess over what production cost the capitalist, a formulation that already merges the two kinds of outlay. Next, that profit is related to the total advance, yielding the rate of profit, where the merger hardens into a percentage apparently governed by the size and turnover of funds. Then competition equalises rates across spheres, yielding prices of production, so that even the amount of profit loses its tie to the surplus produced in the sphere. After that, the total gets subdivided: merchants take a share, lenders take a share, landowners take a share, and each share acquires its own apparent source. By the end of the sequence, the original division of the working day, still occurring every morning in every workplace, has become practically invisible from within the categories that govern economic life. Marx's phrase for the endpoint is the trinity formula, capital yielding profit and interest, land yielding rent, labour yielding wages, three revenues from three seemingly independent sources, which he treats as the completed religion of everyday economic thought. The comparison in this article is the first step of that sequence examined under a microscope, and readers who master the first step can usually reconstruct the rest unaided.

## Firm-level puzzles: profit without production, loss with extraction, and the markup

Test the distinction against the cases that break the synonym view, beginning with profit where no surplus value is produced on the premises. A pure merchant operation, buying finished goods and reselling them, employs staff whose work realises value but creates none, on Marx's classification. Its margin is real, its profit is real, and both are shares transferred from the pool through the discount at which it acquired the goods. A landlord company living from rents on commercial property likewise produces nothing and receives steadily. A holder of government bonds receives interest ultimately sourced from taxation, which itself draws on incomes across the economy. In each case the synonym view must either deny the profit, which the bank statements refute, or credit the recipient with phantom production, which common sense refutes. The distinction handles all three without strain: production of surplus value and appropriation of profit are different functions, often housed in different firms, sometimes in different countries, connected by price, credit, and property systems rather than by proximity. Nothing in the framework requires the exploiter and the beneficiary to be the same person, and a great deal of institutional design in finance exists precisely to separate them.

Now run the test in the harder direction: extraction without profit. The example firm organises its shifts, its workforce adds 200 of new value, half of it surplus, and then the market turns. Perhaps the product's selling price collapses under a flood of imports priced below value. Perhaps a loan taken for expansion carries interest charges of 70 and rent obligations of 40, together exceeding the 100 of surplus, so that after claims are honoured the firm books a loss of 10 and slides toward insolvency. Has exploitation vanished because the bottom line turned red? On the synonym view it must have, retroactively annulling the surplus labour performed. On Marx's view the question confuses production with successful appropriation. The surplus labour happened. The surplus value was realised in part, then drained by rival claimants and adverse prices. Workers can be exploited by an employer who is simultaneously being ruined, a configuration that strikes moral intuition as odd only because moral intuition ties exploitation to the exploiter's enrichment. The technical concept ties it to the division of the working day. Bankruptcy courts are full of firms whose employees worked unpaid hours embodied in output that creditors, landlords, and lenders ultimately divided among themselves.

The markup invites a third confusion, subtler than the other two because a markup genuinely resembles a surplus in miniature. A seller acquires an item for 80 and sells it for 100; the markup of 20, or twenty five percent on cost, looks like value created by the act of selling. Marx's treatment separates the cases. If the seller is a merchant handling goods produced elsewhere, the 20 is commercial profit, a share of pool surplus obtained because the producer sold at 80 a commodity whose value supports a final price of 100. The markup measures the share captured at that point in the chain, not value born in the shop. If the seller is the producer and the markup exceeds what the firm's own surplus would support, the excess is redistribution again, value drawn from elsewhere through favourable prices. And if every seller in a chain marks up in sequence, the final price can tower over the value produced at the origin without any stage having created the difference. Markups describe positions along a distribution chain. Surplus value describes an origin. The persistent habit of pointing at a retail markup and calling it exploitation, or pointing at its thinness and declaring exploitation impossible, are matching errors, each mistaking a distribution coordinate for a production fact.

### How should a reader handle prices of production without losing the distinction?

Prices of production are values after competition has redistributed surplus value so that equal capitals draw equal profit, which means any single price may sit above or below the value of the commodity it attaches to. The reader keeps the distinction by remembering that redistribution moves a total it does not create, so aggregate reasoning stays anchored in production.

## Marxian profit and the profit of mainstream economics

A reader trained in mainstream economics arrives with a well-formed concept already installed, and the honest comparison begins by admitting how much ground the two concepts share. Both traditions use profit for a residual: what remains from revenue after costs are met. Both treat the prospect of it as the motive that organises investment. Both distinguish it from wages and from rent in accounting practice, and both notice that competition tends to squeeze it toward some normal level across industries. The divergences are real but specific, and naming them precisely beats the sweeping claim that the theories simply contradict each other. First, mainstream usage splits into accounting profit and economic profit, where economic profit deducts the opportunity cost of the owner's own funds and labour, so that a firm can show accounting profit while earning zero or negative economic profit. Marx's profit makes no such deduction; interest on the owner's funds is, for Marx, a portion of profit, not a cost standing outside it, because the whole gain over cost price counts as transformed surplus value before its subdivision. Second, mainstream theory explains the residual through a menu of sources: reward for risk bearing, rent on scarce talents or assets, temporary monopoly, innovation windfalls, and disequilibrium gaps that rivals have not yet closed. Marx explains the aggregate residual through one source, unpaid labour time, and treats the menu items as mechanisms that distribute the pool or protect a firm's grip on its share rather than as independent fountains.

Third, and most consequential for empirical work, the two traditions direct measurement differently. The mainstream investigator studies profit as a price-like signal, asking whether its level in a sector indicates barriers to entry or scope for expansion. The Marxian investigator asks a prior question the signal cannot answer: how the new value embodied in output divides between those who produced it and those who hold claims on it. Because published accounts are built on cost price, with wages, depreciation, interest, and rent arrayed as parallel costs, the Marxian categories have to be reconstructed from accounts designed to display something else, a reconstruction whose difficulties the measurement articles in this series take up in detail. The practical upshot for readers of financial journalism is modest but valuable. A reported margin is a fact about a firm's position in distribution. It becomes evidence about exploitation only after composition, turnover, interest, rent, and tax have been peeled away, and even then it speaks about a share of an economy-wide process. Conversely, a firm celebrated for razor thin margins may sit atop a supply chain where surplus extraction is ferocious, the thinness recording only that the firm captures little of what its commercial pressure helps to organise.

## A short history of two vocabularies

Marx did not inherit a tidy distinction and then complicate it. He inherited a muddle and spent two volumes excavating beneath it. Classical political economy, in Adam Smith and David Ricardo, already possessed the notion of profit as the residual of price after wages and rent, and Ricardo in particular had grasped that profitability across the economy moves inversely with wages, a relation he expressed through the labour embodied in wage goods. Yet the classics slid constantly between two bases of comparison. At one moment profit appears as a proportion of the wages advanced, resembling a surplus over the labour fund. At the next it appears as a proportion of the whole stock employed, the rate that governs accumulation and the migration of funds between trades. Ricardo felt the tension acutely in his struggles with the invariable measure of value, because a change in distribution between wages and profit altered the very prices he hoped to explain by labour alone. His successors largely stopped feeling it. In the hands of later textbook writers, profit settled into a single accounting category, its base chosen by convention, its source explained by abstinence, risk, or the productivity of capital itself, each explanation quietly assuming that the residual belonged by nature to the owner of the advance.

Marx's route to the distinction ran through his critique of that inheritance. The early manuscripts and the notebooks that became the Grundrisse show him wrestling with Ricardo's equations, testing whether surplus could be grounded in exchange and concluding it could not: buying cheap and selling dear redistributes value between traders and nets to zero across the whole system, a result the complete guide to surplus value establishes as the no-cheating condition. Production had to be the birthplace, and labour power the unique commodity whose consumption creates more value than it costs. Only with that result secured could the second question be posed properly: given a surplus born in production, why does it present itself as a percentage yield on total stock, and why did the classics, who stood so close, miss the seam between the two ratios? His answer was the inversion account already given, supplemented by a historical observation. The seam is invisible in earlier modes of production, where surplus labour often appears in direct form, as days worked on a lord's demesne or dues rendered in kind, precisely because no total advance exists to hide behind. Capitalism is the first system in which the extraction of surplus labour and its complete disguise are equally perfected, and political economy, arising inside that system, naturally theorised from the disguise.

Engels assembled Volume Three from Marx's drafts after his death, and the editorial history matters for readers who wonder why the profit material feels less finished than the surplus value material of Volume One. The 1864 to 1865 draft was a working manuscript, dense with examples and recalculations, which Engels organised into the seven parts that carry the argument from the transformation of surplus value into profit, through the average rate and prices of production, to the division among profit of enterprise, interest, and rent. The comparatively rough state of the text has licensed a century of argument about whether the transformation between values and prices is consistent, an argument this article signposts rather than joins. For the distinction itself, the textual situation is reassuring: the denominator contrast between the rate of surplus value in Chapter Seven of Volume One and the rate of profit in Part One of Volume Three is among the most explicit and repeatedly stated doctrines in the entire work, anchored in worked numerical illustrations Marx returned to across drafts. Whatever disputes attach to later steps, the first step, the one this comparison exists to teach, stands on the firmest textual ground in the corpus.

## Fixed capital, circulating capital, and the patience the example requires

The worked example treated the advance of 500 as though it were consumed and replaced within a single period, a simplification the reader should now see relaxed, because real capitals tie up much of their constant portion in durable plant. Suppose the example firm owns machinery valued at 2,000, expected to serve for ten periods, alongside the circulating advances already described. In each period the machinery transfers one tenth of its value, 200, into the product as it wears, while materials and energy account for the remaining 200 of constant capital consumed, preserving the figure of 400 used throughout. The capital advanced, however, is no longer 500. It is the circulating 300 of materials and wages plus the full 2,000 embodied in the machine park, a total commitment of 2,300, even though only 500 of value passes through production in the period. Marx's rate of profit for a single turnover relates the period's surplus to the capital advanced, and on that basis the example firm's 100 of surplus yields a far thinner percentage than the twenty percent computed under the simplification. Textbooks handle the discrepancy by annualising and by distinguishing the capital advanced from the cost price of the period's output, and the distinction explains apparent anomalies in profit data, where industries with monumental fixed plant report modest rates on assets alongside vigorous operating results.

Why tolerate the complication in an article devoted to a simple contrast? Because the complication protects the contrast from a cheap refutation. A sceptic might grant the denominator rule for the toy example and then object that real accounting never relates surplus to a clean total anyway, since depreciation schedules, asset revaluations, and leasing arrangements scramble any such magnitude beyond recognition. The objection lands against naive measurement, and the measurement articles elsewhere in the series concede its force at length. It does not land against the conceptual distinction. However accountants slice the advance, the two questions remain different: how the working day divides, and what return the committed funds obtain. Fixed capital deepens the wedge between the answers, because durable plant inflates the second denominator while leaving the first ratio untouched, but depth is not difference of kind. The reader who has followed the machinery case possesses the distinction in its robust form, able to survive contact with balance sheets rather than expiring on first encounter with a depreciation line.

Turnover supplies the mirror complication on the side of time. A retailer whose entire advance cycles monthly generates surplus, or captures a share of it, twelve times a year on the same committed funds, while a shipbuilder's advance sleeps in a hull for two years before returning. Annual rates of profit compress these rhythms into comparability, and the compression is legitimate for investors comparing placements. It is treacherous for anyone inferring the labour relation from the result, because a rapid cycle can multiply a gentle per-cycle extraction into a handsome annual figure, while a slow cycle can disguise severe extraction behind a meagre one. Marx devoted the second volume of Capital largely to these circuits of capital, the least read and most quietly indispensable part of the project, precisely because the surface of economic life is woven from turnover differences. The distinction between surplus value and profit survives that volume too, emerging with a refinement: the rate of surplus value is most naturally stated per working period, the rate of profit most naturally per year on the advance, and comparing them without converting the time bases is a fourth species of denominator error, committed in print with impressive regularity.

## Three readers, one set of accounts

Abstractions earn their keep when they change what a person does with the same document, so consider three readers confronting the example firm's annual report in its simplified form: advance 500, wages 100, surplus embodied in results as a profit before subdivision of 100, return on the advance of twenty percent. The first reader is a trade union researcher preparing a pay claim. The headline profit rate tells the researcher almost nothing usable. The questions that matter concern the division of new value: how the split between the 100 of wages and the 100 of surplus has moved across bargaining rounds, whether productivity gains in the plant have enlarged the new value while the wage portion stagnated, and how much of the surplus drains away as interest and rent before the owners' portion is even struck. Surplus value's rate and the profit share are the researcher's instruments, and the denominator rule is what stops the employer's negotiators from answering a question about the working day with a figure about return on assets, a substitution that occurs in real disputes with metronomic reliability.

The second reader is an investor deciding whether to place funds in the firm or in a rival sector. For this reader the rate of profit is not a mystification but the operative signal, adjusted for risk, growth prospects, and the durability of the firm's pricing position. The investor who learned the distinction gains something subtler than a slogan: an understanding of why the signal moves. A profit rate buoyed by redistribution, by selling into prices of production far above the values the firm produces, rests on the economy-wide pool and on the firm's sheltered position within it, and it erodes when entry or technical change redraws the map. A profit rate grounded in a high rate of surplus value within the firm rests on the labour relation and its local conditions. The two foundations carry different risks, and an investor who cannot tell them apart misprices both. Marxian categories, handled this way, do not replace financial analysis; they annotate its central figure with an account of its supports.

The third reader is a policymaker weighing a proposal, perhaps a levy on high profits or an incentive for automation, and needing to know what each instrument touches. A tax assessed on profit as reported falls on a distributed magnitude, gathering shares that include interest and rent equivalents passing through the firm, and its incidence spreads along the chains of claims in ways the headline rate conceals. An automation incentive, meanwhile, raises the organic composition wherever it succeeds, pressing the general rate of profit downward by the identity while potentially raising the rate of surplus value per remaining worker, a pairing that looks contradictory in ordinary vocabulary and reads as straightforward mechanics in Marx's. The policymaker's errors, when they come, are usually denominator errors dressed as forecasts: expecting profitability and exploitation to rise or fall together, expecting a subsidy to plant to show up as a matching movement in the wage share, expecting the accounts of individual firms to register economy-wide relations. Three readers, one document, and the distinction does different work for each, which is the mark of a distinction worth the twenty thousand words spent on it.

## The strongest objection: a distinction preserved by definition

Fairness to the opposing case requires stating it at the strength its best advocates would recognise, without softening the edges in advance. The objection runs as follows. Profit is an observable magnitude. It appears in ledgers, tax filings, and market reports; two competent accountants, given the same records, will arrive at nearly the same figure, and where they differ the difference can be adjudicated by rules both accept. Surplus value is nothing of the kind. No ledger records it. No instrument measures it. It is computed only after accepting a theory of value that assigns all new value to labour, an assignment the computation then triumphantly confirms. The distinction between the two terms, on this view, is not a discovery about the economy but an artefact of a definitional scheme: define value so that labour alone creates it, and a residue called surplus value appears by construction, available to be contrasted with the profit everyone else was discussing all along. Change the definition, adopt a subjective or marginal productivity account of value instead, and the residue evaporates, leaving profit to be explained by the contributions and scarcities of all cooperating factors. A distinction that exists only inside one theory's vocabulary, the objection concludes, cannot arbitrate between that theory and its rivals, and presenting surplus value as the hidden reality behind profit begs the entire question the labour theory of value was supposed to answer.

The objection gains force from two supporting observations that deserve honest acknowledgment. The first concerns the direction of inference. In practice, researchers who estimate surplus value begin from observed profits, wages, and output values, then reclassify and rearrange those observables according to Marxian categories. The observable is doing the evidential work; the inferred category inherits every uncertainty of the reclassification, plus the assumptions that license it. The second concerns predictive exposure. A theory earns authority by risking itself on outcomes its rivals expect to differ. Critics ask which observation, if it went the wrong way, would persuade defenders of the distinction to abandon it, and they note that answers to that question are often slow in coming, which suggests the distinction functions less as a hypothesis than as a grammatical rule of a school. Versions of this challenge were pressed by economists from Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk onward, and the transformation literature, with its disputes over whether values can be converted into prices of production without contradiction or redundancy, can be read as the long technical shadow cast by this single philosophical objection. Any treatment that waves the challenge away with a paragraph about exploitation's obviousness has not understood it.

The reply begins by conceding the objection's premise in order to contest its conclusion. Yes, surplus value is inferred rather than directly observed, and yes, the inference runs from an accepted theory toward a category rather than from a measuring device toward a number. The concession, however, describes a great deal of respectable science and economics alike. Opportunity cost is inferred, not observed. The natural rate of unemployment is inferred. Potential output, expected inflation, the marginal product of a factor in a joint production process: none of these sits in a ledger, and each is computed within a framework whose assumptions shape the result. The relevant standard is not whether a category is theory laden, since all such categories are, but whether the framework housing it identifies patterns that rival frameworks must then explain by other means, and whether it does so with fewer ad hoc additions. Judged by that standard, the distinction puts a specific pattern on the table: the tendency of profit rates to equalise across sectors whose labour intensities differ enormously. In a world where each firm's gain were truly generated by its own total advance, as the surface grammar of profit suggests, capital-intensive and labour-intensive branches would be expected to show persistently different returns reflecting their different internal productions of gain. What requires explanation, on any theory, is why returns instead gravitate toward a common rate per unit of capital regardless of how much living labour each capital employs. Marx's framework predicts the gravitation from its premises: gains originate in one place, labour, and are then redistributed until capitals of equal size draw alike. Rival frameworks can accommodate the pattern, typically through entry, exit, and price adjustment stories, and accommodation is a perfectly honourable scientific move. The Marxian claim is the narrower one that the pattern falls out of the distinction as a theorem rather than being bolted on as a correction, and that the same premises simultaneously explain why the labour relation vanishes from the accounts. Consilience of that kind is what an inferred category can offer, and offering it is a legitimate answer to the charge of mere definition.

Two further points complete the reply, one modest and one methodological. The modest point is that the distinction retains value even for readers who reject the labour theory of value outright, because the denominator discipline survives the rejection. A reader who believes profit rewards risk, scarcity, and coordination still profits from knowing that a return on total assets and a ratio of gains to wages answer different questions, still benefits from asking what sits under any line, and still avoids the elementary error of inferring the treatment of labour from a return on funds. The comparison, in other words, teaches a transferable piece of statistical hygiene alongside its doctrinal content. The methodological point answers the falsifiability challenge on its own ground. The framework does generate exposures: if profit rates showed no tendency whatsoever to converge across sectors of differing composition over long horizons, if economies with rising organic composition displayed profit rates that tracked the rate of surplus value in lockstep rather than diverging from it, if the subdivision of gross profit into interest, rent, and enterprise portions bore no relation to the scale of surplus generated in production, the architecture connecting the two concepts would be in serious trouble. Defenders dispute how cleanly such tests can be run, which is a normal scientific argument, and the evidence articles in this series take up the record. What the objection establishes, at its strongest, is that the distinction cannot be proven from accounting data alone without theoretical commitments. What it does not establish is that the distinction is empty, since the same verdict, applied consistently, would dissolve half the working vocabulary of economics itself.

## Errors in circulation: a field guide to the standard muddles

Because this comparison is a decision point where readers sort themselves into competence or confusion, the recurring errors deserve a catalogue of their own, each stated in the form in which it actually circulates. The synonym error heads the list: surplus value introduced as Marx's term for profit, sometimes with the excuse that the difference is pedantic. The excuse fails on contact with the equalisation example, where a firm's profit and its produced surplus diverge by design, and it fails again on contact with interest, which no synonymy can accommodate without inventing production inside a loan contract. The denominator swap follows closely: a writer computes or quotes the rate of profit, then draws conclusions about exploitation, or computes the rate of surplus value and pronounces on the health of investment. Both moves appear in respectable journalism and in examination scripts, and both are caught by the single question of what sits under the line. A third error, the leakage fallacy, was flagged earlier and returns here because of its popularity: the belief that the gap between the two rates represents surplus siphoned away by banks and landlords. The gap is arithmetic, not drainage. Division into interest and rent is a separate process that would occur at any pair of rates, and conflating the two processes leaves a reader unable to say whether a change in the gap reflects mechanisation, redistribution, or neither.

A fourth error might be called the margin fallacy: treating a firm's profit margin on sales, its profit divided by revenue, as though it were either of Marx's ratios. Margin introduces revenue as a third denominator, one that includes the transferred value of materials passing through the firm, and it can be thin in a business built on enormous throughput and thick in a boutique operation, without tracking exploitation or return on advance in either case. Supermarket chains are the standard exhibit, reporting margins that look like rounding errors while moving volumes that generate vast absolute sums, some portion of which represents surplus value produced by workers throughout a global supply chain and captured at the till. A fifth error is the sectoral shortcut: assuming that a profitable industry must be an exploiting industry in exact proportion to its profits, or that a struggling industry exploits lightly. Composition and redistribution sever that proportion. A sixth error, beloved of polemic in both directions, treats the rate of profit as a moral thermometer for capitalism as a whole, rising rates proving vigour and virtue, falling rates proving decay, when the identity shows the rate to be a compound of exploitation and technique whose movements underdetermine any verdict until decomposed. The catalogue could extend, but a pattern unifies its entries: every error consists in letting one number do the work of two, and the remedy in every case is the same unglamorous act of reconstructing the fraction and asking where the numerator was born.

## Following the example firm's surplus through a full year of claims

Abstractions about pools and shares become reliable only when a reader has watched one sum of money survive a complete journey, so trace the example firm's 100 of surplus value from the moment the output sells until the last claimant is paid, keeping the article's stylised division fixed: profit of enterprise 40, interest 25, commercial profit 15, ground rent 20. Production ends with commodities embodying 600 of value: 400 replacing the constant advance, 100 replacing wages, 100 of surplus. The firm does not sell directly to the public. It sells to merchant houses, and it sells at a discount, because the merchants must cover their premises, their staff, their spoilage and markdowns, and still obtain the average return on the funds they commit to the trade. In the stylised figures, the merchants' share of 15 is carved out through that wholesale discount: value the firm's workers produced leaves the firm inside the price mechanism before any profit is struck on the firm's own books. A reader scanning only the manufacturer's accounts will therefore never see the full 100. Fifteen of it has already departed, wearing the costume of a lower selling price rather than an itemised payment, which is one reason commercial profit so easily passes for a creation of the selling process itself.

Rent leaves next, and it leaves in plain sight, because the firm operates from leased land and premises in this telling, with the ground beneath the plant owned by a separate proprietor. The rental payment, insofar as it represents ground rent of 20 rather than payment for buildings the landlord actually constructed and maintains, is a transfer of surplus value already produced. Order the payments differently in a given firm and nothing structural changes, but the visibility does: rent appears in accounts as a cost of occupancy, indistinguishable on the page from the cost of heating, and generations of business writers have accordingly treated it as a price of a productive input like any other. Marx's classification insists on the asymmetry. The heating fuel was produced and its value transfers. The ground, as ground, was not produced, and the payment for its use is a toll levied by title on the surplus generated by those who work upon it. Interest then claims its 25. The firm expanded on borrowed money, and the lender's portion is struck by applying the going rate of interest to the principal, a calculation that makes no reference to the firm's workers, its composition, or even, within wide limits, its actual results. The interest bill arrives with the impersonality of a utility charge, and the firm's owners feel it as an external condition of doing business rather than as a share of their own operation's unpaid labour, which is exactly the phenomenology Marx's analysis of interest bearing capital predicts.

What remains, 40, settles into the owners' hands as profit of enterprise, and its psychological career is the most instructive of the four. The owners compare it, not with the surplus their workforce produced, but with the total they and the lender together committed, and they experience it as the yield of their organising activity: the product line chosen, the suppliers bargained down, the shifts arranged, the risks carried through a nervous season. Some of that experience corresponds to real functions, since coordination and judgement genuinely occur and genuinely affect whether surplus is produced and retained or squandered. The Marxian analysis does not deny the functions. It denies the inference from function to source. The manager who supervises brilliantly and the manager who supervises badly preside over the same mechanism in kind: workers dividing their day between replacing their wages and working beyond that point. Supervision can raise or lower the surplus, defend it against leakage, and steer its reinvestment. It does not follow that supervision creates the surplus in the sense in which the surplus labour creates it, any more than a sluice creates the river it channels. Hold the journey in mind whole, 15 gone in a discount, 20 gone in a toll, 25 gone in a financial charge, 40 remaining and misdescribing itself, and the claim that profit is a distributed appearance of surplus value stops sounding like a slogan. It reads as an itinerary.

## Disputes inside the house: what Marxists argue about among themselves

An honest comparison should report that the distinction, while foundational, sits upstream of disputes that divide Marx's own successors, because a reader who meets those disputes later, unprepared, may mistake them for refutations of the distinction itself. The transformation problem is the largest. Marx's procedure in Volume Three moves from values, computed in labour time, to prices of production, computed so that profit rates equalise, and critics since the nineteenth century have pressed on the joint: the inputs to production, the machines and materials a firm buys, are purchased at prices of production rather than at values, so a fully consistent system must transform input prices and output prices together, and whether the two aggregate identities Marx asserted, total price equals total value and total profit equals total surplus value, can both survive a rigorous simultaneous treatment has been contested across generations of formal work. Defenders answer through several reconstructions, including readings that treat the transformation as a temporal sequence rather than a simultaneous system, and readings that redefine the monetary expression of labour time so the identities hold by construction. The dispute is genuine, technical, and unresolved in the sense that no settlement commands the whole field. Its scope, however, is specific. It concerns the exact mapping between the value system and the price system. It does not concern whether the rate of surplus value and the rate of profit have different denominators, since that is true by the definitions both sides of the dispute employ, nor whether interest and rent are intelligible as shares, which every reconstruction preserves.

A second internal dispute concerns the status of unproductive labour and therefore the boundaries of the pool. Marx's texts support more than one classification of commercial, financial, and state employment, and modern Marxian economists diverge measurably when they decide which wage earners count as producing surplus value and which count as being paid out of it. The divergence moves empirical estimates a great deal, as the measurement articles in this series document, while leaving the comparison in this article intact, because the comparison needs only the weaker claim that some labour produces the pool and that profit, interest, and rent are drawn from it under descriptions that hide the drawing. A third dispute, quieter but philosophically rich, asks whether the inversion language commits Marx to a theory of false consciousness, as though workers and capitalists alike were dupes of an illusion that education could dispel. Careful readers resist that flattening. The appearances Marx analyses are objective in the sense that they really govern behaviour and really structure the choices available inside the system; a capitalist who saw through profit to surplus value would still have to earn profit or exit the market. The distinction thus survives its internal critics in the way foundational distinctions usually do, not by being beyond argument but by being the ground on which the arguments are conducted.

## The verdict, and which term answers which question

Comparison and decision articles owe the reader a ruling, and the ruling here concerns not which concept is true and which false, since both name real magnitudes, but which concept is competent to answer which family of questions, with the deciding factor named. The deciding factor is the location of the question. Questions about production, about what happens inside the working day, about how new value divides between those who create it and those who appropriate it, belong to surplus value and its rate. Questions about allocation, about where funds flow, what return a commitment obtains, how gains are shared among owners, lenders, merchants, and landowners, belong to profit and its rate. The verdict follows: the two terms are not rivals and not synonyms but instruments calibrated to different stations of the same circuit, and nearly every confused argument in this territory can be diagnosed as a station error, an answer fetched from the wrong point of the circuit and delivered with confidence. A wage negotiation argued in profit rates and an investment decision argued in exploitation rates are the same mistake in mirror image, each importing a magnitude across a boundary it cannot cross without conversion.

Conversion, where it is legitimate, runs through the material this article has assembled, and its steps can be rehearsed as a discipline. Start from production: establish the new value created and its division, which yields the rate of surplus value and the profit share. Introduce composition: state the constant advance honestly, distinguishing what is consumed in the period from what is committed in durable form, which yields the rate of profit for a single cycle. Introduce time: annualise through turnover, which yields the figure investors actually compare. Introduce distribution: follow the shares out through prices of production, interest, rent, and commercial margins, which yields the profit any particular firm or claimant finally books. Each step is a genuine transformation, adding information about technique, time, and social allocation while subtracting visibility of the origin. A reader who can walk the steps in both directions, from a headline profit figure back toward the working day with explicit allowances at each transformation, and from a working day forward to the headline with the same allowances, has converted the distinction from a memorised contrast into a working instrument. That bidirectional fluency, rather than any allegiance, is the competence this article set out to build.

Certain questions, finally, belong to neither term alone, and naming them guards against overreach in the opposite direction. Whether capitalism tends toward crisis involves the ratios developed here but also credit, realisation, disproportionality between departments, and the world market; the crisis guide linked earlier maps those families without reducing them to one ratio. Whether a given society's distribution is just involves the technical apparatus plus a normative theory that the apparatus by itself does not supply, a boundary Marx himself policed when he mocked programmes that treated distribution as an independent sphere. Whether a particular firm treats its workers decently involves wages, safety, voice, and security, of which the rate of surplus value captures only the division of value, a real dimension and not the whole of a working life. The distinction earns its authority partly by the modesty of its jurisdiction: it explains the form gains take and the source from which they are drawn, and it leaves adjacent questions to the inquiries that own them.

## Checking a claim before repeating it

Public argument about profit moves fast, and a short verification routine, applied before a striking figure is repeated, prevents most of the errors catalogued in this article. The routine has the shape of an interrogation, and each stage of it corresponds to a transformation already described. Ask first what the figure is a percentage of, and refuse to proceed until the denominator is named in plain words: wages, total advance, assets, equity, revenue, or sales all circulate under the single label of profitability in ordinary reporting. Ask next which period and which turnover rhythm the figure summarises, since a quarterly margin and an annual return on a slowly rotating advance are not comparable quantities wearing similar dress. Ask what has been deducted before the figure was struck: interest, rent, depreciation, and tax are treated as costs in some presentations and as distributions of the gain in others, and the same firm can be made to look flush or threadbare by moving those lines above or below the point where profit is declared. Ask, where the claim concerns exploitation, what evidence independent of the profit figure bears on the division of new value, because that division is the actual subject and the profit figure is at best a distant proxy for it. Ask, finally, whether the claim is about one firm or about the system, since the pool logic licenses aggregate statements that firm level figures routinely contradict in detail.

Consider the routine exercised on a familiar headline pattern, stripped of any particular company's identity: profits at a large retailer are reported to have risen sharply, and commentary concludes within the hour that exploitation of the retailer's staff has intensified in proportion. The routine slows the conclusion down. The reported figure is a share of an economy-wide process, swollen perhaps by redistribution toward a firm with exceptional buying power, whose pressure on suppliers transmits demands into factories and farms far from its own payroll. Its own staff may indeed be working under an intensified regime, or may be untouched while the gain originates in procurement, logistics, property, and financial operations. The profit figure cannot discriminate among those sources. Evidence about the division of new value in the retailer's own operations, and about conditions in the supplier workplaces where much of the relevant surplus labour is performed, can. None of this makes the headline useless. It makes the headline the beginning of an inquiry rather than its end, which is where a reader trained by the denominator rule will instinctively place it. Scholars building notes on the distinction for repeated use often keep the three formulas, the identity linking them, and this interrogation routine together on a single reference page; a structured note for exactly that purpose is maintained in the [Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) companion, where the ratios sit alongside the working day material they depend on.

## The circuit of capital, and where each term lives within it

Marx summarises the whole movement in a compact formula: money is advanced to buy commodities, including labour power and means of production, those commodities enter production, and the resulting commodities are sold for more money than was advanced, a sequence written as M to C to M prime, where the prime marks the increment. The formula is worth dwelling on because each term of this article's comparison occupies a distinct station within it. Surplus value is created in the middle station, in the production interval that the dots of the formula conceal, when labour power is consumed and the working day divides into its two portions. Profit is registered at the final station, when the enlarged sum returns and is compared with the sum that set out. Between creation and registration lie the sale, with its risks and discounts, and the whole apparatus of distribution that decides whose hands the increment finally warms. A reader who pictures the circuit stops asking why the two magnitudes differ, since it would be astonishing if a magnitude created in the hidden middle of a process matched, firm by firm, a magnitude registered at the end of it after every intervening claim had been honoured.

The circuit also clarifies why the capitalist's own experience is organised around profit rather than surplus value, without any imputation of bad faith. The capitalist's decisions are taken at the stations of advance and return: how much to commit, to which line, at what expected yield, with what borrowing structure. The middle station appears in that deliberation as cost and throughput, as wages negotiated and output targeted, not as a moral drama about unpaid hours. Even a capitalist who had mastered Volume One would continue to deliberate in rates of profit, because that is the language in which alternatives present themselves and in which failure announces itself. Marx's analysis is therefore not an accusation that businesspeople use the wrong concept in their daily work. It is the claim that the concept their daily work requires conceals, by its construction, the one relation his theory exists to expose. Systems of categories can be simultaneously indispensable for practice and misleading for understanding, and recognising that doubleness is a large part of what reading Volume Three trains.

Reproduction adds a last turn. The increment registered as profit does not simply vanish into consumption; a portion is capitalised, thrown back into the circuit as additional advance, enlarging the scale on which surplus labour is organised in the next round. From the standpoint of the system, profit is thus surplus value in its travelling clothes, the form in which yesterday's unpaid labour becomes tomorrow's expanded command over living labour. The point matters for interpretation because it blocks a sentimental reading in which surplus value is somehow real and profit merely cosmetic. Profit is the operative form, the shape in which the surplus actually moves the world, hires the next shift, builds the next plant, and bids for the next loan. The distinction this article defends is between source and form, not between reality and mirage, and both sides of it must be kept solid if the analysis is to explain anything.

## Monopoly, finance, and the widening distance between the terms

Two features of developed capitalism stretch the distance between production and receipt still further, and a reader equipped with the distinction can describe both without strain. Monopoly, in the broad sense of durable barriers that shelter a firm or sector from the full force of entry, interrupts the equalisation of profit rates. A sheltered capital can hold a return above the average for long stretches, drawing an extra share of the pool through prices its rivals cannot undercut. In Marxian vocabulary such monopoly profit is redistribution with a lock on it: the sheltered firm's workers need not produce a unit more of surplus for the firm to receive a great deal more of the total. The distinction predicts exactly this decoupling wherever barriers are strong, and it equally predicts the ferocity with which firms defend patents, licences, platforms, and brands, since what is being defended is a claim on the pool rather than a technique of production. Observers who equate profit with produced surplus must read a monopoly's earnings as evidence of extraordinary production somewhere inside it. Observers who separate the terms read the same earnings as evidence of an extraordinary position, and position, unlike production, can be legislated, litigated, and lost overnight.

Finance stretches the distance in the dimension of time. Credit allows claims on surplus value to be issued, traded, and compounded long before the underlying labour has been performed, and financial instruments capitalise expected future shares into present prices that then behave like wealth in their own right. Marx glimpsed the structure in his treatment of interest bearing capital and fictitious capital, where a title to a revenue stream acquires a market price governed by the rate of interest rather than by any value produced in a corresponding process. The distinction handles the phenomenon by insisting on the direction of dependence: however elaborate the tower of claims becomes, its storeys are titles to portions of surplus value, and a crisis, in one of its aspects, is the moment when the tower's nominal storeys are forcibly reconciled with the pool actually produced beneath them. A framework that treated profit as self-generating, money breeding money by nature, would have nothing to reconcile and no vocabulary for the violence of the adjustment. Here again the comparison does explanatory work beyond its home territory, which is the practical test of whether a distinction deserves the space this article has given it.

## Three practice variations, worked in the open

Fluency with ratios is a motor skill as much as an insight, and it develops by computing fresh cases rather than rereading settled ones, so this section poses three variations and works each one fully, inviting the reader to cover the answers and derive them independently. First variation: a bakery partnership advances 60 in constant capital across ovens, flour, and fuel for a period, and 40 in wages. The bakers add 80 of new value, of which 40 replaces the wage advance and 40 remains as surplus. For the rate of surplus value, divide 40 by 40 to get one hundred percent. For the rate of profit, divide 40 by the total advance of 100 to get forty percent. The organic composition is 60 over 40, one and a half to one. The profit share of new value is 40 over 80, one half. Notice how the small scale and light equipment of the bakery push the two rates closer together than in the example firm: with composition low, the filter between exploitation and profit is thin, and the owners' forty percent return testifies fairly directly to the even division of the bakers' day. A reader who expected the two rates always to stand far apart has learned something about composition from a single comparison.

Second variation: a data services firm advances 950 in constant capital, dominated by computing infrastructure and licensed systems, and 50 in wages for a small, highly paid technical staff. The staff adds 100 of new value, half replacing wages and half as surplus, so surplus value is 50. Its rate of surplus value is 50 over 50, one hundred percent, identical to the bakery and to the example firm. Its rate of profit is 50 over 1,000, five percent. The organic composition is nineteen to one. The profit share of new value is again one half. Three workplaces, three utterly different profit rates, forty, twenty, and five percent, stand on an identical division of the working day. Any ranking of the three by how exploitative they are, constructed from profit figures, inverts or scrambles the underlying sameness. Any ranking by return on advance, constructed for placing funds, orders them correctly for that purpose. The variation also isolates the pure effect of composition, since nothing about labour differs across the cases: only the mass of dead labour each living worker animates. When a single variable does all the work in an example, the example has done its teaching properly.

Third variation: keep the data services firm's advance exactly as stated, 950 and 50, but let a tight labour market and organised bargaining shift the division of new value, so the staff now adds 100 of new value of which 75 replaces an increased wage bill's equivalent and 25 remains as surplus, with variable capital accordingly restated at 75 and constant capital at 925 to preserve the total advance of 1,000. Recompute carefully, because the exercise is designed to punish haste. Now the surplus value ratio stands at 25 over 75, one third, roughly thirty three percent. Now the profit ratio stands at 25 over 1,000, two and a half percent. The organic composition is 925 over 75, roughly twelve to one. The profit share of new value is one quarter. Two lessons hide in the arithmetic. The mild mannered two and a half percent profit rate coexists with a workforce still performing a quarter of its new value creation as surplus labour, so thin profitability is no certificate of an exploitation free workplace. And the composition moved between the variations even though no machine was bought or sold, because the value composition responds to the wage level as well as to the physical plant, a reminder that the organic composition is a value ratio with a technical basis rather than a count of machines. Readers who can reproduce all three variations unaided, explaining each ratio's movement in a sentence, command the material of this article at the level examinations and editorial desks actually test.

## Lengthening the day and cheapening the wage basket, read through the two rates

The two great routes to a larger surplus, lengthening or intensifying the working day and cheapening the goods that reproduce labour power, leave different signatures across the ratios, and reading the signatures is a further exercise the distinction makes possible. Take the example firm and extend its aggregated day so that surplus labour grows while necessary labour holds: the workforce now adds 240 of new value, of which 100 replaces wages and 140 remains as surplus. The surplus value ratio climbs to one hundred forty percent. The profit rate climbs too, to 140 over 500, twenty eight percent, because the denominators have not moved; only the numerator grew. The profit share of new value rises from one half to seven twelfths. Absolute surplus value, produced by extension, thus announces itself in both rates moving together, and an observer watching profitability alone would, for once, be tracking exploitation fairly closely, since composition stood still while the day stretched.

Productivity growth in the industries supplying workers' consumption writes a subtler signature. Suppose machinery and methods in food, clothing, and housing production improve until the value of labour power falls: the same standard of life that required 100 of wages can now be reproduced for 80, and the firm restates its variable capital at 80 while constant capital adjusts to 420 to preserve a total advance of 500. The working day keeps its length, yet its division shifts, because necessary labour now absorbs a smaller portion: the workforce adds 200 of new value, of which 80 replaces the cheaper labour power and 120 remains as surplus. Relative surplus value has been produced without an hour being added. The surplus value ratio leaps to one hundred fifty percent. The profit rate rises to 120 over 500, twenty four percent. Composition rises as well, from four to one to five and a quarter to one, since the wage portion of the advance shrank while the constant portion absorbed the difference. All three ratios move, each telling a different part of the story: exploitation intensified, profitability improved by less than exploitation did, and the technical balance of the advance tilted further toward dead labour even though the immediate cause was cheaper wage goods rather than new plant in this firm.

Set the two routes side by side and a general lesson about reading economic history emerges. Periods dominated by extension tend to show profit rates and exploitation rates rising in tandem, and the social conflict of such periods gathers around hours, which is why the struggle over the working day occupies the dramatic centre of Volume One. Periods dominated by productivity growth in wage goods show the rates diverging in proportion as composition climbs, and conflict migrates toward the wage bargain, the price of the consumption basket, and the distribution of productivity gains. An analyst who possesses only the profit rate sees the second period as a mild improvement in returns and misses the sharp intensification underneath. An analyst who possesses only the surplus value ratio sees the intensification and misses how thoroughly mechanisation has diluted its effect on the return that governs investment. The comparison defended in this article is not an ornament on Marx's system. It is the minimum equipment required to describe either period without deceiving oneself, and its two worked routes through the example firm give the reader a template that transfers to any set of figures the historical record supplies.

## For students and exam candidates

Examiners return to this distinction because it separates candidates who have memorised vocabulary from candidates who can operate the machinery, and the operating test is almost always numerical or definitional under time pressure. Secure the three formulas first, each with its denominator spoken aloud: surplus value over variable capital, surplus value over constant plus variable capital, constant over variable capital. Then secure the identity connecting them, since a question that gives two ratios and asks for the third is a gift to anyone who can divide the rate of surplus value by one plus the organic composition. Practise the example firm figures until the four numbers, one hundred percent, twenty percent, four to one, and one half, arrive together as a set. In essay answers, lead with the denominator rule before narrating Volume Three, because a marker who sees the deciding distinction in the opening lines reads the rest with confidence. Name the division into profit of enterprise, interest, commercial profit, and rent whenever a question asks where surplus value goes, and reserve a closing sentence for the definitional objection and its reply through the equalisation pattern, which converts a descriptive answer into an evaluative one.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The denominator exercise organises a productive session better than any opening lecture. Give the class the example firm in fragments: the wage bill and the surplus first, inviting them to compute a percentage, then release the constant capital and invite them to compute another, and let the collision between one hundred percent and twenty percent generate the questions the session exists to answer. Students who discover the denominator shift in their own arithmetic retain it far longer than students who receive it as a definition. Follow with the three capitals and ask each group to defend, from the figures alone, which workplace a union should target and which a fund should buy, and the ensuing disagreement teaches the station doctrine without further intervention. Close by distributing a paragraph of financial journalism and asking the class to mark every percentage whose denominator goes unnamed. Assessment design benefits from the same exercise: set one question in which a firm with the lower profit rate exploits its workforce more intensely than its rival, and require candidates to display the denominators that make the result possible. Scripts that merely swap the two terms can then be diagnosed in a single marginal comment rather than a page of corrections, and the class learns that precision, not volume, is what the marking rewards.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For research purposes the distinction functions as a warning label on sources. Company accounts, tax records, and trade statistics were compiled inside the cost price form, so every magnitude extracted from them arrives pre-transformed, with wages, depreciation, interest, and rent arrayed as parallel costs and no line corresponding to surplus value at all. Estimates of exploitation built from such records are reconstructions whose results move with classification decisions, especially the treatment of unproductive labour, and honest reporting of any figure carries its classification with it. Historians face the mirror problem in narrative sources, where memoirs and board papers describe profit as the natural fruit of enterprise and must be read against the grain for traces of the working day. Journalists, finally, should treat any single profitability figure as the start of the denominator interrogation set out in this article, and should be especially wary of the inference from a firm's margin to the condition of its workers, which the pool and equalisation mechanisms forbid in both directions.

## A note on the words themselves

Vocabulary traps surround the distinction, and a reader who can name them avoids a class of errors that no formula can repair afterwards. Surplus value is sometimes shortened to surplus in secondary writing, a compression that invites confusion with the surplus product, the physical excess of output over what producers consume, and with the economist's consumer surplus, a welfare measure from a different theoretical universe altogether. Marx keeps the value term attached because his category concerns value created and appropriated, not physical abundance as such: a society can pile up a surplus product in kind while no surplus value exists, wherever producers own their conditions of work and no wage relation organises production. Profit suffers the opposite affliction, an excess of near neighbours rather than a shortage of precision. Gross profit, net profit, operating profit, and trading profit each draw the deduction line at a different place, including or excluding interest, tax, depreciation, and exceptional items by conventions that vary between jurisdictions and industries. None of those bookkeeping variants maps cleanly onto Marx's profit, which is struck before the subdivision into interest and rent and after only the costs of production proper. Translating between a published accounts category and the Marxian category therefore always requires an explicit bridge, stating which lines were moved and why, and any writer who performs the translation silently has hidden the most contestable step of the argument inside a vocabulary choice. Precision about words is not pedantry in this territory. It is the visible portion of precision about denominators, and the two precisions stand or fall together.

## Closing verdict

Surplus value and profit are two stations of one circuit, and the whole comparison reduces, without remainder, to keeping the stations apart. Surplus value is produced when labour power is used beyond the point at which its own value has been replaced, and it is measured, as a rate, against the wages that purchased the capacity to work. Profit is received when the pooled surplus of the whole economy is distributed through prices, credit, property, and trade, and it is measured, as a rate, against the total funds a claimant has committed. The denominator rule names the first difference, the pool names the second, and the inversion warning names the appearance that makes both differences hard to see: gain presenting itself as the natural yield of things rather than as a claim on the divided working day. A reader who holds those three ideas together can follow Marx from the factory chapters of Volume One to the revenue chapters of Volume Three without losing the thread, can read a balance sheet without being read by it, and can say precisely, in any argument where the words are traded as synonyms, what the difference is and why it decides the question at hand.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between surplus value and profit?

Surplus value is the new value workers produce beyond the value of their labour power, born inside production as the result of surplus labour. Profit is the form in which a firm receives a share of the total surplus value produced across the economy, reckoned against the whole capital it advanced. The two differ in source, in recipient, and above all in denominator: the rate of surplus value divides by variable capital alone, while the rate of profit divides by constant and variable capital together, which is why profit looks like a yield on funds rather than a claim on labour.

### Q: Is profit just another word for surplus value?

No, and treating the words as synonyms breaks every later argument in Marx. Surplus value names a magnitude produced in a particular workplace through the division of the working day. Profit names a magnitude received through distribution, after competition, prices of production, interest, rent, and commercial margins have moved value between firms and sectors. A firm can book profit while producing no surplus value of its own, as merchants and landlords do, and a firm can organise the extraction of surplus value while booking a loss. Synonyms cannot come apart in both directions.

### Q: Why is the rate of profit lower than the rate of surplus value?

Both rates share the same numerator, the surplus value produced, but their denominators differ. The rate of surplus value divides the surplus by variable capital, the wage bill alone. The rate of profit divides the identical surplus by total capital advanced, constant plus variable, which adds machinery, materials, and premises to the base. A given amount divided by a larger base yields a smaller percentage. In the worked example used in this article, a surplus of 100 over wages of 100 gives a rate of surplus value of one hundred percent, while the same 100 over a total advance of 500 gives a rate of profit of twenty percent.

### Q: How is surplus value divided between capitalists?

The total surplus value produced across the economy forms a pool that rival claimants divide through the price, credit, and property systems. Industrial or commercial firms retain a portion as profit, shaped by the average rate of profit that competition tends to establish across capitals of equal size. Money lenders take interest as a share of gross profit. Merchants take commercial profit through the discount between buying and selling prices. Landowners take ground rent for access to land. In the stylised division used in this article, a surplus of 100 settles as 40 of profit of enterprise, 25 of interest, 15 of commercial profit, and 20 of rent.

### Q: Where do interest and rent come from in Marx's system?

Both are portions of surplus value, not independent sources of wealth. Interest is the share of gross profit that a functioning capitalist hands to a lender for the use of money capital, which is why interest can appear to be a natural growth of money itself while actually drawing on unpaid labour performed in the borrower's enterprise. Ground rent is the share a landowner captures by holding title to land that production requires, whether through the advantages of better soil and location or through the barrier that landed property places in the way of free entry. Neither the lender nor the landowner produces the value received.

### Q: Does a loss making firm still extract surplus value?

It can, because extraction and successful appropriation are different events. Extraction concerns production: whether the workforce performs surplus labour and creates value beyond the equivalent of its wages. A firm whose employees work such hours has organised extraction whatever its final accounts show. The surplus may then be drained away by interest charges, rent, falling prices, or competition, leaving the firm with a small profit, no profit, or an outright loss. Judging exploitation from the bottom line mistakes the outcome of distribution for the facts of production, and bankruptcy records contain many firms that exploited their workers thoroughly while failing as businesses.

### Q: What is the organic composition of capital?

The organic composition of capital is the ratio of constant capital to variable capital, the value laid out on means of production set against the value laid out on labour power, insofar as that value ratio reflects the underlying technical relation between the mass of machinery and materials and the number of workers who operate them. In the worked example, constant capital of 400 against variable capital of 100 gives a composition of four to one. A rising composition registers mechanisation, and it is the hinge between the two rates: the rate of profit equals the rate of surplus value divided by one plus the organic composition.

### Q: Why does profit appear to come from the whole capital advanced?

Because of how the gain is calculated in practice. The capitalist compares the sale proceeds with cost price, the undifferentiated sum of everything laid out, and since wages sit inside that sum alongside machinery and materials, the excess over cost price presents itself as a product of the total outlay. Nothing in the transaction singles out the wage portion as the part whose use expanded in production. Marx calls the result an inversion: a relation between people, the division of their working day, appears as a property of things, a fertility belonging to capital as such, and the appearance is generated by the accounting form rather than invented by apologists.

### Q: Is Marx's profit the same as profit in mainstream economics?

The two concepts overlap as residuals, what remains from revenue after costs, but they diverge in construction and explanation. Mainstream usage distinguishes accounting profit from economic profit, which deducts the opportunity cost of the owner's funds and labour, whereas Marx counts interest on the owner's own capital as a portion of profit rather than a cost. Mainstream theory explains the residual through risk, scarcity, innovation, and temporary monopoly. Marx explains the aggregate residual as transformed surplus value and treats those factors as mechanisms that distribute the pool or defend a firm's share of it. The measures answer related but distinct questions.

### Q: What is the difference between a markup and surplus value?

A markup is the difference between a buying price and a selling price at one point in a chain of transactions, expressed as a percentage of cost or of price. Surplus value is value created in production when labour continues beyond necessary labour. A merchant's markup represents commercial profit, a share of surplus value produced elsewhere and captured through the price gap, not value created by the act of selling. A producer's high markup may likewise draw value from elsewhere through favourable prices. Markups locate a claimant along a distribution chain. Surplus value identifies an origin, and confusing a distribution coordinate with an origin produces matching errors in both directions.

### Q: How does the profit of enterprise differ from interest in Marx's account?

Both are portions of one gross profit, split when the functioning capitalist works with borrowed money. Interest goes to the lender as the price of money capital, determined by the competition between lenders and borrowers and experienced as a natural yield of ownership. Profit of enterprise is the residue the borrower retains after interest is paid, and because it remains with the person who organises production, it tends to be misdescribed as a wage for management or a reward for entrepreneurial skill. Marx treats the split as a division of surplus value between two kinds of owner, in which the residue's active appearance conceals its kinship with interest.

### Q: Why can two firms with the same rate of surplus value earn different rates of profit?

Because the rate of profit also depends on the organic composition, which the rate of surplus value ignores. Two workforces may each divide the day evenly, giving a rate of surplus value of one hundred percent in both firms, while one firm advances mostly wages and the other advances a mountain of equipment per worker. The surplus is then divided by very different totals. A bakery advancing 100 in total might earn forty percent, while a data services firm advancing 1,000 on the same division of the day earns five percent. Identical exploitation, divergent profitability: the denominators decide.

### Q: What should a student check first when a question mixes the two rates together?

Check the denominators before touching any numbers. Identify what each percentage is a percentage of, wages alone or the total advance, and rewrite both rates as explicit fractions with their components named. Most mixed questions collapse once that reconstruction is done, because the apparent contradiction between a high rate of exploitation and a low rate of profit is an artefact of comparing percentages built on different bases. After the denominators, check whether the question concerns production or distribution, whether interest and rent have been deducted, and whether prices of production have redistributed the surplus away from the firm being discussed.

### Q: How does commercial profit arise if commerce produces no value?

Commercial capital earns profit by receiving a share of the total surplus value, not by creating value in the act of exchange. Industrial firms sell to merchants below the final selling price, surrendering a portion of the surplus embodied in their goods so that merchants will perform the work of realising value: storing, transporting, displaying, and selling. That discount funds the merchant's costs, including the wages of commercial employees, and yields the merchant the average rate of profit on the capital tied up in shops and warehouses. Commerce is necessary labour for the system and unproductive of new value within Marx's classification, two judgements that coexist without contradiction.

### Q: What does the denominator rule help a journalist avoid when reporting company profits?

It helps a journalist avoid treating a return on total capital, assets, or sales as evidence about how a company's workers are treated or how the value they create is divided. A high profit rate can reflect redistribution toward a sheltered firm rather than intense extraction on its own premises, and a thin margin can sit atop a supply chain where surplus extraction is severe but captured elsewhere. Before drawing conclusions, the rule prompts the journalist to ask what the percentage is a percentage of, what was deducted before it was struck, and what separate evidence bears on the division of new value between wages and surplus.
