## Eighteen Days That Shook Egypt

On the morning of 25 January 2011, a date the Egyptian state had designated National Police Day, tens of thousands of Egyptians began streaming toward Tahrir Square in the center of Cairo, in the opening hours of the revolution that would end thirty years of rule. They came from the working-class districts of Shubra and Imbaba, from the universities of Ain Shams and Cairo, from the commuter trains that carried them in from Giza and beyond. Many carried the Egyptian flag. Some carried banners calling for bread, freedom, and social justice. The police, whose holiday it was, met them with tear gas and batons. By nightfall, clashes had spread to Alexandria, Suez, Ismailia, and Mansoura. What began as a called day of protest against police brutality did not end when the day ended. Eighteen days later, on 11 February 2011, Hosni Mubarak, who had governed Egypt for nearly thirty years, resigned the presidency. The revolution, as it was called by its participants and by much of the world, had toppled a ruler many Egyptians had assumed would die in office, and it had done so in less than three weeks.

![Protesters filling Tahrir Square during the 2011 Egyptian revolution - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

That speed is the first thing any honest account must confront, and it is this article's central claim: the 2011 Egyptian uprising was an eighteen-days-that-shook-Egypt event, a mass mobilization so rapid that it compressed decades of accumulated grievance into a fortnight and a half of street action, and understanding it requires holding together both the astonishing velocity of Mubarak's fall and the long, unfinished aftermath that followed it. The swiftness was real. A regime that had survived assassination attempts, regional wars, economic shocks, and three decades of opposition was brought down not by a coup or an invasion but by people standing in squares, and it happened so fast that most Egyptians, most observers, and even most of the protesters themselves were still absorbing the fact weeks later. But the aftermath was equally real, and it complicates any simple story of triumph. The Supreme Council of the Armed Forces took power, elections followed, a Muslim Brotherhood president was elected and then overthrown by the military within a year, and Egypt entered a new period of authoritarian rule under Abdel Fattah el-Sisi. Historians, political scientists, and Egyptians themselves continue to argue over what 2011 actually was: a revolution, a revolt, a coup-enabled uprising, or something in between. This article does not settle that argument by fiat. It presents the event at its strongest, examines the competing interpretations, and leaves the reader with the evidence to judge.

What the article establishes is straightforward in outline, even where the meaning stays contested. The 2011 uprising was a nationwide wave of protests, strikes, and civil disobedience that began on 25 January and ended with Mubarak's resignation on 11 February, with mass protest centered on Tahrir Square in Cairo as its most visible and symbolically charged arena. Tahrir, a vast traffic circle and plaza in downtown Cairo adjacent to government ministries, the parliament, and the state television building, became a tent city, a stage, and a fortress. The question of how it forced Mubarak out is a story of escalating pressure: marches and street battles in the first days, a police withdrawal from the streets on 28 January that turned the army into the deciding institution, labor strikes spreading across the country in the final days, and a regime that lost the loyalty of its own security forces and the acquiescence of its business elite faster than it could recover either. The role of grievances is equally clear, and it predates 2011 by decades: emergency law, police torture, rigged elections, grinding poverty alongside crony wealth, and the prospect of a hereditary succession to Mubarak's son Gamal had fused into a widely shared conviction that the system could not be reformed from within. Social media and satellite television played a real but contested part: Facebook pages and Twitter feeds helped organizers coordinate, and Al Jazeera broadcast the square's images to the Arab world, but the decisive forces on the ground were organized labor, neighborhood committees, football ultras, and ordinary people making individual decisions to march. As for why the outcome remains debated, the answer is that the same institutions that let Mubarak fall, above all the military, survived the fall and shaped everything after it, which is why the word revolution is used here as the participants used it, while the analytical verdict stays open.

### The Square and the World

Tahrir Square was not chosen at random. Egyptians had been occupying it in protests large and small for a century, from the 1919 revolution against the British to the anti-war and pro-democracy demonstrations of the 2000s. What made January 2011 different was scale and persistence: hundreds of thousands of people held the square for days at a time, in defiance of curfews and attacks by plainclothes thugs, and their occupation was mirrored in Alexandria's Qaed Ibrahim Square, in Suez, where some of the fiercest fighting occurred, and in dozens of other cities and towns. The images traveled globally within hours. Satellite channels carried them across the Arab world, where rulers in Tunis, Amman, Sanaa, and Manama watched with alarm. Western governments, long accustomed to treating Mubarak as a pillar of regional stability, issued statements that shifted week by week, and then day by day, from calls for restraint to calls for reform to the eventual conclusion that the president had to go. The speed of the fall shaped the world's interpretation of it: a regime that collapsed in eighteen days looked like a revolution, whatever the complications that followed.

### The Naming Question

The word deserves a moment of care, because the stakes of naming are high for Egyptians. Those who filled the squares call it a revolution, thawra, and point to the overthrow of a thirty-year ruler by mass popular action as the defining fact. Skeptics argue that a revolution changes the underlying structure of power, and that since the military remained intact and returned to direct rule within two and a half years, what happened was an uprising or a revolt that decapitated a regime without transforming the state. A third reading treats it as a revolution interrupted or betrayed, with the counterrevolution organized by the very institutions the uprising failed to dismantle. This article uses the participants' term, since a historical account must take seriously the self-understanding of the people who made the event, but it keeps these interpretations in view throughout, because the debate is not academic pedantry. It is about what Egyptians won and lost in those eighteen days, and what the rest of the Arab world learned from watching.

## The Long Fuse: Grievances Behind the Explosion

The fuse of 2011 was long. To understand why Egyptians poured into the streets in numbers that startled even the organizers, one must go back not weeks but decades, to the accumulated injuries and insults of life under a system that combined emergency rule, police violence, economic exclusion, and political theater into a daily reality that an entire generation had known and nothing else. Mubarak had taken power in 1981 following [the assassination that brought Mubarak to power](/2016/04/01/anwar-sadat-and-camp-david/), and the state of emergency declared in the aftermath of that killing had never been lifted. For thirty years, Egyptians lived under laws that allowed detention without charge, banned most public gatherings, and placed political life under the supervision of the security services. The emergency was routinely justified by the regime as a weapon against terrorism, and after the Islamist insurgency of the 1990s, which had cost hundreds of lives, many Egyptians accepted or at least tolerated the bargain. But by the 2000s the emergency regime had become something broader and more intimate: a license for the police to operate above the law in ordinary people's lives.

Police brutality was not an abstraction. It was the checkpoint shakedown, the neighborhood station where detainees were beaten, the knowledge that a word from a policeman could land a young man in a cell with no recourse. The case that concentrated this fury was the killing of Khaled Said, a twenty-eight-year-old from Alexandria, in June 2010. Said was dragged from an internet cafe by two plainclothes detectives and beaten to death in the street. The Interior Ministry claimed he had choked on a packet of drugs he tried to swallow; photographs of his battered corpse, smuggled out of the morgue and posted online, told a different story. A Facebook page, We Are All Khaled Said, created by the Google executive Wael Ghonim, drew hundreds of thousands of followers and became one of the central organizing spaces for the protests seven months later. Said's death mattered not because police killings were new in Egypt but because the cover-up was so crude and the evidence so public that it destroyed whatever remained of the official story that the police protected citizens.

Corruption ran alongside violence as a daily grievance. The Mubarak era's economic liberalization, the infitah policies and the privatizations of the 1990s and 2000s, had created a class of spectacularly wealthy businessmen, many of them connected to the ruling National Democratic Party and to the Mubarak family itself. Public assets were sold off in deals that enriched insiders. Land on the Red Sea and in the new desert suburbs went to developers with connections at prices that bore no relation to their value. Meanwhile, wages stagnated, food prices rose, and the poor got poorer. The contrast was visible in the landscape of Cairo itself: gated compounds with private security a few kilometers from informal settlements where millions lived without reliable water or sanitation. Egyptians did not need economists to tell them the game was rigged; they could see it from their balconies. [The three decades of rule examined in our account of Egypt under Hosni Mubarak](/2016/06/01/egypt-under-hosni-mubarak/) trace this fusion of political monopoly and economic privilege in detail, and the key point for 2011 is that the corruption was not hidden. It was flaunted, and the flaunting became part of the insult.

### A Generation with Degrees and No Future

Poverty in Egypt had a particular and politically explosive shape: it struck hardest at the young and the educated. Egypt's population had roughly doubled during Mubarak's presidency, and by 2011 nearly two-thirds of Egyptians were under thirty. The state had kept its old Nasser-era promise of a government job for every university graduate long after it could afford to keep it, and the result was a vast cohort of young people with degrees, smartphones, and expectations, and no plausible path to the life their education had promised. Official unemployment figures hovered in the high single digits, but for Egyptians between twenty and twenty-nine, and especially for young women, the real numbers were far worse, with estimates ranging from one in four to one in three out of work. University graduates drove taxis, worked as waiters, or sat in coffeehouses. The phrase that circulated, half joke and half lament, was that Egypt produced the best-educated unemployed in the world. This generation had grown up entirely under Mubarak. They had never voted in an election that mattered, never known a president other than him, and never seen peaceful political change produce results. Their alienation was not ideological in the first instance. It was the practical, grinding realization that the system had no use for them.

The rigged parliamentary elections of November and December 2010 turned that alienation into active contempt. The National Democratic Party claimed roughly nine out of ten seats in a vote that independent monitors, journalists, and opposition parties described as the most fraudulent in Egypt's modern history. Ballot boxes were stuffed in daylight. Opposition candidates were arrested. Judges who were supposed to supervise the voting were sidelined. The Muslim Brotherhood, which had held 88 seats in the outgoing parliament as independents, won none. The secular opposition won almost nothing. When Egyptians compared the official results to what they had seen with their own eyes at polling stations, the conclusion was inescapable: the regime would not permit even the appearance of competition. For many who had still believed, or wanted to believe, that change could come through the ballot box, the 2010 elections closed the last door. The street was what remained.

### The Succession Question

Above all these grievances hung a question that made the future look like a closed loop: who would follow Mubarak. The president was eighty-two in 2011, had undergone gallbladder surgery in Germany in 2010, and was the subject of constant rumors about his health, rumors the state media neither confirmed nor convincingly denied. Everyone understood that the regime was preparing Gamal Mubarak, the president's younger son, a former investment banker with a circle of business-friendly reformers around him, to inherit the presidency. The machinery was visible: Gamal's rise through the National Democratic Party, the sidelining of old-guard figures, the constitutional amendments of 2005 and 2007 that made an independent candidacy for president nearly impossible. Egyptians called the project tawreeth, inheritance, and the word carried the sting of monarchy in a republic that had been founded on the overthrow of a king. Opposition to the succession united people who agreed on little else: Nasserists who despised the liberalization Gamal represented, Islamists who saw a secular dynasty, young activists who saw the presidency being treated as family property. The aging ruler and the son waiting in the wings made the regime's time horizon visible, and what Egyptians saw in it was not reform but perpetuity.

### The Street as a Political Actor

None of these grievances was new in 2011, and that is the point. Egypt had a deep, twentieth-century tradition of the street as a political actor, and the 2011 uprising drew on it consciously. The 1919 revolution against the British had made mass protest a founding myth of modern Egyptian politics. The bread riots of 1977 had forced Sadat to reverse subsidy cuts within days. The 2000s had seen a wave of protest that, while smaller, built the networks and the habits that 2011 would use: the Kefaya movement's demonstrations against Mubarak's fifth term and the succession project, the judges' revolt of 2006, the April 6 Youth Movement's solidarity with striking textile workers in Mahalla in 2008, and a strike wave in which, by some counts, more than two million workers took part in over three thousand workplace actions between 2004 and 2010. These were not rehearsals in any organized sense. No one in 2008 was planning January 2011. But they taught a generation of activists how to organize, how to face the police, and how to speak to a public that was already angry. When the call went out for 25 January, it landed on ground that decades of grievance and years of smaller mobilizations had prepared. The explosion surprised the world. In Egypt, for those paying attention, the fuse had been visible for a very long time.

## The Spark: Tunisia and the Choice of Police Day

On Friday, 17 December 2010, in the Tunisian market town of Sidi Bouzid, a street vendor named Mohamed Bouazizi set himself on fire outside the governor's office after municipal officials confiscated his cart and weighing scales. He died of his burns on 4 January 2011. In the weeks between those two dates, protests that began as an outcry over his treatment spread from Tunisia's interior towns to the capital, and their demands hardened from jobs and dignity into the removal of President Zine El Abidine Ben Ali. On Friday, 14 January 2011, Ben Ali fled to Saudi Arabia, ending twenty-three years in power. No Arab president had ever been forced from office by street demonstrations before, and the fact registered across the region within hours, carried on the same satellite channels and social networks that had broadcast the uprising itself.

Egyptians watched Tunisia fall in real time. Al Jazeera beamed the demonstrations into living rooms from Alexandria to Aswan, while photographs, video clips, and running commentary circulated on Facebook, Twitter, and the blogs that had grown into a lively oppositional press over the previous decade. Tunisia felt close, not only because of a shared language and a shared satellite footprint, but because the grievances on display were instantly recognizable: jobless graduates, petty corruption, a police state that treated citizens as subjects, and a president whose family appeared to be positioning itself for succession. In Cairo's coffeehouses and Alexandria's workshops, the same question circulated in a dozen forms: if Tunisians could do this, why not Egyptians? Others answered that Egypt was a different proposition. Its security apparatus was larger and more entrenched than Tunisia's, its army sat at the center of the state rather than at its margins, and its rulers had watched Tunis with the close attention of people taking notes. Both readings contained part of the truth, and the argument between them shaped everything that followed.

### The Tunisian Example

What Tunisia supplied was not a plan but a proof. Egyptian oppositionists had spent years arguing, in print and online, that Mubarak's order rested on fear rather than consent, and that a sufficiently large crowd in a sufficiently visible place could expose the fact. Tunisia turned the argument into footage. The chant that crossed the border with the images, "the people want the fall of the regime," gave Egyptian activists a slogan ready-made, and the speed of Ben Ali's collapse, barely four weeks from Bouazizi's death to the presidential plane, suggested that regimes which looked immovable could prove brittle once the streets filled. Egyptian officials drew the opposite lesson in public, insisting through state media that Egypt's stability was of another order entirely, but the insistence itself betrayed the anxiety underneath. Within days of Ben Ali's flight, small solidarity demonstrations appeared outside the Tunisian embassy in Cairo, and the police broke them up with the practiced efficiency that would soon be tested on a far larger scale.

### The Organizers

The call for 25 January did not come from a single headquarters. It emerged from a loose coalition of youth networks whose members had been organizing together, and learning from failure together, for several years. The April 6 Youth Movement dated its founding to the spring of 2008, when young activists, among them Ahmed Maher and Asmaa Mahfoud, built an online solidarity campaign around a planned general strike by textile workers in Mahalla al-Kubra on 6 April of that year. The strike was suppressed and the day of action fizzled, but the network survived as a school of digital mobilization, and its veterans carried the habits of 2008, backup meeting points, rapid reposting of instructions, legal aid phone trees, into the winter of 2011.

Alongside them stood the "We Are All Khaled Said" Facebook page. Khaled Said was a young Alexandrian who died in June 2010 after being beaten by two plainclothes policemen; photographs of his disfigured face, circulated by his family, made him an emblem of police brutality. A Facebook page created in his name, administered anonymously by the Google executive Wael Ghonim, gathered hundreds of thousands of followers and became a clearinghouse for documenting abuse and publicizing calls to action. Ghonim's identity remained hidden behind the page until after the uprising began, which allowed the page to function as a collective voice rather than a personal platform, and its administrators used that voice in mid-January to endorse the 25 January date.

The date acquired a human face on 18 January, when Asmaa Mahfoud posted a video of herself speaking directly to the camera, holding a handwritten sign, urging Egyptians, and young men in particular, to come out on the 25th. The video spread rapidly across Egyptian social media, and Mahfoud's plainspoken challenge, that those who complained about the country's condition online owed it to themselves to appear in person, made her one of the most recognizable figures of the mobilization. Around the same networks moved Mohamed ElBaradei, the former diplomat whose National Association for Change had spent the previous year gathering opposition figures, from liberals to Islamists to leftists, around a platform of constitutional reform, an end to the emergency law, and judicial supervision of elections. ElBaradei's international stature gave the opposition a figurehead the regime could not easily dismiss, and his association's endorsement of the 25 January call helped reassure older activists that the youth networks were not acting alone. Foreign commentators would later describe what happened as a Facebook revolution, a framing that Egyptian activists and many scholars rejected as reductive. Their counterargument, made at its strongest, was that the digital tools mattered less than the decade of accumulated experience behind them: the labor strikes, the street battles with police, the practice of naming meeting points and dispersing and regrouping, all of it learned the hard way before a single Tunisian chant crossed the border.

### The Choice of 25 January

The date itself was the organizers' sharpest piece of symbolism. 25 January was National Police Day, a public holiday honoring the police and commemorating their armed confrontation with British forces at Ismailia in 1952. To call a demonstration against police brutality on the police's own holiday was a deliberate provocation, and the organizers made no secret of the intent. The page and the movement's statements framed the day as a demand for dignity from the very institution being celebrated, and the irony traveled well: it gave journalists a hook, it gave participants a retort to anyone who asked why that Tuesday, and it turned a state ritual into an opposition stage. The date spread through Facebook event pages, Twitter messages, and word of mouth, and the fact that the 25th was a paid holiday worked in the organizers' favor, freeing workers and students who might otherwise have stayed at their desks.

The tactic belonged to a longer lineage than the social media accounts suggested. Egypt's modern history is punctuated by moments when crowds in the street accomplished what petitions and parliaments could not, from the 1919 uprising through the student movements of the 1970s, the bread riots of 1977, and the pro-Palestine and antiwar demonstrations of the early 2000s that had schooled a generation in confronting the Central Security Forces. The young people posting meeting points in January 2011 were drawing, knowingly or not, on [the deeper current of popular politics in modern Egypt](/2015/07/01/nationalism-and-egyptian-identity/): the mass demonstration as a lever against rulers who controlled the parliament, the press, and the courts. That lineage cut two ways. It meant the regime knew exactly what a large crowd in Tahrir could signify, and it meant the police had rehearsed their response many times. In the days before the 25th, the Interior Ministry issued warnings that demonstrations would not be tolerated, plainclothes officers visited known activists, and state newspapers carried assurances that Egypt was not Tunisia. The organizers, for their part, published instructions for evading cordons, listed lawyers' phone numbers for those arrested, and told participants to bring water, vinegar-soaked cloths against tear gas, and Egyptian flags. Both sides were preparing for the confrontation they could feel approaching.

## The Day of Rage: 25 January Hour by Hour

Tuesday, 25 January 2011, dawned cool and clear over Cairo, a holiday morning with the streets emptier than usual. The first gatherings were small and scattered, by design. In the working-class districts of Bulaq Abu al-Ela and Shubra, groups of young people assembled at mosques and side-street corners, deliberately avoiding the main squares where riot police had been stationed since early morning. Similar knots formed in Imbaba, in Mohandiseen near the Mustafa Mahmoud Mosque, and in middle-class neighborhoods whose residents had rarely marched before. The organizers had publicized multiple starting points and urged participants to keep moving through back streets, a tactic meant to prevent the police from sealing off a single assembly point. By late morning the scattered groups had become columns, several hundred strong each, chanting as they walked, and the columns were bending toward the center of the city.

### The Morning Marches

The march on Tahrir Square came from several directions at once. From the west, demonstrators crossed toward the square over the Qasr al-Nil Bridge, where police lines held the approaches and fired tear gas canisters into the advancing crowd. From the north, marchers pushed down from Ramses and Abdel Moneim Riad; from the east, columns wound through the narrow streets of Bulaq and emerged near the Corniche. At several points the police cordons broke. On the bridge and at the square's edges, protesters surged forward in numbers the officers facing them had not expected, and the lines gave way in bursts of confusion, with officers retreating behind shields as the crowd poured through. The chants sharpened as the columns merged. Alongside the Tunisian borrowing, "the people want the fall of the regime," rose a slogan that would define the eighteen days: "bread, freedom, social justice," three words that compressed the economic and political grievances of the previous decade into a single breath. By early afternoon, Tahrir Square itself, a vast traffic circle the authorities had kept clear of demonstrators for years, was filling with people waving Egyptian flags and climbing its central plinth.

Beyond Cairo, the day unfolded on a similar pattern with local variations. In Alexandria, thousands gathered near the Qaid Ibrahim Mosque and marched toward the Corniche, where the Mediterranean city's long tradition of street politics reasserted itself. In Suez, the canal city with its militant labor history, the confrontation turned violent early: police fired tear gas and, by several accounts, live ammunition, and protesters answered with stones. In Mahalla al-Kubra, textile workers who had struck in 2008 marched again, joined by residents of the Nile Delta towns of Mansoura and Tanta. Demonstrations were reported in Ismailia, Port Said, Aswan, and a string of smaller cities, each modest by Cairo's standard but unprecedented in combination. Crowd estimates that day were imprecise, as such estimates always are in the absence of official counts. Journalists and organizers spoke of tens of thousands in Cairo alone, with figures for Tahrir and its approaches ranging from roughly twenty thousand to more than thirty thousand by late afternoon, and tens of thousands more spread across Alexandria, Suez, and the other cities. The government offered lower numbers; the organizers claimed higher ones. What both sides conceded, in their different ways, was that the turnout had exceeded the organizers' own expectations. Many of those in the streets that afternoon had never attended a demonstration before, and their presence, middle-aged professionals, veiled mothers with children, shopkeepers who had closed for the holiday, signaled that the protest had escaped the activist core that called it.

### Tahrir by Nightfall

The police response hardened as the afternoon wore on. Central Security Forces conscripts, braced by officers, fired volley after volley of tear gas into Tahrir and the surrounding streets; water cannon trucks swept the square's edges; baton charges broke into the crowd and dragged away demonstrators, some of them beaten as they were carried off. Plainclothes men with sticks and iron bars, a fixture of Egyptian crowd control, moved through the side streets seizing stragglers. Hundreds of arrests were reported across the country by nightfall, with lawyers' networks struggling to track the detainees as they were bused to security installations. Yet the square held. As dusk settled, the tear gas thinned, vendors threaded through the crowd selling food and flags, and the chanting took on a steadier rhythm. Speakers climbed onto makeshift platforms to denounce the Interior Ministry, and the crowd answered with the day's slogans. For the first time in living memory, Tahrir Square belonged to demonstrators after dark, and the fact of it, the lights, the flags, the sheer density of bodies in a place the regime had declared off-limits, felt to those present like a victory in itself.

In Suez, meanwhile, the day's bloodiest confrontations were producing the uprising's first dead. Reports from the city put the toll at three protesters and one police officer killed in the day's clashes, though accounts of the exact number and the circumstances differed, and the confusion of the street made every figure provisional. The deaths in Suez marked a threshold: the protests were no longer a matter of marches and slogans alone. In Cairo, the night deepened over a square that refused to empty, and the organizers began to speak of staying, of turning a day of rage into something more permanent.

### The After-Midnight Clearance

The regime's answer came in the small hours. Sometime after midnight, with the crowd in Tahrir thinned but still numbering in the thousands, security forces moved in from multiple sides, firing dense volleys of tear gas and advancing with batons behind armored vehicles. Those who remained were beaten back through the square's exits, and hundreds were arrested in the sweep, herded into trucks as the square was systematically emptied. By dawn, Tahrir stood silent under police control, littered with abandoned shoes, torn banners, and spent tear gas canisters, the flags gone and the chanting stilled. The clearance was thorough, and for the Interior Ministry it demonstrated that the state could still retake any ground it chose.

The official response that followed was dismissal. An Interior Ministry statement described the turnout as limited, numbering in the thousands rather than the tens of thousands reported by journalists, and state television carried footage emphasizing calm elsewhere in the country. President Mubarak himself said nothing that day, and the silence from the presidency was read by the opposition as either contempt or paralysis. Regime spokesmen framed the demonstrators as a small minority, unrepresentative of a nation going about its holiday, and insisted that the situation was under control.

The dismissal missed the day's real significance, which the organizers grasped immediately. By most accounts, 25 January had produced the largest anti-government street protests Egypt had seen in decades, larger than anything the Kefaya movement or the judges' protests had managed, and it had done so on a date chosen to mock the police, in the face of explicit warnings, with live ammunition reportedly fired in Suez. The barrier of fear, the calculation that had kept millions at home through years of rigged elections and emergency law, the belief that protest meant certain arrest and probable beating, had cracked in a single afternoon. Tens of thousands of Egyptians had discovered, together and in public, that they were not alone. The square was empty at dawn, but the knowledge of what the day had contained could not be cleared away with tear gas, and it traveled home with every demonstrator who walked out of Cairo's center that morning.

### How did protesters break through police lines on the Day of Rage?

They marched from several working-class neighborhoods at once, splitting police deployments across Cairo faster than commanders could reposition them. In Bulaq and Shubra, sheer numbers overwhelmed static cordons, while in Suez protesters outflanked them entirely. Tear gas thinned the columns, but the marches regrouped and converged on Tahrir by afternoon.

## Tahrir Holds: The Days of Rage, 26 January to 10 February

The Day of Rage did not end the uprising; it escalated it. On 26 January, protests resumed in Cairo, Alexandria, Suez, and other cities, larger in several places than they had been the day before. Police responded with force, and the clashes hardened the protesters' resolve. Suez, the hard-bitten canal city with a long memory of confrontation, became the flashpoint. Street battles there on 26 and 27 January left the first protester dead, a young man whose killing sent a shock through the country. The regime appeared to calculate that overwhelming force would break the momentum. Instead, each death and each injury seemed to widen the circles of sympathy.

On the night of 27 into 28 January, the government reached for a tool no state had ever used against its own population at that scale. Egypt's internet went dark. The five major internet service providers shut down their networks within minutes of each other, and the three mobile phone companies, including Vodafone, suspended voice and data service across most of the country. Egypt vanished from the global network. The move was intended to paralyze the organizers, who had relied on Facebook, Twitter, and text messages to coordinate. It had unintended consequences. The blackout told every Egyptian, even those far from the protests, that something extraordinary was happening, and it drove people who might otherwise have stayed home onto the streets to find out what was going on. Cut off from the network, protesters fell back on older methods: mosque loudspeakers, word of mouth, and the Friday sermon itself.

Friday, 28 January, was called the Friday of Anger. The plan was simple. After the midday prayers, worshippers would pour out of mosques across the country and march on the city centers. In Cairo, the target was Tahrir Square. What happened that day exceeded anything the organizers had dared to hope for. Hundreds of thousands of Egyptians marched in Cairo, Alexandria, Suez, Mansoura, Ismailia, and smaller towns. Police fired tear gas and rubber bullets, but the crowds were too large to contain. On the Qasr al-Nil bridge, which carries marchers from the west bank of the Nile toward downtown Cairo, police made their stand with water cannons and volleys of tear gas. Protesters advanced through the haze, using makeshift shields and their own numbers, and eventually overran the police lines. By evening, the Central Security Forces, the feared riot police of the Interior Ministry, were collapsing. Exhausted, outnumbered, and in some cases abandoning their posts, the police simply withdrew from the streets. Cairo had no police by nightfall. It was a vacuum of authority without precedent in modern Egyptian history.

Into that vacuum stepped the army. Late on 28 January, armored vehicles rolled into Cairo and took up positions at key intersections, bridges, and squares. Soldiers in fatigues stood atop tanks facing crowds of protesters who, having just fought the police all afternoon, had no idea what the soldiers intended. Then something happened that became one of the enduring images of the revolution. The crowds did not flee. They climbed onto the tanks, draped them with Egyptian flags, chanted, and embraced the soldiers. The cry went up: "The army and the people are one hand." The soldiers, under strict orders not to fire, held their positions and their restraint. It was genuine discipline, and it was also calculation, but in that moment it felt like a promise. The regime announced a curfew beginning at 6 p.m., then extended and tightened it repeatedly. Nobody observed it. The crowds stayed in the streets all night, and the army did not enforce it against them.

### The Security Vacuum

The police withdrawal had immediate consequences beyond the demonstrations. On the night of 28 January and in the days that followed, prisons across Egypt were broken open. At the massive Wadi Natrun complex and other facilities, thousands of inmates escaped in coordinated attacks whose sponsorship remains contested to this day. The government attributed the breakouts to armed gangs, Hamas, and Hezbollah operatives. The Muslim Brotherhood, whose imprisoned members included senior figures, denied organizing the attacks. Some analysts have suggested elements within the security apparatus itself orchestrated the chaos to frighten the population into craving order. Whatever the cause, criminals walked out alongside political prisoners, and looting spread through several neighborhoods.

Ordinary Egyptians responded with striking speed. In Cairo, Alexandria, and other cities, neighborhood watch committees, known as lejan shaabiya, formed almost overnight. Men and boys, and in some areas women as well, armed themselves with sticks, kitchen knives, and hunting rifles, built barricades of wrecked cars and sandbags across residential streets, and set up checkpoints through which no one passed without identification and explanation. The committees ran night shifts, communicated by phone and runner, and kept detailed lists of who entered and left. Crime, which many had feared would engulf the cities, was held at bay largely by these self-organized patrols. The experience was transformative for many participants. People who had never spoken to their neighbors discovered collective capacity, and the committees themselves became a source of pride and a quiet argument that Egyptians could govern themselves.

In Tahrir Square, the occupation took root. Thousands, and soon tens of thousands, of Egyptians decided not to leave. They set up tents, hundreds of them, in rows that became familiar neighborhoods of the square. Field clinics appeared, staffed by volunteer doctors and nurses, treating the wounded from the clashes and stocking supplies of gauze, antiseptic, and medicine donated by pharmacies across the city. Checkpoints sprang up at every entrance to the square, manned by volunteers, many of them members of the ultras football supporter groups, who searched bags and checked identity cards. This was partly to keep out plainclothes security men and provocateurs, and partly to reassure the growing number of families arriving that the square was safe. It was, remarkably safe. Harassment of women, a persistent problem in crowded Cairo, was reportedly lower inside the square during the occupation than on ordinary days, and women came in ever larger numbers, with children and grandparents in tow.

The square sustained itself through logistics that would have challenged a small army. Volunteers collected donations of food, blankets, bottled water, and cigarettes at the checkpoints and distributed them from makeshift depots. Restaurants and bakeries in the downtown area sent free meals. Wealthy sympathizers arrived with vans full of supplies. A stage went up near the Mogamma, the hulking government administration building that looms over the square, and from it came speeches, poetry, songs, and the great rolling chants that became the revolution's soundtrack. "The people want the fall of the regime," they roared, and "Bread, freedom, human dignity," and the simple, defiant "He must go." Christians formed protective rings around Muslims at prayer, and Muslims returned the gesture at a Coptic mass held in the square. Workers from the Mahalla textile mills stood beside university students. Women in niqab chanted next to unveiled feminists. The ultras, the hardcore football fans with their experience of street confrontation with police, served as the square's informal security force and its shock troops in clashes. What held them all together was a shared demand, simple enough for a child to understand and radical enough to frighten a regime: the end of Mubarak's rule.

### The Million Man March

By the end of January, the standoff had settled into a rhythm. The regime, shocked by the scale of the uprising and the army's refusal to clear the streets, groped for responses. Mubarak dismissed his cabinet and appointed Omar Suleiman, the intelligence chief, as vice president, the first time in three decades that he had filled the office. It was meant as a signal of seriousness. The protesters read it as a reshuffling of the same faces and dismissed it.

The organizers called for a million-man march on Tuesday, 1 February. The army, in a statement that morning, announced that it recognized the legitimacy of the people's demands and would not use force against them, a remarkable declaration that effectively gave the march its protection. What followed was the largest demonstration in Egypt's modern history. Estimates vary, but observers placed the crowds in the hundreds of thousands in Tahrir alone, with massive marches in Alexandria, Suez, Mansoura, and other cities. The square that day was a sea of flags and faces, peaceful, jubilant, and enormous. That evening, Mubarak gave his second speech of the crisis. He pledged that he would not seek re-election in the scheduled September presidential vote, that he would oversee constitutional amendments, and that he would transfer some powers. It was, by the standards of his presidency, a stunning concession. The protesters rejected it outright. The demand was his immediate departure, not a managed exit in seven months. The chant that night, and in the days that followed, was "Leave, leave, Mubarak."

The regime's answer came the next day. On 2 February, crowds of pro-Mubarak demonstrators, many of them plainclothes police, ruling party members, and hired men by most accounts, though the regime insisted they were genuine supporters, marched on Tahrir from several directions. Among them were men riding horses and camels, a sight that gave the day its name: the Battle of the Camel. The horsemen charged the square's checkpoints, and street fighting erupted that lasted for hours. Protesters fought back with stones, sticks, and Molotov cocktails. The army, stationed around the square, did not intervene to stop the assault, a failure that damaged its standing with the protesters even as the soldiers' individual restraint had earned their trust. By the end of the day, estimates of the dead ranged from several to more than a dozen, with hundreds wounded, though precise figures remain disputed. The violence had the opposite of its intended effect. Egyptians who had been on the fence, watching from home, were horrified by the images of camels charging into a peaceful crowd. Public sympathy for the square, already broad, hardened into something fiercer. The attack unified the protesters and convinced many waverers that the regime would stop at nothing.

The days that followed, from 3 to 9 February, were a stalemate of exhaustion. The regime offered concessions: dialogue with opposition parties through Vice President Suleiman, promises of constitutional reform, the dismissal of more ministers. The protesters, bloodied and emboldened, would not negotiate while Mubarak remained. New energy arrived from unexpected quarters. On 7 February, Wael Ghonim, the Google executive who had anonymously administered the "We Are All Khaled Said" Facebook page, was released after twelve days in detention. He appeared that evening on a popular television talk show, learned on air of the deaths that had occurred while he was held, and wept openly. The interview electrified the country. Ghonim's tears, and his insistence that the young people of the square were the real heroes, brought a new wave of Egyptians into the streets. His release also demonstrated, in the view of many observers, that the regime's detention of activists was becoming a political liability rather than a tool of intimidation.

### The Strikes Spread

Meanwhile, the protest was acquiring an industrial dimension. Labor strikes, which had simmered in Egypt for years before the revolution, began to spread in early February. Workers at the Suez Canal, the country's economic lifeline, walked out. Textile workers in Mahalla al-Kubra, the historic center of Egypt's labor movement, struck. Transport workers, postal workers, and employees of state enterprises joined in cities across the Nile Delta and Upper Egypt. The strikes were partly economic, demanding higher wages and the removal of corrupt managers, and partly political, adding their voices to the demand for Mubarak's departure. For the military leadership, watching the economy seize up, the strikes were a warning that time was not on the regime's side. A political crisis could be managed. A general strike, combined with a continuing occupation of the capital's central square, threatened the functioning of the state itself.

Expectations crested on 10 February. That morning, the Supreme Council of the Armed Forces issued its first public communique, announcing that it was in continuous session and would take measures to safeguard the nation. The language was studied and opaque, but across Egypt it was read as a single message: the army was about to remove Mubarak. Crowds in Tahrir swelled with celebration. Foreign correspondents reported that the end was hours away. CIA Director Leon Panetta told a congressional committee there was, in his words, a strong likelihood Mubarak would step down that evening. Then Mubarak spoke, his third address of the crisis. What followed was one of the most consequential miscalculations of the eighteen days. Instead of resigning, Mubarak delivered a defiant, rambling speech in which he delegated some of his powers to Vice President Suleiman but refused to leave office, insisting he would die on Egyptian soil and remain until September. The square, which had been preparing to celebrate, erupted in fury. The chants turned from demands to rage. Protesters wept, shouted, waved their shoes in contempt. The army's communique had raised hopes the president had just crushed, and the gap between expectation and reality set the stage for the most dangerous night of the revolution.

### How did the tent city in Tahrir Square sustain itself?

Volunteers ran field clinics, food stations, and blanket distribution, while checkpoints at every entrance checked IDs and confiscated weapons. Stages hosted speeches and music, cleanup crews collected trash, and donations flowed in from across Cairo. The camp's discipline kept it peaceful, projected legitimacy to watching cameras, and made any eviction politically costly.

### How did the regime's internet shutdown backfire?

The government cut internet and mobile service on the night of 27 January to blind the organizers. Instead, the blackout pushed undecided Egyptians into the streets to see what was happening and signaled regime panic. Landlines, dial-up connections, and word of mouth kept coordination alive, and the Friday crowds were larger than anything the shutdown was meant to prevent.

## The Army's Calculus
Through all eighteen days, the decisive institution was the one that had placed itself, physically, between the protesters and the regime's police: the Egyptian Armed Forces. To understand what the army did, and what it refused to do, requires understanding what the army was. Egypt's military was not merely a fighting force. It was a vast economic empire, running factories, farms, construction companies, resorts, and consumer goods enterprises, employing hundreds of thousands, and operating largely outside civilian oversight. It was also, in the eyes of most Egyptians, a national institution of a different order than the hated Interior Ministry and its Central Security Forces. The police were the face of daily humiliation: the bribe at the checkpoint, the beating in the station, the emergency law. The army, by contrast, retained the prestige of the 1973 war and the image of the conscript as the nation's son. When Mubarak ordered the army onto the streets on 28 January, he was calling on the one institution whose reputation the crisis had not yet touched.

The generals faced a stark choice. Had they ordered their soldiers to fire on the crowds, the eighteen days might have ended in a bloodbath, or in a fracturing of the army itself. Conscript armies are unreliable instruments of domestic repression, and the officers knew it. The Supreme Council of the Armed Forces, led by Defense Minister Mohamed Hussein Tantawi, calculated that the institution's survival required distance from Mubarak. This was not sympathy for the revolution in any simple sense. It was institutional self-preservation. The army's economic empire, its privileged position, and its historic role as the ultimate arbiter of Egyptian politics all depended on the army emerging from the crisis intact. Firing on unarmed Egyptians would have destroyed the military's standing with the public and risked disobedience, even mutiny, within the ranks. Standing aside, by contrast, cost the generals little and positioned them as the guardians of the nation.

That calculation drew on a deep history. The army had been the maker and unmaker of Egyptian governments since [the army's seizure of political power in 1952](/2015/12/01/1952-revolution-free-officers/), when the Free Officers overthrew the monarchy and established the republic. Every president since, Nasser, Sadat, and Mubarak himself, had come from the military's ranks. The generals of 2011 understood themselves as the heirs of that tradition: the institution that stood above politics, intervening when the nation required it. This self-image made the refusal to fire on protesters legible to the officers as duty rather than disloyalty. They were not, in their own telling, abandoning the president. They were protecting Egypt.

The distinction between the army and the police mattered enormously to how events unfolded. The Central Security Forces were Mubarak's instrument, recruited from the poorest conscripts, brutalized by their officers, and despised by the public. When they collapsed on 28 January, the regime lost its sword. The army was a separate institution with separate interests, and the generals had no sentimental attachment to the Interior Ministry's survival. Indeed, allowing the police to take the blame for the violence of the eighteen days served the army's interests, preserving its own hands as clean. The Republican Guard, the elite force charged with protecting the presidency itself, remained loyal to Mubarak throughout, guarding the presidential palaces and state television. Its loyalty illustrated the limits of the army's defection: the generals would not order soldiers to shoot protesters, but neither would they move against the president directly, at least not yet. They waited, watching the crowds grow, the strikes spread, and the economy stall, until the cost of Mubarak's survival to the institution exceeded the cost of his removal.

On the night of 10 February, that calculation was approaching its breaking point. The square was furious, the country was striking, and the army's communique had committed the generals, in the public's mind, to action. What the generals would do next remained the question on which everything turned.

### How did the Republican Guard's role differ from the army's during the uprising?

The regular army deployed around Tahrir and key sites but refused to fire on crowds, positioning itself as the people's guardian. The Republican Guard, an elite force loyal to the presidency, instead ringed the presidential palaces and state television, protecting Mubarak himself. The split revealed two militaries: one hedging its bets, one holding the ruler's line.

## The People of the Square

### A Cross-Section

The crowds that filled Tahrir Square during the eighteen days were not a single movement wearing one face. They were a collision of Egypt's social worlds, each arriving with its own reasons. University students stood beside professors, some of whom had taught in the very faculties whose gates the students had just marched through. Doctors and lawyers who had spent years navigating a system of favors and surveillance now worked shoulder to shoulder with the unemployed young men who had nothing to lose but patience. Textile workers traveled from the industrial city of Mahalla, carrying a history of strikes that stretched back through the 2000s and a reputation for confronting security forces head on. Civil servants, teachers, and small shopkeepers joined after the first week, when the protests began to look less like a gamble and more like a possibility. People from neighborhoods across Cairo arrived carrying bread they had bought for strangers, and demonstrators traveled in from Alexandria, Suez, and the Delta towns, folding regional grievances into the capital's square.

Among the most organized and the most battle tested were the football ultras, the organized supporters of clubs like Ahly (the Ahlawy) and Zamalek (the White Knights). Years of clashes with police in and around stadiums had taught them how to hold a line, how to retreat and regroup, how to deflect tear gas canisters and organize medical points. When the Central Security Forces pressed hard on Tahrir's approaches, particularly during the violent nights of late January, it was often ultras at the front, directing traffic, building barricades from corrugated metal and scrap, and absorbing the first shock of a police advance. Their presence gave the square a defensive competence it might not otherwise have had, and older protesters, some of them lifelong football fans themselves, spoke of this with a mixture of gratitude and surprise. The ultras' loyalties to rival clubs were suspended inside the square, a truce that lasted through the uprising and, for many, made the unity of those days unforgettable.

Women were present in numbers that reshaped the square's character. Egyptian women had protested before, but Tahrir in 2011 was something different in scale. Young women ran the popular committees that checked identities at the entrances, delivered medical supplies, and staffed the field clinics. Older women brought food from home, arguing with soldiers at the barricades, and mothers arrived with children on their shoulders. The sexual harassment that plagued Cairo's streets was reported by observers to have diminished sharply inside the square, a fact that many women cited afterward as one of the most striking features of the occupation. Whether this was complete safety or a temporary truce under the pressure of solidarity remained debated, and later events would reopen the question, but for the eighteen days, the square held a reputation as a place where women could walk alone at night and be offered a place to sleep by strangers.

The religious composition of Tahrir was one of the uprising's most photographed and most carefully guarded features. Coptic Christians and Muslims stood together in the square, and images of Christians forming protective rings around Muslims at prayer circulated widely, as did reports of the reverse: Muslims guarding a Coptic mass held in the square. Church and mosque both mattered, and slogans explicitly rejected sectarian division. Estimates of the Coptic share of the crowds varied, and no reliable headcount existed for any group, so accounts of the square's religious makeup rested on witness testimony and press observation rather than hard numbers. What could be said with confidence was that sectarian slogans were absent from the main stages and that clashes between Muslims and Christians, a familiar feature of Egyptian public life, did not break out inside Tahrir during the occupation. The unity was real but it was also performed, deliberately and insistently, by people who understood that their adversaries would exploit any visible fracture.

### Chants, symbols, and the square's own society

The chants of Tahrir carried meaning in layers. The most enduring was the demand for "eish, horreya, adala igtima'eya," bread, freedom, social justice, a phrase that tied economic survival to political liberty in a way that no single slogan in Arabic or English could fully translate. Bread, eish, meant not only food but life itself, the daily substance of existence, and pairing it with freedom and social justice told anyone listening that this was not an abstract revolution. It was a revolt about what it took to live. The other chant that defined the square was the Tunisian import: "the people want the fall of the regime," al-sha'b yurid isqat al-nizam, first heard in Tunis and now echoing across North Africa. The word for regime, nizam, was deliberately broad. It meant not only the president but the system that had produced him.

Humor ran through the slogans like a current. Protesters composed rhyming chants overnight, mocking the president's long tenure, the emergency law, and the ruling party's absurdities. Signs compared the regime to expired food, to dinosaurs, to a television program that had run too many seasons. This creativity was a form of armor. People who laughed together in the face of police batons were harder to frighten than people who only had anger, and the wit of the chants became one of the most widely remembered features of the eighteen days.

The square was dense with symbols. The Egyptian flag appeared everywhere, draped from lampposts, painted on faces, wrapped around the shoulders of children. Its ubiquity was a deliberate choice: this was not the uprising of any party or faction but of Egyptians as Egyptians, and the flag was the one symbol everyone could carry without argument. Photographs of Khaled Said, the young man whose death in police custody in Alexandria had become a rallying point in 2010, were carried through the crowds, along with images of those killed during the protests themselves. Makeshift memorials grew at the edges of the square, small shrines of candles, photographs, and handwritten notes where strangers stopped to read the names of the dead. Mourning and defiance sat side by side, and the presence of the martyrs' images reminded every newcomer what the cost had already been.

Inside the square, a society built itself from nothing. Popular committees, lijan sha'beya, formed at every entrance, where volunteers checked identification and searched bags to keep out provocateurs and weapons. Field hospitals were set up in the Omar Makram Mosque and in tents across the square, staffed by doctors who had walked off their shifts and worked around the clock treating tear gas exposure, head wounds, and the injuries of live ammunition. Stages carried microphones where speakers, singers, and ordinary demonstrators took turns. Strangers organized food with startling speed: donors sent in supplies, volunteers cooked in the open air, and distribution lines formed with the discipline of an army canteen. Security patrols moved through the night, trash was collected by volunteers, and lost children were reunited through a makeshift loudspeaker system.

This self organization was remarkable, but it papered over real divisions. The students and professionals of Cairo's middle class did not want the same Egypt as the textile workers from Mahalla, and the Islamists in the square did not share the liberals' vision of what should follow the regime's fall. These differences were deliberately set aside in the name of unity, a tactic rather than a resolution. The class gaps were visible to anyone who looked: the contrast between protesters arriving in taxis and those who had walked for miles, between the educated young fluent in English and the laborers whose demands were about wages and factory conditions. The square's famous unity was therefore both genuine and fragile, a pact among people who needed each other against a common adversary and who knew, even then, that the harder arguments would come later.

## The Fall: 11 February Hour by Hour

By the morning of 11 February 2011, the question consuming Cairo was no longer whether Hosni Mubarak would leave office but how many more hours he could hold on. The evening before, Egyptians had gathered around televisions expecting a resignation after state media promised a major address, and instead Mubarak had spoken for roughly seventeen minutes about staying until September, handing some authority to Omar Suleiman but remaining president. The reaction in Tahrir Square was a roar of rejection, and protest organizers announced a new escalation: marches on Friday toward the presidential palace in Heliopolis and the state television headquarters at Maspero, two of the most sensitive buildings in the Egyptian state. The plan was for Tahrir to stay occupied while columns broke away to surround the symbols of executive power and official information, a strategy meant to show that the uprising could not be contained inside one square.

Friday began with prayer, and then the marches formed. Tens of thousands of people moved through the streets of Cairo toward the presidential palace, a building Mubarak had barely used during his final period in office but which carried the full weight of the office. Others streamed toward Maspero, the towering riverfront headquarters of state television, whose broadcasts had spent two weeks denying the scale of the protests. Security forces did not attempt to stop the columns. That absence was itself a signal: the coercive apparatus that had cleared streets with live ammunition in January was standing aside. Across Egypt, the labor movement added its weight. Workers at the Suez Canal, in the textile factories of Mahalla, in ports, banks, and transport unions walked off the job, and independent estimates of the number of striking workers ran from the hundreds of thousands upward, with union leaders claiming figures over a million that outside observers could not independently verify. The strikes converted the political crisis into an economic one, and for a regime already hemorrhaging legitimacy, the loss of the working week may have mattered as much as the loss of the streets.

At some point in the afternoon, Mubarak left Cairo. Reports later placed his departure around midday, when a helicopter lifted him from the presidential palace toward the military airport and then on to Sharm el-Sheikh, the Red Sea resort town where he kept a residence. State television at first said nothing about the departure, and Egyptians learned of it through news tickers and word of mouth. The image that circulated was one of quiet flight: the president of thirty years slipping out of the capital without ceremony, without a farewell address, without the choreography that dictatorships usually give their endings. Whether he left believing he would return, or understanding that he was finished, remained a matter of speculation among his associates for years afterward, with conflicting accounts appearing in Egyptian and foreign reporting.

The announcement came in the evening, and it was almost shockingly brief. Vice President Omar Suleiman appeared on state television and read a statement of under a minute. Its words, in translation, said that in these difficult circumstances the president had decided to step down from the office of president of the republic and had charged the Supreme Council of the Armed Forces with administering the country's affairs. Suleiman read it without elaboration, without a transition plan, without naming a date for elections, and then the broadcast ended. For eighteen days Egyptians had been trained to wait for long Mubarak speeches; this one lasted seconds, and it took a beat for the meaning to register.

Then the squares erupted. In Tahrir, where people had been standing for hours in the winter cold awaiting news, the square detonated into a celebration that witnesses described as the largest collective outpouring of joy in living Egyptian memory. Strangers embraced. Fireworks burst overhead. Egyptian flags waved from shoulders and balconies, from car windows and minarets. Cars honked in unison across the city, a sound that reporters noted carried for miles. In Alexandria, Suez, Port Said, and Mahalla, crowds poured into the streets within minutes of the broadcast. The news traveled the way it had all week: by mobile phone, by Al Jazeera, by shouts passed from balcony to balcony, by the sudden change in the tone of a crowd that seconds before had been tense and expectant. People who had spent the day in the marches described arriving back at Tahrir to find the square already transformed into something between a festival and a religious holiday.

There was another detail worth recording. Among the celebrations, cleanup began almost immediately in places: volunteers sweeping Tahrir, picking up debris, the same civic impulse that had marked the sit-in from its first days. The revolution had its street parties, and it also had its brooms. And beneath the joy ran a question that a few voices were already asking aloud on that very night, one that would shadow everything that followed: power had been transferred not to a civilian authority but to a council of generals. The Supreme Council of the Armed Forces, led by Field Marshal Mohamed Hussein Tantawi, was now the government of Egypt. The crowds celebrated the fall of the president; the identity of his replacement was a problem for the morning.

## The Contested Aftermath: Transition to Mid-2016

What followed the fall of Mubarak was not the orderly march to democracy that the crowds of 11 February imagined, nor was it the simple restoration of the old order that later skeptics described. The honest telling holds both narratives at once, because both contain truths that the other cannot fully absorb. Supporters of the revolutionary account point to a dictator removed by popular pressure, to elections that had not been held freely in living memory, to a constitution written by an elected assembly rather than a palace. Critics of that account point to the military's uninterrupted grip on real power, to a revolution that toppled one man but left the institutions beneath him standing, to an economy that never delivered on the slogan of bread and social justice. Presenting either side at its strongest is not neutrality for its own sake; it is the only way to write about a transition whose outcome was still being argued in the streets, the courts, and the press through the first half of 2016.

The military moved quickly to fill the vacuum. The Supreme Council of the Armed Forces suspended the 1971 constitution, dissolved the parliament elected in the widely discredited 2010 vote, and announced that it would govern for a transitional period of six months, or until parliamentary and presidential elections could be held. Six months became a year and a half. Field Marshal Tantawi, the defense minister who had served Mubarak for two decades, became the de facto head of state, and the council's communiques, read on state television in the same cadence as the old regime's announcements, governed daily life. The council's supporters argued that only the army could guarantee stability during a dangerous interregnum, and pointed to the army's restraint on the streets as evidence of institutional good faith. Critics contended that the generals were managing the transition to protect their own vast economic interests and political prerogatives, and that each delay in the promised timetable proved it.

In March 2011, Egyptians voted in a referendum on constitutional amendments proposed by a committee appointed by the SCAF, a vote that passed with a large majority. It was the first genuinely free vote in decades, and turnout was high. But the referendum also exposed the first deep fissure of the transition: the divide between those who wanted elections first and those who wanted the constitution written first. Islamists, above all the Muslim Brotherhood, campaigned for the amendments, calculating that early elections would favor the best-organized force in the country, which was theirs. Many liberal and leftist groups opposed the amendments, arguing that a constitution should come before any vote. The "yes" vote won decisively, and the Brotherhood emerged from the referendum as the dominant political actor of the transition, a position its leaders had not held openly under Mubarak.

Parliamentary elections followed, held in stages from November 2011 through January 2012. The Muslim Brotherhood's Freedom and Justice Party won a plurality of seats, with the more conservative Salafist Al-Nour Party taking a strong second place; together the Islamist bloc commanded a large majority of the lower house. Liberals, leftists, and the youth groups that had filled Tahrir performed poorly, fragmented among many small parties. Supporters of the Islamist victory argued that it reflected the genuine preferences of the electorate and that decades of repression had given Islamists the deepest organizational roots in Egyptian society. Critics contended that the result owed more to organizational machinery than to ideological conviction, and some revolutionary activists argued that the Brotherhood had betrayed the square by cutting deals with the military council during 2011. Both readings circulated widely, and neither could be settled by the vote totals alone.

The presidential runoff of June 2012 brought the transition's most dramatic civilian moment. Mohamed Morsi, the Brotherhood's candidate, faced Ahmed Shafik, a former air force commander and Mubarak's last prime minister. Morsi won with roughly 51.7 percent of the vote, becoming Egypt's first freely elected president and its first Islamist head of state. For the Brotherhood, it was a historic vindication; for secular and Coptic Egyptians, many of whom had voted for Morsi as the lesser evil against a Mubarak-era figure, it was a result to be watched with deep apprehension. Days before the runoff, the SCAF had dissolved the Islamist-dominated parliament on the basis of a court ruling, and issued a constitutional declaration preserving sweeping powers for the military, moves that Morsi's supporters denounced as a soft coup and that the council's defenders described as necessary legal corrections. Egypt thus entered its first elected presidency with the elected legislature dissolved and the military claiming legislative authority, a structural contradiction that would not be resolved by the election itself.

Morsi's presidency lasted one turbulent year. His supporters credit him with attempting to assert civilian control over a military that had ruled behind the scenes for six decades, and point to his August 2012 retirement of Tantawi and other senior generals as a genuine act of democratic authority. His critics contend that he governed for the Brotherhood rather than for Egypt, filling institutions with loyalists and failing to build the broad consensus his narrow victory demanded. The crisis came in November 2012, when Morsi issued a constitutional declaration placing his decisions above judicial review until a new constitution was ratified. The declaration's defenders argued that a judiciary packed with Mubarak-era judges was obstructing the elected government and that the measure was temporary. Its opponents called it a power grab that reproduced the authoritarianism the revolution had toppled, and the streets filled again with protests, this time with Islamists and secularists facing each other across barricades rather than standing together against the police. A new constitution was pushed through a referendum in December 2012 amid a boycott by much of the opposition, and the polarization hardened into something that felt, to many observers, like two countries occupying one.

By the spring of 2013, Egypt was in a state of rolling crisis: fuel shortages, power cuts, a collapsing currency, and a political stalemate that neither side could break. A grassroots petition campaign called Tamarod, Arabic for "rebel," claimed to have gathered millions of signatures demanding Morsi's resignation and early presidential elections; the signature totals were widely cited but never independently verified. On 30 June 2013, the first anniversary of Morsi's inauguration, enormous crowds filled the streets of Cairo and other cities. The crowds were described by organizers and sympathetic media as the largest in Egyptian history, with estimates running into the tens of millions; critics of that claim noted that such figures were physically implausible and politically motivated, and neutral observers could only agree that the turnout was very large without endorsing a number. What is not disputed is that the scale of the protests gave the military the political cover it needed. On 3 July, after a forty-eight-hour ultimatum, the armed forces removed Morsi from office, suspended the 2012 constitution, and installed the head of the constitutional court, Adly Mansour, as interim president. Morsi's supporters called it a military coup against a democratically elected president. Its defenders, including many who had protested on 30 June, called it the enforcement of popular will against a president who had lost legitimacy. International reactions split along the same line, with some governments calling it a coup and others declining to use the word.

The aftermath of 3 July was the bloodiest chapter of the entire period. The weeks that followed saw mass sit-ins by Morsi's supporters at Rabaa al-Adawiya and Nahda squares, and on 14 August 2013 security forces dispersed them in an operation that killed hundreds of people. Estimates of the dead ranged from several hundred, in official accounts, to over a thousand, in the accounts of human rights organizations and the Brotherhood; the true figure was never established to universal satisfaction, and the dispersal at Rabaa became one of the most contested events in modern Egyptian history. Morsi's supporters described a massacre of peaceful protesters; the authorities described an armed sit-in that had to be cleared and blamed the Brotherhood for the violence. A crackdown followed that imprisoned thousands of Brotherhood members and leaders, along with secular activists who had opposed both Morsi and the military, and the Brotherhood was declared a terrorist organization by the interim government, a designation its members and sympathizers rejected as political persecution. The emergency law, lifted in 2012, was reimposed in August 2013, restoring the state's sweeping detention powers.

The new order consolidated through 2014. A revised constitution was approved in a January 2014 referendum with an overwhelming official majority, though turnout was disputed and the opposition largely boycotted or was suppressed. In May 2014, Abdel Fattah el-Sisi, the general who had announced Morsi's removal, resigned his military post and won the presidency in an election whose official result gave him over 96 percent of the vote; his supporters presented the landslide as a popular mandate for stability, while critics noted the absence of serious competition and the climate of repression in which the vote was held. Parliamentary elections were delayed repeatedly and finally held in late 2015, producing a legislature dominated by pro-Sisi independents and parties, with the Brotherhood excluded by the ban. Through the first half of 2016, Egypt's economy remained under severe strain: foreign reserves were depleted, the pound was under pressure, and tourism, the industry that had employed millions and symbolized the country's openness, had still not recovered from the successive shocks of 2011, 2013, and the October 2015 downing of a Russian airliner over Sinai, an attack claimed by the Islamic State's local affiliate that devastated the Red Sea resort trade. Supporters of the government argued that security had to come before prosperity and pointed to megaprojects, including the expansion of the Suez Canal opened in August 2015, as signs of ambition. Critics contended that the megaprojects masked structural problems: a bloated public sector, a military economy shielded from scrutiny, and the unmet demand for the bread and social justice of the original slogan.

Framing this passage within the longer arc helps explain why it felt so unresolved. The 2011 uprising belongs to [the longer story of modern Egypt since 1952](/2015/11/01/modern-egypt-since-1952/), and that longer story is largely the story of the military's place in the state. Every president since the 1952 Free Officers' coup had come from the armed forces, and the revolution's central unanswered question was whether Egypt could break that pattern. By mid-2016, the answer was ambiguous at best. A civilian Islamist had won the presidency and been removed by the army; a general had been elected president in a vote his opponents called stage-managed; the emergency-law powers Mubarak had wielded for three decades had returned in new legal dress. The role of the military in politics, which the Tahrir crowds had cheered as the army's protection in February 2011, remained the single largest fact of Egyptian political life, and it was no closer to democratic resolution than it had been on the night the tanks rolled into the square to applause.

The economic demands were equally unfulfilled. The strikes that helped finish Mubarak in February 2011 had been about wages, prices, and dignity as much as about the presidency, and five years later the workers who struck were in many cases worse off: inflation eroding pay, subsidies being cut under pressure from international lenders, and independent unions facing the same restrictions as other civic organizations. Tourism workers in Luxor and Sharm el-Sheikh, who had cheered the revolution's promise of a freer Egypt, found themselves in an industry hollowed out by insecurity and travel warnings. None of this means the revolution achieved nothing; the fall of a thirty-year ruler by popular pressure, the experience of free voting, and the permanent demonstration that Egyptians could bring down a government are facts that no later disappointment erases. But by 1 July 2016, the ledger was honest only if it recorded the debits alongside the credits: the dictator gone, the old order's deepest structures intact, the military still the arbiter of politics, the emergency powers restored, and the economic justice of the original demands still waiting.

### How was the SCAF transition roadmap structured after the resignation?

The Supreme Council of the Armed Forces suspended the constitution, dissolved parliament, and issued a constitutional declaration in March 2011. Its roadmap sequenced a constitutional referendum first, then parliamentary elections, then a presidential vote, with the generals as caretakers throughout. Critics argued the order favored the best-organized forces; the council called it the fastest path to civilian rule.

## The Verdict: Speed and Unfinished Aftermath

Return to the claim this article opened with, because it is the only honest way to close. The eighteen-days-that-shook-Egypt thesis holds that the 2011 uprising toppled a thirty-year ruler in less than three weeks, and that both its astonishing speed and its unfinished aftermath are central to understanding it. The first half of that thesis is a matter of record. No one planned an eighteen-day revolution. The organizers of 25 January hoped for a large protest; they did not expect the police to collapse, the army to defect by inaction, and the presidency to empty within a fortnight. The velocity was the revolution's signature, and it is what separates 2011 from every other episode of Egyptian street politics. The bread riots of 1977 won concessions in days but changed no ruler. The Kefaya demonstrations of the 2000s filled headlines but not squares. In 2011, a ruler who had survived assassination attempts, regional wars, economic crises, and three decades of opposition was gone before February was half over, removed not by a coup or a foreign army but by people standing in public squares and refusing to leave. That is an extraordinary fact, and no later disappointment erases it.

The second half of the thesis is where the argument lives, and it must be stated with the same evenhandedness that governed this article throughout. The triumphant narrative, the one chanted in Tahrir on the night of 11 February, holds that the revolution achieved something real and rare: the removal of an entrenched autocrat by popular action, the shattering of the fear barrier, and, for a season, genuinely competitive politics. Its defenders point out that judging an eighteen-day uprising by the five hard years that followed sets a bar no revolution could clear, and that the millions who marched won something no one can legislate away, the knowledge that they could do it. The it-all-failed narrative, the one that hardened as the years wore on, holds that the old order absorbed the shock and restored itself: the military returned to the center of politics, emergency-style governance crept back, the economy kept struggling, and many of the young activists who made January 2011 were imprisoned, exiled, or silenced. Its defenders point out that a revolution is properly judged by what it builds, not only by what it destroys, and that the structures the uprising left intact, above all the military's political and economic dominance, shaped everything after.

Both narratives are true at once, and that is the verdict. The 2011 Revolution proved that popular power could decapitate a regime in eighteen days; it did not prove that popular power could rebuild a state. The first achievement was real and historic. The second was never attempted under conditions the revolutionaries controlled, because the institution that made the fall possible, the army, also set the terms of what followed.

There is a further layer worth stating plainly, because it explains why the debate stays so heated. The two narratives are not only arguments about the past; they are arguments about what Egyptians should do next, which is why each side hears the other as a political program rather than a historical judgment. Those who stress the triumph tend to treat the eighteen days as proof that mass mobilization works and can be repeated, a usable past for future protest. Those who stress the failure tend to treat the aftermath as proof that street action without organization, leadership, and a plan for the state ends in restoration, a cautionary past against repeating it. Both readings draw selectively on the same evidence. The triumphalist must explain away the prisons; the catastrophist must explain away the fact that a thirty-year ruler fell to unarmed crowds, something almost no observer thought possible in January 2011.

The fairest measure may be comparative. Set Egypt's eighteen days beside the revolutions it is usually compared with, and both the speed and the incompleteness come into focus. The 1919 revolution against the British took months of nationwide uprising to win limited concessions and left the occupation's structure standing for decades more. The 1952 Free Officers' coup changed the state overnight but from above, without mass participation. What happened in 2011 combined the mass character of 1919 with the regime-decapitating speed of 1952, and that combination is what makes it historically distinctive. No other modern Egyptian upheaval removed a ruler so fast through popular action alone. But the comparison also clarifies the failure: unlike 1952, the 2011 uprising produced no new ruling coalition with a plan for the state, and unlike 1919, it won no durable institutional settlement. It was a revolution of removal without a revolution of reconstruction.

To call the revolution a pure triumph is to ignore the prisons and the restored authoritarianism. To call it a pure failure is to insult the memory of the hundreds who died and the millions who marched, and to forget how impossible Mubarak's fall had seemed on the morning of 25 January. The unfinished aftermath is not a footnote to the eighteen days. It is half the story, and any account that hurries past it has not understood what made those eighteen days matter.

## Study and Revision: Holding the Eighteen Days in Memory

The 2011 Revolution rewards study in the same order it happened: grievances, spark, occupation, fall, aftermath. Anchor the five dates that carry the whole story. On 25 January the Day of Rage tested whether the fear barrier would hold, and it did not. On 28 January the Friday of Anger broke the police and brought the army into the streets as the deciding institution. On 2 February the Battle of the Camel turned national sympathy decisively toward the square. On 10 February Mubarak's defiant third speech destroyed the last hope of a negotiated exit. On 11 February a one-minute announcement ended thirty years. Everything else in this article hangs on those five days.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What happened in the 2011 Revolution?

Between 25 January and 11 February 2011, mass protests across Egypt forced President Hosni Mubarak to resign after nearly thirty years in power. The uprising began on National Police Day, when tens of thousands marched in Cairo, Alexandria, Suez, and other cities against police brutality, corruption, poverty, and the absence of political freedom. Tunisia's ouster of its own ruler two weeks earlier had shown Egyptians that entrenched regimes could fall. After a massive Friday of Anger on 28 January, during which police withdrew and the army deployed but refused to fire on crowds, protesters occupied Tahrir Square in central Cairo and held it for two weeks. Strikes spread, Mubarak's televised speeches failed to calm the streets, and on 11 February Vice President Omar Suleiman announced the resignation. Power passed to the Supreme Council of the Armed Forces, opening a turbulent transition whose outcome remained debated years later.

### Q: What was Tahrir Square during the revolution?

Tahrir Square, whose name means Liberation, became the revolution's headquarters, symbol, and stage. From 28 January onward, tens of thousands occupied the vast downtown Cairo traffic circle around the clock, turning it into a tent city with field clinics, food stations, checkpoints, and stages for speeches and music. Holding the square mattered strategically: it proved the protests could not be dispersed, gave journalists a fixed address for the story, and created a visible alternative government of the street. The square's discipline, with volunteers checking IDs and collecting trash, projected legitimacy to watching cameras, while its location beside ministries, parliament, and state television kept pressure on the regime's nerve centers. When Mubarak fell, Tahrir erupted in celebration, and the square's name became shorthand worldwide for the entire uprising.

### Q: How did the Arab Spring reach Egypt?

The Arab Spring reached Egypt through Tunisia's example. When mass protests forced Tunisia's Zine El Abidine Ben Ali to flee on 14 January 2011, Egyptians watched on satellite television and online and drew the obvious parallel: another aging Arab ruler, another police state, another frustrated young population. Egyptian activists, already organized through groups like the April 6 Youth Movement, seized the moment and called for protests on 25 January. The Tunisian slogan demanding the fall of the regime crossed the border intact and echoed in Tahrir. Egypt's uprising then amplified the wave in turn: Mubarak's fall eighteen days later electrified protesters in Libya, Yemen, Bahrain, and Syria, making Egypt the Arab Spring's largest and most-watched chapter.

### Q: Why did Egyptians rise up in 2011?

Egyptians rose up over an accumulation of grievances built across decades. Emergency law had been in force since 1981, allowing detention without charge and underpinning a feared police apparatus. Police brutality was a daily humiliation, symbolized by the June 2010 beating death of Khaled Said in Alexandria. Corruption concentrated wealth among a connected elite while a large share of Egyptians lived in poverty and educated young people faced unemployment or dead-end jobs. The November 2010 parliamentary elections were brazenly rigged, closing the last safety valve of managed political participation. Above it all loomed the succession question: the aging Mubarak appeared to be grooming his son Gamal, which insulted a republic founded on overthrowing a monarchy in 1952. Tunisia's success supplied the final ingredient, proof that the fear barrier could break.

### Q: How did social media fuel the 2011 Revolution?

Social media gave a leaderless protest movement its nervous system. The "We Are All Khaled Said" Facebook page, administered anonymously by Wael Ghonim, turned outrage over police brutality into a community of hundreds of thousands. Activists used Facebook events to set the 25 January date and Twitter hashtags to coordinate march routes in real time. YouTube carried Asmaa Mahfoud's video appeal and footage of police violence that state television would never show. Scholars debate how decisive this was: Egypt had a long tradition of street politics, and most Egyptians were not online. But social media lowered the cost of organizing, let protesters outpace the security services, and broadcast the uprising to the world. When the regime cut the internet on 28 January, the blackout itself became proof of panic.

### Q: How did Mubarak fall in 2011?

Mubarak fell because every pillar of his rule cracked within eighteen days. The protests grew too large for the police, who collapsed on 28 January. The army, deployed to restore order, refused to fire on crowds, depriving the regime of its last coercive tool. Mubarak's three televised speeches misread the moment: concessions that might have worked on 25 January insulted protesters by 10 February, when he delegated powers but refused to resign. Labor strikes in the final days threatened the economy's functioning. International allies, including the United States, publicly pressed for an orderly transition, narrowing his room. On 11 February, with marches converging on the presidential palace and state television building, the military leadership concluded he was finished, and Vice President Omar Suleiman announced the resignation in a statement of under a minute.

### Q: What did the 2011 Revolution achieve?

The revolution's undeniable achievement was the removal of Hosni Mubarak after nearly thirty years, something no election, court, or opposition party had accomplished. It shattered the barrier of fear: millions of Egyptians discovered they could fill the streets and survive, a psychological shift that outlasted every later setback. It forced the region's rulers to reckon with popular power and inspired uprisings across the Arab world. It produced a freer press and a more politically engaged citizenry, at least for a time, and it put police abuse and corruption at the center of public debate. Defenders of the triumphant narrative argue these gains were real even if later events disappointed. The revolution proved that Egyptians, not just generals or parties, could change their country's course.

### Q: Did the 2011 Revolution fail?

This is the revolution's most contested question, and honest historians give both answers their due. The it-failed case notes that by mid-2016 the military was back at the center of politics, emergency-style governance had returned, the economy still struggled, and many activists were imprisoned or exiled; the old order, critics argued, had absorbed the shock and restored itself. The it-succeeded case replies that judging an eighteen-day uprising by five years of aftermath sets an impossible bar: Mubarak fell, the fear barrier broke, and Egyptians voted in genuinely competitive elections in 2012. Most scholars land between the poles: the revolution achieved something real and rare, the removal of an entrenched ruler by popular protest, but failed to dismantle the deeper structures of military dominance and economic exclusion. Its story was unfinished, not simply failed.

### Q: Why did the protests begin on National Police Day?

25 January was National Police Day, a national holiday honoring the police. Activists chose it deliberately to turn the regime's own celebration against it: protesting police brutality on the police's holiday was a pointed insult the whole country would understand, and organizers spread the date through Facebook with slogans contrasting the holiday's official meaning with the Interior Ministry's record. The holiday also meant many Egyptians had the day off, which made it easier to gather. The choice captured the movement's character, peaceful, symbolic, and aimed squarely at the abuses of the security forces. The regime understood the provocation but dismissed the turnout; its failure to grasp how the symbolism would magnify the crowds was among its first miscalculations.

### Q: Who was Khaled Said?

Khaled Said was a young Alexandrian whose death in June 2010 became the revolution's most potent symbol of police brutality. Witnesses said plainclothes policemen dragged him from an internet cafe and beat him to death; the Interior Ministry claimed he had choked on drugs, and two officers were later tried over the killing. Photographs of his battered face in the morgue circulated online and provoked national outrage, because they made an abstract grievance concrete and undeniable. Wael Ghonim's "We Are All Khaled Said" Facebook page, named for him, gathered hundreds of thousands of followers and became an organizing hub for the January protests. Said never set out to be a martyr, but his case distilled everything protesters hated about the police state into a single human story.

### Q: Why did the army refuse to fire on protesters?

Historians attribute the army's restraint to institutional self-interest as much as sympathy. The Egyptian military saw itself as the nation's guardian rather than the ruler's bodyguard; firing on massive peaceful crowds would have shattered that image and risked splitting the ranks, since conscripts came from the same frustrated population. Generals also calculated that Mubarak was finished and that tying the army's fate to his would endanger its vast economic interests and privileged position. The police, hated and already broken, could be sacrificed; the army could not. Some officers may also have genuinely sympathized with the crowds, and the famous scenes of soldiers fraternizing with protesters suggest the divide between the military and the street was thinner than the regime assumed. Whether the generals acted from patriotism or calculation remains debated, but their refusal removed the regime's last resort and effectively decided Mubarak's fate.

### Q: What was the Battle of the Camel?

The Battle of the Camel was the revolution's ugliest day of street fighting. On 2 February 2011, after Mubarak's speech failed to clear Tahrir, men on horses and camels, accompanied by plainclothes regime supporters armed with sticks, stones, and Molotov cocktails, charged into the square to drive protesters out. Tahrir's defenders, including football ultras experienced in street clashes, held their ground through a night of brutal fighting that left several dead and hundreds injured. State media called it a clash between citizens; protesters and journalists identified the attackers as paid Interior Ministry thugs, and later trials examined the regime's role. The battle backfired: televised images of camels charging peaceful demonstrators horrified Egypt and the world, and sympathy for the occupation surged.

### Q: What did Mubarak say in his three speeches during the uprising?

Mubarak addressed the nation three times, and each speech became a case study in too little, too late. On 28 January, after the Friday of Anger, he dismissed the government and promised reforms but refused to step down. On 1 February, following the million-man march, he pledged not to seek re-election and offered a managed transition until September, which protesters rejected with the demand that he leave immediately. On 10 February, with the whole country expecting his resignation, he instead delegated some powers to Vice President Suleiman and insisted on remaining until his term ended. The anticlimax enraged the streets and triggered the next day's marches on the presidential palace. On 11 February it was Suleiman, not Mubarak, who spoke, for less than a minute, to announce the end.

### Q: Who announced Mubarak's resignation?

Vice President Omar Suleiman announced it. On the evening of 11 February 2011, Suleiman, the longtime intelligence chief whom Mubarak had appointed vice president only days earlier in a bid to calm the streets, appeared on state television and read a statement of under a minute: Mubarak had resigned and handed power to the Supreme Council of the Armed Forces. Mubarak himself never addressed the nation to concede; he had already left Cairo for Sharm el-Sheikh, and Egyptians learned of his departure through the announcement. The brevity of the statement matched the suddenness of the fall, as a thirty-year presidency ended in seconds and Tahrir Square erupted in celebration before the broadcast finished.

### Q: What was the Supreme Council of the Armed Forces?

The Supreme Council of the Armed Forces, known as SCAF, was the body of senior generals that ruled Egypt after Mubarak's fall. Headed by Field Marshal Mohamed Hussein Tantawi, Mubarak's longtime defense minister, the council took power on 11 February 2011 promising a short transition to civilian rule. It suspended the constitution, dissolved parliament, and governed by decree while organizing a constitutional referendum and elections. The transition stretched to sixteen months, ending when Mohamed Morsi took office in June 2012. Protesters who had cheered the army in February grew disillusioned as military trials of civilians continued and the council guarded its privileges. SCAF embodied the revolution's central ambiguity: the institution that declined to save Mubarak also declined to surrender power quickly.

### Q: What role did the Muslim Brotherhood play in the 2011 Revolution?

The Muslim Brotherhood, Egypt's oldest Islamist movement, joined the uprising cautiously and then rode it to power. Initially the Brotherhood's leadership hesitated, wary of a crackdown, though many young members protested from 25 January. As Tahrir held, the movement committed fully, and its disciplined organization made it the best-organized civilian force in the transition. It dominated the March 2011 referendum campaign, won a plurality in the 2011-2012 parliamentary elections through its Freedom and Justice Party, and in June 2012 its candidate Mohamed Morsi won the presidency. Critics argued the Brotherhood exploited a revolution it had not started; supporters replied it earned power through elections. Its polarizing year in office ended with Morsi's removal in July 2013, a denouement that split Egyptians bitterly.

### Q: What role did women play in the 2011 Revolution?

Women were central to the 2011 Revolution from its first hours. Asmaa Mahfoud's video appeal helped summon the 25 January crowds; women marched, occupied Tahrir, ran field clinics, documented events on camera, and stood against police charges. The square's relative safety, enforced by volunteer checkpoints, let women participate in numbers rarely seen in Egyptian public life, and the iconic imagery of the uprising is full of female faces. After Mubarak's fall, however, women's rights activists found their demands sidelined in the transition, and incidents like the military's forced virginity tests on detained female protesters in March 2011 exposed how shallow the revolution's gender gains were. The debate over whether the uprising advanced Egyptian women remains unresolved.

### Q: How many people died in the 2011 Revolution?

The death toll remains disputed, which itself reflects the era's contested record-keeping. Egypt's official fact-finding figures and most international tallies put the number killed during the eighteen days between 25 January and 11 February 2011 at roughly 800 to 900, with several thousand injured. Suez, Alexandria, and Cairo saw the heaviest losses, particularly on the Friday of Anger when police opened fire. Some activist counts run higher and some official revisions lower, and the chaos of those days makes precision impossible. What is not disputed is that the dead, honored as martyrs of the revolution, became a moral center of the uprising: their names were chanted in Tahrir, and demands for justice for them fueled protests long after Mubarak fell.
