## The Cost of Arming Ukraine From Poland Is a Ledger, Not a Headline

The cost of arming Ukraine from Poland is usually argued as a slogan rather than tallied as an account. One side says Poland emptied its arsenals into a neighbor's war and left itself thin. The other side says the transfers were surplus, the bill was small, and the strategic return was enormous. Both readings are answers to a question neither side has actually posed correctly. The question is not whether support was expensive or cheap. The question is what it cost, in what currencies, and what it bought back, and whether the buying-back arrived fast enough to matter.

That is an accounting problem, and accounting problems have a discipline the slogans lack. A ledger has two sides. On one side sits everything support consumed: hardware that left Polish depots and did not come back, money that flowed out of a budget that had other claims on it, industrial capacity absorbed by a neighbor's requirements, and a stretch of time during which Polish formations held less equipment than their establishment tables called for. On the other side sits everything support returned: a Russian army fixed and ground down several hundred kilometers east of the Polish border, a modernization program that jumped a decade in a few years because old inventory left and new inventory had to replace it, industrial contracts that turned a domestic sector into something with a real order book, and a set of alliance relationships that support helped purchase. The honest verdict is the difference between those columns, and the difference is not obvious in either direction until you do the work.

This article does that work. It sets out what left, what it cost to give, what came back, and how fast. It names the variable that decides the verdict, which is not the size of the transfers but the speed of the replacement. And it holds itself to the cost question rather than drifting into the lifeline question, the motive question, or the affordability question, each of which belongs to another article in this series. The broader frame of what the Ukraine war reveals about Polish exposure is treated at length in [what the Ukraine war tells us about Poland's own risk](/2026/09/01/what-ukraine-tells-us-about-poland/), and the reasons Warsaw chose to bear the cost at all are the subject of [Poland's stake in Ukraine's war](/2026/10/01/polands-stake-in-ukraines-war/). What follows is narrower and more mercenary: the price, the offsets, and the net.

One framing note before the substance. Precise figures in this domain move, get revised, get classified, and get contested, and a series built to stay citable cannot anchor itself to a number that will be wrong within a year. So the treatment here is structural and relative. Where a magnitude matters, it is given as a pattern or a range with an instruction to confirm the current figure against official statements and open defense reporting. Where a number would be invented to sound authoritative, no number appears. That is a limitation on precision and an improvement in reliability, and for a question this politically charged it is the correct trade.

### What has arming Ukraine actually cost Poland?

It cost Poland three things at once: large quantities of Soviet-pattern and early-generation Western-pattern materiel drawn from existing stocks, direct fiscal outlays across military, humanitarian, and refugee-hosting lines, and a period during which Polish units held less equipment than their tables prescribed. The first two were paid immediately. The third was paid over time and is closing.

Those three currencies do not convert into one another cleanly, which is exactly why the argument stays unresolved. A tank transferred is a physical object with a replacement cost, a residual value, and an opportunity cost, and those three numbers are wildly different from one another. A refugee hosted is a fiscal line, a labor-market entry, and a demographic event, and the sign on the net effect depends on which timeframe you pick. A readiness gap is not a number at all in any honest sense; it is a condition that exists relative to a threat that may or may not materialize during the window in which the gap is open. Anyone who hands you a single figure for what Ukraine support cost Poland has quietly picked one currency, ignored the others, and called the result the answer.

## What Poland Actually Transferred, and What It Cost to Give

The durable, well-established shape of the record is this. Beginning early in the full-scale war that Russia launched in 2022, Poland transferred substantial quantities of ground-combat and support materiel to Ukraine, and did so faster and earlier than most partners. The transfers were heavily weighted toward Soviet-pattern equipment that Ukrainian forces could operate and sustain without a training and logistics bridge, which is precisely why Poland was positioned to give it and most Western partners were not. Main battle tanks of Soviet lineage, infantry fighting vehicles of the same family, self-propelled and towed artillery in Soviet calibers, air-defense systems, ammunition in Soviet-standard natures, and a long tail of trucks, engineering equipment, and spares moved east. Later, Poland participated in transfers of Western-pattern systems as well, including its own domestically produced self-propelled artillery, and it was among the first to break the political seal on Western main battle tanks going to Ukraine at all.

That is the fact layer. The assessment layer begins with what those transfers meant for the giver, and here the framing matters more than the tonnage.

### The surplus argument and where it is right

The strongest version of the it-was-cheap case rests on a real observation. Much of what Poland transferred was legacy inventory that Poland had already decided to retire. Soviet-pattern armor was scheduled for replacement. Soviet-caliber artillery was being displaced by Polish-built systems in NATO calibers. Soviet-standard ammunition stocks had no long-term future in a force converging on alliance standards. On that reading, Poland exported obsolescence. It cleared depot space, avoided disposal costs, discharged maintenance liabilities on aging fleets, and converted material that was heading for the scrapyard or the museum into strategic credit with Kyiv, Washington, and Brussels. The accountant's term for that is a favorable disposal, not a loss.

This argument is right about a great deal and wrong about the part that matters. It is right that the residual value of a late-Soviet main battle tank in a force converging on Western standards is low. It is right that maintaining two incompatible logistics chains is expensive and that shedding one is a saving, not a cost. It is right that the counterfactual for much of this equipment was not a Polish armored division at full strength but a parking lot.

Where it fails is in confusing residual value with combat value. An old tank is worth little on a balance sheet and quite a lot in a line of battle, because a line of battle does not care about depreciation schedules. The relevant question is not what the equipment was worth but what it was doing, and what it was doing was filling establishment tables in Polish formations while newer equipment was still on order. Remove it and the table has a hole in it. The hole does not care that the object which used to fill it was old.

### The gutted-arsenal argument and where it is right

The strongest version of the it-was-devastating case rests on an equally real observation. Poland did not transfer at the margins. It transferred at scale, early, and across categories that matter for ground combat, and it did so from a force structure that was already smaller in equipment terms than its ambitions. Armor, artillery, and ammunition are the three categories a continental land power cannot be casual about, and Poland gave in all three. For a period, Polish formations were demonstrably lighter than their establishment called for, and the replacements were contracts rather than vehicles. A contract does not hold ground.

This argument is right that the gap was real and wrong in its implicit assumption that the gap was permanent or that it was the whole story. It reads a snapshot as a trend. The snapshot showed depleted stocks; the trend showed the fastest ground-force recapitalization in Europe running concurrently. Judging the drawdown without the backfill is like judging a house renovation by photographing the demolition.

### Why both arguments miss the same variable

Both readings share a defect: they treat the cost as a stock rather than a flow. The surplus school looks at what left and prices it at book value. The gutted school looks at what left and prices it at replacement value. Neither asks the question that actually determines the answer, which is how long the hole stays open. A drawdown replaced in eighteen months and a drawdown replaced in eight years are not the same event even if the identical equipment left the depot in both cases. The first is a liquidity event. The second is a structural loss. Everything about the verdict turns on which one Poland experienced, and that is a question about industrial throughput and delivery schedules, not about tonnage transferred.

That is the article's organizing insight, and it produces the framework in the next section.
## The Cost-and-Offset Ledger

The framework this article advances is the Cost-and-Offset Ledger: a two-column tally that prices Polish support to Ukraine in the currencies it was actually paid in, sets each cost against the offset it generated, and produces a net read that is explicitly conditional on the backfill clock. It is not a scoreboard. It is a structure for arguing honestly, because it forces anyone using it to state which currency they are pricing in and what timeframe they are pricing over, which is precisely what the public debate refuses to do.

| Cost line | What was paid | Offset line | What was returned | Net read, and what it depends on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Materiel drawn from stocks | Soviet-pattern armor, artillery, air defense, and ammunition transferred from existing inventory, plus selected Western-pattern systems | Forced and accelerated modernization | Replacement procurement pulled forward by years; legacy logistics chain shed; fleet converging on alliance standards | Favorable if replacement arrives inside the threat window; adverse if it slips |
| Direct fiscal outlay | Military aid, humanitarian assistance, and refugee-hosting costs carried on the national budget | Alliance and partner credit | Reimbursement and partner backfill mechanisms; procurement access; standing in alliance councils | Partially recovered, never fully; the unrecovered share is the true fiscal price |
| Readiness dip | A period in which formations held less equipment than establishment tables prescribed | War fixed away from Polish soil | Russian ground forces attrited and committed several hundred kilometers east | Favorable while the eastern front holds; the offset is contingent, not banked |
| Industrial capacity absorbed | Domestic production lines and depot capacity working Ukrainian requirements | Order book and scale | Domestic sector given sustained demand, investment case, and export credibility | Favorable if capacity expanded rather than merely reallocated |
| Ammunition stock depth | Consumption of war reserves in natures that are slow to rebuild | Demonstrated requirement | Stockpile depth reframed from accounting nuisance to first-order planning variable | Favorable in doctrine, adverse in the interim; the slowest line to close |
| Political and social bandwidth | Domestic argument over burden, refugees, and grain and trade friction | Strategic clarity | National consensus on the threat hardened; defense spending politically survivable | Mixed; the consensus is real, the friction is also real |

Six lines, six offsets, and one variable running under all of them. Read the far-right column and the pattern is unmistakable: almost every favorable net read is conditional on timing. The materiel offset is favorable if replacement lands before the threat does. The readiness offset is favorable while the eastern front absorbs Russian ground power. The ammunition line is favorable eventually and adverse in the interim. The ledger does not deliver a verdict. It delivers a verdict function, and the input to that function is the clock.

### The namable claim: the backfill-clock rule

Here is the rule this article advances, stated so it can be cited and applied elsewhere. **Arming Ukraine cost Poland old stocks and bought newer ones plus a war kept off Polish soil, so the honest accounting weighs a temporary readiness dip and a real fiscal outlay against modernization and a strategic buffer, and the net turns entirely on how fast backfill closes the gap.** Call it the backfill-clock rule. Its practical form is a comparison of two intervals: the interval during which Polish formations are below establishment, and the interval during which a Polish contingency could plausibly arise. If the first closes inside the second, the drawdown was a liquidity event and the support was net-strengthening. If the first outruns the second, the drawdown was a structural loss regardless of how much modernization it eventually produced. Every serious disagreement about the cost of arming Ukraine is, on inspection, a disagreement about those two intervals.

The rule has a useful corollary. Because the second interval is unknowable, the first is the only one a state can act on, which is why the correct policy response to a drawdown is never to regret the transfer and always to compress the replacement. Regret is not a lever. Throughput is.

## The Drawdown Side: Materiel, Money, and the Readiness Dip

Take the cost column line by line, in durable terms, with the assessment marked off from the fact.

### Materiel: what leaving actually removed

The fact is that Poland transferred armor, mechanized infantry vehicles, artillery, air-defense systems, and ammunition in militarily significant quantities. The assessment is what their absence did.

Start with armor. A main battle tank of Soviet lineage in Polish service was, by the standards of the newest Western and Korean-built types Poland has been acquiring, outclassed in fire control, protection, and sustainment. It was also a functioning tank in a functioning battalion with trained crews, established maintenance practices, and a spares chain. When it left, what left was not a book value. What left was a slot in an order of battle that could not be filled by a purchase order. Poland's armored formations spent a period holding fewer vehicles than their structure called for, and the fact that better vehicles were inbound did not change the arithmetic in the interim.

Artillery is the sharper case. Ground combat in the war Poland was supplying is, by every account in the open record, an artillery contest to a degree that surprised most Western planners. Poland transferred artillery in Soviet calibers and, later, self-propelled systems of its own manufacture, which meant it was giving away not only legacy inventory but current production. That distinction matters enormously to the ledger. Giving legacy inventory is disposal. Giving current production is a genuine opportunity cost, because every system that went east was a system that did not enter Polish service that year, and the line could only build so many.

Air defense is the category where the drawdown bit least in structural terms and most in political ones. The quantities involved were smaller and the systems more specialized, but air defense is the capability a state is least willing to be short of, because it is the one that protects everything else. Transferring it required a judgment that the threat to Polish airspace in the near term was lower than the threat to Ukrainian airspace in the immediate term, which was almost certainly correct and which nonetheless felt, to a Polish planner, like handing over an umbrella in an overcast forecast.

Ammunition is the line that closes slowest and therefore matters longest. This is treated at length below, because it is the part of the drawdown that the surplus argument cannot dismiss and the part the gutted argument understates for the wrong reasons.

### Money: the fiscal outlay and its recovery

The fiscal cost of Polish support has three components that are routinely conflated and should not be. There is the value of transferred materiel, which is an accounting entry rather than a cash outflow and whose stated magnitude depends entirely on whether it is priced at book value, replacement value, or original acquisition cost. There is the direct cash cost of procurement, transport, storage, training support, and the administrative apparatus of running a transfer program at scale. And there is the refugee and humanitarian line, which was carried substantially on Polish public budgets and Polish households and which was, in the early phases, the single largest cash item.

Any figure quoted for what Ukraine support cost Poland is a choice about which of those three to include and how to price the first. Confirm current official figures against Polish government statements and open defense reporting before using any number, and treat any single headline figure as an argument rather than a measurement. This series treats affordability as a separate question with its own owner: whether Poland can sustain the defense budget it has committed to is examined in [whether Poland can afford its defense spending](/2026/03/21/can-poland-afford-defense-spending/), and the fiscal capacity analysis lives there rather than being re-litigated here.

What can be said durably about the money is structural. The unrecovered share is the real price. Reimbursement mechanisms, partner backfill arrangements, and European instruments recovered some fraction of the transfer value, and the fraction was neither trivial nor complete. What Poland actually paid, net, is the transfer value minus recoveries, plus the cash lines that no mechanism reimburses, and that residual is a real number that a real budget absorbed. It was not free. Anyone arguing it was free is pricing at book value and ignoring the cash.

### Readiness: the dip that is not a number

The third currency is the one that resists quantification and drives most of the argument. For a period, Polish ground formations held less equipment than their establishment tables specified. That is a durable statement about a structural condition, and it is the honest core of the gutted-arsenal case.

But readiness is not a scalar. A formation short of tanks but holding its artillery, its trained crews, its command structure, and its sustainment is in a different condition than a formation that has lost its people. Equipment gaps close on delivery schedules. Personnel and institutional gaps close on generational timescales. Poland's drawdown was almost entirely in the first category, which is the recoverable one. That does not make the dip harmless. It makes it temporary, which is a different property and the one the backfill-clock rule keys on.

### Political bandwidth: the cost nobody budgets

The fourth currency in the cost column does not appear in any procurement document and is nonetheless expensive. Sustaining a commitment of this size consumed an enormous quantity of governmental attention, parliamentary time, diplomatic effort, and public argument, and attention is the scarcest resource any government has.

Some of that consumption was productive. The argument about support forced a national conversation about the threat that produced the durable consensus underwriting defense spending, which is an offset rather than a cost. But some of it was pure friction with no analytical return: trade and agricultural disputes that soured relations with the very partner being supported, municipal-level resentments where refugee hosting concentrated, and the ordinary political cost of asking a population to fund an emergency that stopped feeling like an emergency. Those disputes consumed ministerial time that had other claims on it and generated domestic positions that later had to be managed rather than simply held.

The reason this line matters analytically rather than merely descriptively is that political bandwidth is the input to everything else in the ledger. Fiscal capacity is a political choice. Industrial expansion requires sustained political commitment to a multi-year investment case. The backfill clock runs faster or slower depending on whether procurement reform, permitting, and workforce policy get the attention they need, and attention spent arguing about grain is attention not spent compressing a delivery schedule. A cost that consumes the resource that funds the offsets is not a minor line, even though it is the one no accounting system captures.

The honest entry is mixed and should stay mixed. The bandwidth cost was substantial, some of it purchased a consensus worth more than it cost, and some of it was simply consumed. Anyone who claims to have priced it has invented a number.

### Did the drawdown leave Poland exposed?

For a defined period, yes, in the specific sense that formations held less equipment than establishment tables prescribed. But the exposure was equipment-side and time-bounded rather than structural, it coincided with Russian ground power being consumed on a front several hundred kilometers east, and replacement contracts were already running when the gap opened.

That answer needs the second clause as much as the first, and the two are the whole argument. The drawdown created a window. The war that caused the drawdown simultaneously suppressed the threat that would have exploited the window. That is not a coincidence and it is not a lucky accident. It is the structural logic of the transaction, and it is why the transaction was rational rather than reckless. Poland spent readiness against a threat that was, at that moment, fully committed elsewhere precisely because the spending was what kept it committed elsewhere. The drawdown and the buffer it purchased are not two facts to be weighed independently. They are one fact viewed from two sides.
## The Offset Side: Modernization, Backfill, and the Bought Buffer

Now the other column. Four offsets carry the weight, and they are not equally solid.

### Offset one: modernization pulled forward

The most measurable offset is that the drawdown functioned as a forcing mechanism on a recapitalization Poland had been approaching gradually. Retiring legacy fleets is politically and institutionally hard when they still work. Depots resist it, budgets defer it, and the fleet limps on for another decade because nothing forces the decision. Transferring the fleet to a partner at war forced the decision in a matter of months. What followed was procurement at a tempo and scale that no peacetime planning cycle would have produced: armor, artillery, rocket artillery, air defense, and aircraft ordered in volumes and on timelines that would have been dismissed as fantasy in any prior budget round.

The causal claim needs care. Poland's recapitalization was not caused by the transfers. It was caused by the same event that caused the transfers, which was a full-scale war on the far side of a neighbor. The transfers accelerated and shaped it rather than originating it. What the transfers did specifically was remove the option of muddling through with legacy equipment, which converted a gradual modernization into an urgent one and, critically, converted an urgent one into a funded one. Empty depots are an argument that survives contact with a finance ministry. Aging depots are not.

The offset is therefore real but should be priced correctly. Poland did not get free modernization. Poland got modernization it was going to pay for anyway, delivered on a schedule it would not otherwise have achieved, in exchange for equipment it was going to retire anyway, at the price of a gap in between. That is a good trade in most timelines. It is not a costless one, and the gap is where the cost lives.

The industrial dimension of that acceleration, and whether Polish industry can actually deliver at the scale the plans assume, is the subject of [Poland's arms industry ambitions](/2026/05/11/polands-arms-industry-ambitions/), which owns the domestic-capacity question. What matters for the ledger here is narrower: the backfill offset is only as good as the delivery schedule behind it, and delivery schedules are an industrial fact, not a political promise.

### Offset two: the war fixed away from Polish soil

The largest offset is also the least bankable, and the tension between those two properties is the honest difficulty at the center of this whole assessment.

The claim is straightforward. Russian ground forces committed to and attrited in Ukraine are Russian ground forces not available for anything else. Formations consumed in eastern Ukraine do not appear on the Belarusian or Kaliningrad approaches. Equipment stocks drawn down to sustain that campaign are stocks not held in reserve for a subsequent one. Doctrine and command relearned under fire is doctrine and command not available for a different theater in the meantime. Whatever the eventual outcome of the war, the intervening period has been one in which the Russian ground threat to NATO's eastern flank was substantially preoccupied, and Polish support materially contributed to keeping it that way.

Priced against the drawdown, this offset is enormous. Poland traded legacy armor for the continued commitment of the army that legacy armor would have had to face. That is not a favorable trade. It is a lopsided one.

Three caveats keep it honest. The first is that the offset is contingent rather than banked. It holds while the front holds. If the eastern front were to collapse, the offset does not merely shrink; it inverts, because the same Russian force that was fixed becomes available, and it becomes available having learned things. The scenario logic of that inversion is worked through in [the question of whether Poland is next if Ukraine falls](/2026/09/21/if-ukraine-falls-is-poland-next/), which owns that branch. The ledger's job is only to note that this offset is a lease, not a purchase.

The second caveat is attribution. Polish support contributed to fixing the Russian army in Ukraine. It did not do so alone, and the counterfactual in which Poland transferred nothing but every other partner behaved identically is not a world in which the front collapsed. Poland's marginal contribution was significant, particularly in the earliest phase when Soviet-pattern equipment was the only equipment Ukrainian forces could immediately absorb and Poland was among the few able to supply it. But the offset should be credited at the margin, not at the total. Poland did not buy the buffer. Poland bought a share of it, at a decisive moment, at a price that looks small against the share.

The third caveat is that this offset cannot be spent. It does not appear in a budget, it does not fill an establishment table, and it does not defend anything if the equipment gap is exploited during the window. It is a reduction in the probability of the contingency, not an increase in the capacity to meet it. Those are different goods, and a state that confuses them has confused hoping with planning.

### Offset three: the industrial order book

The third offset is that sustained Ukrainian demand, plus Polish recapitalization, plus European rearmament arriving simultaneously, handed Polish defense industry something it had not had in the post-Cold War period: a demand signal long enough to justify capital investment.

A defense industrial base does not expand on hope. It expands on orders that outlast an electoral cycle, because the investment case for a new production line requires demand visibility on the timescale of the line's payback. For thirty years the sector lacked that. What the war produced was demand visibility, and what demand visibility produced was investment decisions that had been deferred for a generation.

Whether that investment converts into delivered throughput is the question, and it belongs to the arms-industry article rather than this one. The ledger entry here is conditional and should be stated as such: the industrial offset is favorable if capacity actually expanded, and merely neutral if the same finite capacity was reallocated from one customer to another. Reallocation is not growth. A line running Ukrainian orders instead of Polish orders has not offset anything; it has moved the cost around.

### Offset four: alliance credit

The fourth offset is the softest and should be priced modestly. Poland's early and heavy support bought standing: influence in alliance councils on eastern-flank questions, credibility in Kyiv, and a claim on partners that has translated into procurement access, reimbursement mechanisms, and backfill arrangements.

Standing is real. Standing is also famously perishable, non-transferable, and difficult to convert into anything at the moment you most need it. The honest ledger entry is that alliance credit recovered some fraction of the fiscal cost through concrete mechanisms and generated some quantity of goodwill that may or may not be callable. Count the mechanisms. Discount the goodwill heavily. A state that treats accumulated goodwill as a defense asset has made a category error that the historical record punishes with some regularity.

## The Backfill Clock: Why Timing Decides the Verdict

Everything above converges here. The drawdown was real, the offsets were real, and the net depends on an interval.

### How the clock actually works

The backfill clock starts when equipment leaves and stops when replacement equipment is in service with trained crews in formed units. Those are different moments, and the gap between them is longer than procurement announcements suggest, for reasons that have nothing to do with anyone's bad faith.

A contract is not a delivery. A delivery is not an acceptance. An acceptance is not a fielded system, because the system arrives before the maintainers are trained, the spares chain is stood up, the simulators are installed, and the unit has worked through the collective training progression that turns a vehicle park into a formation. For a genuinely new type entering service, particularly one from an unfamiliar supplier with an unfamiliar sustainment philosophy, that tail runs years past the delivery date. Poland's recapitalization has involved multiple new types from multiple suppliers simultaneously, which is the fastest way to modernize and also the way that maximizes the length of the tail.

So the honest reading of the clock is that it runs longer than the delivery schedule and shorter than the pessimists claim. It runs longer because fielding is not delivering. It runs shorter because the drawdown was concentrated in categories where replacement was already contracted, not spread across categories where nothing was on order.

### The differential rates problem

The clock does not run at one speed. It runs at different speeds by category, and the slowest category sets the true closure date.

Armor closes fastest, because armor is what Poland ordered most aggressively and because armored vehicles are manufactured objects that a supplier with an open line can produce on a schedule. Artillery closes at a moderate rate, constrained by domestic production capacity and the reality that Poland is one customer among several for every relevant line. Air defense closes slowly, because air-defense systems are complex, supply is constrained globally, and demand exploded across Europe at the same moment.

Ammunition closes slowest of all, and this is the finding that most deserves attention. Ammunition production capacity is not elastic. Expanding it requires energetics, propellant, shell bodies, and fuze components, each with its own supply chain, and each of those chains was optimized over three decades for a peacetime consumption rate that the war invalidated in weeks. You cannot buy ammunition throughput with money alone on a short timescale, because the constraint is physical plant that takes years to build. Every European state discovered this simultaneously, which meant they all bid for the same expansion capacity at the same time.

The consequence for the ledger is specific: the ammunition line is the one where the drawdown genuinely bites, because it is the one line where the offset arrives too late to offset. Everything else Poland gave away is being replaced by something better on a schedule measured in years. Ammunition stock depth is being rebuilt against a constraint that money does not relieve. If you want to know where the honest cost of arming Ukraine landed, it landed in shell counts.

### How fast does backfill have to close the gap?

Fast enough to beat a contingency whose timing nobody knows, which means the only defensible standard is as fast as industrial throughput physically allows. Because the threat interval is unknowable, the gap interval is the only side a state controls, so the operative measure is not whether backfill is fast but whether it is at the physical limit.

That reframing is worth sitting with, because it dissolves a false debate. Arguing about whether backfill is fast enough presumes we know what it must be fast enough for. We do not. What we can assess is whether the replacement program is running at the maximum rate physics and industry permit, or whether it is running at the rate that budget cycles, procurement bureaucracy, and political attention permit, which is invariably slower. The gap between those two rates is the only part of the cost of arming Ukraine that is genuinely anyone's fault. The drawdown was a choice with a defensible rationale. A backfill running below the physical limit is not a choice; it is a failure, and it is the one thing in this entire ledger that could still be fixed by deciding to fix it.

Analysts and staffers who want to track that gap rather than argue about it can [build a private, versioned cost-and-offset note in VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), where the ledger's six lines can be maintained with confidence levels attached and updated as delivery schedules move, without the working file leaving the device. For the ongoing monitoring side, [the cost-and-offset checklist on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/poland-russia-risk-monitor.html) turns the framework into a repeatable check that flags which lines are closing at the physical limit and which are closing at the bureaucratic one.
## The Transit Burden: What It Costs to Be the Corridor

One line in the cost column is routinely omitted because it does not look like a cost. Poland did not only give things to Ukraine. Poland became the ground through which nearly everything else that anyone gave to Ukraine had to pass, and being that ground has a price that no transfer register records.

The role itself belongs to another article. How Poland came to occupy that position, what it built to sustain it, and what it means for alliance logistics are treated in [how Poland became Ukraine's lifeline](/2026/10/11/poland-as-ukraines-lifeline/), which owns the corridor question. What belongs here is narrower: the corridor is an entry in the ledger, and it has both a cost side and an offset side that the usual accounting misses entirely.

### The cost side of the corridor

The cost is infrastructure consumption, and infrastructure consumption is the most invisible expense a state can incur, because it shows up years later as a maintenance backlog rather than immediately as a budget line.

Rail is the clearest case. Moving military volumes across a network for a sustained period consumes track life, rolling stock life, and yard capacity, and it does so while displacing commercial traffic that would otherwise have used the same slots. The displacement is a real economic cost borne by shippers and by the rail operator, and the consumption is a real physical cost borne by the network. Neither appears in any tally of what Poland gave Ukraine, and both are genuine.

Road is the same story with worse economics, because heavy military movement degrades pavement at rates far above ordinary commercial loading, and the degradation is concentrated on specific corridors rather than distributed. A handful of routes carry the traffic and a handful of routes take the damage, which means the cost lands on particular regional budgets rather than on the national one that made the decision. That mismatch between who decides and who pays is a permanent feature of corridor states and a reliable source of the domestic friction discussed above.

Then there is the capacity cost, which is subtler. Handling capacity devoted to one flow is handling capacity unavailable for another, and the other flow in this case includes Poland's own recapitalization. Every new system Poland ordered has to arrive, be received, be distributed, and be sustained through the same nodes, the same yards, and substantially the same people who were running the eastward flow. The corridor did not only cost Poland track and pavement. It competed, at the margin, with the backfill that the entire favorable verdict depends on. That is a genuinely uncomfortable entry and it belongs in the ledger.

There is also a security cost. Concentrated, predictable logistics flows through known nodes are exactly the thing that a gray-zone campaign finds attractive, which meant the corridor generated a protection requirement that consumed forces, attention, and money. That requirement is not a transfer to Ukraine by any accounting convention, and it exists solely because of the transfers.

### The offset side of the corridor

Set against that, the corridor generated returns that are larger than they look.

The first is infrastructure investment that outlives the flow. Capacity built to handle a wartime volume does not evaporate when the volume stops. Track improved, yards expanded, handling equipment acquired, and procedures developed are all assets, and they are assets in precisely the categories that alliance reinforcement of the eastern flank would need in a contingency. Poland spent a decade's worth of logistics development in a few years because a real requirement forced it, which is the same forcing mechanism the equipment drawdown applied to modernization, operating in a different domain.

The second is institutional. Running sustained military logistics at volume teaches a system things that exercises do not. Reception, staging, onward movement, and integration are the least glamorous and most decisive functions in any reinforcement scenario, and Poland has been performing them continuously under real conditions with real friction. That is competence acquired at someone else's expense, and it is worth more than the pavement cost.

The third is positional. A state that is the indispensable route for an alliance's support to a partner has a form of leverage that is difficult to acquire any other way. It is not a leverage anyone would design a policy to obtain, and it is nonetheless real, and it underwrites some fraction of the alliance credit discussed above.

The honest net on the corridor line is mildly favorable and highly uncertain, which is an unsatisfying entry and an accurate one. The costs are diffuse, deferred, and regionally concentrated. The offsets are durable, institutional, and hard to price. What can be said with confidence is that the line exists, that it is omitted from essentially every public tally of Poland's Ukraine costs, and that omitting it distorts the account in both directions at once.

## The Ammunition Line: Where the Cost Really Landed

If a reader retains one specific finding from this ledger rather than one framework, it should be this: the cost of arming Ukraine landed hardest in a category that money cannot fix on a short timescale, and the debate has spent its energy arguing about tanks.

### Why ammunition behaves differently from everything else

Every other line in the ledger closes on a purchase. Order the vehicles, take delivery, field them, and the gap closes on a schedule that is knowable and largely controllable. Ammunition does not work that way, for reasons that are physical rather than administrative.

Ammunition production is a chemical and metallurgical industry, not an assembly industry. The binding constraints sit upstream, in energetics and propellant production, in shell-body forging capacity, and in fuze component supply, and each of those is a capital-intensive plant with long lead times, significant regulatory burden, and, in the case of energetics, genuinely hazardous processes that constrain where and how fast capacity can be added. You cannot subcontract your way around a propellant shortage the way you can subcontract a subassembly. The plant is the constraint, and plants take years.

Compounding this is a demand-side fact that made the problem structural rather than national. Three decades of peacetime consumption rates taught every European producer to size capacity for exercises and modest reserves. When the war revealed the actual consumption rate of high-intensity ground combat, every European state discovered the same shortfall in the same month and went to the same suppliers. Simultaneous demand against fixed capacity does not produce more capacity in the short run. It produces a queue and a price.

### What that means for the Polish ledger specifically

Poland transferred ammunition in significant quantities, from stocks that had been sized under the same peacetime assumptions as everyone else's, into a conflict consuming at rates nobody had planned for. The result is the one line where the offset genuinely arrives too late to offset within the window that the backfill-clock rule cares about.

This is not a criticism of Polish procurement. It is a description of a physical constraint that Polish procurement cannot relieve by being better funded or better managed, though it can be relieved somewhat by being started earlier, which Poland did relative to most partners. Poland's investment in domestic energetics and ammunition capacity is precisely the correct response, and it is a response whose payoff is measured in years because the constraint is measured in years.

The honest entry, therefore, is asymmetric. In armor, artillery, and air defense, Poland traded old for new and the trade looks good once the fielding tail is accounted for. In ammunition, Poland traded depth for depth it has not yet rebuilt, against a constraint that is European rather than Polish, and that line remains open. Anyone who wants to argue that arming Ukraine cost Poland something real has their strongest case here, and it is a strong one, and it deserves better than being used as a stand-in for a general argument about tanks.

### Why the constraint is also an argument for the transfers

There is a counterintuitive corollary that the strained-readiness school does not usually confront. The reason Europe discovered its ammunition shortfall at all is that a war revealed the real consumption rate. In the absence of that revelation, every European state including Poland would have continued sizing stocks against a fictional rate, discovering the error only in a contingency where the discovery would have been fatal rather than expensive.

Polish stocks would have been shallow either way. What the transfers changed is that the shallowness was discovered in a war Poland was supporting rather than a war Poland was fighting, with time to respond and partners responding simultaneously. That is not a small distinction. A state that learns its magazines are too shallow while supplying someone else's war has learned it in the cheapest possible classroom. A state that learns it in its own war has learned it in the most expensive one there is.

That does not zero out the ammunition cost. Depth given away is depth given away, and the interim is genuinely uncomfortable. But it reframes the line from a self-inflicted wound into a diagnostic that arrived at a survivable price, and the reframing is analytically defensible rather than rhetorical. The ammunition line is simultaneously the strongest evidence for the cost of arming Ukraine and one of the better arguments that the cost bought something worth having.
## The Sustainability Question: Can Poland Keep Paying?

The ledger so far is retrospective. It prices what was spent and what came back. The forward question is different and harder: whether the arrangement that produced those entries can continue, and what breaks first if it cannot.

### The distinction between a stock cost and a flow cost

The transfers were largely a stock cost. Poland had inventory, the inventory left, and the inventory is being replaced. A stock cost is painful and finite. It shows up once, it is absorbed, and it closes.

Continuing support is a flow cost, and flow costs have a different character entirely. They compete every year with every other claim on the same budget, they compound, and they have no natural termination. The shift from stock to flow is the single most important change in the economics of Polish support, and it went almost unremarked because it happened gradually. In the early phase, Poland was giving things it had. In the sustained phase, Poland is giving things it must buy or build, hosting populations it must fund, and running transit and logistics infrastructure it must maintain. Those are recurring lines, and recurring lines are what strain budgets.

The honest sustainability read is therefore split. The stock cost was absorbable and has been absorbed. The flow cost is the one that has to be argued about every budget cycle, and the argument gets harder as the war's salience fades and domestic claims reassert themselves. That is not a Polish weakness. It is what happens in every democracy that sustains an expensive commitment past the emotional peak of the event that triggered it.

### What actually strains

Three things strain, in ascending order of seriousness.

Fiscal capacity strains least, in the sense that it is the most political and therefore the most changeable. A state that decides a commitment matters can fund it, within limits, by reprioritizing or borrowing. The limits are real and the affordability question is genuinely contested, which is why this series gives it its own treatment rather than a paragraph. But fiscal constraint is a choice architecture, not a wall.

Industrial capacity strains more, because it does not respond to decisions on the timescale that decisions are made. If the requirement is more shells and the plant can make so many shells, the plant makes so many shells, and a supplementary budget does not change the number inside a single production cycle. Industrial constraint is a physical wall with a multi-year door in it.

Political consensus strains most, because it is the input to the other two and it is the one that erodes without anyone deciding to erode it. Polish support has been sustained by a broad domestic agreement about the nature of the threat, and that agreement has held through friction that would have broken a thinner one: agricultural and trade disputes with Kyiv, refugee-hosting burdens carried disproportionately by particular regions and municipalities, and the ordinary resentments that accumulate when a neighbor's emergency becomes a permanent feature of the domestic budget. The consensus has held. The friction is also real, and treating it as noise is how analysts miss inflection points. The domestic politics of that consensus are the subject of [Poland's stake in Ukraine's war](/2026/10/01/polands-stake-in-ukraines-war/), which owns the will-and-opinion question.

### The refugee line and the two-timeframe problem

The refugee and humanitarian line deserves its own treatment because it is the clearest illustration of why single-figure accounting fails.

The fact is that Poland absorbed a very large displaced population very quickly, and that the absorption was carried substantially by public budgets, municipal services, and private households rather than by an international mechanism designed for the purpose. The immediate fiscal effect was a large cost. That is not contested.

The assessment is what the cost looks like over a longer horizon, and here the sign genuinely flips depending on the timeframe. A displaced population is a cost in the first period, when it consumes housing, education, and health services without contributing tax revenue. It becomes something else in later periods to the extent that it enters the labor market, which a substantial share of the Ukrainian arrivals did, at a moment when Poland's demographic trajectory was generating labor shortages. A country with an aging population and a shrinking workforce that receives a large working-age influx has received something with a positive present value under most reasonable assumptions, even after the front-loaded costs.

That is not a comfortable thing to say about a humanitarian emergency, and it should be said carefully, because people are not a line item and the framing risks treating a displacement crisis as a labor policy. The point is analytical rather than moral: anyone quoting the refugee cost as a pure debit has picked the first timeframe and stopped, and anyone quoting it as a demographic windfall has picked the second timeframe and stopped, and the honest entry is that it is a large early cost with a substantially offsetting later effect whose magnitude depends on integration outcomes that are still resolving. Confirm current figures on both the fiscal outlay and the labor-market participation against official statistics before using either side of that entry.

## Vulnerabilities and Buffers in the Support Economy

A ledger tells you what things cost. It does not tell you where the arrangement is fragile. That requires a different question: what would have to go wrong for the cost of arming Ukraine to become a strategic problem rather than an expensive but manageable one?

### Vulnerability one: the concentrated backfill supply chain

Poland's replacement program is concentrated among a small number of suppliers, and concentration is efficient right up until it is not. A supplier's line stoppage, a component shortage, a political disruption in the supplying state, or simple queue position behind a larger customer all translate directly into a longer gap. The diversification Poland pursued across multiple suppliers mitigates this and does not eliminate it, because the suppliers draw on overlapping component chains. A shortage in a specialized component category can slow multiple nominally independent programs simultaneously, which is the failure mode that looks like bad luck and is actually structure.

The buffer against this is redundancy of type and the domestic production share. To the extent that Poland can build a system itself, it is not in anyone's queue. That is the strongest argument for the domestic industrial push, and it is an argument about sovereignty rather than economics, because the domestic option is frequently more expensive per unit and reliably faster to control.

### Vulnerability two: the ammunition floor

The ammunition constraint discussed above is not just slow to close; it is the vulnerability with the shortest fuse, because ammunition is the consumable that determines how long any force can fight regardless of how modern its platforms are. A force with new tanks and shallow magazines is a force with a short war in it.

The buffer here is threefold and none of the three is sufficient alone. There is capacity expansion, which works and takes years. There is allied and European pooling, which works if the pool is not drawn on by everyone at once, which is exactly the scenario in which it would be drawn on. And there is doctrine, meaning a way of fighting that consumes less, which is the buffer nobody likes because it amounts to accepting a constraint rather than removing it. The honest read is that the ammunition floor is being raised at the maximum rate the physical plant permits and that it will remain the binding constraint for the foreseeable phase of the confrontation.

### Vulnerability three: the contingent offset

The largest offset in the ledger is the one that depends on someone else's front holding. That is a structural dependency, and structural dependencies are where assessments go wrong, because they get counted as assets right up until the moment they stop existing.

The buffer against this vulnerability is not economic at all. It is the recapitalization itself. The entire value of closing the backfill gap quickly is that it converts a contingent offset into an owned capability. A Poland that has completed its recapitalization does not need the eastern front to hold in order for its ledger to balance, because it has bought a real capability rather than leased a preoccupation. That is the deep logic of the backfill-clock rule, and it explains why the clock matters more than the tonnage: the clock is the mechanism that turns a lease into a purchase.

### Vulnerability four: the political-economy trap

The subtlest vulnerability is that the offsets are invisible and the costs are visible, which is a permanent structural disadvantage in a democracy.

The cost of arming Ukraine appears in budget documents, in municipal service pressures, in agricultural trade disputes, and in depot inventories. The offset appears in a Russian army that is somewhere else, which is to say it appears nowhere at all, because averted events have no constituency and generate no headlines. This asymmetry means that the political sustainability of support is systematically weaker than its analytical justification, and it does not require anyone to be wrong or cynical for that gap to open. It requires only that people respond to what they can see.

The buffer is the ledger itself, which is a small buffer but a real one. A framework that names the offsets explicitly, prices them honestly, and states their conditions makes the invisible column arguable. It does not make it visible. It makes it citable, which in a policy argument is the next best thing.

### What buffers Poland actually has

Set against those vulnerabilities, four buffers are genuine. Poland has geography, in the sense that its own territory was never the theater and its infrastructure was never under sustained attack, which is the difference between an expensive commitment and a war economy. Poland has alliance embedding, which converts some fraction of the cost into a shared cost through reimbursement and backfill mechanisms and which underwrites the recapitalization credit. Poland has an industrial base with room to grow, which is not the same as an industrial base that has grown but is a genuinely better starting position than most European states had. And Poland has, so far, a domestic consensus that treats the threat as real, which is the buffer that makes the other three fundable.

None of those buffers is a guarantee. All four are conditions that could change. The correct posture toward a buffer is the same as the correct posture toward a contingent offset: use it, do not bank it, and spend the time it buys on closing the gap that would still be open if it disappeared.
## The Economics of a Long Confrontation

The transfers happened inside an assumption nobody stated out loud, which was that this would resolve. Early support was priced as an emergency: give what you have, replace it later, absorb the gap. That is a rational way to price a spike. It is a catastrophic way to price a plateau, and the confrontation has plateaued.

### What changes when the emergency becomes the condition

Three things change when a state stops treating a commitment as an emergency and starts treating it as a standing condition, and each one rewrites a line in the ledger.

The first is that disposal stops being available as a funding source. The early transfers were cheap in part because they came from a stock of things Poland wanted to be rid of. That stock is finite and has largely been spent. Anything transferred now comes from current inventory or current production, which means the disposal discount is gone and every further unit is priced at full opportunity cost. The ledger's most favorable line was a one-time line, and it has been drawn.

The second is that the readiness dip stops being self-limiting. In a spike, the gap closes because deliveries arrive and nothing further leaves. In a plateau, the gap closes only if deliveries outrun continuing transfers, which turns a subtraction problem into a rate problem. A state can be simultaneously recapitalizing at record speed and getting no closer to establishment strength, which is a condition that looks like failure and is actually just arithmetic.

The third is that the offsets have to be re-earned rather than banked. The buffer offset holds while the front holds, which in a long confrontation means it must be continuously repurchased. The alliance-credit offset decays, because standing accrues from what you are doing rather than what you did. The modernization offset is the only one that is genuinely permanent, because a fielded system stays fielded, which is another reason the backfill clock deserves the weight this framework puts on it. In a long confrontation, the only offset you own outright is the one you took delivery of.

### The war-economy question and why Poland is not in one

A recurring analytical error is to describe Poland's defense effort as a war economy. It is not one, and the distinction is load-bearing.

A war economy reallocates production away from civilian use by administrative direction, accepts civilian consumption losses, and subordinates the price mechanism to output targets. Russia's economy has moved substantially in that direction under the pressures of a war it is actually fighting, and the consequences and limits of that move are examined in this series where they belong. Poland has done nothing of the kind. Poland has increased defense spending sharply within a market economy, which means its output is constrained by what suppliers choose to build at prices Poland is willing to pay, and its expansion competes with every other European state doing the same thing at the same time.

That distinction produces a specific and uncomfortable asymmetry. A mobilized war economy can convert political will into output faster than a market economy can, over a limited horizon, because it can simply order the conversion. A market economy converts will into output more slowly and sustains it far longer, because the conversion is voluntary and the underlying prosperity is not being consumed. Poland is on the slow-and-sustainable side of that asymmetry. That is the right side to be on for a long confrontation and the wrong side to be on for a short one, and which kind of confrontation this is remains the open question that everything else hangs from.

### The cost of the confrontation versus the cost of the war

The final reframing in this section is the one that puts the entire ledger in proportion.

Everything tallied above is the cost of supporting a war fought by someone else on someone else's territory. It is a large number in a budget document. It is a rounding error against the cost of a war fought on Polish territory, and the gap between those two magnitudes is not a factor of two or five. It is a difference in kind. A supported war costs a state materiel, money, and a readiness gap. A fought war costs a state its infrastructure, its cities, its people, its economy's productive base, and a reconstruction bill measured in decades. There is no accounting convention under which the first is comparable to the second.

This is not an argument that support was therefore automatically correct, because that reasoning proves too much: it would justify any expenditure by comparing it to catastrophe. It is an argument about proportion. When the debate treats the cost of arming Ukraine as a heavy burden, it is measuring against the wrong baseline. The relevant baseline is not a peaceful Poland that spent nothing. That Poland was not on offer. The relevant baseline is the range of futures available from the moment the invasion began, and against that range the transfers were an inexpensive option on the best of them.

## The Honest Tradeoffs the Ledger Cannot Settle

A framework that claims to settle everything is a framework that is hiding something. Four things the Cost-and-Offset Ledger cannot resolve, stated plainly.

### It cannot price a counterfactual

Every entry in the offset column implicitly compares the world to a world in which Poland did not transfer. That world does not exist and cannot be observed. Reasonable analysts construct it differently, and the construction drives the answer. If you build a counterfactual in which other partners filled the gap Poland would have left, the buffer offset shrinks toward zero and the ledger looks much worse. If you build one in which the early Soviet-pattern transfers were genuinely irreplaceable, the offset is close to the total and the ledger looks superb. The evidence underdetermines the choice. Anyone claiming certainty here is reporting a prior, not a finding.

### It cannot resolve the risk-tolerance question

Whether a temporary readiness gap was an acceptable price is not an empirical question. It is a question about how much probability of a bad outcome a state should accept for a benefit, and reasonable people with identical facts answer it differently because they weight tail risks differently. A planner whose job is to be ready on the worst day will price the gap higher than a strategist whose job is to shape the distribution of days. Both are doing their jobs correctly. The ledger can show them they are disagreeing about risk tolerance rather than about facts, which is useful, and it cannot tell them who is right, which is honest.

### It cannot forecast the backfill clock

The rule this article advances says the net turns on how fast backfill closes the gap. That is a claim about which variable matters, not a claim about what the variable's value will be. Delivery schedules slip. Supply chains surprise. Industrial expansion is announced more reliably than it is achieved. The clock is the right thing to watch precisely because it is uncertain, and a framework that pretended to know its value would be a forecast wearing a framework's clothing.

### It cannot make the invisible visible

The offset column consists substantially of things that did not happen. A war that did not reach Polish territory. Formations that were not committed against Polish approaches. A modernization that would not have been funded. Each of those is defensible as an assessment and none of them is demonstrable as a fact, because averted outcomes leave no evidence. This is a permanent epistemic limitation on the entire question, and it is why the debate about the cost of arming Ukraine will never be settled by data. It will be settled, if at all, by whether the analytical structure holds up, which is the most any framework in this domain can claim.

## Two Schools: Strained Readiness Versus Net Strengthening

Serious analysts disagree about this, and the disagreement deserves its strongest statement on both sides rather than a caricature and a rebuttal.

### The strained-readiness school at full strength

The case runs like this. Poland transferred combat-relevant materiel at scale from a force that was not oversized to begin with, and it did so into a security environment that the transfers themselves proved was dangerous. It accepted a real reduction in fielded capability in exchange for contracts, and contracts are promises. The recapitalization is genuinely impressive on paper and is running into the same constraints every European rearmament is running into: supplier queues, component shortages, and a fielding tail that is longer than the delivery schedule. Meanwhile the ammunition situation is not closing at any rate that matches the requirement, because it cannot.

The school's strongest point is about sequencing risk. Poland reduced its capability first and is acquiring the replacement second, which means it accepted the gap before securing the fill. That sequence is the definition of a bet. The bet has, so far, been winning, because the threat stayed committed elsewhere throughout the window. But a bet that wins was still a bet, and evaluating a decision by its outcome rather than by the information available when it was made is the most common error in strategic assessment. The school's claim is not that Poland was wrong. It is that Poland was exposed and got a favorable roll, and that policy should not be built on the assumption that the next roll is also favorable.

That is a serious argument and it does not depend on alarmism.

### The net-strengthening school at full strength

The case runs like this. Poland exchanged equipment it had already decided to retire for the fastest recapitalization in Europe, a domestic industrial sector with a real order book, alliance standing it has converted into concrete mechanisms, and the continued commitment of the only army that could seriously threaten it, several hundred kilometers east. The readiness gap was real, time-bounded, concentrated in categories where replacement was already contracted, and coincident with a period in which the threat it theoretically exposed Poland to was fully occupied.

The school's strongest point is about what the alternative actually was. The alternative was not a Poland that kept its old tanks and was therefore safer. It was a Poland that kept its old tanks, retired them on the original schedule with no urgency behind the replacement, faced a Russia that was not being consumed in Ukraine, and had no particular claim on partners. That Poland is not stronger. It is weaker in every column, and it is weaker with a worse threat picture. The transfers did not merely purchase offsets. They purchased offsets in the only market that was open.

That is also a serious argument, and it does not depend on complacency.

### What the disagreement actually turns on

Strip both cases down and they are not disagreeing about the facts. They agree on what left, they agree that a gap opened, they agree that recapitalization is running, and they agree that ammunition is the hard line. They are disagreeing about two things: how to evaluate a decision that was made under uncertainty and turned out well, and how long the gap will stay open relative to a threat window nobody can specify.

Neither of those is resolvable by finding more data. The first is a question about decision theory, and the honest answer is that the strained-readiness school is right that outcome is not evidence of judgment, while the net-strengthening school is right that the judgment was defensible on the information available. Both can be true simultaneously and are.

The second is the backfill clock, which is the thing to watch rather than the thing to argue about. Which is the practical payoff of this entire framework: it converts an unresolvable argument about the past into a tractable measurement of the present. Stop debating whether the transfers were wise. Measure whether the replacement is running at the physical limit. That number is knowable, it is actionable, and it is the only part of the ledger anyone can still change.
## Pricing the Transfers: Why Three Valuation Methods Give Three Answers

Underneath the entire public argument sits an unexamined technical question. When someone says the transfers cost a given amount, what valuation method produced that amount? There are three defensible methods, they disagree by large multiples, and almost nobody quoting a figure says which one they used. Understanding the three is the fastest way to stop being misled.

### Method one: book value

Book value prices the equipment at its depreciated accounting worth on the day it left. For a Soviet-pattern main battle tank in the fourth decade of its service life, that number is small. Depreciation schedules assume a system loses value monotonically with age, which for accounting purposes it does.

This method's virtue is that it reflects what the asset was worth to a seller. Its defect is that Poland was not selling. Book value answers the question of what Poland could have realized by disposing of the equipment in a market, and there was no such market at any meaningful price for late-Soviet armor. So book value is a number that describes a transaction that was never available, and using it to price the transfers understates the cost by pricing an alternative that did not exist.

### Method two: replacement value

Replacement value prices the equipment at what it costs to buy the system that now fills its slot. That number is large, because the system now filling the slot is a modern Western or Korean-built platform costing several multiples of what the departed one was worth by any measure.

This method's virtue is that it reflects what filling the hole actually costs. Its defect is that it charges the entire modernization to the transfer, and the modernization was not caused by the transfer. Poland had already decided to buy new platforms. The transfer changed the timing, not the decision. Charging full replacement value against the drawdown double-counts a purchase that was going to happen anyway, which inflates the cost by attributing a pre-existing intention to a subsequent event.

### Method three: opportunity cost of the gap

The third method ignores the equipment's value entirely and prices the interval instead. What did it cost Poland to have formations below establishment for a bounded period, given the threat environment during that period?

This method's virtue is that it prices the thing that actually mattered. Its defect is that it requires assigning a value to a risk that did not materialize, which means the number is a function of the analyst's assumptions about a probability distribution rather than an observation. Under a reading where the Russian ground threat was fully committed and a Polish contingency was remote throughout the window, this method returns a number close to zero. Under a reading where the window was genuinely dangerous, it returns something large. The method is right about what to measure and cannot measure it.

### What this means for the debate

The three methods do not merely differ in magnitude. They answer three different questions: what Poland gave up in realizable value, what filling the hole cost, and what having the hole cost. Those are all legitimate questions and none of them is the cost of arming Ukraine, because there is no such single quantity.

The Cost-and-Offset Ledger's response is to refuse the aggregation. Rather than picking a method and defending a number, it keeps the currencies separate, prices each against its own offset, and lets the reader see which entries are favorable and which are not. That is less satisfying than a headline figure and considerably more honest, and it explains why the debate never resolves: the participants are computing different quantities and arguing as though they were computing the same one.

The practical rule for a reader is simple. When someone quotes a cost, ask which of the three methods produced it. If the answer is book value, the number is understated and the speaker is probably arguing that support was cheap. If the answer is replacement value, the number is overstated and the speaker is probably arguing it was ruinous. If the answer is the gap's opportunity cost, ask what threat probability they assumed during the window, because that assumption is doing all the work.

## Confidence Levels and What Would Change This Assessment

An assessment that does not state its confidence is an opinion in a suit. Here is what this article holds firmly, what it holds provisionally, and what would move each judgment.

### High confidence

That Poland transferred militarily significant quantities of ground-combat materiel, weighted toward Soviet-pattern systems, early and at scale. This is the durable open record and is not seriously contested.

That a period existed in which Polish formations held less equipment than establishment tables prescribed. This follows arithmetically from the transfers and the delivery schedules, and no serious analysis disputes it.

That a large-scale recapitalization is running concurrently and is the fastest in Europe by most measures. Also durable open record.

That ammunition is the category where replacement is slowest and the constraint is physical rather than fiscal. This is a structural fact about the industry that applies to every European producer, and it is confirmed by the uniformity of the shortfall across states with very different budgets and procurement systems.

### Moderate confidence

That the net effect of support on Polish security has been positive. This is the article's central judgment and it rests on the offset column, which contains genuinely uncertain entries. It would be weakened substantially if the recapitalization stalled, if the eastern front were to collapse, or if evidence emerged that the industrial expansion was reallocation rather than growth.

That the modernization offset is largely attributable to the transfers as a forcing mechanism rather than merely coincident with them. This is an inference about political economy and institutional behavior, defensible but not demonstrable. Analysts who believe Poland would have recapitalized at the same pace regardless will weight this offset near zero, and their position is not unreasonable.

That the refugee line turns favorable over a longer horizon. This depends on integration and labor-market participation outcomes that are still resolving, and it should be revisited against current statistics rather than assumed.

### Low confidence

The magnitude of Poland's marginal contribution to fixing the Russian army in Ukraine. Poland contributed, particularly in the early phase when Soviet-pattern equipment was the only immediately absorbable equipment and Poland was among the few able to supply it. Whether other partners would have filled that gap on a similar timeline is unknowable, and the entire size of the largest offset in the ledger depends on the answer.

The closure date of the backfill clock. Delivery schedules slip, fielding tails are longer than announced, and multiple simultaneous new-type introductions compound the problem. Any confident statement about when the gap closes is a forecast, and forecasts in defense procurement have a poor record.

The durability of the domestic political consensus that funds all of this. It has held through real friction and it is not guaranteed to hold indefinitely, and the erosion of such consensuses is famously invisible until it is not.

### What would change the verdict

Four developments would move this assessment materially, and a reader tracking the question should watch for them specifically rather than for headlines.

A stall in the recapitalization would move the verdict from favorable to adverse, because the entire favorable read rests on the gap being an interval. The signal is not a delayed contract; contracts always slip. The signal is fielding milestones sliding while delivery milestones are met, which indicates the tail rather than the schedule is the constraint.

A collapse of the eastern front would not merely remove the largest offset but invert it, because the fixed force becomes available and becomes available having learned. That branch is not this article's to work through, and the ledger's role is only to note that this offset is a lease with a termination clause.

Evidence that the industrial expansion is reallocation rather than growth would remove the industrial offset entirely and would also lengthen the backfill clock, which is the compounding failure mode. Watch for capacity metrics rather than order-book announcements, since an order book measures demand and says nothing about supply.

A rupture in the domestic consensus would convert a funding question into a political one and would slow everything simultaneously. Its early indicators are regional rather than national, because the costs land regionally and the offsets are argued nationally.

None of those four is a prediction. Each is a condition under which a stated judgment would change, which is the only useful form a confidence statement can take. An assessment that cannot name what would falsify it is not an assessment.
## Applying the Ledger: A Method a Reader Can Use

The framework is only worth something if a reader can run it. Here is how, in prose, as a sequence of judgments rather than a calculation.

### Step one: declare the currency

Before pricing anything, state which currency you are pricing in, because the answer differs by currency and the argument only stays honest if the choice is explicit. Materiel, money, readiness, industrial capacity, and political bandwidth are five different accounts, and support was net-favorable in some and net-adverse in others. A verdict that does not name its currency is a verdict that is smuggling one in. The commonest smuggle is to price in money, find a large number, and present it as though it settled the readiness question, which it does not touch.

### Step two: declare the timeframe

Then state the horizon. The refugee line is adverse at one year and plausibly favorable at ten. The materiel line is adverse at two years and favorable at eight. The industrial line is neutral at one year and favorable at five if the capacity expanded. Almost every entry in this ledger changes sign somewhere along the timeline, which means the timeframe is not a detail of the analysis; it is the analysis. Anyone who gives you a verdict without a horizon has picked one silently and is hoping you do not ask.

### Step three: price the counterfactual explicitly

Then state what you are comparing to, and state it as a described world rather than an implied one. The temptation is to compare to a Poland that did nothing and was therefore richer and better stocked. That Poland does not exist, because the event that prompted the transfers also destroyed the world in which not transferring left everything else unchanged. Construct the counterfactual properly: a Poland that withheld, faced the same war, had less claim on partners, retired its legacy fleet on the leisurely original schedule, and confronted whatever the Russian army looked like after a war in which the eastern flank's largest ground power sat it out. Price against that. It is a much less flattering comparison than the one the debate usually makes, and it is the honest one.

### Step four: separate the banked from the leased

Then sort the offsets into two piles. Fielded equipment is banked; it stays yours regardless of what happens next. Modernization delivered is banked. Industrial capacity built is banked. Everything else is leased: the buffer holds while the front holds, the alliance credit persists while the contribution persists, the demographic benefit accrues while the integration holds. Sum the banked column separately from the leased column and look at the ratio. A support policy whose offsets are overwhelmingly leased has bought time rather than capability, which is a legitimate purchase and a fragile one. The ratio tells you how exposed the whole position is to a single adverse turn.

### Step five: check the clock against the physical limit

Then, finally, ask the only question that generates an action: is the backfill running at the rate industry physically permits, or at the rate the bureaucracy permits? The first is a constraint. The second is a choice. If the answer is the second, the difference between them is the part of this cost that is nobody's necessity and somebody's decision.

Run those five steps and you have a defensible position on the cost of arming Ukraine that names its assumptions instead of hiding them. That is not the same as being right. It is the precondition for a useful argument, which is more than the public version of this debate currently offers.

## Recurring Errors in the Cost Debate

Six mistakes recur often enough to be worth naming, because each one has a signature and a reader who learns the signatures can spot a bad argument before evaluating its substance.

The first is book-value pricing. This is the move that prices transferred equipment at its depreciated accounting value, finds a modest number, and concludes the transfers were cheap. The equipment's accounting value and its combat value diverged the moment a war started. Depreciation is a tax concept, not a military one, and a formation that is short of vehicles is short of vehicles regardless of what the vehicles were worth on paper.

The second is replacement-value pricing, which is the same error running the other way. This move prices transferred equipment at what it costs to buy new, produces an enormous number, and concludes the transfers were ruinous. But Poland was not replacing old tanks with identical old tanks. It was replacing them with better ones it had already decided to buy. Charging the full price of the new system against the transfer double-counts a modernization that was happening anyway.

The third is snapshot reading, which prices the drawdown at its deepest point and treats that moment as the condition rather than as the trough of a curve. Every recapitalization has a trough. Photographing the trough and captioning it as the state of the force is a rhetorical device, not an assessment.

The fourth is offset laundering, which counts the war-fixed-away offset as though it were a Polish asset rather than a contingent condition partly attributable to Poland's marginal contribution. It was contingent, and Poland's share of it was a share. Counting the whole buffer as a Polish purchase inflates the offset column enough to make any drawdown look free, which is how the surplus argument reaches its conclusion.

The fifth is timeframe shopping, which selects whichever horizon makes the preferred conclusion true and never states the choice. The refugee line is the favorite target because its sign flips so cleanly, but every line in the ledger is vulnerable to it.

The sixth, and the most consequential, is fault attribution. This is the move that treats the entire cost as the consequence of a decision to transfer, when most of it is the consequence of a decision by Russia to invade a neighbor. Poland did not choose the situation. Poland chose a response within a situation, and the costs of the situation are not chargeable to the response. Confusing those two is how a debate about the price of a policy becomes a debate about the wisdom of solidarity, which is a different argument with a different answer.

## Closing Verdict: How to Read the Cost

The cost of arming Ukraine from Poland was real, substantial, paid in several currencies, and smaller than what it purchased in every timeframe that a reasonable analyst would choose. That is the verdict, and it comes with three conditions attached that are not decoration.

The first condition is the clock. This assessment holds because the backfill is running and the categories in which it is running slowest, principally ammunition, are categories where every European state is equally constrained, which means Poland's relative position is not deteriorating even where its absolute position is uncomfortable. If the recapitalization stalls, the verdict changes, and it changes fast, because the entire favorable read rests on the gap being an interval rather than a state.

The second condition is that the biggest offset is a lease. Poland's most valuable return on this expenditure is that the army it worries about has been occupied elsewhere, and that return terminates on someone else's front. Treating it as a permanent gain is the single most dangerous reading available, and it is dangerous precisely because it is comfortable. The correct use of a leased offset is to spend the term of the lease converting it into something owned, which for Poland means fielding the replacement force before the lease expires. That is not a rhetorical flourish. It is the entire strategic content of this article compressed into one sentence.

The third condition is honesty about what was actually decided. Poland did not choose between arming Ukraine and staying whole. It chose between arming Ukraine and facing the same war with less influence, less urgency behind its own modernization, fewer partners in its debt, and a Russian army with more of its ground power uncommitted. The transfers were not a donation from a position of comfort. They were a purchase made in the only market that was open at a moment when the alternative to buying was not saving money but going without.

Read the ledger that way and the argument that has consumed so much oxygen collapses into something more useful. Support was expensive. It was worth it. And whether it stays worth it is not a question about the past at all. It is a question about how fast the shells and the vehicles arrive, which is measurable, which is happening now, and which is the only line in this entire account that anyone still has a hand on.

One closing note on what this article deliberately did not do. It did not produce a number, because the number does not exist and every published version of it is a valuation method wearing a fact's clothing. It did not adjudicate whether Poland can sustain its defense budget, because that question has an owner elsewhere in this series and deserves the full treatment it gets there. It did not work through what happens if the eastern front gives way, because that branch belongs to the scenario article that owns it. What it did was narrow the question until it became answerable, which meant refusing the aggregation, separating the currencies, naming the offsets and their conditions, and identifying the one variable that decides the verdict.

That variable is the clock, and the clock is running. A reader who takes nothing else from this ledger should take the habit of asking, whenever anyone claims to know what arming Ukraine cost Poland, a single follow-up question: in which currency, over what horizon, against which counterfactual, and how fast is the replacement arriving? Four questions, and almost no version of this argument in circulation survives all four of them.
## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What has arming Ukraine cost Poland in materiel and money?

It cost in three separate currencies that do not convert cleanly into one another. In materiel, Poland transferred Soviet-pattern armor, mechanized infantry vehicles, artillery, air-defense systems, and ammunition from existing stocks, later adding Western-pattern systems including its own domestic production. In money, the outlay spans transfer values, cash costs of procurement and transport, and refugee and humanitarian lines carried on public budgets, partially recovered through reimbursement and partner backfill mechanisms. The unrecovered remainder is the genuine fiscal price. Any single headline figure is a choice about which currencies to include and how to value transferred equipment, so treat quoted totals as arguments rather than measurements, and confirm current figures against official statements and open defense reporting.

### Q: Did supporting Ukraine create a readiness dip for Poland?

Yes, in a specific and bounded sense. For a defined period, Polish ground formations held less equipment than their establishment tables prescribed, which is the honest core of the case that the drawdown had a real cost. The dip was concentrated on the equipment side rather than in personnel, training, or institutional capacity, which matters because equipment gaps close on delivery schedules while human and institutional gaps close on generational timescales. It was also coincident with the Russian ground threat being fully committed several hundred kilometers east, which is the structural logic of the whole transaction. The dip was real, it was time-bounded, and calling it a gutted arsenal misreads a trough as a condition.

### Q: How does backfill offset Poland's Ukraine drawdown?

Backfill closes the gap by replacing what left with newer equipment already under contract, which converts a capability loss into a modernization acceleration. The mechanism has three parts: domestic procurement running at a tempo no peacetime cycle would have produced, partner and allied backfill arrangements that supplied interim or replacement systems, and reimbursement instruments that recovered part of the fiscal value. The offset is genuine but conditional. It only counts once equipment is fielded with trained crews in formed units, which is years past the delivery date for a new type. Backfill announced is not backfill delivered, and backfill delivered is not backfill fielded. The distance between those three states is where most disagreement about the drawdown actually lives.

### Q: Did arming Ukraine strengthen or strain Poland on net?

On the assessment advanced here, it strengthened Poland on net in every reasonable timeframe, with three conditions attached. Poland exchanged legacy inventory it had already decided to retire for a recapitalization it would not otherwise have funded at that pace, a domestic industrial sector with a durable order book, alliance standing converted into concrete mechanisms, and the continued commitment of the only army that could seriously threaten it. The conditions are that the verdict depends on the replacement continuing to arrive, that the largest offset is leased rather than owned because it terminates if the eastern front does, and that the ammunition line remains genuinely open. Strip those conditions away and the verdict becomes complacency. Keep them and it is an assessment.

### Q: Why did Poland trade old stocks by arming Ukraine?

Because Soviet-pattern equipment was the only equipment Ukrainian forces could immediately absorb without a training and logistics bridge, and Poland was among the few states holding it in quantity. That match of supply to immediate requirement is why Poland could contribute decisively in the earliest phase when most Western partners could not. The trade also carried a domestic logic: the legacy fleets were scheduled for retirement, maintained an incompatible logistics chain, and were consuming depot capacity and maintenance budgets. Transferring them cleared the decision that peacetime institutions defer indefinitely. The residual accounting value was low. The combat value was not, which is why the trade produced a real gap and not merely a favorable disposal.

### Q: How fast is Poland closing the gap from arming Ukraine?

The clock runs at different speeds by category, and the slowest category sets the true closure date. Armor closes fastest because it was ordered most aggressively from suppliers with open lines. Artillery closes at a moderate rate, constrained by domestic capacity and by Poland being one customer among several. Air defense closes slowly, because supply is globally constrained and European demand rose everywhere at once. Ammunition closes slowest because the binding constraint is physical plant that takes years to build, not money. The useful measure is not whether closure is fast but whether it is running at the rate industry physically permits rather than the rate procurement bureaucracy permits.

### Q: Did helping Ukraine gut Polish readiness?

No, and the framing itself is the error. Gutting implies a permanent structural loss, and what occurred was a bounded equipment gap running concurrently with the fastest ground-force recapitalization in Europe. The formations kept their people, their command structures, their training pipelines, and their sustainment; what they were short of was vehicles and shells, and vehicles and shells were on contract. Judging the drawdown without the backfill is like judging a renovation by photographing the demolition. That said, the opposite reading is equally wrong: the gap was real, it was accepted before the fill was secured, and evaluating that sequence by its favorable outcome rather than by the information available at the time is its own analytical mistake.

### Q: Was Poland's Ukraine support costless?

No. The surplus argument that prices transfers at depreciated book value, finds a modest number, and concludes support was free is making a specific error: depreciation is a tax concept, not a military one. A formation short of vehicles is short of vehicles regardless of what those vehicles were worth on paper. Beyond materiel, real cash left the budget across procurement, transport, refugee hosting, and humanitarian lines, and reimbursement recovered part of it rather than all of it. Infrastructure was consumed by transit that no transfer register records. Ammunition depth was spent against a constraint that money does not relieve quickly. Support was a good purchase. It was not a free one, and pretending otherwise is how a defensible policy acquires an indefensible argument.

### Q: How does modernization offset the cost of arming Ukraine?

By converting a disposal into a forcing mechanism. Retiring legacy fleets is institutionally hard while they still function, so modernization gets deferred for decades. Transferring those fleets to a partner at war removed the option of muddling through, which turned a gradual recapitalization into an urgent one and, decisively, into a funded one. Empty depots survive contact with a finance ministry in a way that aging depots do not. The offset should be priced accurately, though. Poland did not receive free modernization. It received modernization it was going to pay for anyway, delivered on a schedule it would not otherwise have achieved, in exchange for equipment it was going to retire anyway, at the price of the gap in between.

### Q: What net accounting captures Poland's Ukraine support cost?

The Cost-and-Offset Ledger set out in this article: six cost lines covering materiel, fiscal outlay, the readiness dip, industrial capacity absorbed, ammunition depth, and political bandwidth, each set against the offset it generated, with every net read stated conditionally on timing. The method requires five explicit steps: declare which currency you are pricing in, declare the timeframe, construct the counterfactual as a described world rather than an implied one, separate banked offsets from leased ones, and check whether backfill is running at the physical limit or the bureaucratic one. The ledger does not produce a verdict on its own. It produces a verdict function whose input is the backfill clock, which is the honest structure of the question.

### Q: Does Poland carry the price of arming Ukraine alone?

No, and the recovery mechanisms are one of the more concrete offsets in the ledger. Reimbursement instruments, allied and partner backfill arrangements, and European funding mechanisms recovered a meaningful fraction of the transfer value, and procurement access on favorable terms recovered more indirectly. The recovery was neither trivial nor complete, and the unrecovered share is the true fiscal price. Two caveats keep this honest. Recovery mechanisms are slow, administratively demanding, and frequently deliver value later than the cost was incurred, which is its own burden. And the burden that cannot be shared at all is the readiness gap, because no mechanism reimburses a formation for being short of vehicles during the period it was short of them.

### Q: What would inaction on Ukraine have cost Poland instead?

The counterfactual is the most abused part of this debate, and constructing it properly changes the answer. The alternative was never a Poland that spent nothing and stayed whole, because the event that prompted the transfers also destroyed the world in which withholding left everything else unchanged. The honest counterfactual is a Poland that withheld, faced the same war, retired its legacy fleet on the leisurely original schedule with no urgency behind replacement, held less claim on partners, and confronted a Russian army with more of its ground power uncommitted because the eastern flank's largest ground power had sat the moment out. That Poland is weaker in every column, with a worse threat picture, holding older tanks.

### Q: How do refugees and transit figure into Poland's Ukraine bill?

Both are large and both are systematically mispriced. The refugee line was the single largest early cash item, carried substantially by public budgets, municipal services, and private households rather than an international mechanism. Its sign flips with the timeframe: a clear cost in the first period, plausibly favorable over a longer one as a large working-age population entered a labor market facing demographic contraction. Transit is the line nobody counts at all. Being the corridor consumed track life, pavement, yard capacity, and handling capacity that competed at the margin with Poland's own recapitalization, while generating durable infrastructure, institutional competence in reception and onward movement, and positional leverage. Omitting either distorts the account in both directions simultaneously.

### Q: Which part of the Ukraine drawdown was hardest to replace?

Ammunition, and it is not close. Every other category closes on a purchase: order the vehicles, take delivery, field them, and the gap shuts on a schedule that is knowable. Ammunition production is a chemical and metallurgical industry whose binding constraints sit upstream in energetics, propellant, shell-body forging, and fuze components, each a capital-intensive plant with multi-year lead times and, for energetics, genuine hazard constraints on where capacity can be added. Money does not relieve a physical plant shortage on a short timescale. Compounding it, every European state discovered the same shortfall in the same month and bid for the same expansion capacity, which produced a queue rather than production. That line remains the open one.

### Q: Why is ammunition the sharpest part of the Ukraine drawdown?

Because it is the consumable that determines how long any force can fight regardless of how modern its platforms are. A force with new tanks and shallow magazines is a force with a short war in it, and the sophistication of the platform does not extend the magazine. It is also the category where the offset arrives too late to function as an offset within the window the backfill-clock rule cares about, which makes it the strongest evidence available for the case that support carried a real cost. The counterintuitive corollary is that Polish stocks would have been shallow either way, because peacetime consumption assumptions were wrong everywhere. The transfers meant that error was discovered while supporting someone else's war rather than while fighting one.

### Q: Does arming Ukraine compete with Poland's own force build?

At the margin, yes, and this is the entry the net-strengthening case least likes to confront. Once the disposable legacy stock was spent, further transfers came from current inventory or current production, which means every unit sent east was a unit that did not enter Polish service that year. Domestic production lines running partner requirements are lines not running national ones unless capacity genuinely expanded rather than being reallocated, and reallocation is not growth. Transit and handling capacity devoted to the eastward flow competed with the reception of Poland's own incoming equipment through substantially the same nodes and people. The competition is real, it is second-order against the offsets, and it is not zero.
