Romeo and Juliet reached the Indian curriculum as an instrument of government before it reached Indian readers as a play. English literary study was assembled in the colony, funded by an education grant, defended in a minute of 1835 that dismissed the literatures of India and Arabia in a single sentence, and then examined into permanence by universities founded in 1857. Any honest account of the play in Indian education has to begin with that machinery, because the machinery is documented in a way that literary enthusiasm is not: resolutions, syllabi, prescribed-text lists, examination blueprints. This article reads the play's Indian school and university career from those documents, board by board and level by level, and reaches a verdict that the documents support. The verdict is that the instrument changed hands, unevenly. At the top of the system the transfer is complete and demonstrable: Indian universities teach the whole play, Indian scholars edit the standard collections on its Indian career, and Indian syllabi set Macaulay's Minute itself as an object of study beside the canon it was written to serve. At the bottom of the system the transfer remains partial: in most Indian classrooms the play survives as a few examinable speeches inside an English subject whose sorting power, the power to separate the fluent from the rest, is the one colonial function the subject never surrendered.

![An Indian school edition of Romeo and Juliet beside board syllabus papers - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-27.webp)

The unevenness is the finding, and it has a shape. Where an Indian institution prescribes the play whole, students meet Verona entire: the feud, the fathers, the Friar, the market in daughters that the text sets out in its second scene. Where an institution prescribes extracts, students meet two speeches about beauty and a death they are told about in a summary, and the play's argument about kinship, obedience and arranged marriage evaporates from the lesson. Where the national board prescribes nothing at all, which is the case for the largest school system in the country, the play circulates as reputation, film memory and guidebook paraphrase. Three different plays are being taught under one title, and only one of them is Shakespeare's. The sections that follow establish each claim from the record: the policy that installed English literature, the scholarly argument that named its function, the Calcutta classrooms that answered back within a generation, the examination universities that fixed the canon, and then the board-by-board map of what an Indian student in fact meets, from a Karnataka textbook's three teaching hours to a Delhi university's full-text drama paper.

## The Policy Before the Play: How English Literature Was Installed

The money came first, and the argument about the money came second. The Charter Act of 1813 set aside a lakh of rupees a year for the revival and improvement of literature and the encouragement of the learned natives of India, without settling whether the learning to be encouraged was Sanskrit and Arabic learning or English. That unsettled clause produced two decades of institutional quarrel between Orientalists, who wanted the grant spent on the classical languages of India, and Anglicists, who wanted it spent on English. The quarrel was settled on 2 February 1835, when Thomas Babington Macaulay, Law Member of the Governor-General's Council and president of the General Committee of Public Instruction, wrote the Minute that bears his name. The Minute is quoted in every history of Indian education because its contempt was programmatic. A single shelf of a good European library, Macaulay wrote, was worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia. The purpose of the expenditure followed from the valuation: "We must at present do our best to form a class who may be interpreters between us and the millions whom we govern; a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect."

Governor-General William Bentinck endorsed the Minute within weeks, and the resolution of 7 March 1835 redirected public instruction funds to English literature and science taught through English. Two features of that settlement matter for the play's later career. The first is that the settlement concerned a language and a literature, not a reading list. Macaulay named no plays and prescribed no authors; the Minute argued for English learning as a civilising instrument and left the contents of the curriculum to the institutions that the policy then created or captured. Shakespeare's place in Indian education was therefore not decreed in 1835. It was accumulated afterwards, by colleges that needed teachable texts, by teachers who had themselves been formed on the plays, and by universities that needed examinable ones. The second feature is the candour of the design. The Minute did not pretend that English education was a gift of disinterested knowledge. It was a staffing plan for an empire that could not rule a subcontinent with Englishmen alone, and the literature was the means by which the interpreters were to be made English in taste and morals as well as in grammar. When later critics called the curriculum an instrument of rule, they were not reading a hidden purpose into a neutral syllabus. They were quoting the syllabus's own founding memorandum back at it.

The machinery that carried the design downward was built in stages. Wood's Despatch of 1854, the education despatch sent by the Court of Directors, recommended a connected system from vernacular primary schools to universities, with English as the medium at the higher levels. The universities of Calcutta, Bombay and Madras followed in 1857, modelled on the University of London as examining bodies: they taught nothing themselves at first, but they set syllabi, affiliated colleges and conducted the examinations that decided which texts a college education would consist of. An examining university is a canon-making machine. A play that appears on its lists is reprinted in student editions, glossed, summarised in bazaar notes, translated, and performed by college societies; a play that does not appear is, for the great majority of students, not read at all. English literary study in India grew up inside that machine, and Romeo and Juliet grew with it, carried by the same current that carried the whole Shakespeare canon: the conviction, installed by policy and renewed by examinations, that the plays were the summit of the literature the interpreters were being formed to admire.

## What the Anglicists Defeated: The Orientalist Alternative

The Minute's violence of tone has a context, which is that Macaulay was winning an argument, and the side he defeated had a curriculum of its own. The Orientalist party within the Company's administration, heirs of Warren Hastings and of Sir William Jones, who had founded the Asiatic Society in 1784 and translated Sanskrit drama for European readers, held that India's own learned traditions deserved the state's patronage: that the lakh of rupees should sustain Sanskrit and Arabic colleges, stipends for traditional scholars, and the printing of classical texts. Their India was a civilisation to be governed through its own literatures. The Anglicists, Charles Trevelyan the most relentless of them beside Macaulay, held that those literatures were an obstacle, that European knowledge carried in English was the only fund worth spending, and that the learned classes of India could be detached from their own traditions and re-formed in the rulers'. The dispute ran for years through the General Committee of Public Instruction precisely because both sides agreed on the premise that mattered: education was an instrument of rule, and the only question was which literature would wield it. The Orientalists proposed to rule India through India's classics. The Anglicists proposed to rule India by replacing them.

The counterfactual is instructive for the play's history, because under an Orientalist settlement Shakespeare would have remained what he was in the Calcutta theatres of the 1810s and 1820s: an entertainment, a curiosity of the foreign community, occasionally performed and never prescribed. It was the Anglicist victory, and only that victory, that made the dramatist a syllabus. Wood's Despatch of 1854, the second great state paper, completed the architecture the Minute had sketched: it directed a graded system from vernacular primary schools to English universities, founded departments of public instruction in the provinces, and prescribed the universities that opened three years later. Between the Despatch's machinery and the Minute's ideology, the play's Indian institutional fate was sealed before the first Indian graduate was examined. What the following two centuries contest is not the sealing but the ownership of what was sealed.

One more feature of the defeated side deserves record, because it returns at the end of the story. The Orientalists lost the funding argument, but their premise, that Indian learning possessed classics of the first rank, survived their defeat and was absorbed by the Indian tradition itself: the comparative criticism that later set Kalidasa beside the English dramatist, the university syllabi that teach Sanskrit drama in the classical papers of the same English degrees, the Narendrapur background unit that files the Minute between the rise of Indian publishing and the growth of Indian readership. The Anglicists determined what would be taught. The question of what it should be measured against, which the Orientalists had insisted on asking, was left open for Indian scholars to answer in their own time. The play's Indian curriculum contains both legacies at once: an English text prescribed by the victors of 1835, and a standard of comparison supplied by the losers.

## The Mask Argument: Gauri Viswanathan and the Discipline Invented in a Colony

The scholar who converted that policy history into an argument about literature itself is Gauri Viswanathan, whose essay "The Beginnings of English Literary Study in British India" appeared in the Oxford Literary Review in 1987 and whose book Masks of Conquest: Literary Study and British Rule in India followed in 1989. Viswanathan is the mandatory starting point for this article's question, and her claims are stated here as argument, with her evidence, before the counter-evidence is given its turn. Her first claim is chronological and startling: the discipline of English literature, the organised curricular study of English texts as a moral and cultural formation, was assembled in colonial India before it was institutionalised in England. English was taught in Indian colleges as a subject with syllabi, examinations and a civilising mission while the older English universities still treated their own vernacular literature as unfit for a degree; Cambridge did not establish its English faculty until 1919, generations after Indian students had been examined on English texts as a matter of imperial routine. Her second claim is functional. Under a Gramscian account of hegemony, rule sustained by consent rather than by continuous coercion, the literary curriculum was the consent-manufacturing part of the empire: English literature, she argued, functioned as a mask, camouflaging the agenda of economic and material exploitation behind the humane face of literary cultivation.

The book's career since 1989 is itself part of the curriculum record. Masks of Conquest became the standard citation for the colonial founding of English studies, assigned in the postcolonial literature courses of Indian universities and examined, directly or through its influence, wherever the discipline accounts for itself. The UGC NET's unit on English in India, history, evolution and futures, is unthinkable without the historiography her book consolidated: before it, the discipline's Indian beginning was a administrative footnote; after it, the beginning was the discipline's central embarrassment and central research programme. That a colonial-history argument could become, within a generation, part of the certifying syllabus of the successor system is the mask thesis's most interesting fate. The critique was absorbed by the institution it critiqued, set on the reading lists of the degrees whose ancestry it exposed, and examined by the descendants of the interpreter class. Absorption is not refutation, and Viswanathan's own Gramscian frame predicts it: hegemony maintains itself partly by licensing its critics. But a syllabus that requires the accusation is not the syllabus the accusation described, and the difference between the two is the space in which this article's verdict operates.

The strength of the argument is that it explains the documentary shape of the record better than the innocence it replaced. It explains why a trading company and then a Crown administration spent scarce funds on poetry and drama for a small class of Indian students: the plays and poems were not decoration on the administrative project, they were part of its method, a way of producing admiration for the rulers' culture inside the ruled. It explains why the curriculum privileged moral formation over philology, why the literary text was made to stand as a mirror of ideal English character, and why the enterprise was described by its own architects in the vocabulary of taste, opinion and morals rather than the vocabulary of skill. Viswanathan's own summary, in the introduction to Masks of Conquest, is that the discipline of English "came into its own in an age of colonialism" and that no serious account of its growth can ignore the imperial mission of educating and civilising colonial subjects. Read against Macaulay's Minute, that is less an accusation than a paraphrase. The mask argument's weakness, which the next sections press, is that a mask is worn by somebody, and the students inside the classrooms of Calcutta were not passive faces. The same archive that documents the design also documents the design's failure to control what its students did with the texts, and the failure began almost immediately.

### Why did literary study become a discipline in the colony before the metropole?

English became a discipline in India first because the colony needed it as administration: a curriculum of English texts could manufacture consent and clerks where England itself still treated its own literature as an accomplishment rather than a university subject, a use the metropole adopted only generations later.

The chronology deserves one more degree of precision, because the argument is often repeated in a form looser than Viswanathan made it. She did not claim that no one in England read English literature before India systematised it; schools, dissenting academies and circulating libraries had long made the texts available. Her claim concerns the discipline: the moment when the study of English literature acquired a curriculum, a rationale, an examining apparatus and a state purpose. That moment, on her evidence, belongs to the Indian colleges of the early nineteenth century, where the General Committee of Public Instruction and its successors had to specify what English study was for, since public money was being spent on it in a conquered country. In England the question of purpose was not forced until much later, and when it was forced, the answers given by the new English faculties echoed the colonial rationale: character, taste, national cohesion. The direction of influence is the scandal of the argument. The syllabus travelled from the periphery to the centre, and the play at the centre of that syllabus travelled with it.

## Moral Formation: What the Play Was Hired to Teach

Read the founding documents for their verbs and a pattern emerges that explains the play's selection better than any theory of taste. Macaulay's class was to be formed in taste, opinions and morals: the curriculum's product was a character. The examination universities inherited the assumption that a literary text certifies a moral formation, and the Indian syllabus tradition retained the assumption long after the empire that authored it departed. The Springs course book's presentation of the play is the modern specimen. Its summary teaches the story as a moral exhibit, love against feud, and its questions ask students to admire the speeches correctly. A text introduced to form morals continues to be examined as though forming morals were its function, in a republic whose education policies speak the language of values in every chapter. The continuity should not be mistaken for conspiracy; it is the ordinary inertia of institutions, which keep the shape of a practice after its rationale has been forgotten. But the shape has consequences for the play. A formation text is read for its sentiments, not its structure; its speeches are excerpted, its machinery ignored, its ending treated as a lesson instead of a mechanism. The Karnataka extract and the NCERT silence are two faces of the same inheritance: the play as moral furniture, present where furniture fits and absent where it does not.

The Indian criticism that matters most is the criticism that broke the formation frame. When Singh writes of different Shakespeares and Trivedi archives submission and resistance in the same matrix, the shared move is to treat the play not as a moral object but as a historical event: something that happened in India, repeatedly, with agents and interests. The university classroom that teaches the play beside its installation, beside its films, beside the Minute, has adopted that frame. The school classroom that teaches two speeches as an anthology of admirable feeling has not, and the distance between the two classrooms is, in the end, the distance this article has been measuring all along. It is not a distance between India and England, or between colony and republic. It is the distance between a text inherited and a text interrogated, and both distances exist inside the same national system, separated only by the boundary between school and university.

## The Calcutta Classroom Answers Back: Hindu College, Derozio and Richardson

The institution where the imperial syllabus met its first Indian students was Hindu College, founded in Calcutta in 1817, before Macaulay landed, before the Minute, before the universities. The college was a joint creation of British officials and Bengali reformers, Raja Rammohun Roy among its supporters and the watchmaker-philanthropist David Hare among its organisers, and its founding purpose was the secular education of Hindu boys in English and in the sciences and literature of Europe. Shakespeare entered the Indian classroom there, in a building erected for a subject the colonial state had not yet finished designing. Two of its teachers set the pattern for everything that followed. Henry Louis Vivian Derozio, appointed while still in his late teens and dismissed in 1831 for the radicalism he inspired, taught English literature to students who became the Young Bengal movement: reformers, journalists and iconoclasts who used the imported curriculum as a solvent on the orthodoxies of their own society. David Lester Richardson, the teacher most famed for popularising Shakespeare in early colonial Calcutta, taught the plays as performance: his elocution readings drew Macaulay's own admiration, and he encouraged his students out of the classroom and onto the stage.

The documentary trail from those classrooms is short and decisive. In 1837, students of Hindu College performed scenes from The Merchant of Venice under Richardson's encouragement, the earliest recorded instance of Indian students staging the dramatist their syllabus had given them. By the early 1850s the students of the Hare School, the Metropolitan Academy and the Oriental Academy had joined the enterprise, performing Shakespeare in the Oriental Theatre the academies built for the purpose. The pattern visible in miniature in Calcutta is the pattern the rest of this article tracks at national scale: a curriculum installed for one purpose being used, within twenty years, for purposes its installers had not scripted. Derozio's students did not become the docile interpreters of Macaulay's design; they became its first Indian critics, and they learned the language of their criticism in the classroom the design had built. Scholarship on this early phase has pressed the point against any simple version of the mask thesis. A doctoral study of Shakespeare studies in colonial Bengal, working from the college records, argues that the teaching of the plays at Hindu College was kept free of religious orthodoxy and imparted the secular humanism of the Renaissance, and it challenges the generalisation that Shakespeare teaching in colonial India was simply the political agenda of the rulers wearing a literary disguise. R. K. Dasgupta's survey of Shakespeare in Bengali literature, published in Indian Literature in 1964, had already documented the same doubleness from the literary side: a literature received through an imperial channel and answered, within a generation, in the receiver's own language.

### What did Hindu College students perform in 1837, and why does the performance matter?

Scenes from The Merchant of Venice, performed by Hindu College students in 1837 under their teacher David Lester Richardson. The performance matters because it shows the colonial classroom producing performers within twenty years of the college's founding: the imported text was already being spoken by Indian students on an Indian stage.

Derozio's fate within the college is its own small verdict on the design's limits. Appointed a teacher while still in his teens, he filled his classroom with students who debated free thought, attacked idolatry and caste orthodoxy, and published their heresies in journals of their own founding. The college's managers dismissed him in 1831, judging his influence a danger to the institution, and he died the same year, still in his early twenties. The dismissal proves the classroom was never the docile instrument the policy imagined: its most brilliant teacher had to be expelled for what the imported learning did in Indian minds. At the other end of the college's first century stands Percival, the Anglo-Indian principal of its successor, Presidency College, briefly in 1909, preparing his editions of the plays for the Indian examination market. Expulsion at one end of the century, editorship at the other: the college that began by teaching the dramatist against the orthodoxies of two countries ended by manufacturing the books in which India studied him.

None of this acquits the design. The students who staged Shakespeare in 1837 were a fraction of a fraction: English education in the 1830s reached a thin urban stratum, and the filtration theory embedded in Macaulay's plan, educate a class and let refinement trickle downward, guaranteed that it would stay thin for generations. The point is narrower and more useful. From the first decade in which the record can be checked, the Indian classroom was not a sealed chamber in which a mask did its work undisturbed. It was a contested room, and the contest was conducted partly in Shakespeare, because Shakespeare was what the room contained. When the verdict at the end of this article says the instrument changed hands, the change is dated from here: not from independence, not from the republic's syllabi committees, but from the moment Indian students began performing, translating and arguing with the set texts faster than the set texts could form them.

## 1848: An Indian Othello and a Bengali Romeo and Juliet

Eleven years after the Hindu College performance, the Calcutta record produces two events in a single year that belong in every account of the play's Indian education, because between them they mark the twin routes by which the imported canon became Indian property: performance and translation. In August and September 1848, a Bengali actor, Baishnav Charan Addy, played Othello at the Sans Souci Theatre in Calcutta, on 17 August and again on 12 September. A Calcutta newspaper, the Bengal Hurkaru, reviewed the performance with the condescension of the period: his delivery was somewhat cramped, the paper allowed, but his pronunciation of English was, for a native, remarkably good. The sentence is worth pausing on, because it preserves the exact terms of the colonial classroom's judgment: the Indian performer measured against an English standard, and praised for approximating it. What the review could not see was that the event had already escaped the standard. An Indian actor had taken the tragic lead of the English repertoire in a public theatre, in English, before a European audience, eleven years after the first student scenes. The checklist of Shakespeare on the Calcutta stage compiled by Sukanta Chaudhuri and Ananda Lal records the performance as the landmark it was: the syllabus's pupil had become the stage's protagonist.

The second event of 1848 concerns this play directly. In that year the story of Romeo and Juliet was rendered into Bengali prose by Gurudas Hajra and published in Calcutta. A prose rendering is not a translation of the play; it is the story detached from the verse, the plot of the star-crossed lovers made available to readers of Bengali who had never sat in an English classroom. The distinction matters for curriculum history, because the prose tale is the form in which the play would reach the largest number of Indian readers across the following century and a half: in retellings, in textbook extracts with prose summaries, in guidebook paraphrase. Hajra's rendering stands at the head of that lineage. The full dramatic adaptation followed within the same generation: Charumukh Chitrahara Natak, a Bengali stage adaptation of the play, is dated 1864 in the scholarly accounts of the Bengali Shakespeare tradition, part of the same mid-century enterprise that produced Harachandra Ghosh's adaptation of The Merchant of Venice and, in 1869, Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar's prose version of The Comedy of Errors. Vidyasagar's presence in the list is the tell. The greatest educationist of nineteenth-century Bengal, the reformer of Bengali prose and of the Bengali schoolbook, spent his authority on turning Shakespeare into Bengali reading. Translation on this scale is not submission to a canon. It is the canon's confiscation by its readers.

The venue of Addy's triumph carries the same lesson in institutional form. The Sans Souci was a commercial theatre, not a college hall: the performance was sold to a paying city, reviewed by a commercial press, and judged by an audience that included the European community whose applause the newspapers recorded. The college had trained the performer, but the city ratified him, and the ratification mattered because it demonstrated a demand the curriculum had never planned to create. Calcutta's stage history across the following decades, the student theatres of the academies, the Bengali adaptations, the checklist of productions that Chaudhuri and Lal assembled, shows the demand institutionalising itself outside every syllabus. The play's Indian owners were multiplying faster than its Indian examiners.

The Parsi theatre would later carry the plays, this one among them, across the subcontinent in Urdu and Gujarati adaptation, and the performance history belongs to the series' stage articles rather than to a curriculum study. What belongs here is the educational meaning of the 1848 pair. In the classroom, Shakespeare remained what the policy had made him: an English author, examined in English, a standard against which Indian pronunciation and Indian taste were graded by Indian and English examiners alike. Outside the classroom, in the same city and the same year, an Indian Othello was being applauded and a Bengali Romeo and Juliet was being sold. The curriculum and the culture were already running on different clocks, and the culture's clock was faster. Every later stage of the play's Indian school career replays that difference: the official text lagging behind the appropriated one, the syllabus conceding in one decade what the stage and the translation had settled in the last.

## Translation as a Second Curriculum

Set the translation record beside the syllabus record and a second, unofficial curriculum comes into view, running parallel to the official one and often ahead of it. The official curriculum taught the play in English, to the small class that English reached, in extracts or in whole texts prescribed by committees. The unofficial curriculum taught the story in Bengali, and in time in the subcontinent's other literary languages, to anyone who could buy or borrow a printed book. Hajra's prose rendering of 1848 and the adaptation Charumukh Chitrahara Natak of 1864 are the documented heads of that second curriculum for this play, and Harachandra Ghosh's Merchant adaptation and Vidyasagar's Comedy of Errors stand in the same decades for the canon at large. The translators were not marginal figures working against the education system from outside. Vidyasagar was the education system in Bengal: the architect of its schoolbooks, a reformer of its prose, an inspector of its schools. When the maker of the Bengali textbook tradition spends his authority on Shakespearean prose, translation has ceased to be an act performed on the curriculum and has become an act performed within it, by one of its own chief officers, in the language of the taught rather than the language of the teachers.

The distinction the translations enforce is between the play as examination subject and the play as reading. The examination subject was verse, glossed and annotated, approached through the questions it might generate. The reading was narrative: two houses, a feast, a balcony replaced in the retellings by whatever architecture the adapter's stage or page supplied, a double death and a reconciliation purchased too late. Generations of Indian readers who never opened an English textbook met the second play, and the second play is the one that fed the stage traditions, the Parsi theatre's adaptations and the screen versions that the series treats in its film articles. India's Shakespeare, the Trivedi and Bartholomeusz collection of 2005, organises its whole inquiry around this doubleness: translation, interpretation and performance are its subtitle because those are the three channels through which the prescribed text escaped prescription. A curriculum history that confined itself to syllabi would record a play installed by an empire. The translation record, kept by the same decades, records a play being uninstalled and rebuilt, repeatedly, by its readers. Both records are true, and the verdict of this article is the attempt to weigh them in the same scale.

The prose-tale lineage that Hajra heads has a modern descendant inside the official curriculum itself, and naming it closes the circle. The Karnataka chapter's architecture, a prose summary carrying the plot and two speeches carrying the poetry, is Hajra's 1848 architecture: the story naturalised in accessible prose, the verse admitted in samples. The NCERT readers' reference culture does the same work informally, since the student who meets the lovers through guidebooks and film plots meets Hajra's play, the tale, rather than the dramatist's. Across two centuries the two curricula have braided: the official one periodically absorbing the unofficial one's forms, the unofficial one feeding on the prestige the official one maintains. Translation in this history is not the alternative to the curriculum. It is the curriculum's shadow, its distribution network, and at last its template.

## 1857: The Examination Universities Fix the Canon

If the classroom gave the play its Indian life, the examination gave it its Indian permanence. The universities founded at Calcutta, Bombay and Madras in 1857 were examining bodies on the London model: they prescribed, affiliated and tested, while the teaching happened in colleges scattered across their jurisdictions. For a literary text, prescription by an examining university is the most consequential event that can occur. It creates a guaranteed readership renewed every academic year; it creates a publishing industry of student editions, annotations, paraphrases and model answers; and it creates a professional class of teachers whose own formation, and whose livelihood, is bound to the prescribed list. Shakespeare stood at the head of that list from the universities' first decades, the indispensable author of the English curriculum, and Romeo and Juliet stood among the plays through which generations of Indian graduates passed. The canon the universities fixed was not chosen afresh each generation. It was inherited, reprinted and re-examined, which is why the play's presence in Indian higher education has the character of geology rather than of fashion: layer upon layer of syllabus, each new committee ratifying the deposit the last one left.

The material culture of that permanence survives in the editions. Hugh Melville Percival, an Anglo-Indian teacher who served briefly as principal of Presidency College, the successor institution to Hindu College, and retired in 1911, produced critical editions of Shakespeare plays prepared specifically for Indian students, with the long introductions and apparatus that an examination culture requires. Percival's editions are a small fact with a large meaning. By the first decade of the twentieth century, the traffic in Shakespearean editing no longer ran only from London outward. An editor working in Calcutta was preparing the plays for the Indian examination market, annotating them for the students who would be examined on them by Indian universities. The mask, if mask it was, was being manufactured locally. The same localisation is visible in the examination hall itself: Indian examiners set the questions, Indian professors marked the answers, and the standard of Englishness against which Addy had been patronised in 1848 was, by the early twentieth century, administered by the graduates of the system it had created. None of this dissolved the hierarchy of the canon itself. The plays remained English, the degree remained an English degree, and the prestige of the subject continued to rest on its distance from the vernacular cultures of the students who studied it. But the personnel had changed, and with the personnel, slowly, the questions changed too: Indian criticism of Shakespeare, from Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay's comparisons of Kalidasa's heroines with the dramatist's onward, began to judge the imported canon against the classical literature of India rather than merely receive it.

The affiliating structure multiplied the prescription's reach in a way a teaching university never could. A single university syllabus governed the colleges of an entire province-sized jurisdiction, so a text adopted at Calcutta was read from Patna to Chittagong under one examination, and the publishing market organised itself accordingly: one prescribed list, one edition trade, one pattern of questions, repeated across thousands of candidates a year. The London model, imported whole, had been designed for an examining body serving scattered colleges, and in India it found the largest scatter in the world. The play's entrenchment is not a mystery of taste. It is the arithmetic of affiliation: prescribe a text once, examine it everywhere, and let the reprints compound. Each generation of committees inherited not a choice but a going concern, with textbooks in print, teachers trained on the text, and question banks built across decades. Reform in such a system is possible, and the modern syllabi below show it happening, but the default is always continuation, and for this play continuation is what the record shows.

The universities also fixed the form in which most Indian students would meet the play for the next century: whole, but examined. A degree student read the full text, because the examination could range across any scene; a school student, as the next sections show, read what the school board prescribed, which was rarely the whole. That division, full text at degree level and extracts at school level, was drawn in the nineteenth century and, as the board-by-board evidence below demonstrates, it is still the governing division of the play's Indian curriculum. The colonial system built two doors into the play, a wide one at the university and a narrow one at the school, and postcolonial India, for all its syllabus revisions, has kept both doors on their hinges.

## The Indian Edition: Editors, Annotations and the Examination Economy

A prescribed text manufactures a literature of its own: the student edition. Percival's Calcutta-prepared editions, noticed above, stand early in a line that runs unbroken into the present market: annotated texts of the council's Merchant of Venice produced by Delhi and Meerut publishers for the ICSE cycle, paraphrase-and-summary volumes for state-board students, model-answer compilations keyed to the questions boards have set before. The student edition is where the colonial curriculum and Indian enterprise interlock most tightly. The prescription is inherited; the apparatus is indigenous. Generations of Indian teachers earned their authority, and Indian publishers their capital, by mediating the imported text for the Indian examinee: explaining its jokes, flattening its ambiguities into notes, converting its scenes into the extract questions the examination would ask. The mediation deserves a name in the adjudication, because it complicates both sides of the argument. Against Viswanathan: the apparatus was Indian-made, responsive to Indian classrooms, and often produced by the very class the Minute had manufactured, which had proceeded to manufacture the means of its own instruction. Against the appropriation celebration: the apparatus existed to serve an examination whose texts were not chosen by the mediated, and its characteristic product, the note that replaces the scene, is the extract mentality in its purest commercial form.

The checklist scholarship brought the same double record under scholarly control. Shakespeare on the Calcutta Stage: A Checklist, compiled by Sukanta Chaudhuri and Ananda Lal and published in 2001, did for the performance tradition what the syllabus documents do for the classroom: it fixed names, dates and venues, converting a legendary past into a checkable one, and it is on such checklists that claims like the 1848 landmark in this article rest. R. K. Dasgupta's survey of Shakespeare in Bengali literature had performed the same service for the language tradition a generation earlier. The pattern across both is the one the verdict will formalise. The documentary infrastructure of the play's Indian life, editions, checklists, surveys, editions of the Minute itself for Indian degree students, is Indian-built. An instrument whose blueprints, maintenance manuals and service history are all written by the hands it was imposed on has changed owners in every sense except the sense that matters most to the mask argument: the sense of who chose the instrument in the first place. That choice remains Macaulay's, and no quantity of Indian apparatus unwrites it. The argument between the two camps is therefore not about facts. It is about which fact, the choosing or the keeping, should count as the history.

## One Country, Many Curricula: The Map the Documents Draw

India has no single school curriculum, and any sentence that begins "in India the play is taught" is false until it names a board, a level and a document. Education is a concurrent subject, shared between the union and the states; the union's own board, the Central Board of Secondary Education, teaches from textbooks prepared by the National Council of Educational Research and Training, while a second national system, the Council for the Indian School Certificate Examinations, prescribes its own texts for its affiliated schools, and the states run boards and textbook societies of their own, several of which publish English readers that differ from one another in content, language support and ambition. Above the schools sit hundreds of universities, each with statutory power over its syllabi, working since 2015 within a national choice-based credit template and since 2020 within the framework of a new national education policy. The play's Indian curriculum is therefore not a fact but a map, and the map's most important feature is its unevenness: the same student population meets the whole play, a pair of speeches, or nothing at all, depending on which system's classroom the student happens to sit in.

The constitutional mechanics behind the scatter are worth one paragraph, because they explain why no single reform can settle the play's Indian place. Education stood in the state list of the Constitution as adopted in 1950; the Forty-second Amendment of 1976 moved it to the concurrent list, where union and states may both legislate, with union law prevailing in a conflict. The move authorised national frameworks, the NCERT's model textbooks, the national policies of 1968, 1986 and 2020, without abolishing the states' textbook societies or the universities' statutory autonomy over syllabi. Every level of the guide below therefore answers to a different sovereign: a ministry in Delhi frames, a council in Delhi prescribes for its own affiliated schools, a board in Bengaluru prints Springs, a senate in Madurai or Silchar votes a syllabus. A play entering that system does not enter a curriculum; it negotiates, document by document, with a federation. The colonial system that installed it was, by comparison, a unitary command: one committee's minute, one despatch, three universities on a single model. The empire could install a canon by decision. The republic can only renew one by accumulation, which is precisely what the record shows it doing.

The comparison disciplines the argument. In England, the play's school place is guaranteed by a national framework that names Shakespeare as the one compulsory author and by boards that examine whole plays, as the series account of [Romeo and Juliet in the UK GCSE](/2030/03/08/romeo-and-juliet-uk-gcse/) sets out; the English student cannot avoid the dramatist and usually cannot avoid meeting a complete text. India has no equivalent guarantee, and the survey of [the play in global classrooms](/2030/06/28/play-in-global-classrooms/) shows how unusual that guarantee is by world standards. What India has instead is depth at the top and scatter below: a university system in which the play is entrenched beyond the reach of any single reform, and a school system in which its presence is a local accident of board and textbook. The next four sections document the scatter precisely, because the verdict depends on it: the national board's silence, the council system's whole-play tradition that happens not to choose this play, one state board's extract that does, and the degree syllabi that prescribe the whole text by name.

## The National Readers Without a Play: CBSE and the NCERT Silence

Begin with the largest system. The English readers prescribed by NCERT for the Central Board's secondary and senior-secondary classes are Beehive for Class 9, First Flight for Class 10, Hornbill for Class 11 and Flamingo for Class 12, with their supplementary readers. Their contents are a matter of published record, and the record is unambiguous: no play by Shakespeare appears in any of them, and Romeo and Juliet appears nowhere in the central board's prescribed English course. The readers carry short stories, essays, poems, a one-act play by other hands, speeches and reportage; the dramatist who anchors the English curriculum of the universities is absent from the English curriculum of the national school board. The absence is structural rather than polemical. In the central board's design, English is a language subject before it is a literature subject: it is examined for comprehension, composition, grammar and a measured sampling of prose and poetry, and its textbooks were built to that specification. Drama, which resists the extract-and-comprehension format less easily than the short story but demands ensemble reading and time, fell out of the design, and Shakespeare fell with it.

### Why do the NCERT English readers contain no Shakespeare play?

The NCERT readers were built for a language subject, not a literature degree: English in the central board is examined as comprehension, composition and a sampling of prose and poetry, and the readers follow that design, carrying stories, essays and poems while leaving whole drama, and whole Shakespeare, to other boards and to universities.

What the readers carry instead deserves its due in the record, because the silence is selective rather than philistine. The NCERT course puts Indian writing in English, Indian writing in translation, and the literatures of the wider English-using world in front of the central board's students in quantities no colonial syllabus attempted: the classroom canon of the republic's own board is Indian first in a way the interpreter class's reading never was. A defender of the design can fairly say that the board traded the imported dramatist for the nation's own voices, and that the trade was the decolonising act the critics of 1835 should applaud. The rejoinder is narrower: the trade was made inside a language subject, where literature functions as comprehension material, so the students receive Indian stories as exercises rather than as works, and the whole-play encounter that the colonial system, for all its purposes, did provide at school level in its council lineage is the thing the national board's design has no room for at all.

The consequence for the play is a curious inversion of the colonial arrangement. In the nineteenth century, the state installed Shakespeare in the classroom as an instrument of formation; in the twenty-first, the state's own largest school system does not install him at all, and the formation happens elsewhere: in the council schools and state boards that prescribe him, in the universities that examine him, and in the unofficial curriculum of guidebooks, tuition notes and film that surrounds every board. A CBSE student who meets Romeo and Juliet before university meets the story, not the text: the balcony that the play never names, the suicide pact that the text complicates, the romance that the Friar's warnings frame. The misconception industry that the series has documented in other classrooms flourishes in exactly this gap between reputation and text. None of this makes the NCERT design indefensible; a language subject has defensible priorities, and the readers carry Indian writing in English and in translation in quantities the colonial syllabus never imagined. It does mean that the brief for this article, with its picture of the play as a long-established text across India's schools and universities, is only half a description. At university level the description holds. At the level of the largest school board, the play is not established, not embattled, not anything: it is absent, and the absence is the fact a curriculum history has to record first.

## The Council's Whole Play: CISCE and the Rotating Shakespeare

The second national system makes the opposite choice and, in doing so, supplies the control case for the whole argument. The Council for the Indian School Certificate Examinations builds its secondary literature papers around complete prescribed texts, and a complete Shakespeare play has held the drama place in its lists across the modern period. The documentary trail of the council's own prescribed-text appendices shows the institution at work: at the senior level, the ISC list for the 2024 examination set The Tempest, its opening acts prescribed for Class 11; the ISC list for the 2013 examination offered Macbeth as one of its two compulsory-text options. At the secondary level, the ICSE drama prescription across recent cycles has been The Merchant of Venice, served by a shelf of Indian-published editions with paraphrase and extract-based questions prepared for the council's specimen papers. The council, in short, examines Shakespeare whole, in the manner of the colonial examination tradition from which it descends, and Indian publishers in Delhi and Meerut produce the annotated texts its students require.

The council's lineage explains its fidelity to the form. The Indian School Certificate examinations descend from the school examinations that the University of Cambridge's syndicate conducted in India under the empire; the council itself was constituted in 1958, in the decade after independence, to carry that examination tradition on under Indian control. The institutional choice is worth naming, because it is the appropriation story at board level: an independent republic did not abolish the imperial examination that certified its schoolchildren through English set texts. It nationalised the examiner, kept the architecture, and staffed the prescriptions with Indian committees. The whole-play Shakespeare that the ICSE student meets is therefore neither a colonial survival in the simple sense nor an Indian invention. It is a colonial form re-elected by an Indian institution, the same pattern the university syllabi display, one level down.

What the council's lists also show, read carefully, is that the play occupying the slot rotates, and Romeo and Juliet is not the play the rotation has favoured in the documented cycles. The Merchant of Venice at ICSE and The Tempest and Macbeth at ISC are the recorded occupants; the lovers of Verona belong, in the council's system, to the penumbra of famous Shakespeare rather than to the examined text. This is a finding the celebratory version of the play's Indian career tends to skip, and it sharpens rather than blurs the map. India possesses an institution that preserves the colonial form of Shakespeare study in its purest surviving version, whole play, extract questions, annotated Indian editions, and in the cycles documented here that institution teaches other plays. The play's Indian school presence, where it exists at all, exists elsewhere: in the state boards. The distinction between the institution of Shakespeare and the presence of any particular play is one the rest of this article relies on. Institutions persist; occupants change; and a verdict about the play in Indian education has to be a verdict about its documented occupants' places, not about the institution's loyalty to the canon in general.

## Three Hours in Karnataka: The Play as Extract in Springs

Where the play does live in an Indian school textbook, it lives as an extract, and the fullest documented case is Karnataka's. The Karnataka School Examination and Assessment Board's English course book for the second year of the pre-university course, the two-year bridge between school and degree, is called Springs, and its first chapter is Romeo and Juliet. What the chapter contains is not the play. It contains a prose summary of the story, a note on the author, and two passages of verse: Romeo's speech at the Capulet feast in the fifth scene of the first act, beginning "O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright!" and continuing to the jewel in the Ethiop's ear and the snowy dove among crows, and Juliet's invocation to the night from the second scene of the third act, with its wish that her lover, when she dies, be cut out in little stars. The board's own blueprint for the course allots the unit three teaching hours and three marks. The examination questions the unit generates, as the board's model papers and the state's handbook literature record them, are questions of reference and paraphrase: what the phrase "face of heaven" signifies, whom the snowy dove names, what Romeo had never seen until that night.

Hold the two passages up against the play and the extract's editorial logic becomes visible, because the passages are magnificent and the selection is a falsification. Both speeches are lyrics of praise. Neither contains the feud as anything but background colour; neither contains a father, a bargain, a friar, a corpse. A student who masters the Karnataka unit has met the lovers' eloquence and nothing of the structure that eloquence is trapped inside: not Capulet offering his daughter to Paris in the second scene with the reservation that his will is only part of the matter, not the same father in the fifth act threatening to leave her to hang, beg, starve and die in the streets, not Mercutio's plague on both houses, not the Prince's closing arithmetic of punishment. The extract presents the play as a love poem with a sad ending reported in summary. That is, almost exactly, the misconception the series' pedagogy articles warn against: the celebration reading, installed not by a careless teacher but by a state textbook's table of contents. The handbook literature that surrounds Springs completes the process by filing the unit among the course's poems, one lyric item between Tolstoy and Gibran, to be paraphrased for one mark at a time.

### Why does the Karnataka extract begin at the Capulet feast rather than the street brawl?

The extract was chosen for examination, not for narrative: the feast speech and the night speech are the play's two most anthologised lyrics of praise, self-contained passages that can be set, glossed and marked in one-mark questions, while the brawl needs the feud's whole machinery behind it to mean anything at all.

The pedagogy wrapped around the extract deserves the same documentary care as the extract itself. The state's model question papers and the handbook literature for the course treat the two speeches as comprehension territory: a phrase is quoted, a meaning is demanded, a paraphrase earns the mark. No question in the recorded set asks why the lovers die, what the feud costs the city, or whether the fathers bear the guilt the Prince assigns them at the end; the play's argument is unexaminable because the extract does not contain it. Teachers in Karnataka classrooms are free to supply the argument from their own reading, and many trained in the state's universities, where the whole play is prescribed, carry it with them. The curriculum as a document, however, asks for none of it, and a curriculum history must judge the document, not the best teacher who ever exceeded it.

The defence of the extract writes itself, and it should be recorded before the criticism hardens. Three hours is what a crowded pre-university course can spare; two speeches of this density teach more about verse, image and address than a raced summary of five acts; and the Karnataka student at least reads Shakespeare's words, forty lines of them, in a state textbook printed for every pre-university college in the state, which is forty more lines than the national board's readers offer. The extract is a real presence, and its presence in a Kannada-majority state's English course book, alongside Kuvempu and P. Lankesh and Vandana Shiva, shows the canon being edited by an Indian board for Indian students rather than merely inherited. But the form of the presence governs what the play can mean in that classroom. An extract curriculum produces an extract play: lyric without machinery, love without the marriage market that prices it, a Romeo and Juliet whose catastrophe arrives by summary the way weather arrives by forecast. The colonial classroom at least prescribed the whole text and let the machinery show.

## The Whole Play at Degree Level: Jamia, Assam, Madurai, Anantapur

Cross the boundary into the universities and the play changes size. The degree syllabi name it whole, and the documents can be quoted. Jamia Millia Islamia in New Delhi, a central university founded in 1920 out of the nationalist movement against British rule, prescribes for the second semester of its BA Honours English programme a paper called English Drama I, whose four units run from Marlowe's Doctor Faustus and Jonson's The Alchemist to a third unit consisting of Romeo and Juliet entire and a fourth of The Tempest. Assam University at Silchar, in its four-year undergraduate programme framed under the National Education Policy and effective from the academic year 2023-24, lists the play in its British Drama course paired directly with Baz Luhrmann's film of 1996 as the next unit of study. Fatima College in Madurai, an autonomous women's college, devotes a full unit of its fifth-semester Shakespeare paper to the play under the generic heading the syllabus itself supplies, "Tragic Romance", at twenty-two teaching hours, in a paper that gives Macbeth twenty-four. The Central University of Andhra Pradesh, in its MA English syllabus effective from 2025-26, sets the play in a drama unit between Kyd's Spanish Tragedy and Udall's Ralph Roister Doister, the company of its first English decade. The choice-based credit template that hundreds of colleges follow carries the play a second way as well: its Literature and Cinema elective prescribes the text together with the Zeffirelli film of 1968 and the Luhrmann film of 1996, and Delhi college examination papers set under that template ask candidates to analyse the tomb scene across the two adaptations.

### How does Assam University pair the play with its most famous film?

In its British Drama course for the four-year undergraduate programme that began in the 2023-24 academic year, Assam University lists the play and Baz Luhrmann's 1996 film as consecutive units of one course, so the text is studied with its loudest modern adaptation open beside it rather than screened as a reward.

Above these individual documents sits the template that frames them. The University Grants Commission's model curriculum for the choice-based credit system organises the English degree around period papers, and the paper covering the fourteenth to seventeenth centuries, "British Poetry and Drama: 14th to 17th Centuries", is the shelf on which the play sits in hundreds of departmental adaptations. The template names authors and periods and leaves the particular play to the university, which is why the record shows the same course title carrying different dramatists' works in different states, other syllabi naming Macbeth or Twelfth Night where Jamia and Madurai name the lovers. The template matters because it locates the play's security correctly. Its degree-level place does not rest on a national command; no Indian regulation orders this play taught. It rests on a settled expectation, reproduced by committee after committee working inside a common frame, that the early modern drama paper is where Romeo and Juliet lives. Expectations of that kind outlast regulations, and they explain why syllabus reform in India, which has been frequent and at times radical, reforms everything around the play while renewing the play itself.

Two features of this degree-level record deserve emphasis. The first is that the whole play at university is not a survival but a repeated decision. These are syllabi framed or reframed within the last decade, under a credit system that forced every department to rewrite its lists and under a national policy that invited indigenisation of content. Committees that were free to drop the play, and that did drop or demote other inherited texts, kept this one by name, at Jamia under a drama paper's core units, at Fatima as a quarter of the Shakespeare paper, at the postgraduate level in Andhra Pradesh as a set text in its own right. A canon maintained by re-election is in a different moral position from a canon maintained by inertia, and the Indian university canon of this play is, on the documentary record, re-elected. The second feature is what sits beside the play in the same documents. At Ramakrishna Mission Residential College, Narendrapur, an autonomous college affiliated to the University of Calcutta, the British Drama core course offers the play among its Shakespeare options, and the background unit of the same degree's Indian writing paper lists, as syllabus matter, Macaulay's Minute on Indian Education of 1835, set between the rise of Indian publishing and the growth of an Indian readership. The founding memorandum of the colonial curriculum is itself curriculum material, studied by the descendants of the class it proposed to manufacture. Scholars who keep such syllabus records across revisions, their own and their departments', are doing archival work of exactly the kind a research vault exists for, and a local-first store such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) serves the individual scholar's version of that discipline, holding documents and their versions together under the owner's own audit.

### What does the Narendrapur syllabus place beside the English canon?

The background unit of its Indian Writing in English paper sets Macaulay's Minute on Indian Education of 1835 itself, listed as syllabus matter beside the rise of Indian publishing and readership, so the students study the colonial memorandum that commissioned their subject as part of the subject it commissioned.

## The Critics Take Possession: Indian Shakespeare Scholarship

Between the examination economy and the modern syllabus stands a body of Indian criticism that changed the terms on which the play could be taught at all. Its early landmark is comparative and serene: Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay, the novelist who more than any other founded modern Bengali prose fiction, wrote of Kalidasa's Shakuntala beside the dramatist's Miranda and Desdemona, judging the imported heroines against the classical Indian measure rather than the reverse. The gesture is the opposite of the Hurkaru's 1848 review. Addy had been graded against an English standard; Bankim graded the English canon against a Sanskrit one, in Bengali, for Bengali readers, and found the comparison worth making on both sides. Through the twentieth century the Indian university system produced the scholarship that made such judgments professional: the surveys of the Bengali Shakespeare tradition, the performance checklists, the editions and the histories, culminating in the generation of Jyotsna Singh, Ania Loomba and Poonam Trivedi, whose work has already been cited and whose disagreement structures this article's verdict. Loomba's scholarship, from her studies of colonialism and performance to the Post-Colonial Shakespeares collection she edited with Martin Orkin in 1998, keeps the cost of the canon in view while documenting its remaking; Singh's "different Shakespeares" names the remaking; Trivedi's India's Shakespeare archives it.

The curricular significance of that scholarship is easy to miss because it operates one level above the syllabus. A syllabus prescribes a text; criticism prescribes the questions that may be asked of the text, and the examinations follow the questions. When Jamia's Drama paper or Assam's British Drama course teaches the play beside its film, when a Calcutta-affiliated college sets the Minute itself as background reading, the questions being asked are the questions Indian criticism spent a century formulating: who installed this text, in whose interest, and what has been made of it since. The colonial syllabus had prescribed the play and prescribed reverence as the method. The postcolonial syllabus prescribes the same play and prescribes interrogation as the method, and the continuity of the text conceals the reversal of the method, which is why a bare list of prescriptions, the guide below included, understates the change the verdict has to weigh. A student examined on the play in 1890 was examined on its beauties. A student examined on it in the documented modern syllabi is examined, in part, on its installation. The text survived; the deference did not.

The Indian criticism also supplies the corrective to its own excess, and a curriculum article should record it. The strongest version of the appropriation story can sound as though every Indian encounter with the play were a skirmish in a national struggle, when the documentary record contains multitudes of encounters that were neither submission nor resistance: students who loved the speeches for their sound, teachers who taught the tomb scene for its construction, audiences who wept at the ending without theorising it. Trivedi's matrix phrase survives because it holds both poles at once and leaves the middle, ordinary reading, unnamed but present. The verdict of this article follows the same discipline: the institutional record, installations, prescriptions, examinations, is the ground on which claims are made here, and the interior life of Indian reading, which no syllabus can document, is acknowledged as the vast remainder that institutional history cannot weigh.

## Reading Verona from India: The Marriage Market and the Local Eye

A curriculum is a list; a reading is what happens when the list meets a life. The Indian reading of this play, the one conducted in its classrooms and in the scholarship those classrooms produced, fastens on the machinery that the Karnataka extract cuts away: the play's marriage market. The text is explicit about the market's terms. When Paris sues for Juliet in the second scene, Capulet pleads her age, "My child is yet a stranger in the world; / She hath not seen the change of fourteen years", and then concedes the form of consent the market allowed a daughter: "But woo her, gentle Paris, get her heart; / My will to her consent is but a part." Lady Capulet's courtship advice is to read the bridegroom as a book whose face is the volume and whose beauty is the writing. When the daughter refuses the arranged match, the father's vocabulary for her refusal is eviction: she may hang, beg, starve, die in the streets. Juliet's rebellion is correspondingly precise in its target. "Deny thy father and refuse thy name" is not a lover's effusion; it is a proposal to resign from the kinship system that has priced her, and the speech that follows it, "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet", argues that the name, the family's brand and title deed, is the one thing about Romeo that the marriage market cannot use. The Friar, who solemnises the counter-marriage, prices that too: "These violent delights have violent ends."

The bargaining structure anatomised in the series study of [patriarchy and the marriage market in Verona](/2024/02/23/patriarchy-marriage-market-verona/) is, in an Indian classroom, not an antiquarian reconstruction. It is a description of negotiations that students have watched at their own family tables, conducted under the sanctions of caste endogamy, community honour and parental authority that the Indian sociology of marriage documents in detail. The feud requires no translation at all: two houses, alike in dignity, whose enmity is inherited rather than argued, map onto the clan and community loyalties that Indian students know from the inside, and Mercutio's dying verdict, "A plague o' both your houses!", lands in such a classroom as a judgment on a system, not only on a street fight. This is the substance of the local reading as the Indian scholarship records it. Jyotsna Singh's essay "Different Shakespeares", in the collection Post-Colonial Shakespeares that Ania Loomba and Martin Orkin edited in 1998, argued that the dramatist produced by colonial India and the dramatist remade after independence are different authors made by different institutions, and that the Indian Shakespeare is a product of that remaking rather than a relic of the original deposit. Poonam Trivedi, a Shakespeare scholar at the University of Delhi, gave the remaking its standard archive in the collection India's Shakespeare: Translation, Interpretation, and Performance, published in 2005, whose chapter on Romeo and Juliet in modern Indian disseminations tracks the play's passage through Indian languages, stages and screens. The volume's framing phrase for the whole interaction is a matrix of simultaneous submission and resistance, and its evidence runs from schoolrooms to cinema: the play taught as the rulers' text, and the same play seized as the ruled's material. Whether the seizure redeems the text for its women is a further question, pursued head-on in [Is Romeo and Juliet a Feminist Play?](/2031/04/18/is-romeo-and-juliet-feminist/), and Indian feminist criticism has been among the sharpest in asking it of a play that gives its heroine the canon's most eloquent case against the marriage market and then marries her at thirteen.

The charge runs in the other direction as well, and the Indian criticism has not spared the play on that account. A heroine who defies the marriage market and is dead by fifteen invites a feminist audit as readily as a feminist celebration, and the Indian scholars who have written on the play's teaching have asked whether the curriculum's admiration for Juliet's eloquence quietly endorses the romance that kills her: the disobedience praised, the obedience to the lover's timetable never questioned. The question is sharper in India than in the play's home country precisely because the arrangements Juliet resists have living counterparts, and a classroom that recognises Capulet's bargaining as familiar must also decide whether the catastrophe that follows it reads as tragedy, as warning, or as the system's enforcement by other means. That range of readings, celebratory, cautionary and accusatory, coexisting in one seminar room, is itself the evidence that the text is being used rather than merely venerated. A mask does not generate arguments against the curriculum that installed it. A confiscated instrument does.

Two cautions govern this section's claims, and they are stated as limits rather than decorations. The first is that no classroom transcript is quoted here, because the published record of Indian classroom discussion of the play is thin; the local reading is documented in scholarship, syllabi and the adaptation record, and it is at that level, the level of what Indian critics and committees have written and prescribed, that this article reports it. The second is that the resonance of the marriage market does not make the play an Indian text by nature. It makes it a text whose central institution India also possesses, which is a different and more interesting condition: the play reads as foreign in its language and domestic in its sociology, and the friction between those two facts is where the Indian classroom's distinctive work happens. A Verona in which daughters are assets, marriages are alliances and honour is a family's capital requires no footnote in a country where the same arrangements are debated in its newspapers and its courts. The colonial curriculum imported the language. The sociology was already home.

## Gatekeeper or Ladder: The English Language Underneath the Play

Every claim made so far sits on a prior question, which is not about the play at all: the question of English itself in Indian education. The curriculum that carried Shakespeare into India was an English curriculum, and the language arrived with the double character it has never lost. As gatekeeper, English sorted and still sorts: fluency in it governs access to the professions, to the higher judiciary, to the national media and to the universities' own seminars, and the children of the fluent inherit the advantage the way the children of Verona inherited the feud. As ladder, English has been claimed, by some of the very communities the caste order excluded from Sanskrit learning, as the one Indian language that belongs to no caste and no region, the instrument with which the excluded could address the state and one another across the subcontinent's linguistic frontiers. The two characters are not successive phases. They are simultaneous properties of the same subject, and any verdict on the play's curriculum that ignores them mistakes a literary question for the structural one underneath it.

The ladder's claim has a monument, and the monument is documented. On 30 April 2010, in the village of Bankagaon in Lakhimpur Kheri district, Uttar Pradesh, the foundation was laid for a temple to the Dalit Goddess English. The idol installed there was modelled on the Statue of Liberty, a pen raised in her right hand and books in her left, standing on a computer. The campaigner behind the temple, the Dalit writer and columnist Chandra Bhan Prasad, celebrates Macaulay's birthday each year and is reported calling the author of the 1835 Minute a social revolutionary who made all Indians equal before the law. The provocation is deliberate and it is the strongest single piece of evidence on the appropriation side of this article's argument: a movement of the oppressed venerating the colonial curriculum's architect, because the language his Minute imposed became, in their account, a weapon against the indigenous hierarchy that predated the empire and survived it. Against that evidence stands Ania Loomba's caution, running through her work on the colonial and postcolonial careers of the plays: the celebration of appropriation can become a way of not counting the cost, and the cost is that the ladder's rungs are still rationed, that the English which liberates the few who master it continues to exclude the many who are schooled, badly and briefly, in its name. The National Education Policy of 2020 intervened in the quarrel from the state's side, directing in its fourth chapter that wherever possible the medium of instruction until at least Grade 5, and preferably till Grade 8 and beyond, be the home language, mother tongue, local language or regional language. The direction reopens Macaulay's question from the opposite end: not which literature shall form the interpreters, but in which language the forming of all children should begin. Meanwhile the discipline the Minute founded examines its own history at the highest level: the syllabus for the University Grants Commission's National Eligibility Test, the examination that licenses India's university teachers of English, carries a unit titled "English in India: history, evolution and futures", so that no candidate can be certified to teach the canon without being examined on the canonisation.

The gatekeeping character has its own institutional monument, less photogenic than the temple and far larger: the English-medium school sector. Across the republic's cities and district towns, private schools advertising English as their medium compete for the children of families schooled in other languages, and the advertisement works because the parents' calculation is the same one Macaulay's interpreters made: the language is the toll road to the professions, and the toll is worth paying. The curriculum consequence is a permanent pressure that no policy paper has relieved, visible in the demand side of every board's English subject. The National Education Policy's mother-tongue direction, read against that market, is best understood as a counterweight rather than a settlement: the state directs the early years toward the home language while the market continues to price the colonial language highest, and the child moves between the two valuations for the whole of a school career. The play sits inside the tension as a minor but exact gauge. Where English is a gatekeeping subject, its literature is trimmed to what the gate requires; where English is a formation, the whole play can still be found on the list.

## The Policy Pendulum: Language Formulas After Independence

Independent India inherited the Macaulay settlement and spent seventy years renegotiating its terms without ever quite repealing it. The constitutional settlement made Hindi the union's official language with English continuing in an associate role, and the education commissions of the republic then built the structure whose effects the board-by-board record above displays. The Education Commission of 1964 to 1966, the Kothari Commission, recommended the three-language formula that the National Policy on Education of 1968 adopted: a student would learn the regional language or mother tongue, Hindi, and English, or in Hindi-speaking states a modern Indian language in Hindi's place. The formula's design was federal and conciliatory, and its consequence for the subject this article tracks was decisive: English was entrenched as the link language and the library language of the system, a compulsory subject in nearly every state's schools, while remaining, for most children, a subject studied through the mother tongue rather than the medium of their other learning. Shakespeare's position inside that settlement is exactly the position the documents show. The subject that carries him is universal; the literature within the subject is rationed, because a subject designed to deliver a working language to hundreds of millions cannot also deliver whole plays to all of them.

The state boards, which educate the large majority of Indian schoolchildren, administer that rationing in twenty-eight state variants and the variants of the union territories, each with its own textbook society, its own English readers and its own settlement between the language's utility and its literature. Karnataka's Springs, with its two speeches and three marks, is one state's published answer; the NCERT readers' silence is the union's. Neither answer was imposed by any successor of Macaulay. Both were chosen by Indian boards under Indian policies for Indian classrooms, which is the final complication the verdict must absorb. The unevenness of the play's school presence in India is no longer a colonial artefact, if it ever wholly was. It is the maintained, repeatedly re-chosen outcome of a federal system that guarantees English the subject everywhere and English literature almost nowhere, reserving the whole play for the universities the way the examination universities of 1857 first reserved it. The pendulum that began in 1835, between the imperial installation and the Indian appropriation, has settled into a structure; and a structure, unlike an instrument, has no single pair of hands on it.

## Verdict: The Instrument Changed Hands, Unevenly

The disagreement this article set out to adjudicate can be scored against the documents. Gauri Viswanathan argued that English literary study was built in India as an instrument of colonial control, a mask for exploitation. For the founding period, her case is conclusive, and it is conclusive because the founders said so: the Minute's own sentences describe a class to be manufactured in taste, opinion and morals, and the examination universities then built the manufacturing plant. Poonam Trivedi and the scholars of India's Shakespeare answer that the instrument was seized: translated into Bengali prose within thirteen years of the first student performance, staged by an Indian Othello in 1848, edited for Indian students by Indian-based editors, and finally archived, judged and taught by Indian scholarship as an object with an Indian history. For the stage, the translation record and the universities, her case is also conclusive, and the Narendrapur syllabus, examining the Minute itself beside the canon, is its neatest single proof: a curriculum that studies its own instrumentality has changed hands by definition. Ania Loomba's caution adjudicates between them at the level where both are weakest. The seizure of the play is real and the mask's design is real, but the medium in which both happened, English, still rations its own mastery, and at school level the rationing governs what of the play a child receives: for the largest board, nothing; for a state board's pre-university course, two speeches and a summary.

The play itself supplies the closing measure, as it supplied the opening one. Verona's Prince ends the tragedy by counting the cost of the houses' inheritance: "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love." An inherited curriculum, like an inherited feud, is not chosen by those it governs; it is only, at best, recognised, audited and redirected. The Indian record shows all three operations performed on this play across two centuries: recognised by the critics who named its function, audited by the scholars who wrote its Indian history, redirected by the committees that kept it whole at degree level and by the translators who made it Bengali before it was a generation old in the classroom. What the record does not show is the redirection completed at the base of the system, where the play still arrives, when it arrives, as an anthology of praise-speeches inside a language examination. The verdict therefore stands in the graded form the evidence earned. In India's universities, Romeo and Juliet is an Indian text by re-election and by scholarship, its colonial installation a studied fact rather than a living sentence. In India's schools, the installation outlives its empire in the one place the empire built best: the examination, which decides, state by state and board by board, whether a student meets the lovers whole, meets their two most beautiful speeches, or meets only their reputation. The instrument changed hands. The hands at the top of the system hold the whole play. The hands at the bottom are still being given extracts.

A final precision belongs to the record, because the graded verdict can be misread as a complaint, and it is not one. India's arrangement, whole play at university and rationed literature below, is the arrangement of most of the world; the English GCSE's guarantee is the exception, not the norm, and the play's American school career, treated elsewhere in this run, is an archipelago of individual district choices rather than a national settlement. What distinguishes India is not the unevenness but the documentation and the self-knowledge: no other system on earth examines its own candidates on the history of the curriculum that examines them, sets the founding memorandum of that curriculum as undergraduate reading, and sustains a scholarly literature that treats a school text as a two-hundred-year-old national argument. The play arrived as an instrument of rule. The rule ended. The argument the instrument started, about language, caste, marriage and who may read what, continues in the classrooms the instrument built, and the play, whole or in speeches, is still the pretext for holding it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: When did Shakespeare first enter an Indian classroom?

Shakespeare entered Indian classrooms at Hindu College, Calcutta, founded in 1817 to teach Hindu boys English together with European literature and science. The teaching there predates the famous policy documents: Henry Louis Vivian Derozio taught English literature at the college in the late 1820s, and David Lester Richardson made the plays a speciality of his teaching in the following decade. By 1837 the classroom had produced a performance, with Hindu College students staging scenes from The Merchant of Venice under Richardson's encouragement. The college therefore stands at the head of the record: the dramatist was being taught, and performed, in Calcutta two years after Macaulay's Minute of 1835 proposed the system that would spread the teaching across the subcontinent.

### Q: What did Macaulay's Minute of 1835 actually decide?

The Minute, dated 2 February 1835, settled a funding dispute over the one lakh of rupees the Charter Act of 1813 had set aside for education in India. Thomas Macaulay, as president of the General Committee of Public Instruction, argued that the money should fund English education and English literature rather than Sanskrit and Arabic learning, declaring a single shelf of a good European library worth the whole native literature of India and Arabia. He proposed forming "a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect", to serve as interpreters between the rulers and the governed. Governor General William Bentinck endorsed the argument, and a resolution of 7 March 1835 redirected the funds accordingly.

### Q: Why does Gauri Viswanathan call English literature a mask of conquest?

In Masks of Conquest: Literary Study and British Rule in India, published in 1989, Gauri Viswanathan argued that the literary curriculum was the consent-building part of colonial rule. On her account, drawn from Gramsci's theory of hegemony, English literature functioned as a mask that camouflaged the agenda of economic and material exploitation behind the humane activity of literary cultivation. She also established the chronological scandal at the argument's core: English literature became an organised, examined discipline in colonial India before it was institutionalised in England, because the colony needed the curriculum as administration. Her claim is presented in scholarship as an argument, and later work, including studies of the secular humanism taught at Hindu College, has qualified its reach, but it remains the standard frame for the discipline's Indian beginning.

### Q: Is Romeo and Juliet prescribed for CBSE students?

No Shakespeare play is prescribed in the English course of the Central Board of Secondary Education. The NCERT readers for Classes 9 to 12, Beehive, First Flight, Hornbill and Flamingo, with their supplementary volumes, carry stories, essays, poems and speeches but no drama by Shakespeare, and Romeo and Juliet appears nowhere in the prescribed course. The absence is structural: in the central board's design English is examined as a language subject, for comprehension and composition, rather than as a literature subject built on complete texts. A student in the central board's system who meets the play before university meets the story through reputation, summary or film, while the whole text waits in other boards' syllabi and in the universities.

### Q: Which Indian school textbook prints extracts from Romeo and Juliet?

The documented case is Karnataka's. Springs, the English course book prescribed by the Karnataka School Examination and Assessment Board for the second year of the state's pre-university course, opens with a chapter titled for the play. The chapter prints extracts rather than the whole text: a prose summary of the story is followed by two passages of verse, Romeo's speech at the Capulet feast in the fifth scene of the first act and Juliet's invocation to the night in the second scene of the third act. The board's blueprint for the course allots the unit three teaching hours and three marks, and the questions set on it are questions of reference and paraphrase about the printed speeches.

### Q: Which passages of the play does Karnataka's Springs textbook carry?

Two speeches carry the whole unit. The first is Romeo's speech on first seeing Juliet at the Capulet feast, in the fifth scene of the first act, beginning "O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright!" and running through the images of the rich jewel in an Ethiop's ear and the snowy dove among crows to the admission that he never saw true beauty until that night. The second is Juliet's invocation from the second scene of the third act, "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds", with its wish that Romeo, when she is dead, be cut out in little stars. Both are lyrics of praise; neither passage contains the feud, the marriage bargaining or the deaths, which the textbook's prose summary reports instead.

### Q: Does the ICSE examination set Romeo and Juliet as its drama text?

In the documented cycles, no. The Council for the Indian School Certificate Examinations does institutionalise the whole-play Shakespeare that the colonial examination tradition began, but the play occupying the drama slot rotates. At the secondary level the ICSE prescription across recent cycles has been The Merchant of Venice, served by Indian-published annotated editions aligned to the council's specimen papers. At the senior level the ISC list for the 2024 examination set The Tempest for Class 11, and the ISC list for the 2013 examination offered Macbeth among its compulsory-text options. Romeo and Juliet belongs, in the council's system, to the penumbra of famous Shakespeare rather than to the examined text: the institution preserves the form, while this particular play is not its recorded occupant.

### Q: Which Indian university syllabi name Romeo and Juliet as a set text?

The documented prescriptions span the system's levels and regions. Jamia Millia Islamia in Delhi sets the whole play as the third unit of English Drama I in the second semester of its BA Honours English degree, between Jonson's The Alchemist and The Tempest. Assam University's British Drama course for its four-year undergraduate programme lists the play with Baz Luhrmann's 1996 film as the following unit. Fatima College in Madurai gives the play a twenty-two-hour unit, labelled tragic romance, in the fifth-semester Shakespeare paper of its BA English degree. The Central University of Andhra Pradesh sets it at postgraduate level in its MA English drama unit. Ramakrishna Mission Residential College, Narendrapur, offers it among the Shakespeare options of its British Drama core course.

### Q: When was the story of Romeo and Juliet first rendered into an Indian language?

The story reached Bengali print in 1848, when Gurudas Hajra's prose rendering of the tale was published in Calcutta. A prose rendering is not a translation of the play's verse; it is the plot detached from the poetry, made available to readers of Bengali outside the English classroom, and it stands at the head of the long lineage of Indian-language retellings through which most Indian readers have met the lovers. The full dramatic adaptation followed within the same generation: Charumukh Chitrahara Natak, a Bengali stage adaptation of the play, is dated 1864 in the scholarly accounts of the Bengali Shakespeare tradition, which also record Harachandra Ghosh's adaptation of The Merchant of Venice and Vidyasagar's prose Comedy of Errors of 1869.

### Q: Who was the first Indian actor to play a Shakespearean tragic hero?

Baishnav Charan Addy, also spelled Bostom Churn Addy in the contemporary record, played Othello at the Sans Souci Theatre in Calcutta on 17 August and again on 12 September 1848. The performance is the landmark recorded in Sukanta Chaudhuri and Ananda Lal's checklist of Shakespeare on the Calcutta stage: eleven years after Hindu College students first performed scenes from the plays, an Indian actor had taken the tragic lead of the English repertoire in a public theatre, in English, before a European audience. The Calcutta newspaper the Bengal Hurkaru praised his pronunciation of English as, for a native, remarkably good, a compliment that preserves the colonial classroom's standard even as the event escaped it: the syllabus's pupil had become the stage's protagonist.

### Q: What is India's Shakespeare (2005), and what does its Romeo and Juliet chapter document?

India's Shakespeare: Translation, Interpretation, and Performance, edited by Poonam Trivedi and Dennis Bartholomeusz and published by the University of Delaware Press in 2005, is the standard scholarly collection on the plays' Indian career. Its framing describes the interaction between the imported canon and its Indian receivers as a matrix of simultaneous submission and resistance, and its essays document how the plays were taught, translated, adapted and performed across the subcontinent. Its chapter on Romeo and Juliet in modern Indian disseminations tracks this play's passage through Indian languages, stages and screens. Trivedi, a Shakespeare scholar at the University of Delhi, assembled in the volume the archive on which the appropriation side of the curriculum debate rests.

### Q: Why do Verona's arranged-marriage scenes carry a different charge in Indian classrooms?

Because the institution at the play's centre is not foreign there. Capulet bargains his daughter to Paris while conceding that his will is only part of the matter of her consent; her refusal is answered with the threat that she may hang, beg, starve and die in the streets; Juliet's rebellion is a demand to deny her father and refuse her name. In a society where arranged marriage, caste endogamy, community honour and parental authority remain live arrangements, debated in newspapers and courts, these scenes require no antiquarian reconstruction. The play is foreign in its language and domestic in its sociology, and the friction between those two conditions is the distinctive material of the Indian classroom's work on it, as the published Indian scholarship on the play records.

### Q: What does Jyotsna Singh mean by "different Shakespeares"?

In her essay "Different Shakespeares", published in the collection Post-Colonial Shakespeares that Ania Loomba and Martin Orkin edited in 1998, Jyotsna Singh argued that the Shakespeare produced by colonial India and the Shakespeare remade after independence are effectively different authors, made by different institutions for different purposes. The colonial Shakespeare was an instrument of the education system that installed him: a standard of language, taste and loyalty. The postcolonial Shakespeare is the product of Indian translation, adaptation, performance and criticism, a figure rebuilt by the culture that received him. The phrase has become a shorthand for the argument that a canon in transit is not one object but a succession of objects, each manufactured by the institutions that prescribe, teach and perform it.

### Q: How did the universities founded in 1857 change the play's Indian life?

The universities of Calcutta, Bombay and Madras, founded in 1857, were examining bodies: they prescribed texts, affiliated colleges and tested students, while teaching happened elsewhere. Prescription by an examining university is the most consequential event in a text's curriculum life. It guarantees a readership renewed every academic year, it creates an industry of student editions, annotations and model answers, and it binds a professional class of teachers to the prescribed list. Shakespeare stood at the head of the English curriculum from the universities' first decades, and the play stood among the prescribed texts through which generations of Indian graduates passed. The examination form also fixed the division that still governs the play's Indian career: the whole text at degree level, extracts and summaries below it.

### Q: What does the UGC NET syllabus ask about English in India?

The syllabus for the University Grants Commission's National Eligibility Test in English, the examination that licenses university teachers of the subject, includes a unit titled "English in India: history, evolution and futures". The unit places the language's Indian career, its colonial installation, its postcolonial development and its contested future, inside the certifying knowledge of the discipline itself. The consequence is structural: a candidate cannot be licensed to teach English literature at an Indian university without being examined on the history of the discipline's arrival, including the policy of 1835 that founded it. Alongside it, the Narendrapur degree syllabus that sets Macaulay's Minute as background reading shows the same reflexivity at undergraduate level: the subject studies the document that created it.

### Q: What did the National Education Policy of 2020 direct about the language of classroom instruction?

The policy's fourth chapter directs that, wherever possible, the medium of instruction until at least Grade 5, but preferably till Grade 8 and beyond, will be the home language, mother tongue, local language or regional language. The direction concerns the language in which all subjects begin, not the contents of any literature syllabus, and its significance for the play's curriculum is indirect but deep: it reopens, from the opposite end, the question Macaulay answered in 1835. Macaulay decided which language and literature would form an interpreter class; the 2020 policy decides in which language the forming of every child should start. Between those two decisions lies the whole history of English in Indian education, and the play's place inside it.

### Q: Why did a Dalit campaigner build a temple to the English language?

On 30 April 2010 the foundation was laid at Bankagaon village in Lakhimpur Kheri district, Uttar Pradesh, for a temple to the Dalit Goddess English. The campaigner behind it was Chandra Bhan Prasad, a Dalit writer and columnist. The idol was modelled on the Statue of Liberty, holding a pen raised in one hand and books in the other, standing on a computer. Prasad celebrates Macaulay's birthday each year and is reported calling the author of the 1835 Minute a social revolutionary. The temple's argument is that English, the language of the colonial curriculum, became for communities excluded from Sanskrit learning by the caste order a ladder: a language belonging to no caste and no region, with which the excluded could address the state and one another.

### Q: How many teaching hours does Karnataka allot to the play's extracts?

Three. The blueprint issued by the Karnataka School Examination and Assessment Board for the second-year pre-university English course allots the Romeo and Juliet unit in the Springs course book three teaching hours and three marks. The unit consists of two passages, Romeo's feast speech from the fifth scene of the first act and Juliet's invocation to the night from the second scene of the third act, preceded by a prose summary of the story. The arithmetic is the sharpest single measure of the play's school-level position in India: one of the most famous texts in the language, in the one state textbook that prints it, is worth three hours of teaching and three marks of examination, against the twenty-two hours a Madurai degree syllabus gives the whole play. Set against the three marks, those twenty-two hours measure the distance between the school extract and the university text in the system's own units of account: time allotted, credit awarded, attention commanded.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch India-curriculum guide to argue that the instrument changed hands unevenly?

Begin with the guide's university rows, where the change of hands is documented rather than asserted: whole-play prescriptions renewed by Indian committees within the last decade at Jamia, Assam, Madurai and Anantapur, and a degree syllabus that sets Macaulay's Minute itself beside the canon as an object of study. Then descend the table. Show the council system preserving the colonial examination form while rotating other plays through its drama slot, and the Karnataka row, where the play survives as two speeches inside a language examination. End with the guide's silence: the national board's readers, which contain no play at all. The unevenness is the argument. The instrument changed hands where Indian institutions elect and examine the whole text; it merely changed custodians where the text is an extract, and it never arrived where the syllabus is silent.
