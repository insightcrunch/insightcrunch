In September 1973, in a stadium in Santiago that had been converted into a detention centre, guards recognised a singer among the prisoners because fellow detainees had been singing his repertoire in the cells, passing verses from bunk to bunk in the dark without paper, instruments or rehearsal. That single documented scene contains the whole argument of music and politics history in miniature. A repertoire learned by ear had crossed a prison wall that printed leaflets could not cross, it had assembled frightened strangers into something resembling a public, and it had done so in a form that its captors could hear without being able to confiscate. The thesis defended across the sections that follow is plain. Musical sound gains political force less from the propositions its words state than from two older capacities: it gathers bodies into one place and one tempo, and it lodges in memory among people who may never read a pamphlet. States grasped both capacities early, which is why governments across centuries spent comparable energy paying for performance and forbidding it.

## Why music is politically potent: music and politics history in practice

### How does music influence politics?

Music influences politics by gathering people into shared time, fixing words and loyalties in memory without print, and carrying meanings that listeners can deny when challenged. A crowd singing together experiences agreement physically before anyone argues for it, and a melody learned in childhood can transport a slogan, a grievance or a national story across decades and borders intact.

Part of the potency is physiological before it is ideological. Singing together synchronises breathing, and marching or dancing to a shared pulse synchronises movement, so a gathering organised around sound produces a felt unity that a speech must work much harder to create. Political organisers in many periods described the same discovery in different vocabularies: a meeting that opened with communal singing held its audience longer, raised money more readily and sent people home repeating phrases they had arrived not knowing. The mechanism does not favour any cause. A hymn, a union chorus, a party anthem and a football chant all exploit the same property, which is why an honest account of the subject has to describe the machinery before it judges any particular use of it, and why the machinery itself, rather than the virtue of causes, is the subject here.

A second part of the potency is asymmetry of effort. A political argument must be composed, printed, distributed and defended, and each stage can be interrupted. A tune requires none of that infrastructure once it is lodged in a community. It can be hummed by a person who owns nothing, taught in an evening, altered in transit and recognised from three notes. Dorian Lynskey, the music journalist whose reporting on protest repertoire appeared in British newspapers during the first decade of the twenty first century, observed that protest repertoire survives precisely because it asks so little of its carriers: no printing press, no hall, no permission. That low threshold explains a pattern repeated in the sections ahead, in which authorities who could suppress newspapers, parties and unions found themselves still policing singing, because singing was the one medium their opponents could produce from memory alone.

A third part is emotional licensing. Public life in most societies restricts what may be said plainly, by law, by custom or by fear, yet permits a surprising range of feeling inside performance. Grief that would be seditious in a speech can be sung at a funeral. Mockery that would end a career in print can pass as entertainment on a stage. Longing for a lost country can be expressed as nostalgia for a landscape. Performance therefore becomes a licensed zone where a community rehearses sentiments it cannot yet act on, and rehearsal matters politically because sentiments rehearsed together for years are ready made when circumstances change. When a movement suddenly needs a crowd, it rarely invents one from nothing. It summons the crowd that concerts, services, dances and commemorations had already taught to assemble and to feel in unison.

None of this makes sound a reliable servant of change, and the evidence sections near the end weigh cases where vast musical mobilisation accompanied political failure. Potency is a capacity, not a guarantee. A reservoir is potent whether or not anyone opens the sluice. What the historical record shows consistently is that rulers, parties, churches and movements behaved as if the capacity were real: they budgeted for it, legislated about it, exiled its practitioners and sometimes killed them. Behaviour of that kind, repeated across unrelated regimes, is itself evidence. Institutions rarely spend money and violence on things they believe to be harmless decoration, and the scale of state attention paid to performers, from court chapels to broadcasting ministries, is the first fact any theory of the subject has to explain.

## Memorability without literacy

A melody is a storage technology. Long before cheap print, and in many places long after print arrived, verse set to a tune was the most durable way an illiterate population could hold a text steady across generations. The tune disciplines the words: a syllable that drifts breaks the metre and is corrected by the next singer, so the pairing of melody and text acts as an error checking system that prose memory lacks. Ballads carried news of battles, executions, harvest failures and scandals across early modern Europe in this form, sold as broadsides where print existed and carried by ear where it did not. Coroners, magistrates and censors all understood that a sung account of an event could outrun and outlast the official one, and prosecutions of ballad singers appear in records from several countries as attempts to police a news medium, not to police entertainment.

Religious traditions systematised the same principle. Congregational hymn singing in the Protestant churches turned doctrine into something a ploughman could retain entire, and reformers such as Martin Luther treated congregational song as a teaching instrument of the first rank, commissioning and adapting melodies precisely because a congregation that sang its theology weekly could not easily be argued out of it. The Catholic response included its own investment in memorable chant and vernacular devotion. In West African traditions, praise singing carried genealogy and political commentary in societies where the griot held an recognised office as keeper of lineage memory. In each case the political content rode on the mnemonic vehicle: who a community remembered, and in what moral light, was partly decided by what its singers could make memorable.

The arrival of recording changed the scale without changing the principle. A record could fix one performance and repeat it identically in a million rooms, which strengthened memory while narrowing its local variation. Radio multiplied the effect again, because a broadcast chorus could be learned simultaneously across a whole territory. Yet the older ear based system persisted wherever states controlled print and airwaves, because memory cannot be raided. Samizdat literature in the Soviet Union could be confiscated in a search; a repertoire learned in a kitchen could not. Dissident songwriters in several authoritarian states relied on exactly this property, performing in private apartments to audiences who carried the repertoire away in their heads. The political history of memorability is therefore also a history of enforcement limits: every medium of dissent except memorised song leaves an artefact that can be seized, and that single asymmetry recurs wherever suppression is attempted.

There is a cost attached to the vehicle, and honest treatment requires naming it. What memory holds best is the simple, the repetitive and the emotionally charged, so a sung tradition tends to flatten complicated events into heroes, villains and betrayals. Names survive; qualifications do not. A community may remember for a century that an ancestor was wronged while forgetting the treaty terms, the divided loyalties and the compromises that surrounded the wrong. Political actors have exploited that flattening deliberately, choosing the version of an event that sings well over the version that happened in full. Memorability is thus a double edged inheritance: it preserves the past for people excluded from archives, and it preserves a simplified past that skilled performers can aim at present quarrels like a loaded heirloom.

## Assembly and the crowd

Performance gathers. That banal fact has structural consequences, because in many legal systems assembly itself was the regulated act. Where public meetings required permission, a concert, a dance, a funeral procession or a religious service could provide lawful cover for gathering the same people who were forbidden to meet as a political body. Authorities recognised the substitution and repeatedly legislated against its forms: restrictions on drumming in slave holding societies in the Americas, bans on funeral gatherings in apartheid era South Africa, licensing of dance halls and coffee houses in colonial cities, and permit systems for street performance all belong to one family of measures aimed at the crowd that performance convenes. The crowd is the raw material of politics, since votes, armies, congregations and markets are all crowds organised for a purpose, and any technology that assembles people cheaply will attract the attention of whoever governs them.

The crowd that performance assembles is also rehearsed in coordination. An audience that has clapped, marched, danced or sung in time together has practised acting as one body under direction, following cues from a leader on a platform. Political scientists describing mobilisation often separate organisation from emotion, but a choir, a brass band or a dance floor fuses the two: it is an organisation you can feel. Labour movements understood this and built choirs and bands into their institutions, not as decoration for rallies but as the rally in embryo. Churches understood it earlier. Armies understood it earliest of all, which is why military music is the oldest continuously state funded performance tradition in the record, maintained because commanders testified that men moved, charged and held formation better inside shared rhythm than outside it.

Assembly also creates a temporary public with its own norms. Inside a concert or a carnival, hierarchies can loosen: the servant dances, the official is mocked in a licensed song, strangers address one another. Anthropologists describe such occasions as liminal, a threshold state where ordinary rank is suspended, and rulers have alternately sponsored that suspension as a safety valve and feared it as a rehearsal for disorder. Carnival in Trinidad, charivari in France, and the mumming traditions of the British Isles all carried recognised elements of inversion, in which the low judged the high in costume and rhyme. Whether inversion defused resentment or trained it is a genuine historical dispute with evidence on both sides, and it is treated here as an open question rather than a settled one, because the same festival in different decades can be found doing each.

Scale completes the picture. A village gathering assembles dozens; a stadium concert assembles tens of thousands; a broadcast assembles millions who never share a room yet share a moment. Each enlargement changes the politics. The stadium crowd can be photographed and counted, becoming an argument in itself: a regime displays its rallies, an opposition displays its benefit concert, and the size of the assembly is reported as a measure of support. The broadcast crowd cannot be counted in the same way, but it can be synchronised, so that a whole population hears the same performance at the same hour and knows that everyone else heard it too. That mutual knowledge, the sense of a nation listening together, is one of the strongest political effects performance can produce, and state broadcasters were built to manufacture it deliberately.

## Coded meaning and deniability

Where open statement is dangerous, meaning migrates into forms that can be disowned. A song can carry a second sense understood by its intended audience and invisible, or at least unprovable, to an overhearing authority. The mechanisms vary. A lyric can use a place name that stands for a political destination, a biblical journey that stands for escape, a lover who stands for a country, or a historical ballad whose villain shares a living ruler's habits closely enough for listeners to draw the line themselves. Instrumental performance can code meaning without words at all: a banned anthem quoted inside a symphony, a folk melody associated with a suppressed nation woven into an official concert, a rhythm or instrumentation that signals allegiance to listeners who share the code. The performer, if challenged, can retreat to the innocent reading. The melody is old. The words are about a river. The resemblance is coincidence. Deniability is not a decorative extra on such performance; it is the engineering that lets the message exist at all.

Richard Taruskin, the musicologist whose Text and Act of 1995 and whose Oxford History of Western Music of 2005 treated political readings of repertoire with sustained scepticism, warned against the critic's temptation to find codes everywhere. A resemblance between a melody and a banned tune may be accident; a composer's private programme may be a later invention by admirers; an audience may hear defiance in music its maker intended as entertainment. Taruskin's caution is the necessary counterweight in this section and throughout the article: coded meaning is real, and it is also the easiest of all musical claims to fabricate after the fact, because the code's very secrecy excuses the absence of documents. The disciplined position is to distinguish three grades of case. In the strongest, the code is documented by the people who used it, in letters, memoirs or testimony. In the middle grade, contemporaries reported hearing the second meaning at the time. In the weakest, the code was first alleged decades later by writers with a story to sell. Political history written from the third grade alone is mythology with footnotes.

The spirituals sung by enslaved people in the United States supply the most examined strong grade cases, and also the clearest lesson in grading. Some songs demonstrably carried layered meaning in a community where biblical imagery of exodus and deliverance mapped onto lived experience, and testimony from formerly enslaved people confirms that hearers understood more than overseers did. At the same time, tidy later claims that particular songs functioned as literal maps or signal systems rest on much thinner evidence, and responsible historians separate the confirmed layering of meaning from the decorative precision added by later retelling. The distinction matters beyond one tradition. Coded performance worked because it was genuinely ambiguous, and ambiguity that protects a singer also frustrates a historian. Both facts have to be held together.

Coded meaning also operates in the open societies, in milder form. Commercial performers under contract, broadcasters under regulators and artists dependent on sponsors all learn to phrase dissent at a level of generality that cannot be pinned to a target. A song against war in general, against greed in general, against the powerful in general, collects agreement from listeners who would quarrel over any specific application. Critics of such performance call it safe rebellion; defenders reply that generality is what lets a repertoire travel across factions and decades. Robin Denselow, whose survey When the Music's Over of 1989 traced political pop across the postwar decades, documented both the reach of generalised protest and its bluntness, noting how readily generalised dissent could be absorbed by the very industries it criticised. The code, in commercial conditions, protects the performer not from prison but from the market, and the mechanism is otherwise the same: say it so that it can be heard two ways, and let each audience choose its hearing.

## Church and state sponsorship

Sponsorship is the older half of the story, and the half that polite histories of protest tend to skip. Before any state bothered to censor a song, it had already spent centuries paying singers. The medieval and early modern court maintained a chapel because the ruler's piety had to be audible: elaborate polyphony at mass displayed devotion, wealth and technical command in a single act, and visiting ambassadors reported home on the quality of a prince's choir as they reported on his artillery. Cathedrals and collegiate churches trained generations of composers inside institutions whose first purpose was worship and whose second was the representation of power. Josquin, Lassus, Byrd and Bach all worked inside patronage structures in which the employer's theology and prestige set the terms of employment. None of that makes the resulting repertoire propaganda in the crude sense. It makes it something more durable: a demonstration, repeated weekly, that the existing order could command beauty, discipline and learning, and could gather them under one roof at an appointed hour.

Sponsorship also purchased reach. A ruler who funded a chapel choir was buying a sound that subjects encountered at coronations, funerals, processions and feast days, the calendar of occasions through which authority renewed itself in public. Processional music turned a street into a theatre of rank, with each estate in its place and the whole hierarchy moving to a measured tread. The political work was done less by any text than by the experience of order made audible: many voices, distinct parts, one coordinated result under direction. Defenders of such institutions described them as images of a well governed commonwealth. Critics of the same institutions, from iconoclasts who smashed organs during the English Civil War to revolutionaries who closed church choirs, understood the symbolism just as clearly and attacked the instruments as political objects, not as furniture.

The modern state inherited the habit and enlarged its budget. Ministries of culture, state orchestras, national opera houses, military band establishments, song and dance ensembles and broadcasting orchestras together employed more musicians in the twentieth century than all the courts of earlier centuries combined. Motives were mixed and were stated openly: national prestige abroad, education at home, employment for conservatoire graduates, preservation of folk traditions that officials feared were vanishing, and the cultivation of a shared repertory that citizens of different regions would recognise as theirs. A folk ensemble arranged village dances for the stage and in doing so taught urban audiences a curated version of their own countryside. A national orchestra touring abroad served as an embassy that performed instead of negotiating. To call all of this propaganda flattens real differences between a coercive ministry demanding odes to a leader and a municipal orchestra playing subscription concerts. The sections on authoritarian rule take up the coercive end. The present point is the continuum: every sponsorship carries expectations, and the interesting questions are what the expectations were, who enforced them and what happened to performers who declined them.

Sponsorship by churches followed a parallel logic with a longer memory. Missionary organisations carried hymnody, notation and brass instruments across Africa, Asia and the Pacific, intending conversion and often achieving a musical transformation larger than the religious one, as local musicians absorbed the imported harmony and instruments into their own idioms. Within Europe and the Americas, denominations policed their own sponsored sound, disputing organs, choirs, dance rhythms and vernacular texts in controversies that were simultaneously theological and social, since the party that controlled congregational singing controlled the one text the whole congregation memorised. When a state and a church sponsored rival repertories, ordinary singers could find their voices claimed by two authorities at once, and the friction generated some of the most durable popular repertories on record, from metrical psalms sung against episcopal instruction to gospel styles carried out of storefront churches into commercial studios.

## The national anthem as an invention

### Why do countries have national anthems?

Countries have national anthems because a modern state needs a short, repeatable ceremony in which strangers can perform unity on demand. An anthem gives officials, soldiers, schoolchildren and crowds the same few phrases and the same tune, so loyalty can be displayed together at borders, battles, funerals and games without negotiation or literacy.

The anthem feels ancient and is mostly recent. A handful of melodies acquired national associations early through court or military use, but the general institution, one authorised song standing for the whole state and sung by citizens on civic occasions, was assembled during the long nineteenth century as mass politics, conscript armies and compulsory schooling created a need for portable loyalty. Revolutionary France demonstrated the form's power when a marching song composed in Strasbourg in 1792 spread from army to army faster than any decree, carried by its own singability until the state adopted what the soldiers had already chosen. The lesson was not lost on other governments. A song the people select themselves binds more strongly than a song imposed by ordinance, so successful anthems often began as popular or military favourites that authority ratified after the fact, then surrounded with law, etiquette and schoolroom repetition until the adoption looked like immemorial custom.

An anthem does several jobs at once, and separating them explains its design. It must be singable by untrained voices, which keeps its range narrow and its phrases short. It must be recognisable in a fragment, because bands play it at arrivals and medal ceremonies where seconds matter. It must be solemn enough for funerals and stirring enough for battle, an emotional double duty that pushes its texts toward landscape, sacrifice and collective destiny rather than policy. And it must be ownable: its words name the country, its dead and its enemies in terms broad enough to survive changes of government. Anthems that tied themselves to a dynasty or a party have repeatedly been discarded with the regime, while anthems addressed to rivers, mountains and ancestors have been retained by republics that overthrew the monarchies which adopted them. States choose, in effect, between a song about themselves and a song about the land, and the second choice ages better.

Controversies around anthems are therefore diagnostic: they show exactly where a state's story about itself is contested. Disputes over verses that celebrate victories over neighbours, over texts written by authors later disgraced, over melodies borrowed from another nation's song, and over whether citizens may refuse to stand or sing, all turn on the same underlying question of who the anthem's we includes. Germany's postwar retention of a melody by Haydn with only the third verse of its associated text sung officially is a case of surgical editing, keeping the tune's dignity while retiring words that named expansion. South Africa's post 1994 anthem, which joins material from a hymn long sung by the liberation movement with material from the former state anthem in several languages, is a case of deliberate splicing, an audible negotiated settlement. Both solutions concede the premise of this section: the anthem is an instrument of statecraft, revised when the state revises itself.

A shareable fact belongs here, stated plainly. The melody of the British anthem was at various times used with loyal words in several other European states, and the tune of the German imperial anthem was shared with Britain's, so that two rival courts once honoured their sovereigns to the same notes with different texts. Melodies migrate more easily than loyalties, and an anthem's tune alone proves nothing about national uniqueness. What makes an anthem national is not composition but adoption: law, custom, schooling and repetition consecrating one performance as the state's voice. That is why anthem disputes are never really about music. They are about whether the consecration still commands consent, and they flare whenever consent is being renegotiated for other reasons.

## Nineteenth century nationalism

During the nineteenth century, performance became one of the principal workshops in which nations were imagined into being, and the workshop metaphor is precise: nations were built, by identifiable people, with materials at hand. Folklorists collected peasant songs and published them as evidence that a people possessed a distinct soul older than any empire ruling it. Composers built operas and symphonic poems on national legends, national dances and national landscapes. Choral societies multiplying across German, Scandinavian, Baltic and Slavic lands gave thousands of amateurs the experience of singing the nation into a hall together. Where a people lacked a state, as Poles, Finns, Czechs and Norwegians variously did, cultural nation building preceded political independence and supplied its vocabulary, its heroes and its martyrs. A song festival could assemble a crowd that a political congress could not, and more than one national movement used the festival as its parliament in disguise.

The mechanisms deserve specification, because national music is often described as if it seeped from the soil. In practice it was manufactured by editorial choices. A collector decided which village singer represented the authentic tradition and which town influences counted as corruption. An arranger harmonised a melody in a style learned in Leipzig or Vienna and thereby made the folk sound respectable to the urban audience whose support the movement needed. A composer quoted a dance rhythm, a modal turn or an epic subject and was praised for national character by critics who had been primed to listen for it. Richard Taruskin, in his Oxford History of Western Music of 2005, treated national character in music as largely a matter of such priming: audiences hear nationality in a score when they have been told to listen for it, and the same devices pass unnoticed in a composer assigned to a different nation. That scepticism does not dissolve the phenomenon. It relocates it, from the notes themselves to the institutions, schools, festivals, publishing houses and critical vocabularies, that taught listeners what to hear.

Opera carried the heaviest load, because opera combined mass spectacle, vernacular language and historical subject matter in one expensive public ritual. Italian audiences under Austrian rule in the peninsula turned performances into occasions for patriotic demonstration, attaching political meaning to choruses of exiles and captives whose stage situations mirrored the audience's own. The strength of particular legends about those evenings has been debated by historians, and some famous anecdotes shrink under documentary scrutiny, but the documented core remains: censors vetted libretti line by line, police attended performances, demonstrations at the opera were recorded in official reports, and composers adjusted their work to a political atmosphere everyone present understood. Verdi's name itself became a political acronym in graffiti celebrating the prospective king of a united Italy, a use the composer did not invent and could not control, which anticipates the later sections on performers made into symbols against their intentions.

Nationalism in performance also had an imperial mirror image. The same century that produced liberation repertories produced the musical apparatus of empire: regimental bands on colonial parade grounds, mission hymns in colonial schools, exhibitions in European capitals where colonised musicians performed for metropolitan crowds as specimens of the peoples their flags now ruled. The two repertories influenced each other constantly. Colonised musicians trained in imperial institutions carried notation, harmony and brass technique home and turned them to national purposes; metropolitan composers absorbed melodies brought back by soldiers and collectors and sold them as exotic colour. Any clean separation between the music of national liberation and the music of empire fails on contact with the personnel, who were frequently the same people at different postings. The honest summary is that nineteenth century institutions taught the whole world one connected set of musical technologies, anthem, band, choir, festival, conservatoire, and every political project of the following century, imperial, national and anti colonial, was built with those same tools.

## Composers conscripted by states

Conscription of composers rarely arrived as an order. It arrived as a livelihood. The composer who depended on state theatres, state publishers, state broadcasting and state prizes inhabited a structure in which certain subjects were commissioned, certain styles advanced a career and certain opinions ended one, and the structure did its work through anticipation more efficiently than through commands. A symphony celebrating an anniversary of the state, a cantata for a leader's birthday, an opera on an approved historical subject: these were the taxes ambitious composers paid in several twentieth century systems, and many paid them with genuine craft. The resulting works complicate every simple verdict. A commissioned celebration can contain passages of real power, because its composer was talented and because ceremonial writing was a mastered genre. Judging such a work requires separating three questions that polemic constantly merges: what the patron demanded, what the composer believed and what the notes accomplish. The record answers the first question often, the second rarely and the third only through listening that polemic discourages.

Different systems conscripted differently, and the differences matter for the case studies ahead. The Soviet system centralised patronage in unions that controlled commissions, performance and foreign travel, making the state effectively the only employer for large scale composition. The German National Socialist system worked through a chamber of music that licensed professional activity and excluded Jewish musicians from public musical life by administrative means, while courting internationally famous composers whose prestige it wished to rent. Fascist Italy cultivated modernists and traditionalists in parallel and demanded less stylistic conformity than either. Monarchies and republics of an older pattern conscripted through taste rather than doctrine: the court composer who wished to keep his post wrote for the court's occasions and kept his opinions out of his dedications. In every variant the state was buying the same commodity, the association of its authority with the prestige of serious composition, and composers were selling it at prices denominated in freedom of subject, freedom of style or freedom of speech, usually in that order.

The figure of [Richard Wagner](/2018/03/07/richard-wagner-life-and-music/) stands behind this whole subject, although he died half a century before the twentieth century systems matured. Wagner wrote political theory alongside his operas, attached himself to a revolution in Dresden in 1849 and fled into exile, then attached himself to a king whose patronage built him a theatre. His published writings included an essay attacking Jewish influence in music that later regimes quoted as scripture, and his works were ceremonially embraced by the National Socialist leadership, whose leader's admiration was documented and publicised. Wagner could not control any of that posthumous use, and scholars dispute how much of it his own writings invite. The case is the template for a recurring pattern examined repeatedly below: a composer's documented opinions, a later state's selective embrace, and a repertoire too widely loved to retire, locked together in an argument that each generation reopens because the evidence genuinely supports more than one emphasis.

Refusal had a price list, and it is worth recording that some composers paid it. Emigration was the classic refusal, chosen by Bartok, who left Hungary in 1940 in opposition to its alignment and its racial laws, and by the many German and Austrian musicians who rebuilt their lives in Britain and the United States after 1933. Internal refusal took quieter forms: the composer who wrote for the drawer, the teacher who lost a post and gave private lessons, the conductor who programmed a banned colleague's work and accepted the consequences. Such cases rarely dominate the histories, which prefer the dramatic extremes of collaboration and resistance, but they establish the range of real choices inside conscription systems. Most composers, like most citizens, occupied the middle ground of accommodation, writing what was required, saving their convictions for private works and conversation, and leaving behind a documentary record so mixed that later partisans can harvest it for either verdict. That mixed record, not villainy or heroism, is the normal product of conscription, and the Shostakovich case treated next is famous precisely because it concentrates the mixture in one conspicuous life.

## Music under authoritarian regimes

### Why do governments censor music?

Governments censor music because performance assembles crowds, fixes words in memory and travels across borders and literacy barriers faster than print. A regime that controls newspapers and parties but leaves singing alone has left open the one channel its opponents can produce from memory, so censorship follows performance wherever assembly itself is treated as a threat.

Authoritarian government of performance operates through a recognisable toolkit, and naming its instruments prevents the vague impression that censorship is merely a mood. Licensing decides who may work at all: a professional card, a union membership or a chamber registration stands between the musician and every stage, studio and classroom. Repertoire control decides what may be played: approved lists, vetted programmes, pre performance submission of texts and the quiet disappearance of works by disfavoured composers from catalogues and curricula. Personnel purge decides whose names vanish: Jewish musicians dismissed from German orchestras under racial law, Soviet composers denounced in print and dropped from programmes, South African performers banned from broadcast and from leaving or entering the country. Style doctrine decides how music may sound: campaigns against formalism in the Soviet Union, against jazz as racially and politically degenerate in Germany, against Western popular styles in several revolutionary states, each elevating a preferred sound as the audible image of the regime's values. The toolkit's completeness is the point. A regime does not need to ban every dissonant note if it controls employment, venues, broadcasting and borders, because musicians will censor themselves to eat.

Self censorship is therefore the largest single effect of authoritarian cultural policy and the hardest to document, since it consists of works not written, songs not sung and programmes not proposed. Memoirs from several systems describe the same mental arithmetic at the desk: will this subject endanger the premiere, the post, the passport? Pauline Fairclough, the scholar of Soviet musical life whose A Soviet Credo of 2006 examined how Shostakovich's Fifth Symphony was received and discussed inside the Soviet Union, showed how official interpretation worked to stabilise that arithmetic, supplying approved meanings that performers and audiences could repeat for safety. An approved meaning is a shelter as well as a cage. Once a symphony has been officially declared an optimistic reply to criticism, everyone involved can shelter behind the declaration while hearing in private whatever they need to hear. Authoritarian systems generate such shelters deliberately, because a culture in which every ambiguity is prosecuted would require more police than any state possesses.

Yet the toolkit never achieved the total control its designers announced, and the leaks are structural rather than accidental. Performance is live, variable and embodied: a tempo can drag in protest, an encore can be demanded until it becomes a demonstration, a folk song can be sung at a funeral in a version no office approved. Foreign broadcasts crossed borders that sheet music could not. Private gatherings, kitchens, churches and barracks kept repertories alive outside the licensed sphere entirely. Regimes responded by criminalising the private sphere's music in waves of enforcement that rose and fell with political temperature, which produced the characteristic texture of musical life under such rule: long stretches of grey accommodation interrupted by campaigns, denunciations and exemplary punishments. The [full history of music censorship](/2026/05/07/music-censorship-through-history/) traces those campaigns in detail across countries; the present article's concern is the narrower point that censorship of performance is best understood as crowd control and memory control pursued by administrative means.

One further distinction protects accuracy. Not every restriction imposed by a state is authoritarian in kind. Democracies regulate noise, obscenity on public airwaves, copyright and the use of performance in state ceremony, and they argue about those regulations in legislatures and courts where musicians can and do win. The authoritarian difference lies in the combination of monopoly and unaccountability: one employer, one licensing body, one broadcaster, no appeal, and penalties that reach beyond the work to the worker's family, freedom and life. Collapsing that difference, whether to excuse authoritarian systems or to dramatise democratic disputes, destroys the scale on which the case studies in this article have to be weighed. A banned broadcast in an open society and a banned existence in a closed one are both restrictions, and they are not the same event.

## The Shostakovich problem

Dmitri Shostakovich is the standing test case for every question this article raises about composers under authoritarian rule, because the documentary record of his life supports two incompatible portraits and both portraits are argued by serious scholars from evidence. In one portrait, he was a loyal Soviet artist who absorbed official criticism, corrected his course and produced the ceremonial works his state required alongside personal works of genuine feeling, a servant of the system who believed in much of it and feared the rest. In the other portrait, he was a concealed dissident who encoded contempt for the regime into his scores, whose public statements were extracted under duress and whose triumphant finales were composed to be heard, by those with ears, as forced rejoicing. The stakes of the dispute are unusually high because the answer determines how a large and widely performed repertoire is to be heard, taught and programmed, and because each side accuses the other of a characteristic scholarly sin: naivety about totalitarian pressure on one side, romantic fabrication of resistance on the other.

The factual skeleton is not in dispute. Shostakovich rose young, was denounced in the newspaper Pravda in 1936 after Stalin attended his opera Lady Macbeth of Mtsensk, withdrew his Fourth Symphony and answered in 1937 with a Fifth that official commentary received as a Soviet artist's reply to just criticism. He was decorated, employed and celebrated; he was denounced again in 1948 in the campaign against formalism, dismissed from teaching posts and readmitted to favour; he joined the Communist Party in 1960 under circumstances his defenders and detractors describe differently; he signed public letters, including one condemning the dissident physicist Andrei Sakharov, that admirers of the dissident portrait attribute to pressure; he died in 1975 laden with state honours. Every item on that skeleton is compatible with both portraits, because the system was designed to make loyalty and survival indistinguishable from the outside. That indistinguishability, rather than any single document, is the real subject of the controversy.

The controversy acquired its modern shape with the publication in 1979 of Testimony, a volume presented as Shostakovich's memoirs as related to the musicologist Solomon Volkov. The book's portrait of a secret ironist, mocking the regime inside works the regime applauded, transformed Western reception of the symphonies. Its authenticity was challenged on documentary grounds, including passages shown to recycle previously published material, and defended by the composer's associates and family members in varying degrees. Pauline Fairclough, in A Soviet Credo of 2006, examined the Fifth Symphony's Soviet reception and argued that the work's meanings inside its own culture were richer and less reducible to either official optimism or secret dissent than the post Testimony argument allowed. Richard Taruskin, consistently sceptical of heroic political decoding, treated the dissident portrait as a Western wish projected onto a Soviet life, and warned that hearing encoded resistance everywhere flatters the listener at the composer's expense. Other scholars, including Laurel Fay in her biography of 2000, reconstructed the chronology with care and left the interior questions open where the documents run out.

The stance taken here follows the evidence discipline those scholars collectively model. Statements by Shostakovich made in official settings are evidence of what the system required him to say, and weak evidence of what he believed. Private testimony is evidence, weighted by its proximity to the events and its speaker's interests. The scores are evidence of their construction and their reception, and contested evidence of their maker's politics, since the same finale has been documented as heard in incompatible ways by competent contemporaries. What would have to be true for the loyalist portrait to hold entire is that a composer repeatedly denounced, and repeatedly aware of colleagues arrested and shot, invested his ceremonial works with sincere conviction and his private works with nothing the state would punish. What would have to be true for the dissident portrait to hold entire is that he sustained a coherent secret programme across forty years without leaving unambiguous documentation, in a police state, among intimates who later disagreed about it. Neither requirement is impossible. Neither is established. The case stays open, and the repertoire is large enough to be worth hearing under the discipline of that openness rather than under either party's certainty.

## Exile and the musician abroad

Exile converts a musician into an argument. The performer who leaves a regime, or is expelled by one, carries a biography that audiences abroad read before they hear a note: the departure itself certifies that the home state feared or rejected this artist, and the certification can overshadow the artistry it was meant to advertise. Twentieth century musical life was substantially built by exiles. German and Austrian musicians fleeing after 1933 staffed orchestras, universities and film studios across Britain and the United States. Russian musicians after 1917, and again in the later Soviet decades, carried a repertory and a pedagogy westward. Chilean, Argentine, Greek, South African and Iranian exiles in their turns brought repertoires that host audiences first encountered as political testimony and only later, if at all, as art. The host country's gain is the easy part of the ledger. The exile's part includes language loss, audience loss, the reduction of a complex career to a single departure story, and the discovery that the freedom to perform anything can coexist with indifference toward everything the exile most values.

Exile also splits a reputation into two incompatible halves. At home, the departed musician may be erased from reference books and broadcasts, or denounced as a traitor whose recordings are withdrawn, or quietly kept in the catalogue because the state prefers royalties to consistency. Abroad, the same musician may be celebrated in terms the home audience would not recognise, cast as a symbol of a resistance that the musician's actual politics, usually more tangled, only partly fit. Miriam Makeba's long exile from South Africa, entered after the apartheid government revoked her passport in 1960 on her return from a film premiere abroad, made her an accredited voice of a nation that had expelled her, addressing the United Nations while unable to go home even for her mother's funeral. Her case, belonging more fully to the anti colonial sections ahead, shows the exile's paradox in pure form: the state that silenced her at home amplified her abroad, because expulsion is a confession that a voice matters.

States learned to manage exile as deliberately as they managed censorship. A regime could rid itself of a troublesome artist and simultaneously strip that artist of a domestic audience, calculating that foreign admirers make poor crowds. It could refuse exit to artists whose departure would embarrass it, converting passports into instruments of discipline more precise than any repertoire ban. It could permit a tour and punish the touring musician's family, or permit emigration and then tax the emigrant's reputation at home as desertion. Musicians developed counter strategies of their own: building portable virtuosity that needed no state institution behind it, recording catalogues that travelled independently of their persons, and cultivating the diaspora audiences treated in a later section, whose loyalty did not depend on any government's permission. The history of exile is thus an arms race conducted in biographies, and its score can be read in the personnel lists of the century's orchestras, which are among the most eloquent political documents musical life produced.

## Anti-colonial movements and music

Anti colonial movements inherited a problem that performance was unusually suited to solve. A movement opposing an empire typically lacked a state, an army, a press and often a single language, while its opponent possessed all four. What it could possess, quickly and cheaply, was a repertoire. Songs in vernacular languages could teach a political vocabulary to people the colonial school system had left illiterate, could name the movement's heroes and martyrs in forms memory would retain, and could gather crowds under the legal cover of weddings, funerals, religious festivals and concerts. Colonial administrations recognised the threat in their own idiom and responded with sedition prosecutions of singers, bans on particular songs and drums, surveillance of festivals and the co option of chiefs' musicians into official ceremony. The repertoire and the repression grew together, each documenting the other's importance.

India's independence movement offers the most fully documented integration of performance into anti colonial organisation. Gandhi treated communal singing as a discipline of the movement itself, building prayer meetings around bhajans and making the spinning wheel's rhythm a kind of domestic percussion for the cause, while songs associated with the movement travelled between provinces in translation. The song Vande Mataram, drawn from a nineteenth century novel, functioned for decades as an unofficial anthem of the nationalist cause, sung at congresses and prosecuted in the streets, and its contested religious imagery also generated an internal argument about whom the nation's song included, an argument Indian politics continued after independence. In Africa, the Gold Coast highlife guitarist E. T. Mensah became so identified with the independence era in Ghana that his band performed at the celebrations of 1957, while in Kenya, songs in Kikuyu associated with the independence struggle were banned and their singers detained during the emergency of the 1950s. In Algeria, rai and other popular styles carried coded commentary through the war of independence and its aftermath, their cassette circulation outrunning the reach of official broadcasting. Each national case has its own textures, and the comparative point survives them all: performance let movements rehearse nationhood before they possessed a nation, in the one assembly the empire could not wholly prevent.

The American civil rights movement belongs in this comparative frame as an internal decolonisation of citizenship, and its full narrative is told in [the account of music and the civil rights movement](/2025/08/07/music-and-the-civil-rights-movement/), which this article does not retell. The mechanism level belongs here, because the movement's organisers stated it explicitly. Freedom songs were adapted from gospel and labour repertoire by participants such as Bernice Johnson Reagon, whose scholarship and organising both documented how communal singing in mass meetings, jails and marches converted fear into discipline: people who had sung together in a church at night could hold a line together the next morning. The repertoire was deliberately conservative in form and radical in application, using tunes every participant already owned in memory so that no rehearsal stood between a crowd and its voice. That design choice, portability through familiarity, is the same one anti colonial movements made on other continents with wholly different materials, and the convergence suggests a general law of movement music: under pressure, movements sing what their people already know, and save innovation for safer times.

Honesty requires the counterweight. Colonial states also deployed performance effectively, and anti colonial repertoires were never the only sound in any territory. Military bands, school choirs and mission hymns built real loyalties, and many musicians served the administration that employed them without experiencing the service as betrayal. Nor did movement repertoire guarantee movement virtue: songs that unified a people against an empire could be turned, after victory, against minorities and rivals at home, as the next section records. The mechanism analysis offered throughout this article is deliberately neutral about causes because the mechanisms are neutral, and a history that credits performance only when it approves of the performers is not a history of the mechanism at all.

## Decolonisation and the national orchestra

Independence day required a sound. The new state needed an anthem, a band for the ceremony, a broadcaster to carry the ceremony and, within a few years, a set of cultural institutions that could present the nation to itself and to foreign guests as a going concern. The national orchestra, the national dance company, the national theatre and the state folk ensemble were therefore among the first institutions founded by governments that lacked paved roads between their own cities, and the priority was not frivolous. A flag can be designed in a week; a repertoire takes a generation, and the performance institutions were asked to manufacture in public the continuity that the new state's borders, drawn by departing empires, conspicuously lacked. Ghana under Kwame Nkrumah invested in a national orchestra and in highlife's official celebration; Guinea under Sekou Toure built a system of regional and national orchestras and dance companies, funded by the state and toured as diplomacy; Tanzania, Zambia, Senegal and others made parallel investments at scales their budgets could barely carry.

The founding choices were arguments about identity conducted by other means. An orchestra on the European model asserted that the new nation joined world civilisation as an equal. A folk ensemble asserted that the nation's roots were its own villages, arranged and costumed for the capital. A modern dance band asserted that the nation was young, urban and unembarrassed by pleasure. Most states funded all three assertions simultaneously and let them contradict one another on the same festival programme, which is why the cultural record of the independence decades reads as exuberant rather than coherent. Underneath the exuberance lay genuine administrative achievement: training schools, instrument workshops, notation and recording of traditions whose bearers were ageing, and salaries that let a generation of musicians treat performance as a profession rather than a patron's favour. The losses were real too. State arrangement standardised regional styles into national ones, sometimes flattening the differences it claimed to celebrate, and the official stage could starve unofficial styles of venues and airtime without ever banning them.

The afterlife of these institutions traces the political fortunes of their states with embarrassing fidelity. Where economies contracted and governments changed, orchestras shrank, ensembles were privatised or disbanded, and musicians emigrated into the diaspora circuits described later in this article. Where a founding regime endured, its ensembles endured as living monuments of the founding, sometimes long after the founding ideology had faded from every other ministry. The national orchestra question therefore survives decolonisation as a standing problem in cultural policy: a state that funds a repertoire is choosing an ancestor, and the choice, once embodied in salaries, schools and a concert hall, outlives the choosers. New governments inherit the ancestors along with the debts, and their cultural budgets record, more honestly than their speeches, which parts of the founding story they still intend to keep.

## Revolutionary movements and song

Revolutions sing for practical reasons before they sing for expressive ones. A revolutionary movement must recruit among strangers, hold its recruits through defeat and illegality, mark its dead, teach its doctrine to the illiterate and distinguish its friends from its enemies at a glance or at a hearing. Song performs all six tasks inside a single artefact that costs nothing to reproduce. The French Revolution industrialised the form, generating an enormous output of political song on topical subjects, sung in theatres, sections and armies, and answered by an equally energetic royalist counter repertoire, so that Paris in the 1790s conducted part of its civil argument in competing choruses. The pattern repeated wherever revolution was attempted: the Paris Commune of 1871 produced the song that became the anthem of international socialism, composed by a Communard transport worker; the Russian revolutions of 1917 were accompanied by mass singing that participants' memoirs treat as a barometer of the crowd's allegiance; the Chinese Communist movement built song into its army's political education from its earliest bases onward.

The repertoire of a revolution also performs an act of inheritance. Movements that present themselves as ruptures with the past nevertheless sing old tunes, because a new text on a known melody can be learned by a crowd in one hearing, and because borrowed sanctity is useful: hymn tunes carry religious habits of feeling into secular doctrine, and national tunes carry patriotic feeling into class doctrine. The international socialist anthem exists in two famous melodic settings because its text migrated between tunes as the movement migrated between countries. Labour and revolutionary movements maintained songbooks the way churches maintain hymnals, revising them at congresses, and the revisions are documents of doctrinal history in miniature: songs enter when a faction rises, and vanish from later editions when it falls. To read successive editions of a movement songbook in sequence is to watch an organisation edit its own memory, which is one reason such books were worth confiscating.

[Pete Seeger](/2022/01/07/pete-seeger-life-and-music/), the American folk singer whose career joined the labour movement, the folk revival and the blacklist era in one biography, embodied the transmission mechanism between revolutions and their songbooks. Seeger learned repertoire from the Almanac Singers and from the song collecting institutions of the New Deal years, carried union and peace repertoire into commercial folk music, was blacklisted from American television during the anti communist investigations, and nevertheless saw his adaptations sung by movements on several continents, because the songs had been designed, by him and by the traditions he drew on, to be carried by people he would never meet. His career is treated in its own article; here it establishes the revolutionary repertoire's defining property. Such a repertoire is an open system. Anyone may add a verse, translate a chorus or retire a hero, and the movement's songs survive precisely because no authority, not even the movement itself, can fix their text.

Reaction sings too, and the omission of that fact from many celebratory accounts is a failure of nerve. Counter revolutionary movements, military juntas, fascist parties and colonial administrations all maintained repertoires, songbooks, radio choirs and ceremonial music of their own, using identical mechanisms for opposite allegiances. The Horst Wessel song functioned as a co anthem of the National Socialist state and was protected by law; Franco's Spain promoted its own ceremonial repertoire; the Chilean junta that suppressed one repertoire in 1973 promoted another in its place. A mechanism based account has no difficulty accommodating these cases, because it never claimed the mechanisms belonged to the virtuous. The difficulty belongs to accounts that treat political song as inherently emancipatory. The record shows a technology, available to any organisation that can gather a crowd, and judged, like other technologies, by the purposes and the evidence of each deployment rather than by its form.

## The involuntary political symbol

Some musicians are made into political symbols by other people's decisions, and the making can proceed with very little reference to the musician's own views. A state adopts a composer's work as a national emblem after the composer's death, when no consent can be asked or refused. A movement raises a living singer's portrait on banners for a cause the singer supports only in part, or fears, or has publicly declined. A single performance is photographed, broadcast or mythologised until it stands for a whole era in the public memory, while the performer's remaining forty years of work are reduced to a footnote to one evening. The involuntary symbol is not a marginal case in the politics of performance. It may be the statistical norm, because symbols are manufactured by audiences, states and movements for their own needs, and the musician's intentions are the one ingredient the manufacturing process can most easily do without.

Beethoven's Ninth Symphony is the repertoire's most instructive involuntary emblem. Its finale has served as the anthem of a united Europe, was performed at the fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989 with its text adjusted from joy to freedom, was adopted in the 1970s by the white minority government of Rhodesia as its national anthem with new words, and has been claimed by democratic, communist and liberal traditions in turn. No plausible reading of the composer's intentions can license all of those adoptions, and that is the point: the work's combination of a hummable theme, a text about universal brotherhood and an accumulated prestige made it available to any power needing to sound universal. The music did not change. The consecrating institutions changed around it, and the symbolism followed the consecration rather than the score. Conductors and scholars who objected to particular adoptions discovered the limit of artistic authority: once a work enters public ceremonial use, its makers and their executors hold no veto over its meanings.

Living performers face the same process at higher speed and with personal consequences. A singer photographed at a demonstration becomes the demonstration's face in every subsequent account, whatever the singer's other commitments. A benefit performance attaches a name to a cause in databases and headlines that will outrank the performer's denials. Record companies, managers and movements all participate in the attachment because symbols are commercially and politically useful, and the performer's consent is often obtained once, for one occasion, then treated as transferable to every later use of the image. The few performers who have fought the process describe its exhaustion: correcting the record interviews no one reads, refusing invitations that allies treat as betrayals, watching a simplified public self give speeches the private self never approved. The involuntary symbol pays for other people's clarity with the performer's own complexity, and the payment is rarely acknowledged as a cost by the movements that benefit.

Death completes the process by removing the last possibility of contradiction. A dead musician cannot decline an honour, qualify an endorsement or spoil a commemoration with an inconvenient opinion, which is why states and movements prefer their musical symbols safely dead and why posthumous adoption is the most aggressive form of the pattern. Funeral honours, state burials, stamps, currency, renamed streets and centenary festivals conscript the dead into national narratives at industrial scale. Some conscriptions honour genuine lifelong commitments of the dead. Others invert them: composers who fled a regime are reclaimed by its successors as national glories, singers who were harassed by police in life are celebrated by police bands after death. The reader meets such cases throughout the case study sections below, and the discipline they require is simple to state and hard to practise: always ask who is doing the symbolising, what the symbol is for, and what the musician, when able, actually said.

## The musician who chooses the role

Against the involuntary symbol stands the performer who seeks the political role deliberately, studies its requirements and accepts its discipline. The chosen role has its own anatomy. It begins with a decision about subject: the performer adopts a defined territory, a nation's liberation, a labour cause, a human rights campaign, a party, a faith, and builds repertoire inside it. It continues with organisational work that audiences rarely see: benefit circuits, movement tours, fundraising, the lending of a famous name to committees and petitions, the use of interviews as platforms. It matures into an institutional identity in which the performer is consulted as a political actor, invited to conferences and negotiations, and expected to comment on events far from any stage. The career of such a performer cannot be written as a concert chronology with political decorations. The politics is load bearing, and removing it would collapse the biography.

Chosen commitment brings resources that involuntary symbolism never supplies. The committed performer gains a loyal audience whose loyalty survives commercial fashion, a repertoire with a guaranteed occasion structure of rallies, commemorations and fundraisers, and a significance that outlasts chart positions. Movements repay their artists with protection, organisation and memory: the singer a movement regards as its own will be sung about, archived and defended. There is also a genuine artistic dividend that sceptics understate. A defined political subject concentrates a writer's attention the way a defined form concentrates a poet's; some performers produced their strongest work inside the discipline of a cause, because the cause supplied subjects, audiences and standards of sincerity against which weak work was immediately audible. Commitment, in these cases, functioned as an editor.

The chosen role also imposes a discipline of consistency that the uncommitted performer escapes. A political artist's private conduct becomes public evidence: wealth, alliances, silences and compromises are audited by supporters with a severity no critic applies to entertainers. The performer who sings against a regime and then performs for its broadcaster, who champions a movement and then declines its hardest invitation, discovers that the audience for political work keeps accounts. Movements, for their part, discipline their artists with expectations that can harden into a second censorship, friendlier in manner and similar in effect: the song the movement does not want sung at its rally is discouraged as reliably as the censor discourages it, and the artist who insists may learn that an embrace can narrow a repertoire as effectively as a ban. The sections on cost begin from this symmetry. Power over a repertoire is exercised by patrons, parties and publics as well as by police, and the performer who chooses politics chooses all of them.

## What that choice costs

The costs can be tallied in careers, in catalogues and in private life, and they compound because each one raises the price of the others. The first cost of the chosen political role is repertoire narrowing. A performer identified with a cause finds other material received through the identification: love songs read as allegories, experiments read as betrayals, silence read as statement. Record companies price the identification into contracts, promoters into bookings and broadcasters into playlists, until the performer's commercial centre of gravity sits where the politics put it rather than where the talent ranges. Some performers accept the narrowing as the price of significance. Others discover it late, having imagined the role as an addition to a career and finding it a replacement. The catalogue suffers in a subtler way as well: political work dates faster than any other kind, because its references expire with the campaign, the leader and the law it addressed, so the committed performer's most heartfelt recordings can become period documents within a decade while an apolitical contemporary's love songs remain in circulation. Posterity, which keeps what later audiences can use, is not neutral between the two catalogues.

The second cost is exposure. The political performer becomes a target for the opposing side's state, press and public, in ascending order of danger: hostile reviews, lost bookings, surveillance, prosecution, exile, imprisonment and violence, the full ladder that the imprisonment section below documents case by case. Even at the ladder's lower rungs the costs are cumulative and corrosive. A file kept by a security service shapes a life even when it is never acted on; a tour cancelled under pressure teaches promoters to avoid the name; a visa delayed is a season's income gone. Performers with families transmit the exposure to people who never chose it, and several of the century's political musicians left memoirs or interviews describing the moment a threat aimed at a child or a parent repriced the whole commitment. No honest celebration of the political artist is complete without this ledger, because the same societies that later honour such artists usually billed them, at the time, in currency of exactly this kind.

The third cost falls on the work itself, and performers discuss it more candidly than their admirers do. Political writing under deadline, for occasions, inside a movement's message discipline, rewards clarity over ambiguity and slogans over doubt, while the qualities that make performance art durable are often ambiguity, doubt and formal play. The committed writer must therefore defend the work against the cause's own impatience, insisting on the verse that complicates the message, the arrangement that serves the song rather than the rally, the refusal to rewrite a finished piece into a fresher slogan. Where that defence fails, the catalogue fills with competent occasion pieces that even sympathetic listeners skip. Where it succeeds, the tension itself becomes the art's subject, and the performer's best work dramatises the strain between what the cause needs said and what the singer can honestly sing. Listeners who value political performance should learn to hear that strain rather than demanding its absence, because its absence usually means one side of the bargain has quietly won. ## Imprisonment and suppression

### What happens to musicians who defy a government?

Musicians who defy a government face a ladder of consequences that runs from lost work and banned recordings through surveillance, prosecution and exile to imprisonment and, in the gravest documented cases, death in custody or by execution. The ladder's lower rungs are the commonest, and regimes prefer them because they discipline many performers while punishing few.

Imprisonment is the point where the argument of this article stops being metaphorical. States imprison performers when they judge that a voice, a repertoire or a crowd has become a rival centre of authority, and the judgment is usually correct about the mechanism even when the prosecution's stated charge is something else: sedition, tax offences, morality charges, unauthorised assembly, insult to the state. The Chilean singer Victor Jara, whose case receives its own section below, was detained with thousands of others in the days after the coup of September 1973 and died in custody in the Estadio Chile in Santiago, a fact established by judicial investigation in Chile and commemorated by the renaming of the stadium in his honour in 2003. The Nigerian bandleader Fela Kuti, treated in his own section below, was imprisoned repeatedly by military governments, including a sentence begun in 1984 on a currency charge that Amnesty International classified as political, adopting him as a prisoner of conscience before his release in 1986. The South African singer Miriam Makeba was punished by exclusion rather than imprisonment, her citizenship revoked while abroad. Soviet musicians faced the camp system and the psychiatric hospital as instruments of a state that preferred to pathologise some dissent. Each regime calibrated its instrument, and the calibrations are data: they show what each state feared, which was rarely the notes and usually the audience.

Suppression works on the repertoire as well as the person, and its repertoire form can outlast the regime that imposed it. A banned song unrecorded for a generation may survive only in fragments, in the memory of elderly singers, in a censor's file that preserved what it prohibited. Banned instruments, banned dances and banned languages of song leave gaps in a culture's transmission that later revivalists fill with reconstruction and guesswork, so that the revival, however loving, is partly an invention. Suppression also deforms the surviving mainstream: performers who accommodated filled the stages, schools and airwaves the suppressed vacated, and their dominance was then cited as evidence of public taste. Reading any national repertoire formed under authoritarian rule requires that correction, asking not only what the repertoire contains but whose absence organised it. The empty chair is a historical source, and the discipline of noticing it belongs to the research guidance offered in the closing sections of this article and in its questions and answers.

A final observation belongs to the record rather than to rhetoric. Imprisoned and suppressed performers were not drawn only from the famous. For every name in this article there are accompanists, choir members, teachers, instrument makers and radio producers who absorbed the same machinery without leaving biographies, and in many countries the majority of music's political casualties are anonymous in the sources because their fame was local and their persecutors kept the files. A history that confines itself to celebrated cases understates the phenomenon it describes. The celebrated cases earn their place as evidence of mechanism, documented in unusual detail. They should be read as samples from a larger and darker population, not as the population itself.

## Boycotts and cultural sanctions

A cultural boycott reverses the usual direction of musical politics. Instead of a performer addressing power, performers and institutions collectively withdraw performance from a target state, denying it the normality that visiting artists confer. The instrument was developed most fully against apartheid South Africa. From the late 1950s, South African musicians in exile and their allies campaigned for isolation; the United Nations General Assembly called for cultural boycott measures from the late 1960s; the British Musicians Union and other bodies maintained bans of varying strictness; and in the 1980s the campaign acquired a popular anthem of its own in the 1985 recording by Artists United Against Apartheid, organised by Steven Van Zandt, whose participants pledged not to perform at the Sun City resort in the nominally independent homeland of Bophuthatswana. The resort had become the boycott's test case precisely because it paid exceptional fees to artists willing to break the line, converting each acceptance into a public referendum on the artist's politics. The register of performers who played there, and of those who refused, was kept and published by campaigners with an accountant's care.

The boycott's theory deserves the same mechanism treatment as every other instrument in this article. Its direct economic effect on a large entertainment industry was modest. Its intended effect was reputational and psychological: to make the target state's citizens, and especially its white minority, feel the abnormality of their position in the currency of cancelled pleasures, and to deny the state the photographs of international stars smiling on its stages that certify normal relations. Defenders of engagement replied with a rival mechanism theory, arguing that visiting performers brought outside values, played to mixed audiences where law allowed, employed local musicians and weakened isolation's siege mentality, and that a boycott punished ordinary fans and black South African performers most. Both theories were argued at full strength by serious people at the time, and the historical verdict, insofar as one exists, credits the boycott as one strand in a cable of sanctions, internal resistance and economic pressure rather than as a separable cause. That cable problem, the impossibility of isolating one strand's effect, returns in the evidence sections and should be kept in mind here: boycotts are easy to announce and very hard to evaluate.

Other cases widen the evidence. The cultural boycott of Chile after 1973, in which exiled Chilean groups toured the world while many artists refused Chilean stages, fused boycott with solidarity performance, raising funds and attention simultaneously. Boycotts aimed at the Soviet Union after the invasion of Afghanistan, and the cancellation of exchanges around the Olympic Games of 1980 and 1984, demonstrated the instrument's availability to superpowers and its tendency to be answered in kind, with musicians as the most expendable counters. Sporting and cultural boycotts of Rhodesia accompanied its isolation. In each case the collateral pattern repeats: the artists most damaged are usually citizens of the boycotting or boycotted states with the least political power, young performers losing a tour, teachers losing an exchange, while the governments at issue lose a headline. That pattern does not settle the argument for or against any particular boycott. It does mean that honest advocates price it in advance, and that the pricing question, who pays and who merely poses, belongs inside the ethics of the instrument rather than outside it.

## State broadcasting and what it promoted

Broadcasting gave the state a concert hall in every home, and the political history of twentieth century performance is unintelligible without it. A state broadcaster decides, daily and by schedule, which voices represent the nation to itself. Its music policy works through proportion rather than proclamation: so many hours of folk heritage, so much light entertainment, so much serious repertoire, so much imported popular product, or none. The British Broadcasting Corporation in its early decades pursued an improving mission under John Reith, mixing uplift with entertainment and policing popular styles it judged vulgar, while also building orchestras and commissioning work on a scale no earlier British institution had attempted. Germany's regional broadcasters were coordinated into the National Socialist system after 1933, their dance bands supervised, their Jewish employees dismissed, their schedules bent toward the regime's ceremonial needs. The Soviet broadcasting system treated the microphone as a classroom. Postcolonial broadcasters inherited the transmitter as the new state's loudest voice and programmed independence into being with anthem, highlife, traditional ensembles and presidential speeches sharing one schedule.

State broadcasting also created the definitive archive of what a regime wished to be heard as, and the gaps in those archives are as instructive as their contents. Styles excluded from the airwaves did not die; they migrated to the channels the state controlled least: imported records, border stations broadcasting from neighbouring territories, pirate radio in the British case of the 1960s, cassette cultures in Iran and across the Arab world, and the private party. A map of a country's unofficial listening is therefore a map of its broadcasting policy's failures, and performers learned to work both maps at once, recording the approved version for the state microphone and the other version for the cassette. The career of Umm Kulthum, treated in a case section below, shows the summit of the official map: a singer whose monthly broadcasts on Egyptian radio assembled a transnational Arab audience so reliably that her concerts functioned as a shared national appointment across borders her government's diplomacy could not cross.

Commercial broadcasting, where it dominated, substituted the advertiser for the ministry and produced a different politics of the playlist: formats segmented by audience value, gatekeeping by programme directors and sponsors, and a conservatism about controversy that performers experienced as censorship while its administrators described it as business. The honest comparison refuses both sentimentalities. State systems could fund uncommercial excellence and enforce political conformity; commercial systems could ignore excellence and enforce commercial conformity. Performers navigated whichever system they inhabited, and the repertoire that survives from each bears the fingerprints of its gatekeepers. ## Diaspora politics through music

A diaspora is a nation with its geography removed, and performance is one of the few institutions that can hold such a nation together. The migrant community's concert, dance, record shop and wedding band reproduce the homeland's calendar and language in a rented hall abroad, and they do political work of several distinct kinds at once. They raise money, and diaspora fundraising has financed movements, parties, newspapers, legal defences and, in some conflicts, weapons, a fact that host governments have monitored with justified attention. They maintain memory, teaching children born abroad the repertoire that certifies who they are. They lobby the host country, since a community that can fill a hall can also fill a meeting with a legislator. And they argue among themselves about the homeland's direction, in a debate conducted partly through which performers get booked, which anthems get sung and which faction's benefit fills the larger room.

Michael Veal, the ethnomusicologist whose book on Jamaican dub of 2007 traced how studio practice travelled, documented one influential circuit: Jamaican sound system culture transplanted to Britain and North America, where its dances became institutions of Black British and Caribbean American life and its techniques fed back into the island's own production. The circuit's politics were layered. In Britain, sound system dances and the lovers rock and dub repertoires sustained a community identity under the pressure of racism and policing, and confrontations around blues dances and carnival policing made the dance floor a literal contested territory. In the homeland direction, diaspora audiences became a market and a constituency that Jamaican politicians courted, since migrant remittances and migrant opinion both mattered at election time. Veal's account is valuable to this article because it traces mechanism rather than mood: named studios, named engineers, named routes by which a technique and a politics travelled together in record boxes and on aeroplane tickets.

Other diasporas show the pattern at longer range. Irish traditional performance in American cities funded and sentimentalised Irish nationalism across a century, with dance halls and record sales binding emigrants to a cause most would never see. Armenian, Greek, Palestinian, Tamil, Kurdish, Cuban and Vietnamese diasporas each maintained repertoires in which particular songs function as passports, recognised instantly by insiders and carrying specific allegiances that outsiders hear as mere entertainment. The Cuban case displays the split with unusual clarity, since Miami's exile community and Havana's state institutions curated rival versions of the island's musical patrimony, each treating the other's canon as falsification. A diaspora repertoire is thus never only nostalgic. It is a claim about which homeland is the real one, pressed in the only court, the dance floor and the concert hall, where the claim can be ratified nightly by applause.

## Religious authority and musical restriction

Religious authority is the oldest continuous regulator of performance, older than any state, and its restrictions follow a logic distinct from the censor's. The state fears the crowd; the religious authority fears the body, the trance, the mixed gathering and the rival ecstasy that performance can induce without clerical permission. Debates over instruments in worship, over dancing, over women's voices, over theatre and over the permissible occasions of singing recur inside Christianity, Islam, Judaism and other traditions across centuries, conducted in the vocabulary of theology and decided, in practice, by institutions protecting their monopoly on legitimate rapture. Augustine confessed his anxiety that the pleasure of singing might outrun the words sung. Calvinist traditions restricted congregational sound to unaccompanied psalmody. Several Islamic legal schools restricted instruments and audition while Sufi practice built entire devotional disciplines around listening, and the argument between them is an argument about what sound does to the soul, conducted for over a millennium without resolution because both sides correctly perceive the power in question.

Where religious authority holds state power, its restrictions acquire police enforcement, and the cases measure the difference between doctrine and law. Afghanistan under the Taliban between 1996 and 2001 prohibited most performance, destroyed instruments and recordings in publicised acts, and drove musicians into exile or silence, a prohibition documented by ethnomusicologists and by the musicians who fled to Peshawar and beyond. Iran after 1979 restricted public performance in shifting degrees, banning women's solo singing before mixed audiences and policing concerts and recordings through licensing, while private and studio performance adapted and persisted. Saudi Arabia long restricted public concerts under religious guidance while private musical life continued behind walls. In each case the formal doctrine was only part of the enforcement story: local commanders, ministries and markets interpreted the rules unevenly, and musicians became expert readers of which interpretation governed which room. Uneven enforcement is not hypocrisy incidental to religious restriction; it is one of its regular operating conditions, and performers plan careers around it as sailors plan around weather.

Religious restriction also generates religious counter performance, and the two grow together. Hymn singing became a marker of dissent where authorities favoured liturgical chant; gospel performance carried African American church authority into commercial and political spaces the clergy did not control; qawwali and other devotional forms negotiated their legitimacy generation by generation against critics who heard entertainment where devotees heard prayer. The political content of such disputes is easy to miss if politics is defined as parties and states. A community arguing about whose singing may be heard in its sacred space is arguing about authority, gender, generation and the ownership of tradition, which is politics in its foundational sense, and the outcomes decide real distributions of honour and livelihood. This article therefore treats religious regulation as a branch of its subject proper, not as background colour, while observing the stance discipline that doctrine is reported as its adherents and its critics state it, and adjudicated by neither.

## Respectability, gender and who may perform

Who may be seen performing, in what clothing, before which audience and at what hour, has been policed in every society in this article, and the policing fell hardest on women. The respectable woman's voice was confined for long stretches of European and Asian history to the home, the convent and the church choir screened from view, while the public female singer carried an assumed sexual availability that theatres exploited and moralists denounced. The castrato existed as an institution partly because institutions wanted high voices without permitting women to supply them in church. Geisha, courtesan singers, tavern performers and music hall artists across cultures occupied the same structural position: indispensable to the culture's pleasure, excluded from its respectability, and taxed with the hypocrisy of audiences who paid for what they condemned. A political history of performance that omits this distribution of permission has omitted one of the main ways power operated on the art, quietly, daily and without ever issuing a decree about politics at all.

The twentieth century's recording and broadcasting industries redrew the map without erasing it. A woman's voice on record could enter homes that her person could not, which let singers such as Umm Kulthum build authority of a new kind, a voice both intimate and monumental, while the industry's contracts, session customs and publicity machines continued to price women as novelties and discipline them for ageing in ways male contemporaries escaped. Respectability remained a currency women performers were required to manage as a second career: the gospel singer guarding a church reputation while recording secular work under pressure from both markets, the film playback singer heard by millions and credited to actresses' faces, the pop performer whose political speech was reported as temperament where a man's was reported as conviction. Such asymmetries shaped which political roles were even available. A woman adopting the stance of national voice faced scrutiny of dress, family and conduct that a male counterpart's identical stance never attracted, and movements that relied on her voice at rallies policed her private life as movement property.

Gender policing also governed men, in the register of masculinity and effeminacy, and governed whole genres coded as women's or men's territory. Military and labour repertoires prized a rugged collective masculinity that solo male tenderness could embarrass; female ensemble traditions carried repertories of work, mourning and satire that male authorities dismissed as gossip precisely because the satire named them. The recovery scholarship of the decades before 2010, in ethnomusicology and popular music studies alike, documented how much of the political record had been misfiled under entertainment because its performers were women singing to women. Lullabies taught children their people's enemies and heroes. Wedding songs negotiated dowries, migrations and grievances in the one licensed female public genre. Mourning laments kept accounts of violence that official records omitted. Any mechanism account of the subject stands corrected by that scholarship: the most deniable channel in many societies was the one its rulers had already classified as beneath notice.

## Labour movements and song

The labour movement was the nineteenth and twentieth centuries' most systematic employer of political song after the state itself, and its methods were frankly instrumental. Organisers needed to teach doctrine to workers of many languages, to hold picket lines cheerful under provocation, to raise funds, to mark martyrs and to make a union feel like a fellowship rather than an insurance scheme. Song answered every item on that list, and unions institutionalised the answer: songbooks in dozens of languages, choirs attached to locals and federations, singing as the standard opening of meetings, and the Industrial Workers of the World, founded in 1905, issuing a pocket songbook designed to fit a worker's overalls and to be sung in saloons, boxcars and jails. That book's compiler generation understood parody as logistics: set new words to the hymn or popular tune every worker already knew, and the doctrine travels on memory the recruit already owns, a technique borrowed from revivalist religion and acknowledged as such by the borrowers.

Joe Hill, the Swedish born songwriter of the Industrial Workers of the World, executed in Utah in 1915 after a murder conviction that his supporters contested then and historians have disputed since, became the movement's exemplary case of song outliving its legal frame. His own trial belongs to the contested category this article is careful with: the conviction was obtained, the appeals failed, the execution was carried out, and the question of his guilt has been argued from the evidence in both directions for a century without closure. What is not contested is the mechanism his death activated. The funeral, the ashes distributed to union locals, and the repertoire he left turned a songwriter into a permanent organising asset, proof that a movement can convert its losses into memory more efficiently than its victories. Labour movements kept such accounts deliberately: martyrologies in song, from Joe Hill to the miners and textile workers commemorated in ballads by their own communities, functioned as a pension scheme for courage, assuring recruits that sacrifice would be remembered in the only currency movements fully control.

The labour repertoire also displays every mechanism of this article in miniature, including the unflattering ones. Unions policed their songbooks ideologically, factions sang rival versions, and the singing that unified a hall could intimidate the uncommitted worker who preferred not to be marked. Employers answered with their own instruments: company towns controlled halls and churches, blacklists followed singing organisers, and professional strikebreaking treated the picket line's morale, which the singing maintained, as a military target. As unions institutionalised and, in several countries, entered government or corporatist partnership, their singing thinned into ceremony, and the repertoire migrated to folk revivals, heritage festivals and the keeping of singers like Pete Seeger, whose link to this tradition is recorded in the revolutionary section above. The trajectory from weapon to heritage is not a fall from authenticity. It is the normal biography of a repertoire whose movement won enough to stop needing it nightly, and it poses the question the closing sections take up: whether a repertoire's political work can ever be measured apart from the fortunes of the organisation that sang it.

## War and the mobilising song

War is the state's oldest reason for funding performance and its most candid one. Armies require men and women to move together, endure together and accept risks that no argument can make reasonable, and music has been applied to each requirement with a frankness that peacetime patronage disguises. The drum, fife, bugle and pipes regulated the soldier's day before they decorated it: signals for waking, marching, charging and retiring made rhythm a command system, and the pace setting function of marching music measurably reduced exhaustion on the road, a fact quartermasters documented. Recruiting parties toured with bands because enlistment rose where the colours and the music appeared together, and the regimental band remained a recruiting asset into the age of broadcasting. None of this required the soldier to believe anything. Mobilising performance works first on the body and only later, if at all, on conviction, which is why military music is the purest evidence for this article's thesis that sound's political force is mechanical before it is ideological.

The home front required the other kind of song, and states and industries supplied it in partnership. The First World War's British and American popular output mixed recruiting appeals, comic treatment of hardship and sentimental separation ballads in proportions that shifted with the casualty lists, while music hall and Tin Pan Alley learned that audiences wanted their grief acknowledged in forms they could sing without breaking down in public. The Second World War industrialised the arrangement: government information ministries commissioned and vetted material, broadcasters built forces entertainment into a service arm, and performers such as Vera Lynn in Britain and the Andrews Sisters in the United States became national utilities, their voices scheduled like transport. The Soviet Union mobilised its conservatoires, sending front line concert brigades to perform within range of artillery, and treated the besieged premiere of Shostakovich's Seventh Symphony in Leningrad in 1942, performed by emaciated musicians and broadcast to the city and the enemy, as an act of war by other means, which by the mechanism account it plainly was: an assembly, a memory and a signal, delivered under fire.

War song also records the limits of mobilisation from inside. Soldiers' own repertoires, collected by folklorists in both world wars, are dominated by parody, complaint and obscenity aimed at officers, rations and the official songs themselves, and the parodies use the official tunes, which is the mechanism turning against its owners in real time. A state can issue a repertoire; it cannot issue the meanings a tired battalion will fit to it. Anti war song, from the disillusioned output after 1918 to the repertoire surrounding the Vietnam War, grew in that gap between issued and sung meaning, and drew its force from the credibility of performers who were veterans, or who were heard as speaking for them. The pattern completes the article's recurring correction to propaganda theories of musical politics. Performance in war is powerful, contested in every barracks, and owned by no ministry for long. It mobilises and demoralises through the same channels, often in the same week, and the historian's task is to trace which hearing prevailed in which unit, town and year, rather than to award the whole phenomenon to one side.

## Fela Kuti as a case

Fela Kuti built the most complete one man counter state in the history of popular performance, and his biography tests every mechanism in this article at maximum strength. Born in Abeokuta in 1938 into a family at the centre of Nigerian public life, his mother a leading anti colonial feminist organiser and his father a clergyman and educator, he studied music in London, formed bands in Ghana and Nigeria, and after a 1969 tour of the United States, where he encountered Black Power literature and organisation, returned home to forge Afrobeat: long, rhythmically layered compositions for a large ensemble, sung in Nigerian Pidgin, addressing corruption, military rule and colonial mentality by name. Michael Veal, whose biography Fela: The Life and Times of an African Musical Icon of 2000 remains the standard scholarly account, documented how the music's form served its politics: tracks of twenty minutes and more refused the radio single's discipline, Pidgin refused the elite's English, and the extended groove functioned in performance as a gathering that could hold a crowd in one place for an evening, week after week, in a city where assembly was policed. The full biography belongs to [the life and career of Fela Kuti](/2016/08/21/fela-kuti-life-and-career/); the case here concerns what the Nigerian state did about him, because the state's behaviour is the cleanest available measure of how seriously a government can take a singer.

The state answered with escalating force over two decades. His commune and club in Lagos, declared by Fela a republic outside state authority, was raided repeatedly; in February 1977, after a confrontation between his followers and soldiers, a large military force attacked and burned the compound, destroying his studio, tapes and home, and his mother died the following year from injuries sustained when she was thrown from a window during that raid, facts documented by Nigerian and international reporting and by Veal's biography. Fela answered with repertoire that named the army's conduct and with a coffin carried in protest to the military ruler's residence. He was imprisoned in 1984 on a currency charge, in proceedings Amnesty International treated as political, and released in 1986 after the government changed. He died in 1997, and Lagos gave him a funeral procession of a scale the state had never granted him in life. Stated under the discipline this article applies to political allegiance, the sequence is reported as documented fact with its consequences: a performer built an institution the state could not license, the state destroyed the institution by force, and the repertoire survived both, circulating internationally on record while its maker was being prosecuted at home.

The case also disciplines enthusiasm. Fela's personal rule inside his commune was authoritarian in style, his public statements included claims that contemporaries contested, and his movement built no durable political organisation beyond his own stage; Nigeria's military regimes fell for reasons in which his music was, at most, an accompaniment. What the case proves is narrower and more valuable. It proves that a determined performer with an assembly, a memory system and a deniable wit could embarrass a military state for twenty years, and that the state, possessing every conventional instrument of power, could find no cheaper way to answer than arson, prosecution and prison. The disproportion is the datum. Governments do not burn down metaphors.

## Bob Marley as a case

Bob Marley's career presents the opposite configuration from Fela Kuti's: not a performer besieged by his own state, but a performer courted by rival politicians at home and adopted, after death, as the benign emblem of a nation whose politics he spent his life refusing to join. Born in 1945 in rural Jamaica and raised partly in Kingston's Trenchtown, Marley rose through the island's recording industry from ska through rocksteady to reggae, and absorbed Rastafari, the religious movement that read Ethiopia as Zion and Jamaica as Babylon, as his faith and his repertoire's governing frame. His songs addressed oppression, exodus, judgement and redemption inside that frame, in terms a Kingston sufferer and a London exile could each hear as their own. The mechanism at work was translation: Marley converted a specific theology and a specific island's grievances into a portable idiom of dignity and resistance that audiences with no knowledge of either could adopt as theirs, and the conversion was musical as much as verbal, carried by arrangements of unusual clarity and patience. [The life and career of Bob Marley](/2017/01/21/bob-marley-life-and-career/) gives the chronology; the political case is what Jamaica's politicians, and then the world, did with the portability.

In December 1976, two days before a free concert Marley had agreed to give in Kingston under government sponsorship, gunmen shot him, his wife Rita and his manager at his home; all survived, and Marley performed at the concert with his arm in a sling before leaving Jamaica for an extended period abroad. The shooting's authorship was never judicially established, and this article records it as an unsolved attack in a season of political violence between Jamaica's rival parties, each of which sought association with the island's most famous citizen. In April 1978 Marley returned for the One Love Peace Concert, organised to support a truce between the parties' armed factions, and on stage joined the hands of Prime Minister Michael Manley and opposition leader Edward Seaga above his head, the photograph becoming the defining image of his public life. Both facts belong in the record with their weights distinguished: the concert and the gesture are documented; the claim that the gesture changed Jamaica's trajectory is not, and the truce it celebrated did not hold. What the evening demonstrated was the involuntary symbol mechanism operating at maximum visibility, as two politicians borrowed a singer's moral credit in public because neither possessed enough of his own for the photograph to work without him.

Marley's posthumous career completes the case. He died in 1981, received a state funeral in Jamaica, and was progressively installed as the island's global trademark, his image licensing tourism and merchandise on a scale that would have astonished the Kingston industry he escaped. The theology that structured his work was softened in the installation into a general aura of peace and rebellion, safe for hotel lobbies. Critics of the process, including Jamaican commentators who had known the sharper edges of his commitments, described a national symbol manufactured by deleting the doctrine. Defenders replied that the softening is what symbols are for and that the repertoire remains available in its full strength to any listener who seeks it. Both descriptions are accurate about different objects: the trademark and the catalogue. The case therefore teaches the reader to keep those objects separate whenever a musician becomes a flag, and to ask, of every smiling posthumous emblem, which verses the emblem makers chose not to print on the banner.

## Tupac Shakur as a case

Tupac Shakur forces this article to handle the politics of a performer whose political inheritance was explicit, whose commercial success was enormous and whose public meaning was contested during his life and remains contested, at the distance permitted by the date of this article, in scholarship and journalism. Born in 1971 to Afeni Shakur, a Black Panther Party member who had been tried and acquitted in the Panther 21 case in New York while pregnant with him, he was raised inside movement memory and trained in performance at the Baltimore School for the Arts before his recording career began in California. His albums of the first half of the 1990s addressed police violence, poverty, incarceration and the economics of the drug trade in the first person of the neighbourhoods those forces governed, alongside material celebrating wealth, loyalty and violence, and the coexistence of the two registers in one catalogue is the central interpretive fact of his career. Admirers read the coexistence as documentary honesty about a generation's divided incentives. Critics, including politicians who campaigned against his label's output, read it as commerce exploiting the conditions it claimed to mourn. Both readings name real features of the records, and the stance discipline applied throughout this article requires giving each its strongest form rather than resolving the catalogue into a pamphlet or a product. [The life and career of Tupac Shakur](/2015/05/21/tupac-shakur-life-and-career/) carries the full narrative.

The state's dealings with Shakur illustrate how political attention attaches to a performer whose audience is the prize. His 1992 song addressing police conduct drew a public denunciation from the Vice President of the United States after a defence lawyer cited the recording in a Texas case, an episode in which a vice presidential press operation and a defence strategy each used the performer as an instrument. He was prosecuted and, in 1995, imprisoned on a sexual abuse conviction that he and his supporters contested and that this article records in the separated categories the charter requires: charged, tried, convicted, sentence served after appeal bonds and further proceedings, with the underlying events disputed in print by the parties. He was shot in Las Vegas in September 1996 and died of his injuries, a killing that remained officially unsolved at the date of this article, surrounded by theories this article does not adopt. What the record establishes without theory is the scale of official attention: congressional and vice presidential comment, prosecutions, and a posthumous surveillance of his reputation by journalists and scholars, all directed at a performer whose formal political office was none. The attention is explicable only by the mechanism this article has traced throughout. He commanded an assembly, the young audiences no party could gather, and a memory system, recordings memorised verbatim by millions, and institutions contended over both.

Shakur's case also marks the commercialisation problem in its sharpest form, which the questions and answers below take up as a general question about marketed rebellion. His label profited from the controversy that endangered him; his posthumous catalogue was managed as an estate business of the first rank; and the movement inheritance of his childhood was simultaneously a genuine formation, attested by those who raised and taught him, and a marketing asset, attested by its prominence in his publicity. Untangling formation from asset in such a career cannot be done by inspecting the performer's sincerity, which is unavailable, and is better done by inspecting consequences: which audiences were organised, which vocabularies entered public argument, which institutions changed behaviour, and which merely sold the argument back to its audience at a margin. On those measures the record is mixed in ways that resist both the hagiographic and the dismissive summary, and the mixedness is itself the finding this case contributes to the evidence sections that close the article.

## John Lennon as a case

John Lennon's political career is the most documented instance of a performer moving, deliberately and in public, from involuntary symbolism to chosen activism and then discovering the state's price list. The Beatles' fame had made Lennon a symbol before he held a political position anyone could name; his remark in a 1966 interview about the band's popularity relative to Christianity produced protests and record burnings in the United States that demonstrated how completely a performer's stray sentence could be conscripted. After the band's dissolution he and Yoko Ono converted their celebrity into a sequence of deliberate political performances: a honeymoon staged as a bedside press conference for peace in Amsterdam and Montreal in 1969, billboards in world cities declaring war over if the viewer wanted it, benefit work for causes from striking workers to imprisoned activists, and recordings addressing war, women's subordination, Ireland and his own class formation. Robin Denselow, in When the Music's Over of 1989, placed Lennon's campaigning inside the longer history of political pop and noted its defining gamble: Lennon spent the Beatles' accumulated goodwill, the largest stock of public affection any performer had ever held, as if it were a campaign fund. [The life and career of John Lennon](/2013/02/07/john-lennon-life-and-career/) supplies the chronology around the politics.

The United States government answered in the documentary open. After Lennon moved to New York in 1971 and associated with anti war and left activists, the immigration authorities opened deportation proceedings against him in 1972 on the basis of a 1968 British drug conviction, in a case his lawyers and later historians documented as politically driven, with files released under freedom of information law showing surveillance by the Federal Bureau of Investigation and official interest in neutralising his influence on young voters in an election year. Lennon fought the proceedings for four years, won residence in 1976, and withdrew from public musical life for much of the late 1970s before returning to recording in 1980. He was shot and killed outside his New York home in December 1980 by a disturbed former fan, a crime with no established political motive, recorded here without speculation because the stance discipline of this article refuses to convert every famous death into the argument it is illustrating. The deportation file, not the death, is the case's political evidence: a state with no repertoire ban at its disposal reached for immigration law, which is what states do when the performer is too famous to censor and too foreign to prosecute.

Lennon's afterlife as a symbol illustrates the softening mechanism described in the Marley case, at even larger scale. The peace campaigner whose own government had tried to expel him became, in the decades after 1980, a municipal treasure of that same city and a licensed emblem of a peace so generalised that institutions he campaigned against could quote his best known peace recording without discomfort. Admirers who kept the sharper record in print, the surveillance files, the benefit concerts for unpopular prisoners, the quarrels with his own movement allies, performed the archivist's version of political work: refusing the emblem in favour of the file. The case closes the sequence of four modern cases on the theme they share with the two that follow. A performer's politics, however chosen, enters a market of symbols in which states and publics are stronger bidders than the performer, and the posthumous auction is rigged in favour of the bidder who needs the symbol most anodyne.

## Umm Kulthum as a case

Umm Kulthum is the essential case against any account of musical politics that equates the political with the oppositional, because her power was exercised from the centre of her state, in alliance with its government, at a scale no dissident in this article approached. Born in a Delta village in Egypt around the turn of the twentieth century, she learned singing in her father's religious performance, moved to Cairo in the 1920s, disciplined a formidable voice and an exacting professional apparatus, and by the 1930s stood at the summit of Arab musical life. Her monthly Thursday concerts, broadcast on Egyptian radio from 1934 onward, became a transnational institution: streets emptied across the Arab world on those evenings, and her performances, long, improvisatory within a strict maqam discipline and built on classical and modern Arabic poetry, functioned as a shared cultural appointment for tens of millions who shared no state. She recorded, she managed her image with famous severity, and she accumulated a form of authority that politicians courted because it could not be manufactured by any ministry. [The life and career of Umm Kulthum](/2016/06/07/umm-kulthum-life-and-career/) treats the biography in full.

Her alignment with the Egyptian state after the revolution of 1952 was chosen, public and consequential. She performed for the new order's occasions, was decorated by it, and after Egypt's defeat in the war of 1967 undertook a celebrated fundraising campaign, touring Arab capitals and Paris and donating proceeds to the Egyptian treasury and war effort, an effort documented in detail by her biographers and by the press of several countries. President Gamal Abdel Nasser understood her value precisely: her voice assembled the Arab nation weekly in a way his own speeches, however widely broadcast, could not, because her assembly was voluntary, pleasurable and older than his republic. The relationship was nonetheless a negotiation between two powers, not the absorption of one by the other. She kept control of her repertoire, her fees and her image; the state kept its honours and its requests within the limits she set; and scholars of her career describe a partnership in which each side could embarrass the other and neither could replace the other. Political scientists who reserve the term power for opposition misdescribe such a partnership. The case belongs in this article as proof that the mechanisms of assembly and memory serve incumbents at least as efficiently as insurgents, and that the century's most consequential political singer may have been the one who never sang a protest song.

Her death in 1975 drew a funeral procession in Cairo numbered by contemporary reports in the millions, among the largest public gatherings in Egyptian history, and her posthumous position divided exactly as the involuntary symbol analysis predicts. Successor governments inherited her as national patrimony; her image and voice were deployed by the state broadcaster as the sound of an Egypt that predated and would outlast faction; and listeners across the region continued to hear in the recordings a private repertoire of love, dignity and loss that no state programme had authored. The recordings' resistance to their own official frame may be her case's final lesson. A performer who lends the state her assembly does not thereby transfer her meanings, because meanings live in the memory system the audience maintains, and audiences kept her songs for weddings, grief and endurance long after the occasions of state had been forgotten.

## Victor Jara as a case

Victor Jara is the case in which the mechanisms of this article met the extremity of state violence, and it is written here under the discipline that the facts require no heightening. Born in 1932 in rural Chile, trained in theatre in Santiago, Jara became a central figure of the Nueva Cancion movement, the repertoire of folk based song, much of it drawing on the work of Violeta Parra, that accompanied the Chilean left's rise and the election of Salvador Allende's Popular Unity government in 1970. He performed for the movement as organiser and emblem, directed theatre, taught at the university and understood his work, by his own documented account, as service to a political project. On the morning of the military coup of 11 September 1973 he went to the State Technical University, where he was detained with students and staff and taken to the Estadio Chile, the stadium converted into a detention centre with which this article opened. Fellow detainees later testified that he sang in the stadium and encouraged the prisoners, that guards recognised and singled him out, and that he was beaten and interrogated. He died in the stadium in mid September 1973, of multiple gunshot wounds according to the judicial and forensic findings later established in Chile, and his body was recovered from among the dead by his widow, Joan Jara, whose testimony and archive anchor much of the record. The stadium was renamed for him in 2003. [The life and music of Victor Jara](/2022/02/07/victor-jara-life-and-music/) gives the full account, and the present section confines itself to what the case establishes for the article's argument.

Three findings survive every dispute about detail. First, the junta treated a folk singer as a priority object of violence in its first days, amid a coup's thousand urgencies, which is evidence of the regime's own estimate of what a repertoire and its singer were worth as a rival authority. Second, the repertoire outlived the repression designed to erase it: Chilean exile ensembles carried Nueva Cancion across Europe and the Americas for seventeen years of dictatorship, the stadium songs became international knowledge, and the movement's memory system proved to be stored exactly where the coup could not reach, in the memorised singing of thousands of ordinary participants. Third, the case generated a long legal and archival afterlife, investigations, exhumations and prosecutions pursued in Chile and abroad across decades, in which the evidentiary core was repeatedly the testimony of fellow prisoners about singing. The reader should note what kind of proof that is. The state's violence is documented largely through the memories of people who were held in a stadium and who remembered, among the horrors, who sang. Memory, the article's first mechanism, here functions as the archive of the crime itself.

## Evidence that music changes politics

### Can a song actually change anything?

A song can change politics when it is attached to an organisation that converts feeling into action: it recruits, fundraises, holds morale under repression and marks the moments movements remember. Measured alone, stripped of parties, unions, churches and campaigns, a repertoire's independent effect is small, and honest claims for musical power always name the organisation doing the converting.

The affirmative evidence is strongest at the level of mechanism and movement maintenance, and it should be stated at exactly that strength. Participation research and organisers' testimony converge on the finding that shared singing raises a group's cohesion and its members' willingness to incur costs, and movements that sang kept members through defeats that dissolved quieter organisations. The Estonian case supplies the affirmative argument's most dramatic modern exhibit. During the late Soviet period, mass song festivals in Estonia, a state licensed institution with a century of national history behind it, became the vehicle through which public opposition to Soviet rule assembled in legal form: in 1988, gatherings at the Tallinn song festival grounds drew crowds estimated in the hundreds of thousands, singing repertoire whose banning and unbanning had been a barometer of Moscow's control for decades, and the movement acquired the name the Singing Revolution. Estonia regained independence in 1991 through a process driven by legislatures, negotiations and the Soviet collapse, and no serious account awards the outcome to song alone. The disciplined claim is the one the participants themselves made: the festivals supplied the crowd, the courage rehearsal and the visible proof of numbers at the moment when numbers were the argument. The full lineage of such repertoire is traced in [the survey of protest songs that changed history](/2025/08/21/protest-songs-that-changed-history/), whose claims this article deliberately reads at the mechanism strength described here.

Fundraising and recruitment evidence is more quantifiable and points the same way. Benefit performance converted fame into movement income at documented scale from the labour era through the famine relief concerts of 1971 and 1985, and organisers' records show recruitment at performances that no leaflet campaign matched. Defection and morale evidence from military history runs in both directions, as the war section recorded, and counts for the affirmative where the direction is documented. The cultural boycott evidence, weighed in its own section, supports a reputational effect on the target state, corroborated by that state's own strenuous efforts to break the boycott. Taken together, the affirmative case establishes that performance reliably changes the capacities of political actors: their numbers, funds, cohesion, visibility and morale. A claim pitched at capacities, rather than at outcomes, is supported by named instances on several continents across the whole period this article covers, and it is the strongest claim the evidence will carry without assistance from rhetoric.

## Evidence that it does not

The negative evidence begins with the base rate. For every repertoire attached to a movement that prevailed, the record holds dozens attached to movements that were crushed, ignored or absorbed, and the losers' songs are forgotten precisely because they lost, which corrupts every retrospective sample. Victorian music hall sang imperial confidence for half a century without measurable effect on imperial policy. The protest repertoire of the 1960s, the most commercially successful in history, coexisted with the escalation of the Vietnam War through its peak years and with the election of the candidates it opposed; the war ended through military, diplomatic and congressional processes in which song appears in the record as atmosphere rather than cause. Authoritarian states fell, when they fell, to economic failure, elite defection and war, in sequences where the dissident repertoire is audible as commentary and absent as lever. A mechanism that is present in victory and defeat alike cannot, without further specification, explain either, and intellectual honesty requires saying that the romantic version of this article's subject, in which song bends history by its own force, fails its base rate test completely.

The absorption evidence is equally sobering. Commercial systems metabolised dissent with an efficiency no censor matched: by the early 1970s the gestures, sounds and vocabulary of rebellion were available as fashion, and record companies that had feared political repertoire learned to sell it by stripping its organisational attachments and keeping its posture. Denselow's 1989 survey documented the cycle in detail, performers radicalised by success into spokespeople and then managed back into entertainers, their most political recordings functioning as back catalogue. State systems performed the same absorption from the other side, licensing a quota of licensed satire and youth repertoire whose existence advertised a tolerance the licensing itself disproved. In both systems the performer's dilemma recurs: reach requires the channel, and the channel prices the message. Cases where the price was refused exist and are honoured in this article's case sections, but they are honoured correctly only when the far commoner outcome, payment, is kept in view beside them.

A third body of negative evidence concerns measurement itself. Political outcomes have many causes moving together, and the musical variable is entangled with all of them: movements sing when they are already growing, regimes sponsor performance when they already feel secure or already feel threatened, and the same festival crowd that looks like a cause in a memoir looks like a symptom in a regression. Taruskin's scepticism about political decoding, applied throughout this article to individual works, applies with equal force to whole movements: the temptation to award the song the credit for the crowd's courage is the temptation of every participant historian, and the corrective is comparison. Where comparisons can be made, between similar movements with different musical endowments, the musical variable shrinks toward the organisational one. What survives that shrinkage is real but modest, and the closing section states the article's final position in terms sized to what survives.

## The limits of the claim

The claim this article finally defends is narrower than its subject's reputation and stronger than its sceptics allow. Performance does not decide political outcomes; armies, treasuries, courts and electorates do that. Performance decides, measurably and repeatedly, whether the people who contest those outcomes can gather, remember, endure and recognise one another, and those capacities are preconditions without which the deciding institutions are never challenged at all. A repertoire is infrastructure. Like roads, it serves whoever can use it, it outlasts its builders' intentions, it is maintained by states and movements for opposed purposes with the same techniques, and its absence is felt most by those with no other infrastructure to fall back on: the illiterate, the banned, the exiled and the imprisoned, whose reliance on memory and assembly runs through every section above. The states that sponsored and censored performance with equal energy understood the infrastructure point before the scholars named it. Their budgets are the bibliography of this article's thesis.

Three working rules follow for any reader who uses the history professionally, as a teacher, researcher, performer or citizen. Keep the mechanism and the cause in separate accounts, crediting a repertoire with capacities and an organisation with outcomes. Grade every coded meaning and every heroic anecdote by its documentary distance from the event, and let the grading show in the writing rather than hiding it in a confident tone. And when a musician becomes a symbol, ask who is doing the symbolising and what the symbol costs the person inside it, because the pattern that runs from anthem adoption to posthumous trademarking is the single most reliable predictor in the whole field. The political history of performance is not a pageant of brave singers and frightened tyrants, though it contains both in number. It is the record of a technology of gathering and remembering, contested by every power that ever understood it, and still, at the end of the record surveyed here, unpatented and unpoliced in its oldest form: people in a room, singing from memory, needing nothing that can be confiscated.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What makes music politically powerful?

Music is politically powerful because it performs three tasks that written argument performs badly. It assembles people in one place and synchronises them through shared rhythm, so a crowd experiences agreement physically before debating it. It stores words in memory without literacy, since a melody corrects drifting syllables and lets a text travel for generations among people who own no books. And it carries meanings under deniable cover, letting a singer address insiders while offering outsiders an innocent reading. States have always recognised the combination, which is why they funded chapels, bands and broadcasters with one hand and licensed, censored and exiled performers with the other. The power is a capacity rather than a guarantee: it changes what political actors can do, gathering, remembering and enduring, while armies, treasuries and electorates still decide outcomes. Wherever a government spends money and coercion on performance, the spending itself testifies that the capacity is real.

### Q: What is a protest song?

A protest song is a song whose public function is to oppose a named condition, authority or policy and to invite its hearers to share that opposition. The category is defined by use rather than by sound, so its members include hymns repurposed for picket lines, folk ballads naming unjust officials, soul and reggae recordings addressing state violence, and punk or hip hop tracks attacking governments, sometimes within a single concert. Three features recur across the tradition. The target is identifiable, even when prudence keeps the naming indirect. The song is built for adoption, with a chorus strangers can sing after one hearing. And the song travels with an organisation or a moment, a union, a campaign, a march, that converts its feeling into coordinated action. Songs of general discontent sit at the category's edge: they voice refusal without a target or a vehicle, and history usually files them as political songs in the broader sense rather than protest songs in the strict one.

### Q: How does coded meaning work in music?

Coded meaning works by giving one performance two audiences. Insiders share a key, a place name that stands for a destination, a biblical journey that stands for escape, a historical villain who resembles a living ruler, a banned melody quoted inside a permitted work, and they receive the second message. Outsiders, including censors, hear only the innocent surface, and the performer can retreat to that surface if challenged. The system depends on genuine ambiguity, which is why it protects singers and frustrates historians in equal measure. Responsible accounts therefore grade cases by evidence. The strongest codes are documented by the people who used them, in testimony or letters. Middle cases rest on contemporary reports of listeners hearing the second sense. The weakest are alleged decades later by writers with a story to sell, and should be repeated, if at all, with that distance stated. A resemblance alone proves nothing, because melodies wander innocently between songs all the time.

### Q: Why do states fund orchestras and festivals?

States fund orchestras and festivals because performance is a working instrument of government, not a decoration added after government ends. An orchestra displays national prestige to foreign guests, trains and employs musicians whose skills the state wants kept inside its borders, and gives citizens of different regions a shared repertoire they can recognise as theirs. A festival assembles the population in an ordered, festive form that photographs well and renews loyalty without a speech. Postcolonial governments founded such institutions within years of independence, before many other services existed, because a new state needs to present itself to itself as a continuity. Churches and courts funded performance for centuries on the same logic, legitimacy made audible. The funding always carries expectations, ranging from a municipal orchestra's civic presence to a ministry's demand for ceremonial odes, and the useful question is never whether a funded ensemble is political but what its funder expects, who enforces the expectation and what refusal would cost.

### Q: When were national anthems invented?

National anthems were invented, as a general institution, during the long nineteenth century, although a few melodies carried national associations earlier through court and military use. The modern form, one authorised song standing for the whole state and sung by citizens at ceremonies, matches the needs of mass politics: conscript armies, compulsory schooling and popular sovereignty all required a short ritual in which strangers could perform unity on demand. Revolutionary France showed the model's force when a marching song of 1792 spread between armies faster than any decree and was adopted by the state its soldiers had already chosen. Other nations ratified popular or military favourites, then surrounded them with law, etiquette and schoolroom repetition until recent choices felt immemorial. Anthems addressed to land and ancestors outlasted anthems addressed to dynasties and parties, which were discarded with their regimes. The anthem is therefore best understood as a technology of the modern state wearing the costume of ancient custom.

### Q: How did composers work under dictatorships?

Composers under dictatorships worked inside a structure that disciplined them mainly through livelihood rather than through daily orders. The state typically controlled commissions, theatres, publishing, broadcasting, teaching posts and foreign travel, often through a single union or chamber whose membership was the licence to practise. Ambitious composers paid the system's taxes in ceremonial works for state anniversaries and leaders' birthdays, saving personal expression for chamber music, teaching or works written for the drawer. Style itself was policed in campaigns against formalism, degeneracy or Western influence, and an official interpretation of a major work could serve as a shelter, giving everyone an approved meaning to repeat. Refusal had a price list running from lost premieres to emigration, dismissal and arrest, and most composers, like most citizens, occupied the middle ground of accommodation. The resulting record is genuinely mixed, which is why single verdicts on such careers should be distrusted and why each case has to be reconstructed from documents rather than from the composer's posthumous reputation.

### Q: Was Shostakovich a loyalist or a dissident?

The evidence supports neither portrait entire, and the dispute itself is the reliable finding. Dmitri Shostakovich was denounced in 1936 and again in 1948, decorated and employed by the Soviet state, joined its ruling party in 1960 and signed public letters his admirers attribute to pressure. The loyalist reading, argued from his official career and statements, holds that he served the system and believed in much of it. The dissident reading, spread in the West after the contested memoir volume Testimony appeared in 1979, holds that he encoded contempt for the regime inside works it applauded. Scholars such as Pauline Fairclough and Richard Taruskin, from different directions, showed that both readings flatten a record the system deliberately made ambiguous, since survival and loyalty were designed to look identical. Private testimony is divided, the memoirs' authenticity is disputed, and the scores have been heard in incompatible ways by competent contemporaries. The honest answer is that the question remains open on the documents available.

### Q: Did anti-colonial movements use music deliberately?

Anti colonial movements used music deliberately, organisationally and on every continent where empire was contested. A movement without a state, a press or often a single language could still possess a repertoire, and organisers treated that repertoire as equipment. Songs in vernacular languages taught political vocabulary to people colonial schooling had left illiterate, named heroes and martyrs in forms memory retained, and gathered crowds under the legal cover of weddings, funerals, festivals and concerts. Gandhi built communal singing into the discipline of the Indian independence movement. Ghanaian highlife performed at the independence celebrations of 1957 after years of association with the nationalist cause. Kenyan songs linked to the independence struggle were banned and their singers detained during the emergency of the 1950s. Colonial administrations confirmed the deliberateness by prosecuting singers for sedition and banning particular songs and gatherings. The design principle everywhere was portability through familiarity: movements under pressure sang what their people already knew by heart.

### Q: What is a cultural boycott?

A cultural boycott is the organised withdrawal of performance from a target state in order to deny it the normality that visiting artists confer. Performers refuse engagements, unions instruct members not to accept them, institutions cancel exchanges and tours, and campaigners keep and publish registers of who held the line and who broke it. The instrument was developed most fully against apartheid South Africa, where exile musicians campaigned for isolation from the late 1950s, the United Nations General Assembly endorsed cultural boycott measures from the late 1960s, and the Sun City resort, which paid exceptional fees to artists willing to perform in a nominal homeland, became the campaign's public test case, answered in 1985 by the Artists United Against Apartheid recording. A boycott differs from censorship in direction: censorship is a state silencing its own performers, while a boycott is performers and institutions abroad refusing a state their presence. Its costs fall unevenly, often on young and local artists with the least power.

### Q: Do cultural boycotts work?

Cultural boycotts work at the level of reputation and morale, and their larger effects cannot be separated from the company they keep. Against apartheid South Africa, the boycott denied the state the photographs of international stars on its stages that certify normal relations, made white South Africans feel abnormality in the concrete currency of cancelled pleasures, and gave the liberation movement a visible international constituency. The South African government's sustained efforts to break the boycott, including exceptional fees at Sun City, testify that the target felt the instrument. Yet apartheid ended through a cable of pressures, internal resistance, economic sanctions, military costs and negotiation, and no strand can be isolated and weighed. Defenders of engagement argued at the time that visiting performers brought outside values and employed local musicians, and that argument also names real effects. The measured verdict is that a boycott reliably raises a regime's reputational costs and reliably taxes uninvolved artists, and that claims beyond those two findings exceed the evidence.

### Q: Is state-sponsored music propaganda?

Some state sponsored music is propaganda, and much of it is not, and the distinction lies in intent, monopoly and freedom of refusal rather than in the mere fact of public money. Propaganda in the strict sense is performance commissioned to persuade on behalf of power, inside a system where rival messages are suppressed and refusal is punished. A ceremonial ode required by a ministry that also bans independent repertoire fits that description. A municipal orchestra playing subscription concerts, a state broadcaster funding uncommercial repertoire, or a folk ensemble preserving regional dances operates under expectations, as all patronage does, but the audience can hear alternatives and the musicians can resign without ruin. Between those poles lie the instructive cases: the national ensemble whose arrangements quietly standardise a country's diversity into one official sound, or the independence era orchestra asserting equality with former colonial powers. Calling every sponsored performance propaganda empties the word of use and, worse, excuses the systems where the description is exact.

### Q: What is the difference between protest music and political music?

Political music is the wider category: any performance whose creation, funding, content or use engages power, including anthems, court ceremony, state broadcasting, military bands and patriotic repertoire that supports authority. Protest music is the narrower, oppositional subset: performance directed against a named condition, authority or policy, usually attached to a movement seeking change. The distinction matters because histories that equate the political with the oppositional erase most of the record. The century's largest musical budgets were spent by states on affirmation, not by movements on dissent, and its most consequential political singer may be Umm Kulthum, who sang from the centre of Egyptian power and never recorded a protest song. The categories also overlap in practice. A state anthem can be sung by a crowd as protest against an occupying state, and a protest song can be licensed by a broadcaster as harmless heritage. Classification follows use in context, not the intentions printed on a record sleeve.

### Q: Should musicians speak about politics at all?

The question has no general answer, and the honest response is to set out what each choice involves rather than to award a verdict. A musician who speaks gains the use of a rare asset, an assembled audience and a memorised voice, for causes that may have no other access to either, and accepts exposure that runs from lost bookings through surveillance to the imprisonments documented in several careers. A musician who declines keeps the repertoire open to listeners of every allegiance and refuses a role for which election by fame is a poor qualification, while accepting that silence also has effects and will be used by others as endorsement. Audiences complicate both choices by conscripting performers into symbols regardless of consent. What can be asked of either path is consistency and honesty: the speaking musician should know the subject and price the exposure, including the exposure of family and colleagues, and the silent musician should not sell the appearance of courage that the record does not contain.

### Q: Is it right to separate music from a composer's politics?

Separating a composer's music from the composer's politics is defensible as a listening practice and indefensible as a historical claim, and keeping those two apart dissolves most of the quarrel. A listener may attend to a work's construction and power without auditing its maker's opinions, and no coherent rule requires a concert hall to function as a tribunal. The historical claim is different: where a composer's politics shaped commissions, subjects, dedications, institutional power or the persecution of colleagues, that record belongs in any honest account of the career, and suppressing it falsifies the biography. Richard Wagner is the standing case, since his published attacks on Jewish influence in music, his revolutionary exile and his posthumous adoption by a murderous regime are facts of different kinds that polemic constantly merges. The disciplined approach separates what the composer wrote and did, what later powers made of the work, and what the work accomplishes for a listener, and answers for each separately rather than trading one against the others.

### Q: How do I research a song's political context?

Researching a song's political context begins with documents closest to the performance and works outward. Establish the basic record first: who wrote and first performed the song, when, where, for what occasion and under whose sponsorship, using contemporary newspapers, programmes, union or broadcaster archives and the performer's own dated statements. Then trace the song's career, its bans, broadcasts, translations, parodies and adoptions, distinguishing in every source between what was alleged, what was charged, what was proved and what was settled later. Memoirs are evidence weighted by their distance from events and their writers' interests, and official interpretations issued under authoritarian rule are evidence of what the state required, not necessarily of what audiences heard. Compare at least two scholarly accounts that disagree, and note the disagreement rather than averaging it away. Keep organised notes with dates and sources attached to each claim, so that when accounts conflict, and they will, the conflict itself is documented instead of smoothed into a single confident story.

### Q: How do I tell sincere protest from marketed rebellion?

Sincerity itself cannot be inspected, so the workable test examines conduct, cost and consequence instead of a performer's inner state. Conduct asks whether the political commitment appears across the career, in repertoire, alliances and organisational work, or only in the publicity cycle of one release. Cost asks what the performer risked or gave up: bookings declined, markets closed, legal exposure accepted, income redirected to the cause, since marketed rebellion is usually engineered to cost nothing and to offend only audiences the marketing plan had already written off. Consequence asks what the work did in the world, which organisations used it, which funds it raised, which vocabularies it carried into public argument. A performer can pass all three tests and still sell records happily, because sincerity and commerce are not opposites. A performer who fails all three may still have written a song a movement finds useful, since audiences regularly rescue work from its marketing, and that rescue is also part of the record.

### Q: Which political recordings are essential listening?

Essential listening in political repertoire is best organised by function rather than by fame, so that each recording teaches a mechanism. Begin with assembly: the freedom songs recorded from the American civil rights movement, where gospel forms were adapted in mass meetings for courage and coordination. Continue with the state voice: Umm Kulthum's broadcast performances, which show a singer assembling a transnational audience from the centre of power. Add the counter state: Fela Kuti's long Afrobeat recordings of the 1970s, built in Nigerian Pidgin to hold a Lagos crowd against a military government. Add coded and exiled repertoires: Chilean Nueva Cancion as carried by exile ensembles after 1973, and South African jazz and choral recordings made under and against apartheid. Add the anthem and the mass festival, Estonian song festival recordings, and the labour songbook tradition carried by Pete Seeger. Heard in that order, the set demonstrates gathering, memory, coding, sponsorship and refusal more clearly than any ranking of greatest tracks.

### Q: How should I listen to music made under censorship?

Listening to music made under censorship is an exercise in hearing two things at once: the work, and the conditions that decided what the work could be. Begin by learning the conditions concretely, which office licensed performance, which campaign was running in the year of composition, what happened to the composer's colleagues, because vague awareness of oppression produces vague listening. Attend to the official interpretation attached to major works and treat it as a document of the system rather than as a key to the score. Notice absences as evidence: the unrecorded repertoire, the emigrated performers, the subjects no one set. Resist the twin temptations that ruin such listening, hearing secret resistance in every bar, which flatters the listener and fabricates codes, and hearing only compliance, which ignores the private works, the drawer manuscripts and the testimony of those who were there. Where scholars disagree, as they do over Shostakovich, programme the disagreement itself: hear the finale both ways, and let the undecidability be the historical fact it is.

### Q: How did radio broadcasting shape political music?

Radio broadcasting reshaped political music by giving every state a concert hall in every home and every movement a problem of access. A broadcaster's music policy works through proportion: hours assigned to folk heritage, light entertainment, serious repertoire and imports together define the nation's official sound more effectively than any manifesto. State systems used the schedule to teach unity, from the improving mission of the early British Broadcasting Corporation to the ceremonial programming of authoritarian broadcasters and the independence schedules of postcolonial stations. Umm Kulthum's monthly broadcasts assembled an Arab audience across borders that diplomacy could not cross, proving the medium's power at its summit. Exclusion from the airwaves pushed styles into border stations, imported records, pirate transmitters and cassette cultures, so a country's unofficial listening maps its broadcaster's failures. Radio also fixed performances into repeatable objects, weakening local variation while multiplying reach, and made the playlist, rather than the concert, the primary site where musical politics was administered.

### Q: What happened to music after independence movements won?

Victory converted movement repertoire into state property, with gains and deformations that followed predictably from the conversion. The new government adopted an anthem, often a movement song edited for office, founded national orchestras, dance companies and broadcasters, and put the liberation repertoire into schools and ceremonies where a generation learned it as official memory. Musicians who had been prosecuted became honoured founders, salaried and archived. The deformations were structural. Songs of a coalition were edited toward the ruling faction's version, heroes of rival tendencies faded from the songbooks, and regional styles were standardised into a national sound by state arrangers. Repertoires that had thrived on deniability struggled in the open, where a coded chorus of the struggle years had to survive as literal state policy. Where economies later contracted, the ensembles shrank, musicians emigrated into diaspora circuits, and the founding repertoire survived as heritage, honoured at anniversaries and detached from the organisation that once sang it nightly. Independence, for music, is not an ending but a change of employer.
