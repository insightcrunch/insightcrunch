On 23 April 1985 a five judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court of India dismissed the appeal of Mohd. Ahmed Khan, an Indore advocate, and confirmed that his divorced wife Shah Bano Begum could claim maintenance from him under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, a secular provision written for every citizen unable to maintain herself. Thirteen months later, on 19 May 1986, the President of India gave assent to the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act, a statute whose operative clauses removed divorced Muslim women from the reach of that same section and reassigned their post-divorce support to the iddat period, to relatives, and to the state Wakf Boards. The claim defended in this article is that the episode joining those two dates did more than settle one woman's maintenance or reverse one judgment. The Shah Bano case renegotiated, at the level of architecture, the relationship between religious family law and state intervention in India. The 1985 verdict stated one architectural position: a universal welfare statute enacted by Parliament could reach inside a religious community's family law wherever destitution threatened, and no rule of that family law could bar the door. The 1986 Act stated the opposite position: a legislature responding to a mobilised community could redraw the boundary, restore the family law's own allocation of obligation, and confine the secular statute at the community's edge. Every later contest in this field, the Danial Latifi judgment of 28 September 2001, the Shayara Bano judgment of 22 August 2017, and the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act of 2019, has argued from one of these two poles or against the other. The episode is therefore best read neither as a simple victory and defeat for women's rights nor as a simple episode of electoral calculation, though both readings capture real evidence. It is best read as the founding contest of a two pole architecture that Indian law and politics have continued to inhabit, amend, evade, and reopen for more than three decades.

## From Indore to the Constitution Bench: The Litigation of 1978 to 1985

Shah Bano Begum married Mohd. Ahmed Khan in Indore in 1932. The marriage lasted forty six years. Khan, a practising advocate, took a second wife, and in 1975 he drove Shah Bano and the children of the first marriage from the matrimonial home. In April 1978 she petitioned the Judicial Magistrate at Indore under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure 1973, the provision that obliges a person of sufficient means to maintain a wife, including a divorced wife who has not remarried, who is unable to maintain herself. The magistrate's order of 6 August 1979 awarded her twenty five rupees a month. On revision, the Madhya Pradesh High Court raised the award to one hundred seventy nine rupees and twenty paise a month, a figure that recorded, in its awkward precision, the distance between what the petitioner had sought and what a court could measure of a professional man's means. In November 1978, while the maintenance proceedings were alive, Khan pronounced talaq and divorced her. He had paid a dower, the mehr recorded in the marriage contract, of three thousand rupees, and he had paid maintenance during the three months of iddat that follow a divorce under Muslim law. His case, carried upward through the appellate courts, was that those two payments exhausted his obligation: Muslim law fixed the duty, the duty had been discharged, and Section 125 could not be used to extend it.

The Supreme Court heard the appeal as a Constitution Bench because the question was constitutional in weight even though the statute was a criminal procedure provision of modest sums. Section 125 sits in Chapter IX of the Code, sections 125 to 128, a chapter whose purpose the Court had already described in earlier maintenance litigation as the prevention of vagrancy and destitution. The provision is religion neutral on its face. It speaks of wives, children, and parents, and it fixes its inquiry on means and neglect rather than on ritual status. Khan's argument required the Court to read an exception into that neutrality: where a religious family law prescribed its own complete scheme of post-divorce obligation, the secular chapter should stop at the scheme's boundary. Two earlier lines of authority pulled against each other. In Bai Tahira v Ali Hussain Fidaalli Chothia, decided in 1979, and in Fuzlunbi v K. Khader Vali, decided in 1980, benches of the Court had already held that Section 125 operated regardless of the husband's personal law and that payments under that law could be set off against, but could not extinguish, the statutory duty where the divorced woman remained unable to maintain herself. In each case the Court treated the Code as a welfare measure cutting across community boundaries. The Constitution Bench of 1985 was therefore not writing on blank paper. It was asked to confirm or to unsettle a direction that smaller benches had set, and the political system understood the stakes: whichever way the five judges ruled, the ruling would define whether a uniform statutory floor of maintenance existed beneath every religious family law in the country.

### How much maintenance did the courts award Shah Bano at each stage?

The Indore magistrate awarded Shah Bano twenty five rupees a month on 6 August 1979. The Madhya Pradesh High Court raised the award to one hundred seventy nine rupees and twenty paise a month. The Supreme Court confirmed the enhanced figure on 23 April 1985, holding the husband's obligation intact under Section 125.

Those were the three orders in sequence: the magistrate's award of 6 August 1979 in her Section 125 petition, filed in April 1978 after Mohd. Ahmed Khan expelled her from the Indore home in 1975; the High Court's enhancement on the husband's revision; and the Constitution Bench's confirmation in 1985.

The figures matter because they expose the scale of the constitutional question relative to the money at stake. One hundred seventy nine rupees a month was, even by the standards of 1979, a subsistence sum, and the husband who resisted it was a man of professional standing who had contracted a second marriage. The litigation's energy came from principle rather than amount. Khan's defence was a boundary defence: the mehr of three thousand rupees and the iddat maintenance represented, under the law he invoked, the full and final settlement of a husband's obligation, and a magistrate's court applying a criminal procedure chapter had no warrant to rewrite that settlement. Shah Bano's claim was equally a boundary claim from the other side: she had been married for forty six years, she had borne and raised the children of the marriage, she had no independent means, and the statute book of the republic contained a provision addressed to exactly her condition, written without reference to the religion of the claimant. The courts below the Supreme Court resolved the dispute by the ordinary tools of the Code. The magistrate asked whether she was unable to maintain herself and whether the husband had means and had neglected her; both answers were yes. The High Court asked whether the sum matched the parties' circumstances; it adjusted the sum upward. Neither court treated the husband's religious law as a jurisdictional wall. That ordinariness is itself evidence for the architectural reading: by 1978 the secular maintenance chapter already functioned, in the lower judiciary, as a floor beneath family law, and the appeal to Delhi asked the highest court to say whether that floor was law or error.

## The Judgment of 23 April 1985 Stated by Its Holding

The bench that decided Mohd. Ahmed Khan v Shah Bano Begum consisted of Chief Justice Y. V. Chandrachud and Justices D. A. Desai, O. Chinnappa Reddy, E. S. Venkataramiah, and Rangnath Misra. The judgment of 23 April 1985, delivered by the Chief Justice for a unanimous court, dismissed the appeal. Its holdings can be stated in sequence, and the sequence matters, because later controversy often attacked a version of the judgment that the text does not contain. First, the Court held that Section 125 is a measure of social justice embodied in the Code, that it applies to every citizen irrespective of religion, and that it cuts across the barriers of caste, creed, and community wherever the condition of inability to maintain oneself is proved. Second, the Court held that the Code's provision prevails where a conflict is alleged with family law, because the statutory duty rests on the fact of destitution and neglect rather than on the marital tie itself; a divorced wife who has not remarried remains a wife for the purposes of the section by its own express explanation. Third, the Court held that mehr, the dower payable under Muslim law, is not a sum payable on divorce within the meaning of Section 127(3)(b), the clause that cancels a maintenance order where the woman has received the whole sum payable under her customary or personal law on divorce. Mehr, the Court reasoned, is an obligation arising from the marriage contract itself, a mark of respect to the wife, and not a consideration for, or a composition of, the divorce. Fourth, the Court addressed the Quran directly. Citing the aiyats 241 and 242 of Surah Al-Baqara in the translation of Abdullah Yusuf Ali, the judgment held that the Quranic text itself enjoins a provision for divorced women, and that there was accordingly no conflict between the Code and the scripture properly read: the claim that Section 125 offended Muslim law failed on the law's own terms. Fifth, and most quoted in the years that followed, the Court expressed regret that Article 44 of the Constitution, the directive principle committing the state to endeavour to secure a uniform civil code, had remained a dead letter, and observed that a common civil code would help the cause of national integration by removing disparate loyalties to laws with competing ideologies.

Each holding is a holding about architecture. The first two assign the secular statute a reach that no denominational rule can contract. The third prevents a private law payment from being recharacterised as a buyout of the public duty. The fourth contests, on the community's own textual ground, the assertion that intervention is intrusion, an assertion the Court could have avoided by resting on statutory supremacy alone. The fifth connects a maintenance chapter of a procedural code to the largest unfinished item of the constitutional settlement on religion and law, the uniform civil code that the Constituent Assembly had placed in the directive principles in November 1948 after the debates traced elsewhere in this pillar through [the post-1947 plural-civilisational settlement](/2018/12/28/secularism-constituent-assembly-debates/). A reader who confines attention to the operative order, maintenance confirmed at the High Court's figure, will miss the judgment's real force. The operative order fed one elderly woman. The reasoning announced that beneath every system of religious family law in India there lay a statutory floor, administered by magistrates, indifferent to theology, and triggered by need. That announcement, not the one hundred seventy nine rupees, is what the political mobilisation of 1985 and 1986 set out to answer.

### What did the Supreme Court actually hold in the Shah Bano judgment?

The Constitution Bench held that Section 125 applied to Shah Bano as a secular welfare provision, that mehr was a marriage obligation and not a payment on divorce, and that the Quran's verses on provision for divorced women supported rather than barred her claim to maintenance from her former husband.

In the fuller statement of 23 April 1985, the unanimous bench held that Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, a secular maintenance provision, applied to a divorced Muslim woman unable to maintain herself, that the dower called mehr was not a payment on divorce capable of cancelling the statutory duty, and that the Quran's own verses on provision for divorced women did not conflict with the Code.

The judgment's discipline deserves notice, because the public argument of the following year frequently described a more intrusive decision than the one delivered. The Court did not invalidate any rule of Muslim law. It did not direct Parliament to enact a uniform civil code; it regretted an inoperative directive principle, in language that several earlier benches had also used. It did not award maintenance for life in defiance of the iddat structure; it confirmed a modest monthly sum under a chapter that any magistrate could vary or cancel on changed circumstances, including on the wife's remarriage. The intrusion, if intrusion it was, consisted in the hierarchy the Court asserted: where Parliament has legislated a welfare duty in general terms, community law may supplement the duty but may not repeal it for the community's own members. That hierarchy is the first pole of the architecture. Its opposite number, enacted thirteen months later, would assert that Parliament could itself perform the repeal, community by community, when persuaded that the general statute had trespassed on ground a religious tradition claimed as its own.

## Architectural Position One: A Secular Floor Beneath Every Family Law

To see why the 1985 position is architectural rather than merely doctrinal, consider what it implies for the three institutions it touches. For the magistracy, the position converts a dispersed welfare chapter into a jurisdiction of first resort for divorced women of every community; the forum is local, cheap, and summary, and its inquiry is factual. For Parliament, the position treats legislative silence as acquiescence in universality: the Code of 1973 had re-enacted the maintenance chapter with the divorced wife expressly included, and the Court read that inclusion as deliberate. For religious family law, the position assigns a defined and subordinate place: the tradition governs the making and dissolution of marriage, the incidents of dower and inheritance, and the internal economy of the family, but the moment a divorced woman faces destitution, a public duty overlays the tradition's allocation. The floor does not replace the building. It does, however, decide who bears the cost when the building's own arrangements fail a dependent member, and it answers: the former husband, as a matter of public law, before the cost can fall on the woman herself or on the state.

The Nehruvian settlement had handled religious family law asymmetrically, and the asymmetry is the baseline against which 1985 must be measured. Between 1955 and 1956 Parliament comprehensively codified and reformed Hindu law, on marriage, succession, adoption, and guardianship, while leaving Muslim law substantially uncodified, a distribution of reforming energy whose logic is examined in this series through [the Nehruvian-era institutional baseline](/2019/01/25/nehruvian-era-settlement-three-tensions/). The stated reasons at the time mixed principle and prudence: a majority community's law could be reformed by a legislature in which that community predominated, while reform of a minority's law, a decade after Partition, risked reading as majoritarian coercion. The result was a settlement under which the state's reforming hand pressed hard on one tradition and lightly on another, and under which the maintenance floor of the criminal procedure codes, inherited from the Code of 1898 and renewed in 1973, did quiet universal work beneath both. The Shah Bano judgment made that quiet work visible and principled. It said, in effect, that the universal floor was not an accident of drafting but the operative expression of the republic's commitment that no citizen's subsistence after divorce would be determined solely by a denominational rule. Once stated in those terms by a Constitution Bench, the floor could no longer be treated as background. It had become a position, and positions in a democracy invite counter-positions.

The counter-position also had a prehistory, and intellectual honesty requires stating it at strength. The claim that family law is constitutive of religious identity, and that state alteration of family law is therefore a graver intrusion than state regulation of commerce or crime, runs through the Constituent Assembly debates of 1948, where Muslim members including Naziruddin Ahmad and Hussain Imam defended personal law as an essential religious practice while accepting, in the event, the placement of the uniform civil code among the non-justiciable directive principles. The claim resurfaced in the parliamentary debates on the Hindu Code Bill, where opponents asked why reform should begin with one community, and it structured the All India Muslim Personal Law Board's self-understanding from the Board's founding in 1972 as a watchdog body charged with defending the shariat's application to Indian Muslims against legislative encroachment. On this view, the 1985 judgment's fourth holding, its reading of the Quran, was the deepest provocation of all: a bench of the secular state, none of whose members professed Islam, had certified what the Quran did and did not enjoin, and had done so in the course of overruling the community's own interpretive authorities. Defenders of the judgment replied that the Court had merely answered the argument presented to it, since Khan's case rested on a claimed conflict between the Code and Islam, and that the verses cited were the same verses reformist Muslim scholars had long cited. Both accounts are documented. The architectural reading does not require choosing between them; it requires noticing that the dispute was, from its first day, a dispute about who holds interpretive and legislative authority over a community's family law, and that the maintenance of one Indore pensioner was the occasion rather than the substance of the quarrel.

## The Mobilisation of 1985 to 1986: A Community Answers a Court

The All India Muslim Personal Law Board responded to the judgment within weeks. Its statements of May 1985 characterised the verdict as an interference in the shariat and demanded legislative correction, and the demand was carried through the second half of 1985 by a campaign of public meetings, resolutions by religious and civic bodies, and deputations to the government of Rajiv Gandhi. The campaign's most effective parliamentary voice was Syed Shahabuddin, diplomat turned politician and editor, whose interventions framed the issue as one of minority identity under majoritarian pressure: a community that had accepted the Indian republic's Constitution, he argued, had done so on the assurance that its religious law would remain its own, and a maintenance chapter could not be allowed to dissolve that assurance by instalments. Inside the government, the contrary case was argued in Parliament by Arif Mohammad Khan, a minister of state, whose speech defending the judgment on the floor of the Lok Sabha in 1985 reasoned from the Quran and from the welfare purpose of Section 125, and who maintained that the verdict protected precisely the women the community's leadership claimed to defend. The coexistence of those two Muslim voices, a minister of the Crown's successor government defending the Court against a movement that claimed to speak for the community, is the single most important fact about the politics of that year. The argument was never between a community and an outside world. It was an argument within the community about who spoke for it, conducted simultaneously in the idiom of theology, of minority rights, and of party competition.

Shah Bano herself was overtaken by the campaign that bore her name. In November 1985 a public statement attributed to her, issued at Indore under the eyes of community leaders, distanced her from the judgment obtained in her name and associated her with the demand that the verdict not be applied to Muslims; historians of the episode, beginning with Asghar Ali Engineer's contemporaneous collection The Shah Bano Controversy of 1987, have recorded both the statement and the pressure surrounding its making. She died in Indore in 1992, having become a symbol in an argument whose terms she did not set. The government, meanwhile, moved through the winter of 1985 and 1986 from defence of the judgment toward its legislative reversal. The calculations attributed to the Rajiv Gandhi administration by its critics are familiar: a Congress government holding more than four hundred Lok Sabha seats after the election of December 1984 nonetheless faced assembly contests in which Muslim voters were pivotal, feared the consolidation of Muslim opinion behind opposition parties, and treated the Personal Law Board's campaign as a signal of electoral cost. Defenders of the government's shift, including speakers in the 1986 debates, stated a different rationale in public: that a judgment perceived by a minority as an assault on its law would corrode the minority's confidence in the constitutional order, that Parliament and not the Court was the proper forum for altering family law, and that the proposed statute protected divorced women by a different route, through the iddat provision, the dower, and the obligations of relatives and Wakf Boards. Both the cynical and the stated rationale belong in the record, attributed to their holders. The architectural reading adds only this: whichever motive is credited, the instrument chosen was not an appeal, a review, or a constitutional amendment. It was an ordinary statute that redrew, for one community, the boundary the Court had declared uniform. The precedent thereby set concerned the permeability of every future judgment in this field to organised legislative response.

On 1 February 1986, in the same political season, a district court at Faizabad ordered the locks on the Babri Masjid at Ayodhya opened to Hindu worshippers, an event in the long administrative history of that site whose template had been set in 1949, as the preceding article in this pillar records through [the religious-place-state-administration template](/2020/04/14/1949-babri-masjid-lock-up-politics/). Commentators across the spectrum, from Engineer's 1987 volume onward, have linked the two decisions of that season, the unlocking at Ayodhya and the reversal of Shah Bano, as a paired balancing by a government anxious to hold Hindu and Muslim constituencies simultaneously. The linkage is an interpretation, advanced by named critics and disputed by the government's defenders, and it should be carried as interpretation rather than as settled fact. What is settled is the sequence and its consequence: within fifteen weeks of each other, the state moved the Ayodhya dispute toward Hindu access and moved the maintenance dispute toward communal restoration, and the opposition Bharatiya Janata Party drew from the pair a durable charge of governmental appeasement in both directions at once, a charge that fed the mass politics of the following years.

### Who defended the judgment inside the government in 1985?

Arif Mohammad Khan, a minister of state in Rajiv Gandhi's government, defended the judgment in the Lok Sabha in 1985. He argued from the Quranic verses on provision for divorced women and from the welfare purpose of Section 125, maintaining that the verdict protected Muslim women rather than injuring their community.

In that 1985 speech on the floor of the Lok Sabha, Khan argued from the Quranic verses on provision for divorced women and from the welfare purpose of Section 125, and maintained that the verdict protected divorced Muslim women rather than injuring the community whose law was said to be threatened by the ruling.

Khan's position inside the cabinet did not prevail. The Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 25 February 1986 by Z. R. Ansari, the minister in charge of the measure, and Khan's dissent from the government's new line ended, in the months after the Act, with his departure from the council of ministers. His 1985 speech survives in the parliamentary record as the fullest official statement of the first architectural position ever made by a member of the government that then abandoned it, and it is the standard against which the 1986 debates can be measured: the government's own earlier argument had conceded nothing to the claim that the Code trespassed on the Quran, and the bill's supporters were therefore obliged to argue not that the judgment was wrong in law but that its consequences for communal confidence required its displacement. That displacement, and the doctrine of legislative supremacy over family law settlements that it implied, is the bridge from the mobilisation to the statute.

## The Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act 1986, Section by Section

The Act received the President's assent on 19 May 1986. Its long title announced a statute to protect the rights of Muslim women who have been divorced by, or have obtained divorce from, their husbands, and its text ran to seven sections. Section 2 defined its terms, including the iddat period: three menstrual courses after the divorce where the woman is subject to menstruation, three lunar months where she is not, and, where she is pregnant at the divorce, the period until the child's delivery. Section 3 provided that a divorced woman is entitled to a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance to be made and paid to her within the iddat period by her former husband, to her mehr and to the properties given to her before, at, or after the marriage by her relatives, friends, the husband, or the husband's relatives and friends, and it armed the entitlement with a magistrate's enforcement order on her application. Section 4 addressed the woman who, after the iddat period, has not remarried and is unable to maintain herself: the magistrate may order maintenance from her relatives in proportion to the property they would inherit from her, beginning with her children and parents, and failing relatives able to pay, from the State Wakf Board established in the state where she resides. Section 5 allowed a divorced couple, by joint declaration at the first hearing of an application under the Act, to elect to be governed instead by sections 125 to 128 of the Code. Section 6 conferred rule making power, and Section 7 made transitional provision for pending Section 125 applications.

Read as architecture, the statute performs three operations. It converts an open ended public duty, monthly maintenance until remarriage or death, into a closed settlement concentrated within the iddat period, whose adequacy the magistrate assesses in the divorce's immediate aftermath. It relocates the long tail of obligation from the former husband to the woman's natal and marital kin, ranked by inheritance shares, and from them to a denominational welfare institution, the Wakf Board, so that the community's own structures resume the burden the Code had shifted to an individual husband under public law. And it preserves, in Section 5, an opt-in door back to the secular chapter, available only where both former spouses jointly choose it, a condition that inverts the 1985 default: universality had been the rule and exemption the argument; after May 1986 exemption was the rule and universality required the consent of the very party whose obligation was in issue. Critics of the Act, including members who voted against it and the women's organisations that petitioned against its passage, named each operation plainly in the debates of February to May 1986. Supporters answered that the iddat provision, properly valued, could exceed years of monthly driblets, that the mehr and property clauses secured assets a maintenance order never reached, and that Sections 3 and 4 together formed a complete code of post-divorce support consonant with the community's law. The statute's later career in the courts, examined below, would vindicate fragments of each side's reading in turn.

The map's lesson is the article's claim in miniature. No row of the table closes the argument. The 1986 row answers the 1985 row; the 2001 row answers 1986 by reinterpretation; the 2017 and 2019 rows move the contest from maintenance to the validity and punishment of a divorce practice, carrying the same underlying question, whose law governs the Muslim family when the republic's norms and the tradition's rules diverge, into new doctrinal territory. A settlement that must be re-argued in 2001, 2017, and 2019 is not a settlement that failed once. It is a contested settlement, in the sense this pillar's opening article gives that term through [the contested-settlement framework](/2020/04/09/indian-secularism-contested-settlement/): an arrangement whose terms remain permanently open to renegotiation by courts, legislatures, and movements, each armed with one of the two architectural positions the founding episode supplied.

## Architectural Position Two: The Legislature Redraws the Boundary

The second position deserves the same structural statement as the first, neither caricatured as surrender nor celebrated as restoration. For the magistracy, the 1986 Act narrowed the summary jurisdiction: the magistrate remained the forum, but the inquiry changed from continuing need to a single assessment made at the divorce's edge. For Parliament, the Act asserted a power with implications beyond maintenance: if a general welfare statute could be disapplied to one community by name, then every universal provision touching family life stood subject to community specific revision, and the uniformity of the Code became a default rather than a rule. For religious family law, the Act restored the tradition's own allocation of obligation, iddat support from the husband, kin support thereafter, communal welfare at the margin, and recognised that allocation as the governing scheme for the community's divorced women. The position's defenders, in the Board's statements and in the bill's parliamentary debates, grounded it in constitutional text: Articles 25 and 26 protect religious practice and denominational autonomy, Article 29 protects minority culture, and the directive principle favouring a uniform civil code is, by the Constitution's own Article 37, unenforceable by any court. On that grounding, the 1985 judgment had inverted the constitutional design, treating a non-justiciable aspiration as a warrant for judicial pressure and a procedural code as a lever of uniformity. The position's critics answered from Articles 14, 15, and 21: a classification that left divorced Muslim women, alone among divorced women, outside the general maintenance chapter discriminated on the ground of religion and sex, and denied them the equal protection of a law whose purpose was the prevention of destitution. Both groundings are constitutional. The contest between them is the contest the Constitution's text leaves open, and it is precisely the openness that the architectural reading foregrounds.

Two features of the second position explain its durability as a pole. The first is its theory of representation. The Act's passage rested on a claim, pressed by the Personal Law Board and accepted by the government, that the Board and the movement behind it spoke for Indian Muslims on a question of religious law. That claim was contested at the time, by Arif Mohammad Khan from within the government, by Muslim women's organisations whose petitions reached Parliament, and by reformist scholars such as Asghar Ali Engineer, who argued in The Shah Bano Controversy that the Board represented a clerical and patriarchal stratum rather than the community's whole membership, and that its defence of the tradition doubled as a defence of male privilege within the tradition. The second feature is the position's reversibility. Because the second position was enacted by ordinary legislation, it remained exposed to the judiciary's interpretive power in a way a constitutional amendment would not have been. The exposure was visible in advance: Flavia Agnes, the family law scholar whose essays through the 1990s anatomised the Act's operation, noted that the phrase reasonable and fair provision in Section 3 was an open texture through which a court determined to protect divorced women could drive much of the old law back in. The Latifi bench of 2001 did exactly that. A pole established by statute can be bent by judgment, as a pole established by judgment can be displaced by statute, and the alternation between those two instruments, court and legislature trading the initiative across thirty four years, is the mechanism by which the contested settlement renews itself.

## The Critics on Every Side: Gender, Community, and the Uses of a Case

No honest account of the episode can arrange its critics on a single axis. The women's movement of the 1980s, which had won its spurs in the anti-rape campaigns after the Mathura judgment of 1979 and the criminal law amendments of 1983, received the Shah Bano verdict as an instrument ready to hand: a secular maintenance right, confirmed by a Constitution Bench, that any divorced woman could invoke in a local court without entering the thickets of her community's law. Organisations of women, including Muslim women's groups in Bombay and Delhi whose names recur in the petitioning record of 1985 and 1986, demanded the verdict's defence and, after the bill appeared, its withdrawal. Their framing was the gender-justice framing, and its evidence was the material condition of divorced women: the iddat period ends, the mehr is often nominal or deferred beyond reach, relatives ordered to pay under Section 4 were frequently as poor as the claimant, and Wakf Boards, underfunded and litigated against, proved reluctant payers of last resort. The framing's weakness, which its own best exponents acknowledged, was strategic rather than evidentiary. A campaign heard by the wider public as an attack on Muslim law could consolidate the very clerical authority it opposed, and could lend the Hindu majoritarian movement, then gathering force around Ayodhya, a borrowed vocabulary of women's rights that its own record on gender did not sustain. Agnes's essays of the period and after are the classic statement of this doubleness: she defended the maintenance right, documented the Act's failures in the lower courts, and simultaneously warned that the uniform civil code demand, in the mouths of the majoritarian right, was an instrument aimed at a minority rather than at patriarchy, which flourished unexamined inside the majority's own codified law.

Engineer's reading cuts across the same field from within Muslim reformism. A Bohra scholar and activist who had himself faced excommunication for challenging clerical authority, Engineer argued in 1987 that the Quran, read whole, supported a generous post-divorce provision, that the ulema's campaign defended institutional power rather than scripture, and that the government's reversal betrayed both the Constitution and the women. His volume collected the debate's documents, the Board's statements, the parliamentary speeches, the press commentary, and remains the primary contemporaneous archive, which is why it is named among this article's mandatory sources. Werner Menski, writing in Modern Indian Family Law in 2001, placed the episode in a longer jurisprudential frame: Indian family laws, he argued, had always developed by a process of angrezi shariat and its analogues, state law and religious norm continuously reshaping each other, and the Shah Bano sequence was a particularly visible round of a negotiation as old as the colonial codifications. The political-strategic reading, finally, belongs to the opposition parties and to much editorial commentary of 1986: the reversal was read as a transaction, Muslim votes purchased with a statute, and the unlocking of the Babri Masjid gates in the same season was folded into the same ledger as a payment to the other side. That reading's evidence is circumstantial but not negligible, and it shaped the Bharatiya Janata Party's self-presentation as the party of genuine secularism against a Congress it charged with pseudo-secular bargaining, a presentation whose organisational vehicle had been founded in 1980 and whose mass phase, the Ayodhya mobilisation led by L. K. Advani from 1989, drew directly on the accumulated charges of the 1980s, as this series records through [the post-1989 explicit-Hindutva phase context](/2019/10/05/1980-founding-bjp-explained/) and [the post-1989 Hindutva mass-mobilisation context](/2019/10/10/advani-rath-yatra-ayodhya-movement/).

### Why did the women's movement distrust the uniform civil code demand?

Flavia Agnes and other family law scholars argued that the uniform civil code demand, raised most loudly during the Ayodhya mobilisation, targeted the Muslim minority more than it pursued gender justice, since the majority's own codified Hindu law retained serious inequalities in property, guardianship, and marriage that its champions left unexamined.

Agnes and other family law scholars of the 1980s and 1990s documented that the uniform civil code, demanded most loudly by the Hindu majoritarian movement during the Ayodhya years, was advanced as an instrument against the Muslim minority rather than as a programme for gender justice within every community's law, including the majority's own codified Hindu law, whose provisions on property and guardianship retained deep inequalities of their own.

The adjudication this article owes the reader can begin here, because the three framings divide the evidence rather than the facts. No serious party disputes the chronology, the texts, or the votes. The gender-justice framing owns the evidence of women's material outcomes, and on that evidence the 1986 Act, as administered in its first decade, left many divorced Muslim women worse off than the Code would have done, a finding the Latifi litigation itself presupposed. The political-strategic framing owns the evidence of timing and constituency management, and no chronology of 1985 and 1986 can be written without it. The architectural framing owns the evidence neither of the others can digest: the persistence of the contest across four decades and three further national moments, long after the electoral calculations of 1986 were spent and the original litigants were dead. A reading that explains only 1986 will not explain 2001 or 2017. A reading that explains the whole trajectory must treat 1985 and 1986 as the fixing of two durable positions between which Indian institutions continue to oscillate, and that is the reading defended here.

## Danial Latifi, 28 September 2001: The Act Read Against Itself

Danial Latifi, the senior advocate who had argued Shah Bano's case in 1985, returned to the Supreme Court as petitioner to challenge the 1986 Act's constitutional validity after its passage, and the challenge waited fifteen years for its answer. On 28 September 2001 a five judge Constitution Bench, in Danial Latifi v Union of India, upheld the Act and, in the same movement, transformed its meaning. The Court's reasoning proceeded in three steps. First, a statute must be read, where its language permits, to avoid unconstitutionality, and the Act could be read so. Second, the reasonable and fair provision that Section 3 requires the husband to make and pay within the iddat period is not confined to the needs of the iddat period itself: a divorced woman's provision must extend to her future needs, for the whole of her life until remarriage, quantified and discharged within the iddat window. On that construction, the Act yielded a lump sum whose substance corresponded to the maintenance the Code would have delivered by instalments, and the charge that the Act condemned divorced Muslim women to destitution failed. Third, the Court reasoned in explicitly welfare terms: the solution of the community's divorced women's difficulty did not lie in declaring the Act void, which would leave the women remediless, but in construing the Act so that its promise of protection was real. Latifi himself, the petitioner, thus lost the challenge and won the substance, a doubleness the judgment's critics and admirers have both remarked.

The Latifi construction is the architectural contest's most instructive moment, because it demonstrates the judiciary's counter-move to legislative reversal. Parliament in 1986 had used the form of a community specific statute to displace a judgment; the Court in 2001 used the technique of reading down, or rather reading up, to restore the displaced substance inside the displacing text. Neither institution acknowledged defeat. The Act stands on the statute book unrepealed, its Section 3 glossed to mean what its movers in 1986 had insisted it did not need to mean because the Code's approach was wrong in principle. Magistrates across India applied the glossed Act, and later benches extended its logic: in Iqbal Bano v State of Uttar Pradesh in 2007 the Court confirmed the wide reach of Section 125 proceedings, and in Shabana Bano v Imran Khan, decided on 4 December 2009, the Court held that a divorced Muslim woman's petition under Section 125 remained maintainable so long as she did not remarry, the 1986 Act notwithstanding, where she had not obtained relief under that Act. The boundary drawn in 1986 thus became, in practice, a boundary with gates, and the gates were manned by the lower judiciary applying a Constitution Bench's construction. Whether Latifi represents the first pole's victory inside the second pole's fortress, or a stable compromise in which each pole keeps its form and yields its substance, is a genuine interpretive dispute among scholars, and the article marks it as disputed. Menski's negotiation model predicts exactly such hybrid outcomes. Agnes, documenting outcomes for litigants, treated the gloss as a real but fragile gain, dependent on lawyers who knew to plead it and magistrates willing to quantify future need generously. The government's law officers, for their part, could maintain that no judgment had struck down Parliament's Act. Every institution preserved its position. The contest continued.

## The Act in the Lower Courts, 1986 to 2001: Fifteen Years of Evasion and Return

The statute's first fifteen years supply the empirical test of the two parliamentary cases made for and against it in 1986. The supporters' case predicted that a fair provision assessed at divorce, together with mehr and property, would leave divorced women secured at a stroke. The critics' case predicted destitution administered by reluctant relatives and insolvent Wakf Boards. The reported decisions of the High Courts through the late 1980s and 1990s show a judiciary unwilling to let the critics' prediction come wholly true. Several High Courts, reading Section 3 before any Constitution Bench had glossed it, held that the reasonable and fair provision must be measured against the woman's needs beyond the iddat period, since a provision exhausted in three months could not answer the statute's own protective title. Other courts quantified the iddat obligation by capitalising years of notional monthly maintenance into a single sum, arriving by arithmetic at figures indistinguishable from a generous Section 125 award. The Kerala and Bombay High Courts were prominent in this expansive line, and the Andhra Pradesh and Gujarat courts contributed rulings on the enforceability of the mehr and property clauses. Where courts read the Act narrowly, the consequences the critics had forecast appeared in the record: applications against Wakf Boards that pleaded empty coffers, relatives whose inherited shares were theoretical, and women returned to the magistrate's court in second and third rounds of litigation that the summary Code chapter had been designed to foreclose. The pattern across the fifteen years is neither the supporters' vindication nor uniform failure. It is a record of institutional resistance, in which the forum the Act had retained, the magistracy and the High Courts supervising it, worked the statute's open phrases toward the outcomes the displaced judgment had mandated. That resistance is a datum for the architectural reading: the first position, expelled from the statute book's text in 1986, survived inside the judiciary's habits of construction, and Latifi in 2001 ratified at the summit what the lower courts had practised at the base.

The Personal Law Board's own trajectory across the same years completes the picture of a pole under strain. The Board defended the Act as a settlement and resisted each expansive construction as a fresh encroachment, intervening in litigation and restating its doctrine that maintenance beyond iddat formed no part of the shariat's scheme. Yet the Board also confronted, from within the community, a generation of Muslim women's organisations that refused the choice between faith and maintenance. The Bharatiya Muslim Mahila Andolan, founded in 2007 by Zakia Soman and Noorjehan Safia Niaz, would later campaign simultaneously against instant triple talaq and against the majoritarian framing of reform, demanding codification of Muslim family law from within an Islamic framework of justice. Its emergence belongs to the next decade, but its genealogy runs back to the petitioning women of 1985 and 1986 whose existence the Board's claim to speak for the community had required the public to forget. A researcher tracing that genealogy in the Board's partially preserved deliberation records, and in the press archives of the campaign years, meets the practical problem of protecting fragile documentary trails, the same problem of source protection addressed for journalists in the account published at [the VaultBook guide to protecting sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), where the methods for keeping sensitive records safe are described in detail. The archive's partial survival is itself part of the record: the under-cited deliberations of 1985 and 1986, preserved incompletely at the Board's Lucknow offices, document a leadership aware that its claim to represent the community was the campaign's central asset and its central vulnerability.

## Shayara Bano, 22 August 2017: The Contest Moves from Maintenance to Divorce Itself

For three decades the two poles contested the consequences of divorce. In 2017 the contest moved to the divorce's own validity. Shayara Bano of Uttarakhand, divorced by a speed post letter carrying an instant triple talaq, talaq-e-biddat, petitioned the Supreme Court against the practice, and her petition was heard with connected petitions by a five judge Constitution Bench in May 2017. On 22 August 2017 the Court held, by a majority of three to two, that instant triple talaq was unconstitutional and set it aside. The majority was a composite. Justices Rohinton Nariman and Uday Lalit held the practice manifestly arbitrary under Article 14, reasoning that a mode of divorce available at the husband's whim, irrevocable in the instant of its utterance, failed the equality guarantee, and that the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act of 1937, by giving the practice statutory recognition, made it law subject to fundamental rights review. Justice Kurian Joseph concurred in the result on different ground: instant triple talaq, he held, was against the Quran and the shariat itself, and what was bad in theology was bad in law. Chief Justice J. S. Khehar, joined by Justice Abdul Nazeer, dissented: the practice formed part of the community's religious law protected by Article 25, its reform was Parliament's work and not the Court's, and the majority should have injuncted the practice for six months while inviting legislation. The dissent's institutional logic is the second architectural position in judicial dress, deference to the legislature and to the tradition. The majority's two routes to one result, a secular equality route and an internal theological route, between them restate the first position's two strategies of 1985, the universal statute and the scripture read against the custodians.

The judgment's kinship with 1985 is structural and acknowledged on all sides. As in Shah Bano, a bench divided over the source of its authority to act: the text of the Constitution, the text of the Quran, or the claimed boundary between them. As in 1986, the parliamentary sequel arrived within two years, and as in 1986 it chose a form its critics called disproportionate to the judicial holding it claimed to implement. The sequence also displays the contest's migration across subject matter. Maintenance in 1985 concerned the economics of a divorce already valid. The 2017 petitions, brought by women including Shayara Bano, Ishrat Jahan, Gulshan Parveen, Afreen Rahman, and Atiya Sabri, and supported by Muslim women's organisations as interveners, concerned whether a marriage could be ended at all by a unilateral instant formula, and the organisations' presence in the courtroom marked the distance travelled since 1985: the community's women appeared no longer as the objects of a contest between a court and a board, but as litigants and authors of the claim. The Personal Law Board, respondent in defence of the practice, found its representative claim opposed in form as well as in substance. Whatever view is taken of the merits, the architecture of participation had changed, and the change is one of the post-1986 trajectory's settled facts.

## The Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act 2019: Intervention in Penal Form

Parliament enacted the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act in the monsoon session of 2019, after two ordinances of September 2018 and January 2019 and an earlier bill that had passed the Lok Sabha in December 2017 and stalled in the Rajya Sabha. The Act declared a pronouncement of instant triple talaq void and illegal and made it an offence punishable with imprisonment of up to three years and a fine, cognizable on the complaint of the wife or her close relatives and compoundable at her instance, with provision for her subsistence allowance and custody of minor children. The government of Narendra Modi defended the statute as the completion of the 2017 judgment and as a measure of gender justice, and the Prime Minister's Independence Day address of 2019 counted it among his government's emancipatory acts. Critics in the parliamentary debates, including members of the Congress, the Left parties, and the All India Majlis-e-Ittehadul Muslimeen, argued that criminalising a pronouncement the Court had already voided punished a nullity, that imprisonment of the husband destroyed the maintenance and reconciliation prospects the law claimed to protect, and that the singling out of one community's already invalid practice for penal treatment, when desertion and bigamy in other communities attracted no equivalent offence, disclosed a majoritarian purpose. Defenders answered that deterrence required the penal clause, that the wife controlled the complaint and could compound it, and that subsistence and custody provisions answered the destitution argument. Each side's case is on the record of July 2019, and the architectural reading notes what the exchange reveals: by 2019 the first position commanded the legislature itself. The party that had built its rise partly on denouncing the 1986 reversal enacted, a generation later, the most direct statutory intervention in Muslim divorce law since independence, and defended it in the vocabulary of the 1985 judgment. The poles had not changed. Their occupants had.

The uniform civil code thread runs beneath the 2019 moment and connects it to the pillar's wider frame. The code that Article 44 imagines has been demanded, deferred, studied, and promised across the whole period: the Law Commission's consultation paper of 31 August 2018 concluded that a uniform civil code was neither necessary nor desirable at that stage and recommended instead the reform of discriminatory provisions within each family law, a conclusion quoted by every side against the others ever since. The integrated framework through which this series reads the Modi era's renegotiation of the founding tensions, set out in [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/), treats the personal law question as one strand of a general reordering, and the 2019 Act fits that reading: intervention arrived not as uniformity but as targeted legislation on one community's practice, uniformity's rhetoric carrying particularist content. Goa's civil code, inherited from Portuguese rule and retained after 1961, continued to be cited by all parties as precedent, proof, or warning according to need. The code question, like the maintenance question before it, remains a contested settlement whose terms each decade renegotiates without closing.

## Adjudicating the Three Readings: What Each Framing Explains and What It Misses

The gender-justice reading explains the stakes for the women inside the cases and predicts, correctly, the material investigation that any honest sequel must conduct: it asks what divorced women actually received, from husbands, relatives, boards, and magistrates, and its findings disciplines every other reading. Where it underperforms is in explaining the behaviour of institutions that did not share its premises. It cannot say why a Constitution Bench in 2001 saved the very Act the reading condemns, except by treating the saving construction as a defeat disguised, nor why Muslim women's organisations of the 2000s refused the uniform code banner while demanding codified rights within an Islamic frame. The political-strategic reading explains the timing of 1986, the Faizabad conjunction, and the rhetorical uses to which every party put the episode, and it correctly identifies the representative claim, the Board speaks for the community, as the campaign's operative fiction and prize. Where it underperforms is in explaining duration. The electoral arithmetic of 1986 was obsolete by 1989, yet the contest structured litigation in 2001, 2009, and 2017, and legislation in 2019, under governments of opposed coalitions whose strategists had no stake in Rajiv Gandhi's calculations. A transaction cannot explain a tradition. The architectural reading, defended here, explains the duration by treating the episode as the creation of two transferable positions, each with a constitutional grounding, an institutional carrier, and a repertoire of instruments: the universal statute and the saving construction on one side, the community specific statute and the deference doctrine on the other. It explains why the Latifi bench could restore the substance of 1985 while affirming the form of 1986, why the Shayara Bano majority split over its own authority, and why the 2019 legislature intervened in penal form on ground a judgment had already cleared. It also explains the episode's export value as precedent in argument: every later dispute in the pillar, over places of worship, over citizenship's religious filters, over temple entry, is argued by the participants themselves in the grammar of boundary and floor that Shah Bano and its reversal fixed. The architectural reading's admitted limit is that it can sound bloodless beside the record of individual hardship, and the limit is real. Shah Bano Begum's twenty five rupees, raised to one hundred seventy nine, is not an abstraction, and a reading that forgets the sums in admiring the structure has lost the thread. The claim is narrower than a total theory: the episode's long public life, which is the fact a reference article must explain, is the life of an architectural contest, and the human stakes are the reason the contest mattered at each of its renewals.

The scholarly literature's centre of gravity has moved toward this reading by accumulation rather than by manifesto. Engineer's 1987 documentary volume, partisan for reform, nevertheless preserved the materials from which the architectural account is built, including the Board's internal reasoning. Agnes's essays supplied the lower courts' evidence. Menski's Modern Indian Family Law of 2001 generalised the negotiation model across all the personal laws. The comparative frame of this pillar, which reads the republic's management of religion as a settlement perpetually contested rather than a problem solved at the founding, owes its clearest single demonstration to the maintenance contest: four decades, five national moments, two poles, no closure. The same frame governs the pillar's treatment of the Ayodhya dispute from its 1949 administrative origins through the movement years handled in Pillar 6, and the Shah Bano article's place in the pillar is to supply the frame's legal institutional proof, the demonstration that even a decided case and a passed Act, the two most final instruments the republic possesses, could not between them settle the question they jointly posed.

## A Contested Settlement: The Boundary That Every Generation Redraws

What, then, did the episode of 1985 and 1986 renegotiate? It renegotiated the default settings of the Indian state's engagement with religious family law. Before April 1985 the default, visible in the magistrates' routine application of the Code, was universality unexamined: the floor existed because no one had forced the question of its authority. The judgment examined it and grounded it in the Constitution's welfare commitments and, provocatively, in the community's own scripture. The mobilisation and the Act answered by relocating the default: exemption became the examined position, grounded in the Constitution's religious freedom clauses and in a democratic claim about who may speak for a tradition. After May 1986 every participant in the field has had to argue from one default against the other, and the instruments have multiplied: reading down, opting in, codifying from within, voiding a practice, criminalising its utterance. The deeper continuity is the one the founding generation bequeathed when it placed the uniform civil code in the directive principles and the protection of religious practice in the fundamental rights, a deliberate and unstable pairing whose tensions the Constituent Assembly's secularism debates left expressly unresolved, as the account of [the architectural-inheritance framework](/2020/02/19/poona-pact-ambedkar-gandhi-1932/) in the reservation pillar shows for a parallel inheritance. Shah Bano is the maintenance chapter's working out of that pairing, nothing more local and nothing less. The woman whose name the case carries received her maintenance under a judgment the republic then spent thirty four years arguing about, and the argument, not any single outcome within it, is the episode's bequest to Indian public law. The two poles stand. The boundary between a citizen's claim on the general law and a community's claim on its own law is redrawn by each generation that inherits the contest, and the redrawing, conducted in courtrooms and legislatures and movements, is the form the Indian secular settlement takes when it is working as it was built to work: not in repose, but in contention.

## The Long Prehistory: Maintenance Law from the Code of 1898 to the Judgments of 1979 and 1980

The maintenance chapter Shah Bano invoked was older than the republic. The Code of Criminal Procedure of 1898, in its Section 488, had empowered magistrates to order a man of means to pay a monthly allowance to a wife or child unable to maintain themselves, and the colonial state administered that provision across community lines for half a century without treating religious family law as a bar. The provision's theory was frankly fiscal and disciplinary: destitute dependants became charges on the public or recruits to disorder, and the state preferred to fix the cost on the person whose obligation every moral system in the territory recognised. When independent India re-enacted the Code in 1973, Parliament widened the chapter in one decisive respect. The old law had spoken of wives; the new Section 125, through its explanation, expressly included a woman who had been divorced by, or had obtained a divorce from, her husband and had not remarried. The inclusion was debated and deliberate, a legislative judgment that the welfare duty survived the marriage's dissolution. It applied to India entire, and for five years after 1973 it generated the ordinary run of magistrates' orders without national controversy. The question that reached the Constitution Bench in 1985 was therefore not whether Parliament could legislate a post-divorce maintenance duty, Parliament plainly had, but whether a community's law could carve its members out of a duty Parliament had imposed in general terms on grounds of need.

Two Supreme Court decisions at the decade's turn had already answered that question in the negative, and their facts show why the answer kept recurring. In Bai Tahira v Ali Hussain Fidaalli Chothia, decided in 1979, Justice V. R. Krishna Iyer held for the Court that a divorced Muslim woman's claim under Section 125 survived the payment of mehr and of maintenance for the iddat period, treating the statutory right as independent of, and additional to, the sums her community's law provided, and reading Section 127(3)(b) strictly so that only a sum genuinely payable on divorce could cancel the order. In Fuzlunbi v K. Khader Vali, decided in 1980, the Court repeated the holding against a husband who had paid a larger mehr, with Justice Krishna Iyer again insisting that the Code's benign provisions be construed to advance their welfare object and that illusory or token payments could not be dressed as the whole sum payable on divorce. Between them, the two judgments established that the secular floor was already the law of the land half a decade before Shah Bano's appeal was heard, and that a Constitution Bench was being asked in 1985 not to innovate but to ratify or reverse a settled line. That context qualifies the drama of April 1985. The verdict's novelty lay not in its holding but in its scale: a five judge bench, a Chief Justice's opinion, a direct engagement with the Quran, and a public lament over Article 44 converted a line of maintenance doctrine into a national event. The mobilisation that followed was a response to the conversion, to doctrine spoken in the register of constitutional destiny, and the government's eventual answer was addressed to the register as much as to the rule.

## The Personal Law Board and the Doctrine of the Boundary

The All India Muslim Personal Law Board, founded at Bombay in 1972 and consolidated through 1973 under the leadership of Maulana Mufti Atiqur Rahman Usmani and Maulana Syed Shah Minnatullah Rahmani among others, existed before the Shah Bano litigation and explained itself as a defensive institution: a united front of the community's schools of law and religious organisations, created to watch legislatures and courts for encroachments on the shariat's application in India and to coordinate resistance when encroachment threatened. Its doctrine held that Muslim family law was not a department of civil regulation but a part of the faith's practice, protected by the Constitution's guarantee of religious freedom, and that the community's acceptance of the Indian Union after 1947 had rested on an assurance, traced to the Constituent Assembly's settlement, that this domain would be governed by the community's own norms and reformed, if at all, from within. The Board's authority was representative rather than electoral: it spoke through the standing of its member organisations, the seminaries of Deoband and Lucknow, the Jamaat-e-Islami, the Jamiat Ulama-i-Hind, and the personal law scholars attached to them, and its claim to voice the community's mind derived from that clerical and organisational weight. The claim had been tested before 1985 in smaller arenas, in campaigns against adoption law reform and against state level proposals touching Muslim endowments, and the Board entered the Shah Bano year with a practised repertoire: resolutions, delegations to ministers, coordinated Friday sermon themes, and the patient construction of a single public position out of schools that disagreed among themselves on much else.

Stating the Board's doctrine at strength, as the charter of this series requires, means recognising its internal logic. A minority that has surrendered political sovereignty to a democratic state in which it forms roughly an eighth of the electorate holds few domains in which its own norms govern by right. Family law, on the Board's account, is the chief such domain, and each statutory incursion, however humane its occasion, reduces the domain and rehearses the minority for the uniform code that would abolish it. The maintenance of a divorced woman appears, through this lens, as a boundary dispute in which the rupee figure is the least important element: what matters is whether the republic's general law or the community's law decides the obligations that divorce creates. The Board's critics from within the community, Engineer the most systematic, answered the doctrine at its root. The shariat's own texts, they argued, were richer and more generous to divorced women than the Board's defensive codification admitted; the Board's boundary protected, in practice, the male householder's freedom to divorce cheaply, and dressed a patriarchal interest as a minority right; and a community's authenticity could not be made to depend on the destitution of its divorced daughters. Both positions were argued in 1985 and 1986 in pamphlets, in the Urdu and English press, and on the floor of Parliament, and both survived the Act: the Board's archive of the campaign, preserved partially at its Lucknow offices, and the reformist literature that answered it remain the twin primary records from which the episode's inner history must be written.

## The Parliamentary Debates of February to May 1986: The Arguments on the Record

The bill that Z. R. Ansari introduced on 25 February 1986 was debated in a Parliament where the ruling party held one of the largest majorities in the republic's history, and the outcome was never in arithmetical doubt; the debates matter because they fixed, on the official record, the justifications each side would be held to afterward. The government's speakers defended the measure as protection, pointing to its very title, and as restoration: the judgment, they said, had unsettled a community's faith in the constitutional compact; the bill returned divorced women to a scheme of support rooted in their own law, strengthened rather than weakened by the obligations laid on relatives and Wakf Boards; and Parliament would be failing its duty if it left the repair of a social wound to the slow correction of courts. The opposition's case divided by party. Members of the Janata tradition and of the Left attacked the bill as a surrender of the state's duty to women and as an offence against the equality clauses, warning that a maintenance right withdrawn from one community's women by name could be withdrawn from others by precedent. Members speaking for the Hindu right attacked it from the opposite flank, as proof that the ruling party practised a secularism of bargains, and used the debate to press the uniform civil code demand that the government's own minister, Arif Mohammad Khan, had echoed from the other side the year before. Muslim members were themselves divided on the record: the bill's supporters invoked the community's sentiment and the Board's mandate, while dissenting Muslim voices, in the House and in the petitioning outside it, insisted that divorced women's hunger was not a communal symbol and that the House was legislating against the weakest members of the community in the community's name.

Three features of the debates repay attention from the architectural reading. The first is the near absence of the Code's own logic from the government's case: the welfare purpose that had carried Section 125 through two Supreme Court benches was answered not on its merits but by displacement, the claim that another scheme, communally rooted, would do the work instead. The second is the treatment of the judiciary. Speakers for the bill were careful to deny any intention of disrespecting the Court, a courtesy that coexisted with the bill's plain function of reversing the Court's operative rule for the community most affected by it; the episode thereby normalised a technique, reversal by community specific statute under professions of institutional respect, that later decades would reuse. The third is the debate's silence about money at scale. No speaker costed the obligations Section 4 laid on Wakf Boards, institutions whose revenues were largely committed to mosques, graveyards, and stipends and whose capacity to maintain thousands of divorced women existed, as the following years showed, mostly in the statute's imagination. The Act passed both Houses in May 1986 and received assent on 19 May. The government had its settlement. The courts, the Board, and the women in whose names all parties had spoken inherited its administration, and the argument moved, for fifteen years, from the legislature back to the forum the Act had retained and could not control: the magistrate's court, where the settlement's abstractions met individual destitution one application at a time.

## Codification from Within: The Road Not Taken in 1986 and Taken Up Afterward

A third possibility haunted the debates of 1986 without ever commanding a majority: that Muslim family law in India be codified and reformed by legislation as Hindu law had been codified in 1955 and 1956, with the content of the reform drawn from the tradition's own resources and the initiative shared between the state and the community's reformers. The Hindu code precedent cut in two directions. It proved that a religious family law could be restated by Parliament in modern statutory form, on marriage, succession, adoption, and maintenance, without the sky falling on the majority community. It also proved, in the memory of every minority politician, that codification in India had been a majoritarian project applied to the majority's own house, and its extension to a minority's house by the same hands raised the question of consent that the Board's entire doctrine existed to press. The reformers of 1985 and 1986, Engineer among them, proposed to cut the knot by source: a code whose maintenance provisions rested on the Quranic verses the Supreme Court itself had cited, whose divorce provisions required arbitration and reconciliation procedures the scripture described, and whose drafters included the community's own jurists and women's organisations, could claim an Islamic legitimacy no secular judgment could confer and no clerical campaign could easily anathematise. The proposal went nowhere in 1986, when the government's need was for a quick restoration and the Board's need was for no codification at all, since a code, once written, belongs to the legislature that wrote it and can be amended by majorities the community does not control.

The road reappeared, in altered conditions, a generation later. The Bharatiya Muslim Mahila Andolan published in 2014 a draft code of Muslim family law built from Quranic sources, the product of consultations with women across several states, proposing a regulated talaq procedure with mandatory arbitration, a mehr fixed as a genuine economic provision, and maintenance obligations framed as religious duties rather than as concessions to a secular code. Whatever its legislative prospects, the draft changed the debate's geometry by demonstrating, in documentary form, that codification from within was a writable text and not a debating point, and its authors carried the argument into the Shayara Bano litigation as interveners three years later. The Law Commission's consultation paper of 31 August 2018, recommending reform inside each family law over a uniform code imposed across them, converged with the Andolan's method from the state's side. Between them, the draft code of 2014 and the consultation paper of 2018 mark the distance the argument travelled from 1986: the question had ceased to be whether the tradition or the state would govern, the two poles in their pure form, and had become a contest over authorship, over whose hand writes the reform and in whose vocabulary it is justified. The architectural reading accommodates that development without strain. The poles established in 1985 and 1986 named the possible locations of authority, the general law and the communal law. The codification movement proposed a third location, the tradition restated as statute with the community's own reformers holding the pen, and the contest among the three locations is the form the contested settlement took in the new century.

## Section 125 in Practice: How the Floor Worked Before and After the Storm

To grasp what the 1986 Act withdrew, a reader must see the machinery it withdrew women from. A Section 125 application was, by design, among the simplest proceedings in Indian law. The divorced wife filed before the Judicial Magistrate of the district where she lived, where her husband lived, or where they had last lived together; she stated that she was unable to maintain herself, that the respondent had sufficient means, and that he had neglected or refused to maintain her. The magistrate could order interim support and was directed to dispose of the application swiftly, in practice within months rather than the years a civil suit consumed. The ceiling on the monthly sum, fixed at five hundred rupees in the Code as enacted in 1973, was raised by several state amendments and finally removed by the central amendment of 2001, but the ceiling mattered less than the proceeding's character: it was summary, it was local, it required no proof of the marriage's validity beyond the fact of the relationship the respondent had himself lived, and its order was enforceable by warrant and by imprisonment of the defaulter for up to a month for each month of default. The remedy's modesty was its strength. A woman without money for lawyers could reach it, a woman in a distant district could reach it, and the obligation it created ran month by month against a husband whose circumstances the magistrate could revisit. For divorced Muslim women between 1973 and 1986 this machinery stood open on the same terms as for every other citizen, and the reported cases of Bai Tahira and Fuzlunbi show women using it in exactly that ordinary way.

The Act of 1986 replaced that standing invitation with a single settlement event. The woman instead applied, in the first instance, for a composite order: the reasonable and fair provision, the mehr, and the return of property, all to be assessed at the divorce's aftermath and discharged within the iddat period. The design assumed assets to divide and a husband able to capitalise years of support into one payment. Where the assumption held, the settlement could be generous, and the Latifi construction later compelled magistrates to make it generous by measuring the provision against lifetime need. Where the assumption failed, where the husband was poor, evasive, or imprisoned by his own defaults, the design's second tier engaged: relatives ordered to pay in proportion to inheritance shares they might never realise, and behind them the Wakf Board, an institution whose income in most states was committed many times over. The empirical literature that accumulated through the 1990s, in Agnes's case studies and in the reports of women's legal aid organisations, records both branches of that fork, the capitalised settlements that worked and the paper entitlements that dissolved into further litigation. It also records an adaptation nobody had designed: lawyers for divorced Muslim women learned to plead around the Act's edge, filing under the Code where a Section 3 order had not been obtained, pressing the Section 5 election where a husband could be brought to consent, and building, case by case, the practice that Shabana Bano would ratify in 2009. The floor, removed by statute, was rebuilt by advocacy in the interstices of the statute that removed it, and the rebuilding is among the strongest pieces of evidence that the architectural contest of 1985 and 1986 describes a living system rather than a concluded transaction.

## What the Episode Changed for Every Later Argument in This Field

The final service the architectural reading performs is to explain the episode's gravitational pull on disputes that share none of its facts. When the Places of Worship (Special Provisions) Act of 1991 froze the religious character of sites as at 15 August 1947, Parliament legislated a boundary and an exemption, Ayodhya, in a single text, and every subsequent argument over surveys and suits at Varanasi and Mathura has reproduced the Shah Bano grammar: a general rule, a community specific exception, and a contest over which is the rule and which the exception. When the citizenship legislation of 2019 introduced a religious classification into a general statute, the government's defenders argued from welfare and refuge, its critics from the equality clauses, and both sides' pamphlets reached for 1985 and 1986 as the standing illustration of what the republic does when a universal text meets a communal claim. When the temple entry question reached the Constitution Bench in the Sabarimala litigation of 2018, the majority and the dissent divided over the same fork the maintenance contest had mapped: whether a constitutional norm of equality reaches inside a denomination's practice, or whether the denomination's autonomy, and the legislature's deference, mark the norm's limit. None of these disputes is the Shah Bano dispute. All of them are argued in its vocabulary because the episode of 1985 and 1986 was the first occasion on which the republic's institutions, in sequence and in public, stated the two positions in their pure forms and demonstrated that neither a Constitution Bench nor a Parliament with four hundred seats could make its statement final. A field whose founding contest ends in demonstrated non-finality will argue every later case as a reopening, and the reopening habit, visible across this pillar's subjects from Ayodhya to the citizenship register, is the deepest of the episode's consequences.

There is a human residue that no framework should be allowed to tidy away. The litigation began because a woman married in 1932, expelled in 1975, and divorced in 1978 asked a magistrate for a sum on which to live, and it ended, in her lifetime, with the sum confirmed by the highest court and the rule that confirmed it withdrawn for women of her community by the legislature. She was praised as a pioneer and denounced as an instrument, courted by delegations and pressed into a statement against her own judgment, and she died in 1992 in the city where she had filed her petition fourteen years earlier. The two poles her case established have organised a generation of law. The case itself organised nothing for her beyond the maintenance she was owed, and the disproportion between the litigant's stake and the argument's career is the truest measure of what the episode was: a small claim that arrived, by the accidents of bench composition, political season, and communal organisation, at the exact joint where the Indian constitutional settlement was weakest, and split it open for everyone to see. The split has been managed, glossed, legislated around, and argued over ever since. It has not been closed, and the argument of this article is that it was never the kind of split that closing was available for: the Constitution that promises equality before a general law and promises a minority its own law in the same breath has arranged for the argument to recur, and Shah Bano is simply the name the recurrence carries in the law of maintenance.

One further consequence of Latifi deserves record, because it governs the Act's meaning for the women who still litigate under it. The construction the Constitution Bench adopted was not self-executing generosity: it required the magistrate, at the moment of divorce, to quantify a lifetime of need on evidence taken in a summary proceeding, and it required the husband to discharge that capitalised sum within the iddat window on pain of the Act's enforcement machinery. Benches of the High Courts spent the following decade working out the arithmetic, testing claimed provisions against the parties' standard of living during the marriage, the woman's age and health, the children's needs, and the husband's real rather than declared means. Where the arithmetic was done honestly, the Act as glossed could outperform the old monthly regime, sparing the woman the recurring humiliation of executing a small order against a defaulting former husband month after month. Where it was done carelessly, the lump sum froze a lifetime's entitlement at a figure fixed by a bad afternoon's evidence, with no variation clause to correct the error. The Latifi settlement therefore transferred the contest's centre of gravity one final time, from the question of which law applies to the question of how a number is reached, and practitioners' literature of the 2000s, Agnes's later essays among it, treats valuation, not doctrine, as the ground on which divorced Muslim women's outcomes are decided. Doctrine had supplied the poles. Arithmetic decided the cases.

## The Provinces and the Press: How the Argument Travelled Beyond Delhi

The national drama had provincial theatres, and the record from the states disciplines any account that treats 1985 and 1986 as a conversation among Delhi institutions alone. In Madhya Pradesh, where the litigation began, the Indore bar and the state's Urdu press followed the case from its magistrate court days, and the November 1985 statement attributed to Shah Bano was staged in Indore precisely because the petitioner's own city was the one place where her voice could be set against her lawyers'. In Maharashtra, the Bombay women's organisations that had formed during the anti-rape campaigns supplied much of the petitioning energy against the bill, and the state's legal aid networks generated, through the following decade, a disproportionate share of the case law that read Section 3 expansively; the Bombay High Court's willingness to quantify the fair provision against lifetime need anticipated Latifi by years. In Kerala, where Muslim politics ran through the Indian Union Muslim League's participation in coalition governments, the debate took a distinctive institutional form: the League's representatives balanced the Board's doctrine against the disciplines of office, and Kerala's courts contributed rulings on the mehr and property clauses that treated the Act's entitlements as floors for negotiation rather than as ceilings on obligation. In Uttar Pradesh, the Board's Lucknow heartland, the campaign's organisational depth was greatest, and the state's politics absorbed the episode into the larger realignments that would carry the Ayodhya movement and the Mandal upheaval into the elections of 1989 and 1991. The press divided along lines that prefigured the later media map: the English language national dailies editorialised overwhelmingly for the judgment and against the bill, the Urdu press carried the Board's case and the reformers' answers side by side, and the Hindi press of the northern states folded the episode, early and fatefully, into the Ayodhya story, so that readers of one paper met Shah Bano as a wronged grandmother, readers of another as a symbol of minority privilege, and readers of a third as an item in a ledger of governmental bargains. Engineer's 1987 volume, which reprinted commentary from across these presses, remains the best single evidence that the episode had no single public: it had several publics, each reading a different case, and the Act that Parliament passed in May 1986 was addressed to all of them at once, which is one reason it satisfied none of them for long. The architectural contest travelled because the institutions that carry arguments in India, courts, parties, boards, presses, and movements, each found in the episode a precedent cut to its own hand, and a precedent that every institution can use is a precedent that no institution can retire.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Shah Bano case?

The Shah Bano case was the maintenance litigation brought by Shah Bano Begum of Indore, who petitioned in April 1978 under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure after her husband, the advocate Mohd. Ahmed Khan, expelled her from the home in 1975 and divorced her in November 1978. The magistrate awarded her twenty five rupees a month, the Madhya Pradesh High Court raised the sum to one hundred seventy nine rupees and twenty paise, and Khan appealed on the ground that mehr and iddat payments under Muslim law exhausted his duty. A five judge Constitution Bench dismissed the appeal on 23 April 1985.

### Q: What did the 1985 Supreme Court verdict say?

The unanimous verdict of 23 April 1985, delivered by Chief Justice Y. V. Chandrachud, held that Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure is a secular welfare provision applying to every citizen regardless of religion, that a divorced Muslim woman unable to maintain herself could claim maintenance under it, and that mehr, the dower owed under Muslim law, is a marriage obligation rather than a payment on divorce capable of cancelling the statutory duty under Section 127(3)(b). The Court also held, citing verses 241 and 242 of Surah Al-Baqara, that the Quran itself enjoins provision for divorced women, and it regretted that Article 44's uniform civil code directive had remained a dead letter.

### Q: What is the 1986 Muslim Women Act?

The Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act 1986, which received the President's assent on 19 May 1986, is the statute Parliament enacted for divorced Muslim women after the Shah Bano verdict. Section 3 entitles a divorced woman to a reasonable and fair provision and maintenance made and paid by her former husband within the iddat period, together with her mehr and the properties given to her at or around the marriage. Section 4 directs that a woman still unable to maintain herself after iddat, and who has not remarried, be supported by relatives in proportion to their inheritance shares, and failing them by the State Wakf Board.

### Q: Why did Rajiv Gandhi pass the 1986 Act?

The Rajiv Gandhi government's stated rationale in the debates of February to May 1986 was that the Shah Bano verdict had shaken Muslim confidence in the constitutional assurance that religious law would remain the community's own, and that Parliament, not the Court, was the proper forum for altering family law; the bill's supporters argued that the iddat provision, mehr, and the duties of relatives and Wakf Boards protected divorced women by a route consonant with Muslim law. Critics attributed a strategic motive: the All India Muslim Personal Law Board's campaign of 1985 had signalled electoral costs among Muslim voters, and the government, facing assembly contests, chose to purchase communal calm with a statute, a charge the government denied.

### Q: What is the Danial Latifi verdict?

Danial Latifi v Union of India, decided on 28 September 2001 by a five judge Constitution Bench, upheld the 1986 Act against constitutional challenge while transforming its construction. The Court held that the reasonable and fair provision which Section 3 requires the husband to make and pay within the iddat period must be measured by the divorced woman's future needs for the whole period until remarriage, so that the Act, properly read, delivers in a lump sum within iddat a substance equivalent to the maintenance available under Section 125 of the Code. Danial Latifi, who had been Shah Bano's own counsel in 1985, lost the challenge and won the substance.

### Q: How is Shah Bano related to triple talaq?

The connection is architectural rather than direct. The Shah Bano judgment of 1985 and the Act of 1986 established the two poles between which disputes over Muslim family law and state intervention have since been argued: universal statutory norms reaching into the community's law, and legislative deference to that law's own allocations. When Shayara Bano's petition against instant triple talaq reached a Constitution Bench, the majority of 22 August 2017 that set the practice aside reasoned in the first pole's grammar, testing the practice against Article 14 and against the Quran itself, and Parliament's 2019 Act criminalising the practice completed the sequence, as the 1986 Act had answered the earlier judgment from the opposite pole.

### Q: What is the iddat period?

Iddat is the waiting period a Muslim woman observes after a divorce or the death of her husband, during which she may not remarry and during which, after a divorce, the former husband owes her maintenance under Muslim law. Section 2 of the 1986 Act defines the iddat period for a divorced woman as three menstrual courses where she is subject to menstruation, three lunar months where she is not, and, where she is pregnant when the divorce takes effect, the interval between the divorce and the delivery of the child or the termination of the pregnancy, whichever is earlier. The 1986 Act concentrated the husband's provision and maintenance duty inside this window.

### Q: Who were the five judges on the Constitution Bench that decided Shah Bano?

The bench consisted of Chief Justice Y. V. Chandrachud, who delivered the unanimous judgment on 23 April 1985, and Justices D. A. Desai, O. Chinnappa Reddy, E. S. Venkataramiah, and Rangnath Misra. No separate concurring or dissenting opinion was filed, a unanimity that gave the holdings unusual public weight in the controversy that followed. Chief Justice Chandrachud's judgment carried the bench's signature holdings: the secular character of Section 125, the treatment of mehr as a marriage obligation rather than a divorce payment, the reading of the Quranic verses on provision for divorced women, and the expression of regret that the uniform civil code directive in Article 44 had remained unimplemented since 1950.

### Q: What did the Madhya Pradesh High Court decide before the case reached the Supreme Court?

Hearing Mohd. Ahmed Khan's revision against the Indore magistrate's order of 6 August 1979, which had awarded Shah Bano twenty five rupees a month, the Madhya Pradesh High Court enhanced her maintenance to one hundred seventy nine rupees and twenty paise a month, accepting that she was unable to maintain herself and that her husband, a practising advocate who had taken a second wife, had the means the magistrate's smaller figure understated. Khan then carried the case to the Supreme Court, where the maintenance question was argued as a constitutional question about the reach of Section 125 over Muslim law, and the High Court's enhanced figure was the award the Constitution Bench ultimately confirmed.

### Q: Which Quranic verses did the Shah Bano judgment discuss?

The judgment discussed verses 241 and 242 of Surah Al-Baqara, the second chapter of the Quran, in Abdullah Yusuf Ali's English translation, which speak of a duty to provide for divorced women on a reasonable scale as an obligation binding on the righteous. Chief Justice Chandrachud cited the verses to answer the appellant's claim of a conflict between Section 125 and Islam: if the scripture itself enjoined provision for a divorced wife, the secular statute that ordered such provision could not be characterised as an intrusion upon the faith. The passage became the most contested part of the judgment, with the Personal Law Board objecting that a secular bench had certified the meaning of the Quran against the community's interpretive authorities.

### Q: Who was Z. R. Ansari and what role did he play in the 1986 Act?

Z. R. Ansari was the minister who introduced the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Bill in the Lok Sabha on 25 February 1986 and piloted it through its passage in May of that year. As the measure's official mover he carried the government's stated case: that the Shah Bano judgment had disturbed the community's confidence, that family law reform belonged to Parliament, and that the bill's combination of iddat provision, mehr, and the obligations of relatives and Wakf Boards protected divorced women within a framework their own law recognised. His stewardship made him the public face of the reversal, while Arif Mohammad Khan, the minister who had defended the judgment in 1985, became the public face of the government's abandoned position.

### Q: What happened to Shah Bano after the judgment delivered in her name?

Shah Bano returned to Indore, where in November 1985 a public statement attributed to her distanced her from the judgment and associated her with the demand that its rule not be applied to Muslims; contemporaneous accounts collected by Asghar Ali Engineer in 1987 recorded both the statement and the pressure of community attention surrounding its issue. She took no further part in the national argument that continued to use her name, received the maintenance the courts had awarded, and died in Indore in 1992. Her litigation outlived her by decades: the Act passed against her judgment was re-read in Danial Latifi in 2001, and her case remains the standard citation for the contest between statutory welfare law and religious family law in India.

### Q: How did Asghar Ali Engineer read the Shah Bano episode in 1987?

Asghar Ali Engineer, the Bohra reformist scholar, published The Shah Bano Controversy in 1987 as both an archive and an argument. The archive assembled the debate's documents: the Personal Law Board's statements, the parliamentary speeches, the press commentary, and the record of the campaign. The argument held that the Quran, read whole, supported generous post-divorce provision, that the clerical campaign against the verdict defended institutional and male authority rather than scripture, and that the Rajiv Gandhi government's reversal betrayed the Constitution's promise to divorced women. Engineer's volume remains the canonical contemporaneous treatment of the episode, and his reading supplied the reformist Muslim position with its most quoted scholarly statement during the decades of litigation that followed.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court hold in Shabana Bano v Imran Khan in 2009?

In Shabana Bano v Imran Khan, decided on 4 December 2009, the Supreme Court held that a divorced Muslim woman's petition under Section 125 of the Code of Criminal Procedure remained maintainable even after the 1986 Act, so long as she had not remarried and had not obtained relief that exhausted her claim under that Act. The ruling confirmed that the Act had not sealed the boundary it appeared to draw in 1986: the secular maintenance chapter continued to operate for divorced Muslim women who came to the magistrate without an effective Section 3 provision behind them. The decision belongs to the line of cases, running through Iqbal Bano in 2007, in which the judiciary kept the Code's door open after Parliament had seemed to close it.

### Q: How did the Bharatiya Muslim Mahila Andolan change the personal law argument?

The Bharatiya Muslim Mahila Andolan, founded in 2007 by Zakia Soman and Noorjehan Safia Niaz, organised Muslim women as authors of the reform argument rather than as its objects. The movement demanded codification of Muslim family law within an Islamic framework of justice, campaigned against instant triple talaq while refusing the majoritarian framing in which that campaign was often wrapped, and intervened in the Shayara Bano litigation of 2017 in support of the petitioners. Its emergence altered the representative structure of the debate: after 2007, the claim that a clerical board spoke unchallenged for the community on family law faced an organised contradiction from within the community's own membership, voiced by women claiming both their faith and their constitutional rights in the same argument.

### Q: What is Section 5 of the 1986 Act and why does it matter?

Section 5 of the Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Divorce) Act 1986 permits a divorced couple, if both declare jointly in writing at the first hearing of the woman's application under the Act, to elect to be governed by sections 125 to 128 of the Code of Criminal Procedure instead of by the Act. The option matters architecturally because it inverts the default the Shah Bano judgment had established. Before May 1986 the secular maintenance chapter applied to a divorced Muslim woman unless an exception was made out; after the Act, the community specific scheme applied unless both former spouses agreed otherwise, a consent the husband whose obligation was in issue had every incentive to withhold, which is why critics treated the section as a formal rather than a practical door back to the Code.

### Q: What penalty did the 2019 triple talaq Act prescribe?

The Muslim Women (Protection of Rights on Marriage) Act 2019 declared a pronouncement of instant triple talaq void and illegal and made the pronouncement an offence punishable with imprisonment for a term extending to three years, together with a fine. The offence was made cognizable on the complaint of the wife or a person related to her by blood or marriage, and compoundable at her instance with the magistrate's permission. The Act separately entitled the wife to a subsistence allowance for herself and her dependent children, in an amount the magistrate determines, and to custody of her minor children. Critics in the July 2019 debates objected that imprisoning the husband punished a pronouncement the Supreme Court had already rendered legally ineffective, while defenders argued deterrence required the penal clause.

### Q: How did Justice Kurian Joseph's reasoning in Shayara Bano differ from the plurality?

Justice Kurian Joseph concurred in setting aside instant triple talaq on 22 August 2017 but reasoned from within the Islamic sources rather than from the equality code. Where Justices Nariman and Lalit held the practice manifestly arbitrary under Article 14, Justice Joseph held that instant triple talaq was against the Quran and the shariat itself, and that what was bad in theology was bad in law, so the practice could claim no protection as an essential religious practice. His route reproduced, inside the 2017 majority, the same internal scriptural strategy Chief Justice Chandrachud had used in 1985, when the Shah Bano bench answered a claimed conflict between the Code and Islam by reading the Quranic verses on provision for divorced women against the appellant's construction.

### Q: What did the Law Commission conclude about a uniform civil code in 2018?

In its consultation paper on reform of family law released on 31 August 2018, the Law Commission of India concluded that a uniform civil code was neither necessary nor desirable at that stage, and recommended instead that discriminatory provisions within each community's family law be identified and reformed, with equality between men and women inside every personal law treated as the operative goal. The paper addressed marriage, divorce, maintenance, inheritance, and adoption across the personal laws, and it was quoted thereafter by every side of the argument: by opponents of the code as an expert verdict against uniformity, and by the code's supporters as a consultation document that settled nothing and left the Article 44 directive standing for a future Parliament to act upon.

### Q: Why did the Supreme Court treat mehr as a marriage obligation rather than a divorce payment?

The appellant's route to cancelling Shah Bano's maintenance ran through Section 127(3)(b) of the Code, which directs a magistrate to cancel a maintenance order where the divorced woman has received the whole sum payable to her under her customary or personal law on divorce. Mohd. Ahmed Khan argued that the mehr of three thousand rupees was that sum. The Court answered that mehr is an obligation imposed by the marriage contract itself, a sum the husband undertakes to pay as a mark of respect to the wife, recoverable by her as a debt independent of any divorce, and not a consideration paid for the divorce or in settlement of its consequences. Mehr therefore could not trigger the cancellation clause, and the maintenance order stood.
