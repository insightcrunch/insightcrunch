Shakespeare wrote the love tragedy twice, once near the start of his tragic career and once near its summit, and the two attempts stand at opposite poles of the form. *Romeo and Juliet*, composed in the middle of the 1590s, gives the form to a pair of teenagers in a single Italian city, kills them inside a week, and lets a feud neither lover made supply the pressure that crushes them. *Antony and Cleopatra*, composed a decade later in 1606 or 1607, gives the form to a Roman triumvir and an Egyptian queen, stretches their entanglement across ten years of Mediterranean history, and lets the government of the world supply the pressure. Both plays end in a double suicide inside a monument. Almost nothing else about the endings is the same, and the distance between those two endings is the distance the playwright travelled as a tragedian.

![The Capulet vault in Verona set against Cleopatra's monument in Alexandria, the two death chambers of Shakespeare's love tragedies - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-30.webp)

The comparison has an authority behind it older than the modern syllabus. A. C. Bradley, whose *Shakespearean Tragedy* of 1904 set the terms for a century of argument, paused in his first lecture to mark the pair off from everything around them: "it is only in the love-tragedies, *Romeo and Juliet* and *Antony and Cleopatra*, that the heroine is as much the centre of the action as the hero. The rest," he added of the single-hero tragedies, "are single stars." Bradley then set both plays aside, devoting his lectures to Hamlet, Othello, Lear and Macbeth, and the habit of setting them aside has proved durable. The Verona play survives in the general imagination as an isolated monument to young love, quoted at weddings and detached from the career that produced it. The Egyptian play survives as a connoisseur's pleasure, admired for its poetry and suspected of shapelessness. Each play is diminished by the isolation. Read together, they explain each other.

The verdict argued through this comparison is that the later play answers the earlier one, point by point, as if the older dramatist had taken the young man's design out of a drawer and rebuilt it at the opposite scale. Everything the Verona lovers are denied, the Egyptian lovers receive in excess. Romeo and Juliet have no time: four elapsed days carry them from a feast to a tomb. Antony and Cleopatra have a decade, and spend it in separations, reconciliations, embassies and wars. Romeo and Juliet have no experience: each is the other's first serious knowledge of the world. Antony and Cleopatra have pasts that fill the stage behind them, dead spouses, former lovers, children, campaigns. Romeo and Juliet have no audience: their love must hide in orchards, cells and a borrowed ladder, and Verona learns of it over their corpses. Antony and Cleopatra have the whole Roman world for an audience, pricing their affair in provinces and reporting it in senates. None of the gifts saves the second pair. Time teaches them suspicion, experience teaches them management, and fame turns their love into an instrument their enemy can aim at them. What kills in Verona is a feud working through accident. What kills in Egypt is a political order working through choice, the lovers' own choices included.

That difference in the killing mechanism is the comparison's real subject. The paired contrasts of the standard account, youth against maturity, privacy against empire, haste against history, are genuine, and each is examined in turn below. But the contrasts converge on a single finding about the range of the love tragedy in this one career: the form can be built at the scale of a household or at the scale of a world empire, with lovers who know nothing or lovers who know everything, and it holds at both scales, because its true constant is neither innocence nor experience but a love that the surrounding order cannot house. Verona's order is two families and a prince too weak to police them. Egypt's order is a triumvirate hardening into an empire under a man who never raises his voice. Both orders leave the lovers exactly one private room, and in both plays that room is a tomb.

Quotations from the Verona play follow the Folger Shakespeare text of *Romeo and Juliet*, and quotations from the Egyptian play follow the Folger text of *Antony and Cleopatra*; act and scene references use the Folger scene divisions. The Verona mechanics of the final scene, the speed of the poison and the misreadings inside the vault, are treated at passage strength here, since the series gives them their own full hearings elsewhere. The Egyptian mechanics get the fuller treatment, because the comparison turns on them.

## Two Endings, One Shape: The Double Suicides in the Tomb and the Monument

Both plays end the same way on a cast list: the man dead by his own hand first, the woman dead by hers second, the two bodies together in a burial chamber while the state's representative pronounces the closing words. The resemblance is real, and it has encouraged a lazy habit of treating the endings as one ending written twice. They are opposite constructions. The Verona ending is a machine of minutes, assembled from an undelivered letter, a servant's ride and a potion timed to the hour, and its horror is the horror of near-arrival: help walks into the vault minutes after the draught has done its work. The Egyptian ending is a ceremony of days, assembled from a false message, a botched sword-stroke and a queen's negotiations with her conqueror, and its horror is of a different kind: everything happens at the pace the dying choose, and the choosing is the tragedy. A full anatomy of the Verona scene belongs to [the account of Act 5, Scene 3, the tomb and the double suicide](/2023/12/08/act-5-scene-3-tomb-double-suicide/); the contrast needs only its clock.

Romeo enters the Capulet vault believing a report that is, in every checkable particular, accurate. Balthasar saw the funeral, saw the body laid in the monument, and rode to Mantua with the news. The inference Romeo draws, that the death is real, is the inference any sane witness would draw, and the poison he buys acts before any second thought can form. His death is the work of seconds once the cup is raised: "O true apothecary! Thy drugs are quick. Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3). Juliet wakes to find the cup empty and the body warm, hears the watch approaching, and finishes the scene's arithmetic with the dagger she finds in his belt: "O happy dagger! This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3). Between his death and hers there is no interval in which a different outcome can insert itself. The double suicide in Verona is a single event with two beats, driven by error in one case and by knowledge in the other, and both beats fall inside one continuous scene of discovery.

Antony's death occupies the better part of two scenes and fails at its first attempt. The report that kills him is a lie, and the lie is Cleopatra's own. Shut inside her monument after the final collapse at Alexandria, dreading his rage and hoping to soften it, she sends the eunuch Mardian with word that she has killed herself, her last word spoken on Antony's name. The message is a test and a shield. It produces the one result she cannot have priced in. Antony, who has spent the play oscillating between Alexandria and Rome, between the queen and his honour, receives the news as the removal of the last reason to negotiate with life. He asks his attendant Eros to kill him. Eros, who has sworn to do it rather than watch him captured, turns the sword on himself instead. Antony reads the servant's death as a lesson in shame and falls on his own blade with a vow that converts the act into a wedding: "I will be a bridegroom in my death, and run into't as to a lover's bed" (4.14). The stroke is misjudged. He does not die. Carried to the monument, hauled bleeding up its wall by Cleopatra and her women in the play's most physically arduous image of devotion, he dies slowly, in her presence, with time to ask her safety, to counsel her to trust one of Caesar's men, and to hear her grief begin. "I am dying, Egypt, dying" (4.15) is a sentence Romeo is never given the leisure to pronounce.

The contrast in tempo is not decorative. Verona kills its lovers faster than information can travel; Egypt kills its lovers slower than information can be corrected. Cleopatra is alive while Antony believes her dead, exactly as Juliet is alive while Romeo believes her dead, but the Egyptian scene builds a bridge across the error and the Verona scene does not. Antony is told the truth. He dies anyway, because the wound is already in him. Juliet's Romeo is never told anything; the truth arrives with the Friar, carrying a crowbar and a lantern, after the poison has closed the question. One ending is an error that cannot be recalled in time. The other is an error recalled in time and found to be beside the point, since the dying has already been chosen on other grounds: Antony has lost the world, his fleet, his land army, his honour in Roman eyes, and the false report merely names the hour. Cleopatra's lie is the occasion of his death in a sense the Friar's letter never is in Romeo's. Remove the lie, and Antony still stands in a conquered city with Caesar's terms in his ears. Remove the letter's failure, and Romeo and Juliet live.

Cleopatra's own death completes the inversion. Juliet dies within minutes of waking, by an instrument borrowed from her husband's body, in a place she did not choose, wearing the clothes of her supposed corpse. Cleopatra dies after an interval filled with statecraft. She is taken by Caesar's men, guarded against exactly the act she intends, visited by Caesar in person and measured against his intentions, warned by Dolabella that the triumph in Rome will display her alive, and left to stage her exit as a state occasion. She sends for country clothing and receives a basket of figs with the asps hidden inside it, delivered by a clown whose chatter about the worm delays the scene like a held breath. Then she robes. "Give me my robe, put on my crown; I have immortal longings in me" (5.2). The woman who entered the play on a barge, dressed as Venus before the eyes of Alexandria, exits it dressed as a queen before the eyes of her attendants and the spies who will report her. Her last speeches claim the two things Caesar's victory was designed to deny her: a marriage and a transcendence. "Husband, I come: now to that name my courage prove my title" (5.2) asserts the wedding Rome never ratified. "I am fire and air; my other elements I give to baser life" (5.2) asserts an essence the triumph cannot carry in a cage. Charmian, dying after her, straightens the crown on the dead queen's head with the words "Your crown's awry; I'll mend it, and then play," and the play's last act of love is an act of wardrobe.

One further symmetry locks the two endings together, a matter of ground. Both women die in their own dynastic earth. Juliet's vault is the Capulet monument, the family's permanent asset, already holding Tybalt and generations of ancestors, and her tragedy ends where her family's memory is stored. Romeo dies on that ground as a trespasser, a Montague smuggled by death into the enemy's tomb, which is why the reconciliation must be staged over the bodies with such ceremony: the ground itself records the offence his presence commits. Cleopatra's monument is likewise her own, a royal tomb she has prepared and stocked with treasure, and Antony dies inside it as a guest, hauled up its outer wall by the household of the woman whose dynasty the tomb proclaims. Caesar's soldiers guard the building at the end, and still the ground defeats him. He can annex Egypt; he cannot repossess the chamber's meaning, since the woman inside it has already converted it from a prison into a shrine by the manner of her dying there. Each play ends, that is, in a room belonging to the heroine's house, with the hero present by violation or by invitation, and the state standing outside the door reading the inscription.

The endings also differ in the direction grief travels. Verona's catastrophe radiates upward into the old. The dawn assembly in the vault is a gathering of parents, and it begins with a further death reported at the door: Montague enters with the news that his wife has died in the night, "grief of my son's exile hath stopped her breath" (5.3), so the feud's final tally includes a mother killed by sorrow offstage, in bed, without a scene. The young die by instruments; the old die by information. Egypt has no parent generation to bereave, because its principals are the parent generation. Grief there radiates downward and outward instead: downward to the attendants, Iras dead before her mistress, Charmian dying at the crown, Enobarbus already dead of his defection in a ditch; outward to the provinces and client kingdoms whose settlements die with the queen. Verona's ending is a family funeral that becomes a civic rite. Egypt's ending is a state funeral that keeps threatening to become an intimate one, as a servant girl straightens a crown and a conqueror finds himself pronouncing an epitaph he would rather have delivered over a prisoner.

### Why does Antony take so long to die when Romeo dies within moments of drinking?

The difference is mechanical before it is symbolic, and the mechanics carry the meaning. Romeo's poison is bought for speed and praised for it on receipt, and the Folger text gives him three lines between the draught and the fall.

Antony's sword is a soldier's weapon used by a weakened man in a botched stroke, and the text insists on the failure: he must be carried, hoisted and laid down inside the monument, dying across a scene break with his consciousness intact. Speed belongs to the boy who acts on a single report. Slowness belongs to the veteran whose death must be witnessed, interpreted and answered by the woman who survives him.

The tempo gap widens in the second death of each play. Juliet's waking and dying are compressed into one unbroken stage sequence, so that the audience holds the empty cup, the warm body and the dagger in a single span of attention. Cleopatra's dying is the longest final movement in the tragedies, a full scene of preparation, disguise, negotiation and ceremony after Antony's death has already closed his play inside hers. Verona's ending asks how little time a catastrophe needs. Egypt's ending asks what a great personage does with time once catastrophe is certain, and the answer, a coronation performed backwards, is the mature tragedy's signature.

### What does Cleopatra gain by surviving Antony when Juliet gains nothing by surviving Romeo?

Cleopatra gains the ending. Antony's botched, protracted death hands her the authorship of everything that follows: the refusal of Caesar's terms, the testing of his envoys, the robing, the asps, the final image of the crowned queen that Rome must bury rather than exhibit.

Juliet survives Romeo by minutes and gains only the knowledge of his death and the dagger that answers it, because Verona's plot has left her no resources but her body: no crown, no attendants with baskets, no enemy to outwit, no interval in which to compose a meaning. The Egyptian heroine's survival is measured in scenes and filled with decisions. The Verona heroine's survival is measured in lines and filled with a single decision, taken at once.

Behind the difference stands a difference in what each woman is. Cleopatra is a sovereign with a state's machinery at her command even in defeat, guards to deceive, treasure to bury, messengers to send. Juliet is a daughter in her father's house, and the one instrument she was ever given, the Friar's potion, has spent itself. The plays distribute agency along the same gradient as everything else in the comparison. The mature lovers own the means of their deaths as they owned the means of their lives, fleets, treasuries, wardrobes, servants. The young lovers borrow every instrument they use: a friar's vial, an apothecary's dram, a husband's dagger.

## Young First Love Against Mature Passion: What Each Couple Brings to the Meeting

The standard contrast calls one pair young and the other middle-aged, and stops there, as if the difference were a matter of birthdays. The plays make it a difference of equipment. Romeo and Juliet meet as beginners, and the text is precise about the girl's standing: Juliet is thirteen, weeks short of fourteen, with her mother negotiating a marriage she has not yet considered in earnest. Romeo's years go unstated, a young man of the city's leisure class, and his emotional history at the meeting consists of one unreturned devotion, to the unseen Rosaline, conducted entirely in borrowed Petrarchan postures and abandoned inside a single speech at the sight of Juliet's face. The lovers' first exchange, the shared sonnet built line by line at the Capulet feast, is a masterpiece of mutual improvisation between two people who have never done anything of consequence before. Its freshness is the point. Neither brings a past into the scene, so neither brings a defence.

Antony and Cleopatra meet as incumbents. Their first encounter in the play's present action is a resumption, not a beginning: the famous meeting at Tarsus on the river Cydnus, where Cleopatra arrived on her gilded barge dressed as Venus, happened years before the action opens and reaches the audience as report, in Enobarbus's set-piece description to Roman colleagues: "The barge she sat in, like a burnished throne, burned on the water: the poop was beaten gold" (2.2). The passage versifies, at times nearly word for word, the account in North's translation of Plutarch, and its placement matters. The Verona lovers' meeting is enacted; the Egyptian lovers' meeting is remembered, narrated, turned into the founding legend that every character in the play already knows and prices. Romeo and Juliet fall in love in front of the audience. Antony and Cleopatra are introduced as a love the world has already finished discussing.

The pasts are specific, and the play keeps them in view. Cleopatra had been the lover of Julius Caesar and, in her own retrospective phrase, a sight at which great Pompey made his eyes grow in his brow; she rules a kingdom she has held through two Roman civil wars and intends to hold through a third. She can narrate her own youth as a closed era: "My salad days, when I was green in judgment: cold in blood" (1.5), spoken of the Caesar years by a woman who regards her younger self with amused contempt. Antony arrives with a wife in Rome, Fulvia, whose death early in the action frees and shames him in the same moment, a reputation as one of the three pillars of the world, a soldier's body going to seed, and a head of hair the play repeatedly notices turning. History, behind the fiction, gives the pair fifty-three and thirty-nine years at their deaths. The text needs no numbers. It builds maturity as texture: these are people for whom love is one institution among several they administer, and who know the cost of everything because they have paid most prices already.

Enobarbus's verdict on Cleopatra is the mature play's answer to the cult of first love: "Age cannot wither her, nor custom stale her infinite variety: other women cloy the appetites they feed, but she makes hungry where most she satisfies" (2.2). The praise is precisely anti-juvenile. Variety, the accumulated repertoire of a woman who has been a queen, a lover, a mother and a negotiator for two decades, is named as the erotic asset. Juliet's power is the opposite endowment: a concentration without repertoire, a mind encountering each experience at full voltage because nothing has been spent. The plays thus refuse the easy hierarchy in both directions. Experience in Egypt produces the grander poetry of desire and the worse judgment; Antony's knowledge of the world does not extend to knowledge of what Cleopatra's false death-message will do to him, and Cleopatra's knowledge of Antony, deeper than any other character's, still misprices his despair by one fatal degree. Innocence in Verona produces catastrophic ignorance of fact and a flawless moral aim: neither young lover ever misreads the other's heart.

The Rosaline episode, treated at passage here, sharpens the asymmetry. Romeo's transfer from the unseen girl to Juliet inside minutes has been read as fickleness since the eighteenth century, and the Friar states the charge to his face: young men's love lies in their eyes rather than their hearts. Cleopatra's history contains transfers of allegiance on a geopolitical scale, Caesar to Antony, with kingdoms in the balance, and no character in the play calls her fickle for them; Rome calls her worse things, but the play's own accounting treats her past as statecraft and survival. The comparison exposes the double standard without needing to preach it. A boy's change of object is a moral failing. A queen's change of protector is a policy. What neither reading captures, and the paired plays do, is that constancy under pressure is the actual test both works apply. Romeo, mocked for inconstancy, never wavers after the feast, through banishment, a death report and a tomb. Cleopatra, celebrated for the constancy of her desire across a decade, sends the one message that kills her lover, out of a manager's instinct the young lovers never acquire.

The sister contrast stands behind both meetings. Shakespeare lowered his heroine's age against his source, where Brooke's Juliet is sixteen, and the lowering is a designed intensifier: thirteen strips the girl of every adult resource and leaves her with language, nerve and a friar. No corresponding adjustment was needed in Egypt, where the history arrived with its principals already monumental, and the playwright's work ran the other way, humanising monuments with appetites, jokes and rages until their scale became a kind of intimacy.

### How old are the four lovers, and what does the gap between the couples change?

Juliet is thirteen, short of fourteen by weeks; Romeo is a youth of unstated years whose experience begins in the play. Cleopatra and Antony are given no ages in the text, yet every signal runs the other way: dead spouses, grown reputations, children, a shared history reaching back before the action.

And behind them the historical record of a general in his fifties and a queen near forty. The gap changes the tragedy's physics. The young pair is destroyed by what they cannot know. The older pair is destroyed with full knowledge of the stakes, the odds and each other, which removes ignorance as an excuse and replaces accident with decision as the engine of ruin.

Age also governs what each love is for. The Verona affair is a foundation: marriage, household, a future the lovers sketch in a single night of planning, ladders and letters and a friar's long game. The Egyptian affair is a summation: neither lover imagines a domestic future, and their scenes together are festivals of the present tense, feasting, gaming, dressing, remembering. Romeo and Juliet die before their love can be tested by duration. Antony and Cleopatra die after duration has tested it by every means except indifference, and the play's cruel finding is that duration is not a defence. A love of ten years' standing, with kingdoms staked on it and children born to it, can still be ended by one unverified message, exactly as a love of four days can.

## Private Verona, Public Empire: The Two Worlds That Watch the Lovers

Verona is a small stage for a large catastrophe: two households, a prince, a friar, a nurse, a handful of young men with swords. The love affair at its centre is, by the standards of its world, a private matter, and its privacy is the plot's first condition. The marriage is secret because both houses would kill it on sight. The lovers meet in an orchard, a cell, a bedroom reached by a rope ladder; they communicate through two intermediaries; they exist as a couple in almost no public space at all. When the truth finally becomes public, in the vault at dawn, it is public over two corpses, and the survivors' reconciliation takes the form of building statues, as if the city could only ever process the affair as an image. The Chorus tells the audience everything and the city nothing. Secrecy is so complete that the play's one fully informed adult, the Friar, becomes a single point of failure: one detained messenger, and the private world collapses because it has no other channels.

Egypt and Rome invert every term. The affair between Antony and Cleopatra is the least private event in the world it inhabits. It is reported by messengers in nearly every scene, debated by soldiers, priced by the triumvirate, and weaponised by Octavius Caesar, whose propaganda war against Antony runs on the Egyptian liaison as its central exhibit. The play opens not with a Chorus but with a Roman soldier's complaint, Philo measuring his general's dotage against a military standard: "Nay, but this dotage of our general's overflows the measure" (1.1). Measurement is Rome's idiom, and the affair is measured continuously: in legions defecting, in provinces given away, in the spectacle of the Donations at Alexandria, where Antony enthroned Cleopatra and her children over eastern kingdoms and Caesar reads the inventory aloud at Rome as an indictment (3.6). Where Verona's lovers must invent secrecy, Egypt's lovers cannot buy it. Their most intimate scenes are crowded with attendants, eunuchs, messengers and spies, and their quarrels are conducted in the knowledge that every witness is also a correspondent.

The public scale changes the love's nature, and the play is exact about the exchange rate. Antony's answer to Philo's complaint sets the Egyptian valuation against the Roman one in a single gesture: "Let Rome in Tiber melt, and the wide arch of the ranged empire fall. Here is my space" (1.1). The line is magnificent and it is a lie he must keep paying for, since the empire he waves away is also his identity, his army's loyalty and his children's inheritance. Verona's lovers never face the question of what their love costs the world, because their world is small enough to be bankrupted by two funerals. Antony's love costs the Roman world a civil war, the eastern provinces their allegiance, the republic its last equilibrium. The bill is presented scene by scene: Pompey's uneasy alliance, the marriage to Octavia contracted as a treaty seal, Caesar offering his sister in the hope that the triumvirs' hearts be knit "with an unslipping knot" (2.2), her abandonment, the defections of soldiers who can no longer read their general's conduct as Roman. Enobarbus, the play's resident intelligence, finally defects on a judgment of pure accountancy: the cause has become unwinnable because the general has subordinated it to the affair. He dies of the defection, of a broken heart in a ditch at night, the one death in either play caused by an assessment.

Each play also keeps an official candidate at the margin of the love, the partner the order approves, and the candidates rhyme. Paris is Verona's Octavia: the suitable match, county and kin to the Prince, chosen by the parents, courteous, brave, and entirely unwanted by the heroine. The Egyptian play gives the role to a woman and raises its stakes, since Octavia arrives with a treaty in her train and a brother's empire behind her, and the play treats her with a respect that quietly indicts both camps: she pleads between husband and brother in Rome, is received in Egypt's story only as report and insult, and exits the action with her dignity intact and her marriage a fiction everyone has signed. Paris dies at the tomb defending, as he supposes, his bride's corpse against a vandal, granted a gallantry the plot never asked of him. Octavia survives into history as its respected widow. The approved candidates are the tragedies' control cases for the order's judgement: everything the world recommends, offered in person, and refused not from perversity but because the heart in each play has already signed elsewhere. Their presence also measures the heroines. Juliet must deceive Paris to his face in a friar's cell, politely, with a knife in reserve. Cleopatra never has to meet Octavia at all; empire arranges that the wives of its drama contend by embassy and rumour, which is the public world's version of the same cruelty.

The galley scene off Misenum shows the public world at its most private, and the contrast with Verona's cells is instructive. The triumvirs and Pompey feast aboard a ship, Lepidus drinks himself into a stretcher, and Menas whispers to his master the offer that would settle the world in a minute: cut the cable, let the sea take the three rulers of the earth, and be master alone. Pompey refuses the treachery he would have thanked Menas for performing unasked, a distinction of honour so fine it might be a Verona sentiment, and the empire's fate is weighed in a boat cabin by torchlight. Verona's equivalent decisions happen in a friar's cell and a girl's bedroom, at the same whispering register. The plays agree that history turns in small rooms. They disagree about the furniture: one room holds a map of the world, the other a rope ladder.

Privacy, in the comparison, turns out to be neither a blessing nor a curse but a distribution of risk. The Verona love is fragile because it is hidden: it has secrecy's strength, freedom from interference, and secrecy's weakness, no witnesses, no redundancy, no appeal. The Egyptian love is fragile because it is exposed: it has fame's strength, an army of servants and a treasury, and fame's weakness, an enemy who can attack the love itself as a military objective. Octavius never needs to defeat Antony in Egypt's bedroom; he defeats him in Rome's imagination, mailing the affair home as treason until Antony's own soldiers begin to desert the story before they desert the man. The feud in Verona works in the opposite direction, inward and downward: it never learns the lovers' names as a couple until the Prince assembles the evidence over the bodies. One order kills a love by ignoring it. The other kills a love by publicising it.

The two plays announce their watching worlds in their first minutes, and the announcements are mirror images. Verona's play opens with a Chorus in sonnet form, a voice outside the fiction with perfect information, which gives away the ending, prices the performance at "the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue), and then, after a second sonnet, withdraws from the play entirely, leaving the city to misread events unwitnessed. Egypt's play opens inside the fiction, with Philo, a partisan Roman with partial information, complaining that his general's heart has burst its military banks. Judgment in the young play begins omniscient and evaporates. Judgment in the old play begins prejudiced and multiplies, soldier, servant, ambassador, triumvir, until the whole Mediterranean is a gallery. The Chorus's disappearance, examined in its own hearing elsewhere in the series, and the Roman gallery's expansion are the same formal decision read in two directions: the early play trusts a frame and discards it, the late play trusts no frame and builds the judging into every scene.

### What does Antony's love cost the Roman world, and what does Romeo's love cost Verona?

Antony's love costs Rome a settlement, a war and a frontier. The triumvirate's balance, sealed by his marriage to Octavia, breaks on the affair; the Donations hand eastern kingdoms to Cleopatra's children; Actium follows, and with it the end of the republic's long experiment in shared power.

Romeo's love costs Verona its heirs and its peace: Tybalt, Mercutio and Paris dead in the feud's machinery, the lovers dead by its pressure, a prince bereaved of kinsmen, two houses extinguished in the persons they were saving themselves for. The Egyptian bill is denominated in provinces. The Verona bill is denominated in a single generation.

The difference in denomination changes the survivors' task. Octavius inherits a world and must merely administer the victory, which he does with chilling competence, converting the lovers into a famous grave. Escalus inherits a street and must convert the catastrophe into a reconciliation, which he does with statues and a handshake between fathers who have nothing left to trade. Empire absorbs its love tragedy as an ornament of the new order. The city-state absorbs its love tragedy as the new order's founding grief. Both absorptions are chilling in the same way: the machine that killed the lovers proves perfectly capable of commemorating them.

## Haste Against History: The Clocks of the Two Plays

The Verona play is the most compressed tragedy its author ever built. Its action, as the series calendar hearings establish, touches five calendar days and consumes about four of them in elapsed time: a Sunday brawl, a Sunday-night feast, a Monday marriage and a Monday street fight, banishment, a potion, a funeral mistaken for the real thing, a Thursday dawn in the vault. Arthur Brooke's poem, the play's English source, spread the same events across months of courtship, and the compression is the playwright's single most consequential alteration. Everything in the tragedy breathes the pressure of the schedule. The wedding is advanced a day by Capulet's impatience and the whole machinery of rescue snaps. The lovers' own idiom is haste: contracts made in an evening, a marriage within a day, a suicide within minutes of a report. Even the Chorus's second sonnet is a tiny drama of replacement at speed: "Now old desire doth in his deathbed lie, and young affection gapes to be his heir" (2.0). The old love is buried and the new one crowned inside fourteen lines, the play's tempo stated as its subject.

The Egyptian play sprawls across roughly a decade of history, from the aftermath of the triumvirate's formation through the meeting at Tarsus, the Parthian campaigns, the marriage to Octavia, the Donations, Actium and the fall of Alexandria, in a sequence of more than forty scenes that hop between Rome, Egypt, Athens, Sicily and the sea. By the crude measure of clock and calendar it is the least hasty tragedy of the career. Yet the sensation in performance is of a different kind of speed: scenes arrive like dispatches, battles are reported at the moment of their turning, messengers interrupt feasts, and the play's last act gathers a whole war's end into days. The Egyptian play compresses a decade with a historian's ruthlessness, discarding years between scenes, while the Verona play dilates a weekend until it fills a tragedy. Haste in one is a property of the lovers. Haste in the other is a property of the editing.

The distinction decides where each tragedy locates its fatal pressure. Verona's lovers are hurried by other people's clocks: Capulet advancing the wedding, the Prince's sentence running, the potion's forty-two hours expiring on a schedule the Friar set and could not amend. Their haste is real but it is also assigned, the tempo of a world that gives the young no slack. Egypt's lovers are hurried by history itself, which in their play has a face and a name: Octavius, whose genius is for tempo, who strikes at Actium before the eastern alliance can settle, who follows the fugitives to Alexandria within the year, who sends envoys, deadlines and finally an army at a pace the feasting, gaming, remembering court of Alexandria cannot match. Antony complains of Caesar's speed in the language of a man out-generalled by a calendar. The young lovers race a potion. The old lovers race an empire's consolidation, and both races end in a monument because a monument is where a race against time is finally allowed to stop.

There is a further symmetry the comparison brings out. Each play gives its lovers a device for cheating time, and each device fails on schedule. The Friar's potion promises Juliet a purchased pause, forty-two hours of counterfeit death inside which the world can be rearranged. It very nearly works; it fails by the width of a detained letter and a servant's faster horse. Cleopatra's devices are grander and just as mortal: the barge at Tarsus that restaged her as a goddess outside time, the monument she retires into as a queen already entombed, dressing for a death she schedules herself. Her cheating of time very nearly works too. She escapes the triumph; she does not escape the grave, and the play's closing image grants her the only victory over time available in either tragedy, a fame Caesar himself must pronounce. Verona's cheat was a drug and failed by minutes. Egypt's cheat was theatre and failed by nothing at all, except that its prize was a reputation instead of a life.

The stage arithmetic repeats the point in numbers a company manager can feel. The Verona play runs to twenty-four scenes in the modern division and makes its famous promise of two hours' traffic, a claim its concentrated construction nearly honours; uncut, it plays as the swiftest of the tragedies. The Egyptian play runs to forty-two scenes, changes its ground among three continents and a sea battle, and in full text is among the longest evenings its author ever asked of a company. Yet the shorter-span play covers four days and the longer evening covers a decade, so the audience's felt ratio of story to stage time inverts the page ratio. Verona dilates. Egypt elides. Both are forms of haste, and each play's lovers die of the local variety: the young pair of a weekend squeezed until it bursts, the old pair of a decade with its years stolen out of it, leaving the deaths adjacent though the lives were long.

Waiting, the passive tense of haste, is distributed between the plays along the same lines. Juliet's Act 2 wait for the Nurse stretches three hours of stage-reported time into the play's most famous exercise in impatience, and her epithalamium begs the sun's horses to gallop and bring the night at a run: the young heroine experiences duration as an insult, a delay between desire and its object with nothing inside it. Cleopatra's waits are measured in years, through Antony's Roman marriage, his campaigns in the east, his returns and departures, and she fills them as a sovereign fills an interregnum, with embassies, intelligence, theatre and policy. Antony's own long waits, the Alexandrian seasons between campaigns, are filled with feasting that the Romans read as sloth and the play reads as a rival civilisation's calendar. The young lovers wait in minutes and curse the clock. The old lovers wait in years and build palaces inside the interval. Both are killed in the end by schedules other people keep, the Friar's forty-two hours in one play and Caesar's campaigning season in the other, and the waits turn out to have been the tragedies' real subject all along: what a love does with the time it is given, and what the world does with the same time.

### How much time passes inside each play, from the lovers' meeting to their deaths?

Verona allots the love about four days of elapsed action within five calendar days: the feast, the marriage, the banishment and the tomb fall inside one working week, and the lovers spend more of those days apart, exiled or entombed, than together.

Egypt allots the love the better part of a decade of history, from the meeting at Tarsus in the triumvirate's first years to the fall of Alexandria after Actium, with wars, a political marriage and the birth of children inside the span, though the stage shows the decade in flashes, discarding whole years between scenes. The boy and girl get a long weekend. The general and the queen get an era.

The era does them no better service than the weekend. Both spans end at the same address because the tragedies measure time not by its quantity but by its margin: the distance between the lovers' arrangements and the world's deadline. Verona's margin is an afternoon, the gap between a letter's arrival and a servant's. Egypt's margin is wider in years and identical in structure, the gap between Cleopatra's management of events and Caesar's. In both plays the margin closes from the outside, on a schedule the lovers can see coming and cannot amend, which is why the haste-versus-history contrast finally dissolves into a single mechanism viewed at two magnifications.

## Love Crowned by Death, Death Chosen as Love: The Liebestod Question

Critics reach for a German word when they describe these endings, and the word needs its papers checked before it is allowed into the comparison. *Liebestod*, love-death, won its currency from Wagner's *Tristan und Isolde*, completed in 1859 and first staged in 1865, the opera in which Isolde dies transfigured over Tristan's body. Even there the label is a souvenir rather than a title: Wagner applied the word to the opera's prelude, called the closing scene Isolde's transfiguration, and watched Franz Liszt's piano transcription of 1867 fix *Liebestod* to the finale in the public mind. As a term of criticism it names the doctrine that love finds its completion, its only adequate consummation, in death. The word is therefore a nineteenth-century instrument, fashioned for a medieval legend reworked by a Romantic composer, and laying it across two Jacobean and Elizabethan plays is an act of criticism, not of description. The wider tradition the word points at is real enough, and the series examines [the liebestod pattern, love and death entwined](/2024/01/05/love-and-death-liebestod-analysis/), from Pyramus and Thisbe forward. The question for the comparison is narrower: how far does each play, on its own evidence, teach the doctrine the word imports?

The Verona play, examined cold, teaches something more austere. Its lovers do not seek death as love's fulfilment. Romeo seeks death as an escape from a world in which Juliet is dead; the suicide is an inference from a false premise, not a consummation. Juliet, waking to the fact, seeks the same escape by the nearest instrument. The play's own voices refuse to crown the outcome. The Friar calls the scene a lamentable chance. The Prince distributes punishment. The fathers commission statues, which is the city's way of converting the deaths into an emblem after the fact. The lovers' union in death is real as stage imagery, the two bodies in one vault, the bridal metaphors Juliet herself used for the tomb when she asked that her wedding bed be made in the monument where Tybalt lay. But the imagery is the survivors' and the audience's construction. Inside the fiction, death is not love's completion; it is love's amputation, performed by error, and the play keeps the accounts separate with almost legal care.

The Egyptian play comes far closer to the doctrine, and characteristically qualifies it in the act of stating it. Antony frames his suicide in the language of the marriage bed, running to death as to a lover's embrace, and the frame is a deliberate self-coronation by a man who has lost every other title. Cleopatra frames hers as a wedding and a coronation at once, claiming the name of husband for the dead man and dressing in the robes of her first entrance, so that the final scene reads as the marriage ceremony the play never staged, performed at a tomb, with death as the officiant. Here the lovers themselves, not the survivors, do the naming. If the liebestod label belongs anywhere in the canon before Wagner, it belongs to Alexandria, where the principals compose their deaths as the meaning of their love and say so in set speeches. Yet the qualification stands in the same scene. Cleopatra also dies to defeat a political enemy, to avoid being led through Rome in triumph, hoisted and shown to the shouting crowd of a censuring city, sung in ballads by scald rhymers. Her death is a love-death and a state action, an erotic apotheosis and a propaganda victory, and the play declines to separate the strands. The Verona deaths are purer as love and emptier as statements. The Egyptian deaths are richer as statements and hopelessly impure as love, mixed with policy, vanity, fear and pride, which the play counts not as adulteration but as the truth about how great persons die.

The comparison thus reverses the usual classroom assignment. The young play, marketed for centuries as the supreme love-death, is the one whose text resists the category, insisting on accident, error and waste. The old play, handled as a political tragedy with an erotic engine, is the one that builds the love-death deliberately, gives it speeches, costumes and a theology of fire and air. The cliche isolates the Verona play from its sibling and misreads both in the process, pinning the liebestod rosette on the tragedy of accident and withholding it from the tragedy of design.

The refinement the texts demand is about timing. Verona's lovers do reach for the language of marriage in death, and they reach for it early, as defiance and as dread, never as ceremony. Juliet, ordered to marry Paris, answers her mother with a vow that her bridal bed be made instead in the dim monument where Tybalt lies, the tomb offered as the only alternative venue her father's house has left her. The figure is a threat extracted under pressure, a girl's rhetoric of refusal, and when the actual tomb scene arrives the rhetoric is gone: her waking speech is inventory and decision, cup, warmth, noise, dagger, with no bridal vocabulary left in it. Egypt reverses the sequence. Its lovers spend the play in the vocabulary of appetite, policy and reproach, and produce the bridal language at the end, at the moment of performance, as the ceremony itself: his bridegroom's run to the lover's bed, her robing and her claim of the husband's name. Verona imagines the love-death in advance and then suffers it inarticulately. Egypt refuses to imagine it and then composes it on the spot. The doctrine the German word names belongs, where it belongs at all, to the composers.

Both plays descend, at whatever distance, from the ancient template the liebestod tradition claims as its own first exhibit: Pyramus and Thisbe, Ovid's Babylonian pair, divided by a wall, undone by a lioness and a bloodied veil, dying by sword and by sword in sequence under a mulberry tree. The template's presence inside the young play is a matter of record rather than influence-hunting, since Shakespeare staged the story as farce in the mechanicals' entertainment in *A Midsummer Night's Dream*, in the same mid-1590s seasons, and gave the Verona tomb scene the template's exact furniture, a rendezvous at a tomb, a male suicide on a misread body, a female suicide on the male's weapon. The Egyptian play strips the template of its accidents. No lioness, no veil, no misreading of evidence in the Verona manner; Cleopatra's false message is the one borrowed device, and it is a lie told by a principal rather than a mishap of the evidence. The career's movement across the pair can be measured on this single motif: the ancient love-death begins as a machine of errors, is laughed at in a comedy, is rebuilt as a tragedy of error in Verona, and is finally rebuilt as a tragedy of intention in Egypt, where every error has an author and the authors are the lovers themselves.

### Which play lets its lovers choose death, and which play has death choose the lovers?

Egypt's lovers choose, at every stage, with open eyes. Antony chooses the stroke after weighing capture against it; Cleopatra chooses the asp after weighing the triumph against it, negotiates the interval, selects the instrument and rehearses the meaning. Verona's lovers are chosen by death in the grammatical sense: a quarantined friar, a servant's accurate report, a potion's timetable.

A feud arranges the rest, and the young pair contribute only the final gestures, his draught and her dagger, to a conclusion assembled elsewhere. Choice is the mature tragedy's grim privilege. The young tragedy grants its lovers agency everywhere except over the event that defines them.

The distinction explains the endings' different emotional temperatures. An audience leaves the Verona vault arguing with the machinery, rehearsing the small changes that would have saved everyone, because the play invites the argument: the catastrophe was avoidable at a dozen points. An audience leaves the Egyptian monument arguing with the principals, because the catastrophe there was authored by them in full knowledge, and the argument is about whether the authorship redeems it. Accident produces protest. Choice produces judgment. One career found room for both temperatures inside a single form, eleven years apart.

## The Women at the Centre: Juliet's Brevity and Cleopatra's Ceremony

Bradley's observation that only in these two tragedies is the heroine as much the centre as the hero deserves a section of its own, because the comparison's every axis runs through the women. In both plays the man dies first and the woman completes the action, which means each play's final movement is a heroine alone with a body, deciding. The decisions differ in scale exactly as everything else does. Juliet wakes in a vault, inventories her situation in a handful of lines, finds the dagger and acts before the watch can arrive. Cleopatra, told of Antony's death, has a kingdom's apparatus to wind down and an enemy to outmanoeuvre, and her completion of the action takes a full act of receptions, inventories, betrayals and robing. Brevity and ceremony are the heroines' signatures, and each signature is a theory of what a person is. Juliet is what she does in the instant of knowledge. Cleopatra is what she arranges to have been.

Juliet's intelligence, graded in its own hearing in this series, is the young play's counterweight to its machinery of accident: the heroine reasons better than anyone around her and is killed by logistics anyway. Her great solo, the potion soliloquy, is a risk assessment conducted item by item, failure modes enumerated and priced before she drinks. Her handling of Paris in the Friar's cell, saying sentences true in one sense and false in the sense he takes, is advocacy of a professional standard from a girl who has never been allowed an unshepherded conversation. The play gives her no public instrument at all. Her kingdom is a bedroom, her treasury is a rope ladder, her intelligence service is a nurse, and when the state finally hears her story it hears it from the Friar's confession, with her corpse as the exhibit.

Cleopatra's intelligence is of the opposite constitution, a ruler's, exercised through representation. Her first entrance in the story, retold by Enobarbus, is a diplomatic operation staged as a pageant: she answers a summons to account for herself by arriving as a goddess, converting a defendant's appearance into a conqueror's procession and taking the summoned meeting's host prisoner instead. The method never changes. She tests Antony with feigned rages and a feigned death, receives Caesar's ambassador Thidias and lets him kiss her hand so that the report will do her work in Antony's camp, and in the final act faces Octavius himself with a false inventory of her treasure, an accountant's lie told to keep a reserve against captivity. When her treasurer Seleucus betrays the reserve, her fury is a sovereign's fury at a servant's breach, and the scene matters to the comparison because it shows the mature heroine's statecraft failing in miniature, in a ledger, minutes before it succeeds in the grand manner, in a death Caesar cannot confiscate. Juliet never gets to fail at statecraft. She is never issued any.

The Egyptian heroine also thinks about her own representation with a sophistication the young play nowhere attempts. Facing the prospect of the triumph, she imagines the afterlife of the story in Roman street theatre: "I shall see some squeaking Cleopatra boy my greatness in the posture of a whore" (5.2). The line is a queen's horror at bad casting and a playwright's joke at his own company's expense, since a boy is speaking it, and it crystallises the mature play's awareness that love at this altitude is always also a performance judged by audiences who will restage it badly. Juliet's corresponding dread is not of representation but of the tomb itself, the vault's charnel smells and mad ancestors, faced and priced in the soliloquy. One heroine fears being misrepresented after death. The other fears death's physical address. The distance between those fears is the distance between a life lived in public and a life lived in a house.

Motherhood completes the gradient. Cleopatra's children are instruments and hostages of policy throughout: Caesar reads the Donations aloud at Rome, her sons and daughter enthroned over eastern kingdoms as Antony's offence against the state, and her death leaves a dynasty to be carried off into the victor's household, which history duly records. The love in Egypt is dynastic in its consequences whether the lovers will it or not. In Verona the lovers die before any household forms, and the parental generation surrounds them as hazard rather than heritage: a mother who was herself a bride at Juliet's age and reads the fact as a recommendation, a father whose affection converts to tyranny in one scene of refusal. The feud's true casualty list, the comparison insists, includes the unborn: Verona loses the grandchildren who might have ended the quarrel by existing, and Egypt loses the kings who might have continued one. Both plays end with the future cancelled and only the monuments, statues in one city, a grave in the other, left to impersonate it.

### Why do both plays end with the heroine deciding alone beside the hero's body?

Because each play builds toward a transfer of authorship. The men spend their plays inside systems that narrate them, a feud's script in Verona, a triumvirate's ledger in Egypt, and die when the system closes. The women, who have been managed by fathers, friars, husbands and conquerors, inherit the ending un-managed.

What each does with the inheritance is the play's last statement about her. Juliet spends it in one act of fidelity, executed in lines. Cleopatra spends it in an act of authorship, executed in scenes, writing the version of events that Caesar is then obliged to read aloud to posterity.

The pattern also explains the plays' stage lives. The tomb scene and the monument scene are the two great tests of a leading actress in the early canon, the one demanding that an entire tragedy be discharged inside a few minutes of verse, the other demanding that a few minutes of action, an asp held to the breast, be expanded into an entire tragedy. Directors cast their Juliets for compression and their Cleopatras for duration, and audiences remember the performances in the same currencies: a Juliet is recalled for the speed of the dagger, a Cleopatra for the slowness of the robing.

## Two Sources, Two Scales: A Novella's Street and a Historian's Empire

Behind the two tragedies stand two kinds of book, and the comparison's contrasts of scale descend from that bibliographical fact. The Verona story reached the playwright through the Italian novella tradition, Luigi da Porto's tale reworked by Bandello, carried into French by Boaistuau and into English by Arthur Brooke's long poem of 1562 and William Painter's prose of 1566. It is a private story in every fibre: invented persons, one city, a feud whose cause no teller bothers to state, and a moralising frame, supplied most heavily by Brooke's preface, that reads the lovers as a warning against disobedient desire. The matter arrives small and judged. The playwright's labour on it runs toward enlargement from within: months of courtship compressed into days, a nurse's confidante promoted to a principal, a wit named Mercutio invented almost from air and given the play's most electric verse, a prince built into a failing state. Everything structural in the tragedy is an addition or a compression. The source supplied a street; the play built the city that could kill on it.

The Egyptian story reached the playwright through the most public kind of book the Renaissance owned, Plutarch's *Lives*, in Sir Thomas North's translation of 1579, itself Englished from Jacques Amyot's French. Plutarch wrote biography as statecraft's mirror, and his Antony is a study of a great nature ruined by appetite and flattery, set on the largest stage the ancient world provided. The matter arrives vast and half-judged already, its outcome the foundation of the Roman Empire. The playwright's labour on it runs, surprisingly, toward interiority. The sequence of events is followed with a fidelity the young play never shows its source, war by war and embassy by embassy, while the meaning is relocated into rooms Plutarch never entered: Cleopatra's palace at its games and rages, Enobarbus promoted from a defecting officer in the history to the play's resident intelligence and finally its broken heart, Charmian and Iras given a mistress's-eye view of the queen, a clown admitted with a basket of figs to delay an empress's death with talk of worms. Where the young play rewrites its source's shape and keeps its persons, the old play keeps its source's shape and rewrites its persons' inner lives.

The barge passage is the famous exhibit of the second method, and its mechanism deserves stating plainly. North's prose describes Cleopatra's arrival at Tarsus in the Cydnus, the gilded stern, the purple sails, the silver oars, the perfumes reaching the shore. The play lifts the inventory almost item for item and entrusts it to Enobarbus, in Rome, answering colleagues who think in ledgers. Verse does not merely decorate the prose here; it reassigns it. In Plutarch the barge is a historian's evidence of luxury's power. In the play it is a lover's testimony delivered by a sceptic, the strongest kind, and its gorgeousness becomes an argument Antony is winning in a room he is absent from. The young play performs the equivalent operation in reverse on its own materials: the sonnet the lovers share at the feast takes the most bookish form in the period's love poetry and converts it into an action, two strangers building a poem as a way of touching hands. One play versifies a document into a seduction recalled. The other dramatises a document into a seduction enacted. The comparison of sources is finally a comparison of what each age of the author believed a book was for.

Setting follows source with the same fidelity. Verona, in the novella line, is a stage city of no described geography, a few streets, two houses, a church, a vault, portable to any walled town in Italy and never once surveyed. The Egyptian play's world is a surveyed empire, Alexandria, Rome, Athens, Sicyon, Actium, Misenum, places whose distances are military facts and whose names arrive with armies attached. A messenger in Verona crosses a street and can be detained by a quarantine in a house along it. A messenger in Egypt crosses the Mediterranean, and his news is a campaign's turning. The tragedies' opposed senses of space, the corridor and the map, are inherited from the kinds of narrative each source was: a tale told of one town's scandal, and a life written across the known world.

## The Critics on the Pair: Bradley's Shelf, Snyder's Machine, Fitz's Mirror

The criticism of these plays has a founding irony. The critic who named them as a pair also shelved them. Bradley's *Shakespearean Tragedy* opens by acknowledging the two love-tragedies as the plays in which the heroine stands at the centre beside the hero, a distinction he grants no other tragedies, and then excludes both from the four lectures' sequence of Hamlet, Othello, King Lear and Macbeth. His reasons differ by play, and the difference is diagnostic. Of the Verona play he wrote that it is "a pure tragedy, but it is an early work, and in some respects an immature one," and his construction lecture assigns its catastrophe to "the opposition of their houses, which, aided by accidents, produces a catastrophe." Accident, for Bradley, was a technical demotion: the mature tragedies generate their endings from character, while this one imports its ending from chance, from a letter undelivered and a waking mistimed by a minute. Of the Egyptian play he wrote that it belongs among the "tragic histories or historical tragedies," in which the playwright laboured under an obligation to follow his historical authority. One play is too free, inventing a catastrophe out of mishaps; the other is too bound, transcribing Plutarch. Between the two demotions, the pair Bradley himself had identified as the canon's only heroine-centred tragedies disappears from his main argument.

Susan Snyder's *The Comic Matrix of Shakespeare's Tragedies* of 1979 supplies the answer for the Verona half, and its precision is worth stating. Snyder reads the play as a romantic comedy whose machinery is run past its proper terminus: the blocking fathers, the go-between nurse, the friendly friar, the secret marriage, the device of the feigned death, each a stock component of the comic form, each functioning exactly as comedy prescribes, until the machinery's products begin to kill. On her reading the accidents Bradley deplored are not blemishes on the design but the design itself, comedy's equipment failing on schedule in a world that has stopped supplying comedy's protections. Her scheme of the tragedies famously proceeds from this play through Hamlet and Othello to King Lear, comedy and tragedy passing from polar opposition into a single compound, and it pointedly stops there. The Egyptian play stands outside her matrix entirely, a decade of history too large for the comic frame, which is itself a finding: the later love tragedy was built by discarding the earlier one's engine.

For the Egyptian half, the corrective arrives from L. T. Fitz, whose essay "Egyptian Queens and Male Reviewers," published in *Shakespeare Quarterly* in 1977, audited two centuries of commentary on Cleopatra and found the Roman prosecution brief copied out as criticism. Fitz documented the double standard with a bookkeeper's patience: conduct praised as largeness of soul in Antony, his generosity, his appetite, his oscillation between duty and pleasure, is condemned as cunning, sensuality and treachery in Cleopatra; the queen is blamed for interesting herself in politics, the general blamed for neglecting them, and the same act changes its moral name with the sex of the actor. Her remedy was to reread the play's Rome with the same suspicion its critics reserved for Egypt: the bride-bartering that seals Antony's marriage to Octavia, the scramble for ascendancy among the triumvirs, the unctuous hypocrisy of Octavius in the closing scenes, whose grief for Antony arrives precisely when it costs nothing. The essay reframes the comparison's public and private axis. Rome in the play is not the voice of order judging an Egyptian disorder. Rome is a rival disorder, better dressed, and its final judgment on the lovers, delivered by Caesar over the bodies, concedes the case it spent five acts prosecuting: "She shall be buried by her Antony: no grave upon the earth shall clip in it a pair so famous" (5.2).

The editors stand as the comparison's documentary witnesses, and their evidence is an asymmetry. John Wilders's Arden edition of *Antony and Cleopatra*, published in 1995, rests on a single early text, the First Folio of 1623, the play having been entered in the Stationers' Register by Edward Blount in May 1608 and then withheld from print for fifteen years; the editor's task is stewardship of one witness, comparatively clean, probably derived from a carefully prepared manuscript. René Weis's Arden edition of *Romeo and Juliet* adjudicates between competing early witnesses, the 1597 quarto, the fuller 1599 quarto and the Folio, a text argued over line by line. The young play survives in a crowd of documents and the old play in a solitary one, which suits their natures with an aptness no archivist arranged: the tragedy of the crowded street comes down in crowded copies, the tragedy of empire in a single state paper. Editors also fix the comparison's frame in time. The dating evidence Weis assembles places the Verona play's composition in 1595 or 1595 to 1596, and the Egyptian play's registration and its influence on Samuel Daniel's revised *Cleopatra* place its composition in 1606 or 1607, eleven years later, after the four tragedies Bradley lectured on were already written. The later love tragedy is thus not a young man's form outgrown but a master's form revisited, with Hamlet's soliloquy, Othello's handkerchief, Lear's storm and Macbeth's dagger all standing between the two attempts.

The stage history of the pair repeats the criticism's double gesture in theatrical terms, honouring one play by altering it and the other by abridging it. David Garrick's celebrated adaptation of the Verona play, from 1748, rewrote the tomb scene so that Juliet wakes while Romeo still lives and the lovers are granted a final exchange, an alteration audiences accepted for generations: the accident at the plot's heart was felt, even by admirers, as a defect to be repaired by sentiment. Garrick's 1759 staging of the Egyptian play, from Edward Capell's script, proceeded by the opposite surgery, abridging only, cutting a text held to be too large for an evening rather than too cruel at its close. One play was patronised for its machinery and the other for its dimensions, and both patronages descend from Bradley's double demotion. The unaltered texts make the sterner and better argument, that the machinery is the meaning in Verona and the dimensions are the meaning in Egypt.

The adjudication among these witnesses resolves as follows. Bradley is right about the pairing and wrong about both plays, in opposite directions, and the opposition is the interesting part. Verona's catastrophe is not beneath the mature standard because it runs on accident; Snyder shows the accident to be a chosen instrument, comedy's machinery heard breaking, and Bradley's own concession that only in these two plays is the heroine the action's co-centre quietly grants the young play the structural originality his demotion denies it. Egypt's tragedy is not a transcription of authority; its most celebrated effects are inventions or vast expansions, Enobarbus promoted from a name in Plutarch to the play's chorus of intelligence, the domestic scenes in Alexandria built from nothing, the death of Cleopatra composed as ceremony where the source offers a paragraph. Fitz completes the correction by showing that the Roman frame through which Bradley's generation read the play is a party to the action, not its bench. A reader who keeps the editions, the North Plutarch extracts and the performance records for both plays in one research archive, the kind of gathering a tool like [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) exists to hold, can test each of these claims against the documents in an afternoon, which is more than the shelved reputations of the two plays have usually been granted.

## The Verdict: One Career, Two Poles of the Love Tragedy

The comparison's yield can be stated in one sentence and then defended. The later play is the earlier play's controlled experiment repeated with every variable inverted, and the result of the experiment is that the catastrophe survives the inversion intact. Give the lovers time, and time arms their enemy and corrodes their trust. Give them experience, and experience teaches the woman to manage the man with a lie and the man to answer the lie with a sword. Give them a world that watches, and the watching becomes a weapon in a colder hand than any feud. Deny them all three gifts, as Verona does, and they die of a detained letter. Grant them all three, as Egypt does, and they die of their own statecraft. The love tragedy, the pair demonstrates, is not a story about young people that its author happened to revisit in middle age. It is a form with two stable configurations, the tragedy of deprivation and the tragedy of abundance, and this career is the one that built both.

That finding adjusts the standing of each play. The Verona tragedy is usually praised for its poetry and forgiven for its plot, as if the poetry were the achievement and the plot an embarrassment of accidents. Set beside its sibling, the plot emerges as the argument: a love with no time, no knowledge and no witnesses is killed by the first failure of communication, and the play's famous haste is the sound of a margin closing. The Egyptian tragedy is usually praised for its poetry and forgiven for its sprawl, as if the sprawl were the price of the verse. Set beside its sibling, the sprawl emerges as the argument too: a love with every resource is killed by the uses its owners and its enemies make of those resources, and the play's forty-two scenes are the sound of a margin closing at the speed of history. Neither play is the other's draft or correction. Each is the other's control group.

The pairing also retires, finally, the notion that the young play's intensity is a young man's luck. Read alone, the Verona tragedy can be mistaken for a prodigy's single flare, lyric genius outrunning construction. Read against the Egyptian tragedy, its construction shows the same intelligence the old play displays, working at chamber scale: the heroine-centred double action Bradley noticed, the frame that speaks and withdraws, the source compressed until its joints creak with meaning. The master of 1606 did not surpass the design of 1595 so much as re-run it under maximum load, in the way an engineer returns to a bridge design with a wider river. What changed across the eleven years is not the presence of design but the author's trust in his audience to follow design without a Chorus, across forty-two scenes and a decade, with the judging voices distributed among soldiers and servants instead of announced from a frame.

The series sets the Verona play against two further tragedies in companion comparisons, and the Egyptian comparison supplies the key to both. Against [Othello, where jealousy performs the feud's work from inside a marriage](/2031/03/07/vs-othello-jealousy-and-love/), the Egyptian play marks the far end of the scale of knowledge: Othello dies believing a manufactured lie about a wife who never managed him at all, while Antony dies believing a lie his wife manufactured, in a marriage of managers. Against [Hamlet, where a young man's delay answers the lovers' haste](/2031/03/14/vs-hamlet-young-death/), the Egyptian play again extends the scale: Hamlet's delay is a boy's scruple stretched over months, while Antony's ruin includes delays of years, a whole middle age of postponement, and ends in the same discovery, that the postponed act arrives too late in a form its delayer no longer controls. The intertext web matters because the cliche this series exists to refuse, the Verona play as a self-contained valentine, cannot survive contact with any of its siblings. Beside Cleopatra's monument, the Capulet vault takes its proper dimensions: not the whole of love tragedy, but its first and purest pole, the configuration in which love has nothing and loses it, set for ever against the configuration in which love has everything and loses that.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which of the two plays was written first, and roughly how many years of Shakespeare's career separate them?

The Verona play was written first, around 1595 or 1595-96, early in its author's career as a sharer in the Chamberlain's Men; the Egyptian play followed some eleven years later, around 1606-07, when the company had become the King's Men and its author was the period's leading tragedian. The interval spans most of the canonical tragedies: *Hamlet*, *Othello*, *King Lear* and *Macbeth* all fall between the two love tragedies, which is why the later play reads as an answer written from the far side of a revolution in its author's practice. The documents mark the distance dryly. The young play was printed in quarto in 1597 and again in 1599. The old play has no quarto at all; it was entered in the Stationers' Register in 1608 and first printed in the Folio of 1623, the single authoritative witness. A decade's craft separates a sonnet-built chamber tragedy from a forty-two-scene world history, and the comparison between them is, among its other uses, a career measurement: the same dramatist, the same subject, the poles of everything learned in between.

### Q: Why does Charmian straighten Cleopatra's crown after the queen is dead?

Because the tableau is the message. Charmian, herself dying from the second asp, is discovered by Caesar's guards beside her mistress's body, and her last act before falling is to adjust the crown on the dead queen's head, answering the guard's question about whether this was well done with the judgment that it is fitting for a princess descended of so many royal kings. Inside the comparison, the gesture is Egypt's equivalent of Verona's closing choreography, the fathers' hands joined over the bodies and the statues promised in gold: a final piece of stage management by which the dead are converted into the version of themselves the living intend history to keep. The difference measures the two worlds. In Verona the conversion is performed by the state, the Prince presiding, the families paying. In Egypt it is performed by a servant girl with her last breath, against the state's arrival, and Caesar can only ratify what a waiting-woman has already arranged. The crown, straightened, is Cleopatra's authorship made visible, her final argument that she died a queen and not a captive, entered into the record by the humblest witness on the stage.

### Q: What false report of Cleopatra's death does Antony believe, and how is it unlike Balthasar's report in Verona?

Cleopatra, fearing Antony's rage after he blames her fleet's flight at Actium for his ruin, sends the eunuch Mardian with a scripted falsehood: report that she has killed herself, that her last word was Antony's name, and make the message sound wrung from grief. Antony believes it without reserve, falls on his sword in imitation of the death reported to him, and is told, once the wound is beyond recall, that the queen lives and has taken refuge in her monument. Balthasar's report in Verona is the same mechanism with its morality inverted. Balthasar tells the truth: he saw Juliet laid in the Capulet vault, and his eyes did not deceive him, since the body was hers and the sleep only counterfeit. The Egyptian lie is deliberate, authored by a principal testing a lover's temper with a fabricated death, and it kills by succeeding. The Veronese truth is accidental in its falsity, an honest message about a staged appearance, and it kills by arriving where the Friar's correcting letter did not. One hero is destroyed by a woman's fiction; the other by a servant's fact, and the comparison's judgment on the two worlds hangs in that difference.

### Q: How does death by the asp differ from death by the dagger as each play stages it?

The dagger in Verona is an instrument of haste seized from the scene itself. Juliet finds it on Romeo's body, takes it with a sentence of welcome, and dies within lines, the play's tempo unbroken to the final second of her life; the weapon is a husband's property turned to a wife's use, and its staging is pure compression. The asp in Egypt is an instrument of ceremony procured in advance. It arrives hidden in a basket of figs, is delayed by a clown's country talk about the worm's biting, and is applied by a robed and crowned queen to breast and arm in a tableau she has rehearsed in speech after speech. Where the dagger's death is discovered, still warm, by the entering watch, the asp's death is designed to be discovered, a composition in gold cloth and diadem that Caesar's men find already arranged like an altar. The instruments state the tragedies' theses in props. Verona's love dies by whatever lies nearest, in a borrowed tomb, at the speed of panic. Egypt's love dies by appointment, in a royal monument, at the pace of ritual, and the poison's slowness is part of the sovereignty being asserted.

### Q: What does Caesar promise the dead lovers' burial that no one in Verona can offer?

Caesar, standing over the bodies in the monument, orders the state funeral of the Egyptian state itself: Cleopatra shall be buried beside Antony, and no grave on earth shall hold a pair so famous, with the army attending in solemn show before marching for Rome. The promise outbids anything available in Verona because of who makes it. The Verona lovers are commemorated by their fathers, two private citizens commissioning statues in pure gold, and presided over by a Prince whose authority the catastrophe has just exposed as failing; the city's grief is genuine and its instruments are those of a family. The Egyptian lovers are commemorated by their conqueror, the master of the world, who converts them into an imperial monument as an act of state, annexing their fame along with their country. No one in Verona can offer the Egyptian pair's portion, a grave guaranteed by the power that rules the earth, and no one in Egypt can offer the Veronese pair's portion, commemoration by kin who loved them and were changed by losing them. Each ending's honour is priced in its own world's currency, and the currencies do not exchange.

### Q: Why does Cleopatra put on her robe and crown before taking the asp to her breast?

Because the death is an investiture as much as a suicide. Cleopatra has her attendants robe her and set the crown on her head, calls for the regalia of the goddess Isis in whose dress she has governed and been worshipped, and announces that she has immortal longings in her. The robing converts an execution she refuses to wait for into a coronation she performs herself, and it answers the triumph she dreads point by point: where Caesar's procession would display her in chains as a trophy, her monument displays her on a golden bed as a sovereign, and the difference between the two displays is the last territory she holds. The comparison with Verona sharpens the meaning. Juliet dies in the dress of her interrupted life, a bride taken from a feast to a vault, with no costume available to her but the shroud she imagined in her terror before drinking the potion. Cleopatra dies in the dress of her office, chosen, assisted and witnessed. One heroine's death is costumed by circumstance. The other's is costumed by command, and the command, at the end, is the last proof of the queenship the war was fought to deny.

### Q: What judging witness does each play set beside its lovers?

Verona sets the Prince. Escalus presides over the city's order, bans the brawling on pain of death, arrives at the vault to conduct the inquest, and closes the play by distributing praise, blame and pardon, including a share of judgment on himself for winking at the discords. He is a single, lawful, fallible judge, the state's conscience in one person, and the lovers' story reaches him only when it is finished. Egypt sets a gallery. Philo judges in the first speech, Enobarbus judges with a friend's ambivalence until judgment breaks his heart, Agrippa and Maecenas judge as staff officers, Pompey judges as a rival, and Caesar judges as the future, finally standing in the monument to deliver the epitaph. The judging in the later play is distributed among the interested, so that every verdict arrives compromised by its speaker's stake, Rome's moral account of the love being inseparable from Rome's material account of the empire. Verona's lovers face a court. Egypt's lovers face a market of opinions, quoted in every camp, and the tragedy of reputation in the public world is that the market, not the court, writes the record Caesar eventually reads aloud.

### Q: What do Verona's golden statues and Egypt's famous grave share as acts of public memory?

Both are settlements negotiated in precious material, attempts by the surviving order to convert an intolerable story into a bearable monument. The fathers in Verona end their feud by competitive generosity, each promising the other's child a statue in pure gold, so that the lovers enter the city's memory as civic ornaments paid for by the men whose quarrel killed them. Caesar in Egypt orders the joint burial and the solemn show, so that the lovers enter the empire's memory as a famous pair underwritten by the man whose victory killed them. In each case the commemorators are the catastrophe's co-authors, and the commemoration is partly penance and partly appropriation: the order that destroyed the love acquires the love's story as an asset. The young play makes the transaction domestic and guild-like, goldsmiths' work for a city's square. The old play makes it imperial, a grave guaranteed against the whole earth. What the two share, beneath the difference of scale, is the discovery that public memory is the final prize the lovers' deaths award, and that it is always awarded to the survivors, who spend it as they choose.

### Q: How long have the Egyptian lovers known each other before the catastrophe, and what does that history buy them that one evening cannot?

Around a decade, from Cleopatra's procession up the Cydnus to meet Antony at Tarsus in 41 BC to the deaths at Alexandria in 30 BC, a span the play compresses into forty-two scenes but keeps reminding its audience of through references to campaigns, children, marriages and settlements made across those years. The history buys the Egyptian lovers what the Verona lovers are never given time to acquire: knowledge of each other's worst. Cleopatra has seen Antony defeated, uxorious, raging and ridiculous; Antony has been deceived, tested and abandoned by Cleopatra in council and in battle; and their love persists not in innocence of these facts but across them, renewed at the monument with an information-complete consent. One evening at a Verona feast can produce vows, a shared sonnet and a marriage, but it cannot produce a shared past, and the plays' opposed tragedies follow from the possession and the lack. Romeo and Juliet die for a future they were denied. Antony and Cleopatra die at the end of a past they are denied permission to keep, and their catastrophe has the weight of inventory, a whole furnished life carried into the tomb piece by piece.

### Q: What part does Octavius play in the Egyptian tragedy that no figure in Verona can play?

Octavius is the future arriving in person, a rival principal whose victory is total, permanent and historically ratified, and whose existence converts the love story into a change of world order. He fights the lovers in council and at sea, outlasts them by discipline, visits the captive queen in a contest of performances that both parties know is theatre, and closes the play as the sole ruler of the world pronouncing the lovers' epitaph and ordering their burial. Verona has no equivalent because Verona's order is not conquering anything; the Prince can only preserve a status quo, fining and pardoning inside a city whose troubles are internal. Octavius does not preserve. He replaces, annexing Egypt, extinguishing a dynasty and founding the settlement under which the audience's own tradition said an empire and an era began. His presence bends the tragedy into history: every scene between the lovers is also a scene in the transfer of the world, and Cleopatra's death is timed against his triumph as the last act of statecraft available to her. No Verona figure can play the part because the young play's world has no outside of that size pressing on the lovers' door.

### Q: How do the two openings, a sonnet from a Chorus and a soldier's complaint, frame the same subject in opposite ways?

The Verona play opens outside its fiction, with a Chorus speaking a sonnet that names the lovers, their houses and their deaths before any character appears, so the audience watches the action as a sentence already passed; the Egyptian play opens inside its fiction, with Philo, a Roman officer, complaining to a colleague that their general's heart has burst the buckles of its military discipline for a gypsy's face. The subject is one, a great man's or a young pair's love set against the order that condemns it, but the framings are epistemic opposites. Verona's voice is omniscient, external and finished: it knows the price and says the story is worth two hours of attention. Egypt's voice is partisan, internal and provisional: a subordinate's grievance, offered in the hearing of the audience the lovers are about to enter and charm. The early play asks its watchers to judge with the ending known, which breeds pity. The later play asks its watchers to judge through interested testimony, which breeds suspicion, and each tragedy's whole machinery of witnesses grows from its first minute's choice about who may be trusted to describe a love.

### Q: Why does Cleopatra send word that she is dead, and what does her lie expose that Juliet's honesty never risks?

Cleopatra sends Mardian because Antony, after the flight at Actium, has turned on her in a rage that blames her ships for his disgrace, and she retreats to the monument to manage a temper she cannot safely meet. The false report is an instrument, designed to convert his anger into grief and grief into a return on her terms; it is statecraft applied to a lover, the habit of a ruler who has governed men by theatre for twenty years. The lie exposes the mature passion's constitutive doubleness: in Egypt even sincerity must be staged to be effective, and a message about the heart travels by eunuch, script and rehearsed sob. Juliet's honesty never risks that exposure because her love has no instrumental repertoire to protect. Her deceptions are defensive and borrowed, equivocations in a friar's cell arranged by other hands, and her one great stratagem, the potion, is aimed at her father's household, never at Romeo. She hides facts from the world to reach her husband. Cleopatra fabricates a fact to govern hers, and the fabrication, believed, kills him before the correction can arrive.

### Q: What children do the Egyptian lovers leave behind, and how does parenthood change the price of their deaths?

The Egyptian lovers leave four children whose existence the play keeps in its political foreground: Caesarion, Cleopatra's son by Julius Caesar, and the three children of her union with Antony, the twins Alexander Helios and Cleopatra Selene and their younger brother Ptolemy Philadelphus. Caesar's report of the Donations of Alexandria reads their enthronement aloud at Rome as an item in the indictment, the eastern kingdoms settled on infants being Antony's offence made genealogical. Parenthood changes the price of the Egyptian deaths from a private catastrophe into a dynastic extinction: the parents' suicides orphan a rival royal house, and the settlement that follows disposes of the children along with the kingdom, Caesarion killed as a competing Caesar, the younger children absorbed into the victor's household. Verona's lovers leave no one. Their deaths extinguish futures rather than persons, the unborn heirs of Montague and Capulet, and the feud's settlement is priced accordingly, in statues rather than successions. The comparison measures one of its widest gaps here: a love with descendants is a fact of state that a conqueror must administer, while a love without them is a story a city must only agree to remember.

### Q: Are Antony and Cleopatra married in their play as the Verona lovers are married in theirs?

No, and the absence is structural. The Verona lovers are married on stage, in Friar Laurence's cell, in a ceremony the play presents entire, with the sacrament's language and a witness, so that every later claim of wife and husband in the tragedy rests on a performed and binding act. The Egyptian lovers are given no ceremony at all. The play shows Antony married instead to Octavia, in a match arranged between the triumvirs and reported with full diplomatic weight, while his union with Cleopatra exists as consortship, pageantry and proclamation, the Donations styling them a royal couple before the eastern crowd without any rite the drama pauses to perform. Cleopatra claims the title of wife only in the grammar of her dying speech, naming Antony her husband as she takes the asp, a marriage asserted at the moment it can no longer be contradicted or ratified. Plutarch records a union celebrated in Egyptian fashion that Rome declined to recognise, and the play preserves exactly that ambiguity. Verona's tragedy is of a marriage the world refuses to know. Egypt's is of a marriage the world refuses to call one, and the refusal is among the causes of the war.

### Q: From what kind of source does each play come, and how does the source shape the tragedy's scale?

The Verona play descends from a novella tradition, the Italian tale of Luigi da Porto reworked by Bandello and carried into English by Arthur Brooke's poem of 1562 and William Painter's prose, a story of invented private citizens in a single city, moralised by its tellers as a warning. The Egyptian play descends from historiography, Plutarch's *Life of Antony* in Sir Thomas North's translation, a documented statesman's career written across the known world and already half-judged by its author. The source kind fixes the tragedy's scale before a line is written. A novella supplies a street, two households and a feud without a stated cause, and the playwright's labour runs inward, inventing pressure, company and calendar. A *Life* supplies fleets, provinces and a decade of settlements, and the playwright's labour runs outward from the record, inventing interiors the history never entered: palace games, a sceptic's grief, a clown with figs. Each play's faithfulness is the other's opposite. The young play rewrites its source's shape, squeezing months into days. The old play keeps its source's sequence of wars and embassies and rewrites only the inner lives, which is why one tragedy feels invented and the other excavated.

### Q: Which ending leaves the larger public wreckage behind it, a city's heirs or an empire's settlement?

Egypt's, by every material measure, and Verona's, by the measure of proportion. The Egyptian catastrophe annexes a kingdom to Rome, extinguishes the Ptolemaic dynasty, kills Caesarion, disposes of three royal children, removes one triumvir and leaves a single ruler in possession of the world; the settlement is an era's foundation, entered in the histories as the end of the Roman republic's long crisis. No private grief in the play is allowed to compete with that arithmetic, which is why Caesar, and not a parent, delivers the commemoration. Verona's wreckage is a street's width by comparison: two houses bereaved of their only heirs, a count dead, a mother dead of grief, a friar disgraced, a prince's authority shamed, and a feud ended by exhaustion rather than settlement. Yet within its scale the loss is as total as Egypt's, since both cities' futures are what has died; Montague and Capulet have no succession to negotiate, only statues to commission. The comparison's answer is therefore double. In the currency of territory and population the empire's settlement dwarfs the city's. In the currency of what each world can no longer become, the small play's ending is the more complete extinction.

### Q: Why does Cleopatra dread being led in Caesar's triumph, and what display does Verona substitute for a triumph?

Cleopatra dreads the triumph because it is the Roman state's final act of authorship over her: the conquered queen displayed in procession through Rome, hooted by the crowd, handled by what she calls saucy lictors, and then handed over to the street theatres, where hired comedians would stage her story coarsely for a mob. Her suicide is timed and costumed to confiscate that spectacle in advance, substituting a monument tableau of her own design for the procession Caesar had designed. Verona substitutes for a triumph a pair of statues. The fathers, ending the feud, promise each other's dead child an image in pure gold, so that the lovers are displayed perpetually in the city's square as honoured effigies rather than paraded once as captives. The two displays are the same institution read from opposite ends of fortune. A triumph exhibits the enemy's body to prove the state's power over it. A statue exhibits the beloved's image to confess the community's debt to it. Cleopatra, who cannot be given a statue by her conqueror, manufactures the equivalent herself out of robe, crown and asp, and Caesar's closing speech ratifies the substitution he came to prevent.

### Q: How do the heroines' final speeches differ: Cleopatra's ceremony against Juliet's brevity?

Cleopatra's final speeches are a ceremony distributed across an act: instructions to attendants, a contest of courtesy with Caesar, the robing, the invocation of Antony as husband, the address to the asp as a lover's knot, farewells to her women, and a running commentary that composes her death for its discoverers while it happens. The speech is statecraft continued by other means, a queen issuing the authorised version of her end to every witness in the room, and its length is its meaning, since duration is the sovereignty she still commands. Juliet's final speech is a handful of lines in a vault filling with armed men: a refusal to leave, a reading of the cup in Romeo's hand, a kiss tested for poison, a noise interpreted, and the dagger welcomed as a sheath in a sentence that completes itself as the act does. Where Cleopatra speaks to be recorded, Juliet speaks only to decide, and the verse obeys the same contrast, expansive periods in Egypt, staccato clauses in Verona. The two speeches are their plays in miniature, and a production is usually judged by whether its heroines can sustain, respectively, the long composition and the short one.
