The Indira Gandhi Raj Narain 1975 case is usually remembered as the lawsuit that helped trigger a national Emergency, and that memory, though accurate as chronology, buries the judgment that ended the case. On 7 November 1975 a five judge Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court of India did two things in a single order. The bench struck down clauses (4) and (5) of Article 329A, the provisions through which the Thirty-Ninth Amendment had placed the election of a Prime Minister beyond the reach of any court and had declared a pending election dispute decided by legislative command. The same bench then applied the election law as Parliament had rewritten it during August 1975 and upheld Indira Gandhi's 1971 election from Rae Bareli, setting aside the Allahabad High Court judgment of 12 June 1975 that had voided that election and disqualified her for six years. This article defends one claim about that double outcome. The November 1975 verdict was the first operational application of the basic structure doctrine after Kesavananda Bharati in 1973, and its two component structure, a constitutional amendment clause destroyed for damaging the basic structure alongside an election saved by ordinary legislation, set the pattern through which the doctrine worked for decades afterward.

## The Rae Bareli election of 1971 and the petition Raj Narain filed

The case began as an ordinary election petition with extraordinary parties. In the general election of March 1971, held after Indira Gandhi advised the dissolution of the Lok Sabha more than a year before its term expired, the Prime Minister contested the Rae Bareli constituency in Uttar Pradesh against Raj Narain, the combative socialist leader who had opposed her as the candidate of the combined opposition effort in the constituency. The wider election gave her Congress (R) a commanding majority on the Garibi Hatao platform, a mandate examined in [the 1971 election and the Bangladesh war](/2019/04/12/1971-election-bangladesh-war-india/). At Rae Bareli the returning officer declared Indira Gandhi elected by a margin exceeding one lakh votes. Raj Narain refused the verdict of the ballot and chose the verdict of a court instead. In April 1971 he filed Election Petition No. 5 of 1971 in the Allahabad High Court, the forum that the Representation of the People Act 1951 assigns to challenges against parliamentary elections, and asked the court to declare the Prime Minister's election void.

The petition charged corrupt practices under section 123 of the Representation of the People Act, and its allegations ranged across the whole conduct of a high profile campaign. Raj Narain alleged that Indira Gandhi had obtained the assistance of Yashpal Kapoor, an officer on special duty in the Prime Minister's Secretariat and a gazetted officer of the Government of India, for the furtherance of her election prospects at a time when Kapoor remained in government service. He alleged that officers of the Uttar Pradesh administration, including the district authorities at Rae Bareli, had built rostrums, arranged loudspeakers and supplied other facilities for her election meetings, again within the prohibition of section 123(7), which forbids a candidate from obtaining the assistance of named classes of government servants. The petition further alleged the use of Indian Air Force aircraft and helicopters for campaign travel, expenditure beyond the statutory ceiling, the distribution of goods to voters, and appeals framed around the election symbols of rival candidates. Each charge, if proved, carried the same statutory consequence under the Act as it then stood: the election would be void, and the returned candidate would face disqualification.

Two features of the petition matter for everything that followed. The first is that an election petition is a creature of statute rather than of common law. The rights it creates, the grounds it recognises and the remedies it grants all come from the Representation of the People Act, a point the Supreme Court would repeat in November 1975 when the amended text of that Act decided the outcome. The second is that the petition put the Prime Minister herself inside a trial. Election petitions proceed on evidence, with witnesses examined and cross examined, documents proved and disputed, and findings recorded issue by issue. The personal dimension of that trial, a serving Prime Minister summoned to answer a defeated rival's charges in open court, formed part of [the personality and institution question that defined Indira Gandhi's career](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/). Raj Narain, represented by the advocate Shanti Bhushan, pursued the petition through four years of procedural combat before a single judge at Allahabad could reach the merits.

The constitutional ground shifted beneath the litigation while the trial crawled forward. In April 1973, midway through the petition's life, a thirteen judge bench decided Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala and held, by the narrowest of margins, that Parliament could amend the Constitution under Article 368 but could not damage or destroy its basic structure. The announcement of that doctrine, traced in [the basic-structure-doctrine establishment](/2020/08/02/kesavananda-bharati-1973-reanalysed/), meant that by the time the election case reached its climax, Indian law possessed a weapon whose first battlefield use had not yet occurred. The election petition also brushed against the privilege of the state. When a witness from the Uttar Pradesh confidential department declined to produce documents on grounds of state privilege, the dispute travelled to the Supreme Court, which in January 1975, in the connected litigation titled State of Uttar Pradesh v Raj Narain, allowed the claim of privilege in part and returned the trial to Allahabad. The name of that privilege appeal, with Raj Narain as respondent, later attached itself to the whole saga.

## A Prime Minister in the witness box at Allahabad

The trial before Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha in the Allahabad High Court became, in February and March 1975, a proceeding without precedent in the republic. Indira Gandhi entered the witness box on 18 March 1975, the first serving Prime Minister of India to give evidence in a court, and submitted to cross examination by Shanti Bhushan on the details of her own campaign. The spectacle mattered less than the record it produced. Election law turns on dated acts by named persons, and the evidence at Allahabad fixed the dates around which the entire case would turn. Yashpal Kapoor had tendered his resignation from government service by a letter dated 13 January 1971, expressed to take effect from 14 January 1971. The acceptance of that resignation was notified in the Gazette on 25 January 1971. Between those dates Kapoor had spoken at election meetings for Indira Gandhi, including meetings in the constituency during January 1971. The High Court would have to decide whether Kapoor remained a government servant in law during the days he campaigned, and whether the candidate had obtained his assistance within the meaning of section 123(7).

The rostrum charge turned on a parallel set of dated facts. The district administration at Rae Bareli, acting through its officers, had arranged the construction of rostrums and the installation of loudspeakers for the Prime Minister's election meetings in February 1971, and had supplied facilities that a private campaign would ordinarily arrange for itself. The defence answered that district officers acted in discharge of their duties toward a visiting Prime Minister, that security and protocol required official arrangements, and that assistance rendered by an officer in the purported discharge of official duty stood outside the mischief of section 123(7). The evidence also covered the charges that Justice Sinha would eventually reject. Witnesses spoke to the alleged use of Air Force aircraft, to expenditure accounts, to blankets and liquor said to have been distributed, and to speeches said to have appealed to voters on prohibited grounds. The petitioner's case, witness by witness, either hardened into a finding or dissolved for want of proof.

Arguments closed on 23 May 1975, the last working day before the High Court's summer vacation. The interval between argument and judgment carried its own tensions, recorded later by participants and historians: reports circulated of pressure around the court, and Justice Sinha delivered his judgment on 12 June 1975 under a grant of leave that the whole country awaited. Granville Austin, in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), treats the Allahabad proceedings as a demonstration that the forms of law retained their force even when the defendant was the head of government, because the trial judge insisted on evidence, on procedure and on findings rather than on the political weight of the parties. That insistence is the thread to hold. Whatever the November 1975 Supreme Court eventually did with the Allahabad findings, it worked on a record that a single judge had built through the ordinary discipline of a trial, and the discipline of that record shaped the questions the Constitution Bench could ask.

## The Allahabad judgment of 12 June 1975 stated by its holdings

Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha allowed Election Petition No. 5 of 1971 in part, and the part he allowed was enough to unseat a Prime Minister. On the Kapoor charge he held that Yashpal Kapoor remained in the service of the Government of India until 25 January 1971, the date on which the acceptance of his resignation was notified, and that Indira Gandhi had obtained and procured Kapoor's assistance for the furtherance of her election prospects during that period, including Kapoor's campaign speeches of January 1971. That conduct, the judgment held, was a corrupt practice under section 123(7) of the Representation of the People Act. On the rostrum charge he held that the officers of the Uttar Pradesh government at Rae Bareli had, at the instance of the returned candidate's election organisation, constructed rostrums and arranged loudspeakers and allied facilities for her election meetings, and that obtaining such assistance from government servants of the prohibited classes was likewise a corrupt practice under the same clause. Two findings of corrupt practice sufficed in law. The judgment declared the election of Indira Gandhi from Rae Bareli void, and, applying the disqualification that the Act attached to a finding of corrupt practice, barred her from contesting elections and holding elected office for six years from the date of the judgment.

### What did the Allahabad High Court actually hold on 12 June 1975?

The Allahabad High Court held two charges proved and the rest unproved. Justice Sinha found that Indira Gandhi obtained Yashpal Kapoor's assistance while Kapoor remained a gazetted officer, and that district officers built rostrums and arranged loudspeakers for her meetings. Both were corrupt practices under section 123(7), so her election was declared void and she was disqualified for six years.

The rejected charges deserve equal weight in any honest account, because they mark the boundary Justice Sinha drew between proof and allegation. He did not find proved the charges concerning the use of Indian Air Force aircraft and helicopters as a corrupt practice attributable to the candidate under the Act. He rejected the allegation that campaign expenditure had exceeded the statutory ceiling in the manner pleaded. He declined to sustain the charges built on the alleged distribution of blankets and liquor and on the alleged appeal in the name of a rival symbol. On issue after issue the judgment separated what the petitioner's evidence established from what it merely asserted. The result was a verdict that unseated the Prime Minister on two comparatively narrow grounds of statutory election law rather than on the sweeping indictment the petition had sought. A fuller treatment of the trial and the verdict appears in [the Allahabad judgment of June 1975](/2019/04/17/allahabad-judgment-june-1975-indira/), which carries the petition's history in detail.

Justice Sinha then did a further thing that shaped the fortnight ahead. On the application of the Prime Minister's counsel, he stayed the operation of his own judgment for twenty days, unconditionally, so that the Congress Party could settle its leadership question and the respondent could pursue an appeal. The stay converted an immediate vacancy into a dated deadline. Twenty days from 12 June ran to the first days of July, and every political actor in Delhi could read the calendar. The judgment's legal content was narrow. Its political content was immense, because a Prime Minister found guilty of corrupt practices by a High Court, holding office on borrowed time under a stay granted by the very judge who had convicted her, faced demands for resignation from the opposition and from within public life that no governing party could treat as routine.

Raj Narain's stated case, in the petition and in the argument Shanti Bhushan built upon it, was that election law either binds the powerful or binds nobody. His petition treated the Prime Minister as a candidate like any other, answerable for the assistance her campaign accepted and for the officers whose labour her meetings consumed. The government's stated defence, in the High Court and afterward, was that the two findings rested on technical constructions of section 123(7) that the facts could not bear: Kapoor had resigned before he campaigned in substance, whatever the Gazette date said, and district officers who arranged facilities for a Prime Minister's visit acted under official duty rather than as campaign assistance. Both positions were argued at their strongest in the courts that heard them, and both survived into the Supreme Court appeal in recognisable form. The Allahabad judgment chose between them on the law as it stood in June 1975. The appeal would be decided, in November, on law that Parliament changed in August.

## Thirteen days in June: the stay, the rally and the Emergency

Indira Gandhi appealed to the Supreme Court, and the appeal came first before a vacation bench of a single judge, Justice V. R. Krishna Iyer, on 24 June 1975. The order Justice Krishna Iyer made that day was a conditional stay, and its conditions drew a precise line through the Prime Minister's position. The Allahabad judgment's disqualifying operation remained stayed until the appeal was decided, so Indira Gandhi continued as a member of the Lok Sabha and as Prime Minister. She could attend the Lok Sabha, address it and take part in its proceedings in her capacity as Prime Minister. She could not, however, vote in the House as a member, and she was not entitled to draw the salary and allowances of a member of Parliament while the stay operated. The order kept the government functioning and kept the judgment alive at the same time. It satisfied neither camp. The opposition read the conditions as confirmation that the Prime Minister held office under a judicial cloud. The government read the stay as vindication enough to continue.

The political confrontation escalated on the following day. On 25 June 1975 Jayaprakash Narayan addressed a mass rally at the Ramlila Maidan in Delhi and called upon the police and the armed forces to refuse orders they judged illegal, a call the government treated as incitement to indiscipline in the services. That night, on the advice of the Prime Minister, President Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed signed a proclamation of Emergency under Article 352 of the Constitution on the ground that the security of India was threatened by internal disturbance. Arrests of opposition leaders began before dawn on 26 June 1975, preventive detention under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act emptied the opposition benches, and pre censorship descended on the press. The sequence matters to this article for one institutional reason. The Emergency did not decide the election case. It changed the conditions under which the election case would be decided, including the composition of the Parliament that would rewrite the applicable law and the freedom of the public arena in which the Supreme Court's eventual judgment would be received. The Emergency as a political order, its excesses and its apparatus, belongs to [the 1975-1977 Emergency political-architectural context](/2019/04/23/emergency-1975-1977-reanalysed/), and the verdict of the electorate upon it belongs to the Janata interregnum that followed. The election case ran on a separate track through the same months.

One further event of those days belongs in the record because the November judgment cannot be understood without it. Nani Palkhivala, the eminent constitutional advocate who had argued Kesavananda Bharati and who had been engaged in the Prime Minister's defence, returned her brief after the Emergency proclamation, unwilling to appear for a government that had proclaimed it. The gesture, widely reported, signalled that the coming appeal would be fought in a courtroom where the country's most celebrated constitutional lawyer stood aside on a point of conscience, while Shanti Bhushan remained briefed for Raj Narain. The Supreme Court fixed the appeal for hearing before a Constitution Bench in August 1975. Before the bench could sit, Parliament intervened in the litigation itself, and it is to that intervention, executed in two statutes during July and August 1975, that the case owes its constitutional magnitude.

## Parliament rewrites the ground in July and August 1975

Parliament assembled on 21 July 1975 in a session held under Emergency conditions, with leading opposition members in detention and the press under censorship. In the space of three weeks the governing majority enacted the measures that transformed a pending election appeal into a constitutional confrontation. The Thirty-Eighth Amendment, enacted at the end of July 1975, barred judicial review of the Emergency proclamation itself. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act 1975, passed by both Houses in the first week of August and brought into force on 6 August 1975, rewrote the Representation of the People Act with retrospective effect, and it rewrote precisely the provisions on which the Allahabad findings rested. The Constitution (Thirty-Ninth Amendment) Act 1975, which received the President's assent on 10 August 1975, then amended the Constitution itself by inserting Article 329A. The two statutes worked at different levels, and the difference between those levels decided the case. One statute changed ordinary law. The other attempted to change the Constitution's allocation of power. The Supreme Court would sustain the first and strike the second.

### Which laws did Parliament change before the Supreme Court heard the appeal?

Parliament changed the law at two levels in August 1975. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act rewrote the Representation of the People Act retrospectively, redefining a candidate and fixing resignation dates by Gazette notification. The Thirty-Ninth Amendment then inserted Article 329A, removing the Prime Minister's election from court jurisdiction altogether.

The Election Laws (Amendment) Act attacked each Allahabad finding at its root. It amended the definition of candidate in section 79(b) of the principal Act so that a person counts as a candidate from nomination, and it gave the amended definition retrospective operation. Indira Gandhi had filed her nomination papers at Rae Bareli on 1 February 1971, so assistance obtained before that date could no longer be assistance obtained by a candidate at all. It added an explanation to section 123 under which the publication in the Official Gazette of a government servant's resignation, with the date from which the resignation took effect, is conclusive proof of that date. Kapoor's Gazette notification recorded his resignation as effective from 14 January 1971, so the amended law treated him as out of government service from 14 January, whatever the interval between his letter of 13 January and the notification of 25 January might have meant under the unamended Act. It further provided that assistance rendered by a government officer in the discharge or purported discharge of official duty would not count as assistance for the furtherance of a candidate's prospects, a provision aimed at the rostrum finding, where district officers had acted under colour of their duties toward a visiting Prime Minister. Retrospection did the rest. The amended Act governed pending proceedings, so the Supreme Court in November would test the Allahabad findings against a statute book that had been rewritten after the findings were recorded.

Article 329A attempted something categorically larger. Its opening clauses provided that the election to either House of Parliament of a person holding the office of Prime Minister or of Speaker of the Lok Sabha could be called in question only before an authority or forum established by a law made by Parliament, and that the existing election law would not apply to such elections in its ordinary form. Clause (4), the provision at the heart of the case, then addressed elections already decided and disputes already pending. It declared that no election of such a person should be deemed void or ever to have become void on any ground, that any order of a court declaring such an election void would itself be void, and that pending petitions and appeals touching such an election would abate and stand disposed of in conformity with the declaration. Clause (5) operated with it to dispose of the pending appeal on the terms clause (4) dictated. The architecture deserves plain statement. Clause (4) did not change the law governing future elections and leave courts to apply the change. It reached into a named, pending dispute, annulled a court's order by constitutional text, and pronounced the result itself. In substance it was a legislative judgment in a single identified case, dressed as a constitutional amendment.

The government's stated defence of the package, advanced by its law officers in Parliament and later in court, held that Parliament's constituent power under Article 368, as settled by Kesavananda Bharati, permitted it to restructure the forum for prime ministerial election disputes, that validating statutes are a familiar legislative instrument, and that the governance of the country could not be held hostage to technical findings in a single election trial. Raj Narain's stated case in reply held that clause (4) crossed the line Kesavananda had drawn: it damaged free and fair elections by abolishing their judicial enforcement, it usurped the judicial function by deciding a pending case, and it placed one office holder's election beyond the law that governed every other member's. Students assembling the primary texts for this dispute can keep the amendment, the judgment and the election statute open side by side through [an office file viewer for working with Excel, DOCX and PPTX documents](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html), which suits the documentary character of the record. The bench that assembled in August 1975 therefore faced three questions rather than one. Could the amendment stand. Could the amended election law stand. And on the law that survived, did the election stand.

The November judgment repays study as a structure before it repays study as a narrative, and the table below, labelled here as the Two-Component Verdict Map, sets out that structure in citable form. Its first row records the component that destroyed a constitutional provision. Its second row records the component that saved an election. Its remaining rows record the pattern the combination established and the named applications through which later benches worked the pattern out. Read against the map, the chronology of August to November 1975 rearranges itself. The Emergency context explains why Parliament legislated in haste and why the courtroom atmosphere was charged, but the map shows that the judgment's durable content lies in its division of labour between the two components. Component One spoke to the Constitution and has been cited ever since. Component Two spoke to a repealed and rewritten statute and has almost no life outside the case. Commentators who merge the components, whether to praise the bench for courage or to condemn it for surrender, misdescribe the document the five judges signed. The hearing that produced the map began on 11 August 1975, the day after the Thirty-Ninth Amendment received assent, and the arguments the bench heard map onto the same two components with unusual clarity.

## The Kesavananda inheritance: the doctrine the 1975 bench received

To grasp what the November bench did, a reader must first grasp how uncertain the instrument was that it picked up. Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala, decided on 24 April 1973, had produced eleven opinions from thirteen judges and a short summary, signed by nine, recording that the amending power under Article 368 does not enable Parliament to alter the basic structure or framework of the Constitution. The summary settled the existence of the limit. It did not settle the limit's content with any precision, because the judges who accepted the limit listed its features differently, and it did not settle the standard by which a court would decide that a given amendment had crossed from alteration into damage. No provision had been struck down in Kesavananda itself. The Twenty-Fourth, Twenty-Fifth and Twenty-Ninth Amendments survived their challenge, the Twenty-Fifth in part, and a government lawyer reading the case in 1974 could plausibly maintain that the doctrine was a ceiling the courts had described but would never enforce against a determined Parliament. The long argument over amendment and judicial review that runs from [the first constitutional amendment of 1951](/2019/01/14/first-constitutional-amendment-1951/) through Golak Nath in 1967 to Kesavananda had ended, for the moment, in a proposition whose practical force remained untested.

The political branches had answered Kesavananda within two days in a register that signalled how the doctrine would be contested. On 26 April 1973 the government appointed Justice A. N. Ray as Chief Justice of India, passing over Justices J. M. Shelat, K. S. Hegde and A. N. Grover, the three senior judges who had held against the government's position in Kesavananda, and all three resigned. The supersession announced that the executive retained instruments of its own in the contest over constitutional interpretation, including the power of appointment to the court's leadership. When the election appeal reached the Supreme Court in 1975, the Chief Justice presiding over the Constitution Bench was therefore a judge elevated after a supersession that the bar had protested as punishment for independent opinions, a fact that hung over the November judgment's reception and that makes its first limb, the destruction of the government's amendment, harder to file under any simple theory of judicial subservience. Justice Khanna's presence on the bench carried the opposite resonance: the author of the most analytically influential Kesavananda opinion, the judge who had held that the amending power stops at the Constitution's identity, would sit in judgment on the first amendment accused of crossing his line.

The bench also received the doctrine in a procedural posture that sharpened every difficulty. In Kesavananda the court had reviewed amendments of general application in challenges brought after enactment, with no individual litigant's fate turning on the answer in the same hearing. In the election appeal the constitutional question and the personal question were fused. If clause (4) stood, the appellant kept her seat by force of the amendment itself. If clause (4) fell, the bench still had to decide the election under a statute amended in the appellant's favour while her appeal was pending. Every route through the case therefore exposed the bench to a charge: uphold the clause and the doctrine dies unborn; strike the clause and apply the amended statute and the court is accused of theatre; strike the clause and refuse the statute's retrospectivity and the court is accused of deciding the election on law Parliament had repealed. The two track method the bench adopted was its answer to that trilemma, and the method's elegance should not disguise the narrowness of the path. The bench treated the constituent act and the legislative act as different species of power, held the first to the Kesavananda limit and the second to the ordinary rules of statutory interpretation, and accepted the outcome that the combination produced. Doctrine supplied the limit. Statute supplied the result. November 1975 is the first case in the reports where that division of labour can be watched working end to end.

## Article 329 and the original design for deciding election disputes

The framers of the Constitution had not left the forum for election disputes to chance, and clause (4) cannot be measured without the design it displaced. Article 329, as adopted in 1949, opens with a bar: no election to either House of Parliament or to a state legislature may be called in question except by an election petition presented to such authority and in such manner as may be provided by law made by the appropriate legislature. The Constituent Assembly's choice, working within [the founding settlement's three tensions](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/), was to channel every election challenge into a single statutory remedy and to forbid collateral attack through the ordinary writ jurisdiction while an election process ran its course. The Supreme Court gave the design its classic reading in N. P. Ponnuswami v Returning Officer in 1952, holding that election is a word of wide import covering the whole process from notification to declaration, and that the statutory petition is the exclusive route for questioning a completed election. Parliament then enacted the Representation of the People Acts of 1950 and 1951 to supply the machinery: grounds of challenge, corrupt practices defined in section 123, trial by the High Court and appeal to the Supreme Court.

Two consequences of that design framed the 1975 dispute. The first is that the election petition, though statutory, was never a lesser species of adjudication. A High Court trying a petition ascertains facts on evidence, applies the statute and declares consequences that include the unseating of the returned candidate, functions that are judicial in everything but the source of the jurisdiction. Justice Mathew's November opinion drew directly on this characterisation: a process that decides who truly represents a constituency, by findings on contested evidence, is part of the democratic machinery itself, and its abolition for a protected class is not a mere change of forum. The second consequence is that Parliament's undoubted power to redesign the forum prospectively, by committing petitions to tribunals or by altering grounds and procedure, differs in kind from a power to annul outcomes retrospectively in identified cases. The defence in 1975 leaned heavily on the first power. The bench's answer turned on the second. Clause (4) did not prospectively assign prime ministerial petitions to a new tribunal whose decisions courts would respect. It extinguished a pending trial's result and a pending appeal's subject matter by name and office, and declared the contested election valid by constitutional fiat. Measured against the Article 329 design, in which disputes are decided by adjudication under law, clause (4) replaced adjudication with command for the two offices where command was most dangerous, because the holder of one of them led the very majority that enacted the replacement.

The design argument also explains why the equality objection, pressed by Shanti Bhushan and adopted in Justice Chandrachud's opinion, carried weight beyond formal symmetry. Under the unamended design every member of the Lok Sabha, from the newest backbencher to the Prime Minister, held a seat subject to the same petition, the same grounds and the same court. Clause (4) withdrew that common exposure from the Prime Minister and the Speaker alone, and it did so at the moment when the exposure had bitten. A classification drawn around two offices might have been defended in the abstract as protecting governmental stability. Drawn in August 1975, around a judgment of June 1975, in an appeal listed for that month, the classification read to the bench as what it was: a rule for a case. The opinions that struck clause (4) return repeatedly to this fusion of generality in form and singularity in purpose, and the fusion is the factual predicate of the operationalisation claim. The doctrine's first application arose against an amendment whose defect was visible on its face and in its calendar.

## Clause (4) read closely: the anatomy of a legislative judgment

Clause (4) of Article 329A rewards the slow reading the bench gave it, because its defects accumulate phrase by phrase. The clause addressed the elections of persons who hold or have held the office of Prime Minister or Speaker, and it provided, in substance, that notwithstanding anything in the Constitution, no such election would be called in question on the ground that a corrupt practice had been committed, that the election would not be deemed void or ever to have become void, and that any petition or appeal pending in any court in respect of such an election would abate, with any order made by a court in such a proceeding declared void. Each element performs a distinct institutional act. The opening immunity removes a ground of challenge. The deeming provision rewrites the past, converting an election declared void by a court into an election that in law was never void. The abatement provision terminates live proceedings without adjudication. The annulment of court orders reaches backward into the judicial record and erases it. A legislature performing the first act alone might claim to be reforming election law. A legislature performing all four acts together, in the middle of an appeal it was certain to be affected by, was exercising judgment in the older and stricter sense: finding the facts settled, applying its preferred outcome and ordering the case closed.

The clause's defenders in 1975 had genuine arguments, and the bench's opinions engage them rather than caricature them. Validation statutes had an honourable lineage in Indian practice, curing defects in taxation, land and municipal law by retrospectively supplying the authority whose absence had invalidated official action. Parliament could also point to Article 329 itself, which commits the design of election dispute machinery to legislation, and to comparative practice in which legislatures reserve roles in judging the elections of their own members. The bench's answer discriminated among these arguments instead of rejecting them wholesale. Validation, the opinions reasoned in their different idioms, cures a defect in the law under which action was taken; it does not declare the result of an adjudication that has already occurred under law Parliament left standing at the time. Forum design allocates future disputes; it does not recall a dispute from the court seised of it and decide the dispute in the legislature's chamber. Comparative legislative judging of elections operates, where it operates, as the original design of a constitution rather than as an amendment inserted to reverse a named judgment. The discrimination is the doctrinal payload. After November 1975, a government wishing to protect an election outcome by law knows the boundary the case drew: it may change the law, prospectively or even retrospectively, and take its chances on adjudication under the changed law, as the Election Laws (Amendment) Act did and survived. It may not abolish the adjudication and announce the winner, as clause (4) did and fell.

Clause (5), the clause's companion, completed the mechanism by directing that the pending appeal itself be disposed of in conformity with clause (4), converting the Supreme Court's docket into an instrument of the amendment. The bench struck clause (5) with clause (4), and the pairing matters for the pattern claim. The November judgment did not merely announce that some future amendment of this type would be invalid. It invalidated the mechanism at the point of application, in the very appeal the mechanism was built to terminate, and then proceeded to hear and decide that appeal on the surviving law. Operationalisation, in the strict sense this article gives the word, means exactly that sequence: a doctrine stated in one case is used in a later case to change the legal position of the parties before the court. By that measure the doctrine had been announced in 1973 and discussed in the privilege litigation and elsewhere, but it was operationalised on 7 November 1975 and not before.

## The arguments of August to October 1975

The Constitution Bench that heard the appeals consisted of Chief Justice A. N. Ray and Justices H. R. Khanna, K. K. Mathew, M. H. Beg and Y. V. Chandrachud. Two appeals lay before them. Civil Appeal No. 887 of 1975 was Indira Gandhi's appeal against the Allahabad judgment. Civil Appeal No. 909 of 1975, with Raj Narain's cross objections, carried the attack on the new statutes into the same hearing. The bench heard argument across the autumn of 1975, in a Delhi where the Emergency remained in force, opposition politicians remained in detention and the newspapers that reported the proceedings did so under censorship. The arguments, reconstructed from the judgment's own account of them, divided into the constitutional attack and the statutory defence.

Shanti Bhushan, for Raj Narain, put the constitutional attack at its highest. Clause (4) of Article 329A, he argued, damaged the basic structure that Kesavananda had placed beyond amendment. It destroyed the resolution of election disputes by an independent judiciary, which he presented as an incident of democracy itself, since an election whose validity no court can examine is an election whose fairness rests on the word of the winner's majority. It offended equality, because it lifted the elections of two office holders out of the legal regime governing the elections of all other members. It violated the separation of powers, because Parliament had not legislated a rule but decided a case, annulling a judicial order in a pending appeal and declaring its own side the winner. And it offended the rule of law, because it made the Constitution's amending power an instrument for defeating a judgment in a cause to which the head of the government was a party. The attack deliberately framed clause (4) as a test of whether Kesavananda meant anything in practice. A doctrine that could not reach an amendment of this character, the argument implied, would remain a proposition in a law report rather than a limit on power.

The defence, advanced by the Attorney General and the government's counsel, met the attack on the terrain Kesavananda itself had marked. The amending power, the defence argued, extends to every part of the Constitution, and Kesavananda forbade only damage to the basic structure; restructuring the forum for a narrow class of election disputes, the defence maintained, damaged no basic feature, because Parliament could have committed such disputes to a tribunal of its own design from the beginning under the scheme of Article 329. Validation, the defence continued, is a recognised legislative technique: a legislature may remove the basis of a judgment by changing the underlying law, and courts routinely give effect to the change. The Allahabad findings, on this view, had always rested on constructions of a statute that Parliament was entitled to clarify with retrospective effect, as it had done in the Election Laws (Amendment) Act. As for the election itself, the defence submitted that on the amended law neither the Kapoor assistance nor the rostrum assistance constituted a corrupt practice, and that the appeal should be allowed on that ground alone. Between the attack and the defence stood the question the bench could not escape. If clause (4) fell, the election would be decided by the amended statute. If clause (4) stood, there would be nothing left to decide.

The proceeding also carried an undercurrent that the judgment only partly reveals. The Supreme Court Registry's file for the appeal, covering the months from the Thirty-Ninth Amendment to the November hearing, preserves the paper trail of a case being reconstituted while it was being argued: applications, amended grounds and exchanges over the very jurisdiction of the court to proceed. T. R. Andhyarujina, in The Kesavananda Bharati Case (2011), writing as a participant in the Kesavananda litigation and as a close observer of its sequel, emphasises how completely the August amendments had altered the appeal's character between its filing in June and its hearing in the autumn. The appeal Indira Gandhi filed was an election appeal. The appeal the bench decided was a constitutional case about the limits of amendment, with an election appeal attached. That transformation is the factual core of the operationalisation claim this article defends.

## The judgment of 7 November 1975 stated judge by judge

The bench delivered judgment on 7 November 1975, in five separate opinions, and the operative order had two limbs. Clauses (4) and (5) of Article 329A were declared unconstitutional and void. The Allahabad judgment was set aside and Indira Gandhi's election was upheld, on the footing of the election law as amended. Because the five opinions reasoned separately, the grounds matter judge by judge, and they are stated here from the opinions themselves rather than from any composite paraphrase.

Chief Justice A. N. Ray located the vice of clause (4) in the rule of law and in the clause's character as an exercise of naked power over a pending cause. A provision that declared a court's order void, disposed of a pending appeal by constitutional text and validated an election without adjudication, in his reasoning, subordinated law to the will of a temporary majority and damaged a feature of the Constitution that amendment could not reach. Justice H. R. Khanna, the judge whose Kesavananda opinion had done so much to frame the doctrine, held that democracy is a basic feature of the Constitution and that free and fair elections are an inseparable part of democracy. Clause (4), in Justice Khanna's reasoning, destroyed the mechanism by which the fairness of an election could be tested, because it abolished the forum for deciding the Prime Minister's election dispute while providing no adjudicatory substitute, and a right to challenge an election without a forum is no right at all. Justice K. K. Mathew held that the resolution of election disputes through the judicial process, by ascertaining facts and applying law to them, belongs to the democratic structure of the Constitution, and that clause (4), by withdrawing that process and substituting a legislative pronouncement of validity, damaged the structure Kesavananda protected. A healthy democracy, his opinion reasoned, can function only where the possibility of free and fair elections is secured by law, and the impugned clause destroyed that possibility for the highest executive office in the land.

Justice Y. V. Chandrachud held clause (4) invalid on grounds that included the separation of powers and equality. The clause, in his reasoning, transferred a purely judicial function, the determination of a disputed election on evidence, to the legislature, and thereby damaged the separation that the Constitution maintains between the organs of the state. It also created an unjustifiable privilege for the holders of two offices, whose elections would stand validated by constitutional command while every other legislator's election remained examinable in court, offending the equality principle that runs through the constitutional text. Justice M. H. Beg wrote the opinion most occupied with the statutory questions, upholding the validity of the amended election law and examining the amended provisions in detail; his opinion formed part of the bench's collective disposition under which clause (4) could not save the Allahabad findings from re-examination, and the appeal fell to be decided on the statute as amended. The five opinions differed in route and in emphasis, as the Kesavananda opinions had differed, but the order they produced was single and clear. For the first time since the doctrine was announced in April 1973, a provision of a constitutional amendment lay struck down for damaging the basic structure.

### Why did the bench strike clause (4) yet decide the election itself?

The bench struck clause (4) because it decided a pending case by constitutional command, abolishing the judicial forum for a Prime Minister's election dispute and validating an election without adjudication. The opinions grounded that result in free and fair elections, the rule of law, equality and the separation of powers. The bench tried the election under the amended statute.

The second limb of the order applied the amended Representation of the People Act to the Allahabad findings, and on the amended Act the findings could not stand. On the Kapoor charge, the judgment reasoned through the amended definition and the added explanation together. Indira Gandhi filed her nomination paper on 1 February 1971, and under the amended definition of candidate she became a candidate only on that date, so assistance obtained in January 1971 was not assistance obtained by a candidate within section 123(7). The added explanation independently resolved the resignation date: the Gazette notification of Kapoor's resignation stated its effect from 14 January 1971, and the explanation made that published date conclusive, so Kapoor had ceased to be in government service from 14 January for the purposes of the clause. On the rostrum charge, the amended Act's protection for assistance rendered by a government officer in the discharge or purported discharge of official duty removed the district officers' arrangements from the category of corrupt practice. The bench also upheld the constitutional validity of the Representation of the People (Amendment) Act 1974 and the Election Laws (Amendment) Act 1975 against the challenges pressed in the cross appeal, treating retrospective clarification of election law as an exercise of ordinary legislative power that a court gives effect to in pending proceedings. Raj Narain's cross appeal, which had sought findings on charges Justice Sinha had rejected, failed as well. The election stood.

## What the verdict operationalised: from declared doctrine to working instrument

Kesavananda Bharati, decided on 24 April 1973 by a bench of thirteen, had announced a limit and then declined, in the case before it, to apply that limit to strike down the amendments under challenge. The doctrine therefore existed for two and a half years as a proposition awaiting an occasion. The occasion arrived in a form nobody drafting the Kesavananda opinions had foreseen: not a property amendment or a rights amendment, but an amendment whose entire purpose was to dispose of one litigant's pending appeal. The November 1975 bench made the doctrine work. It identified features of the basic structure with a precision the Kesavananda opinions, scattered across eleven judgments, had not achieved. Free and fair elections entered the canon of basic features in this case, named as such in the reasoning of the bench and carried into every later enumeration. Judicial review of election disputes, the rule of law and the separation of powers were each tied, in a named opinion, to the invalidity of a named clause. The work of the pillar hub on [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/) supplies the standard by which such moments are ranked, and the November verdict meets it on the evidence of its text: a doctrine that had been declared became, on 7 November 1975, a doctrine that decided.

The operationalisation also fixed a method. Later benches inherited from this case a two track discipline that judges have followed whenever amendment and statute arrive together. The amendment is tested against the basic structure, feature by feature, on the reasoning of Kesavananda as this case applied it. The statute is tested against the Constitution as it survives that inquiry, and conduct is judged under the statute as validly amended, retrospectively where Parliament has so provided. Minerva Mills in 1980, Waman Rao in 1981, I. R. Coelho in 2007 and K. Puttaswamy in 2017 all move along the track laid down in November 1975, even where their benches enlarged the doctrine's content. The method also connects the case to [the constitutional rights framework](/2018/12/06/ambedkar-drafting-committee-constitution/), because the equality reasoning in Justice Chandrachud's opinion treated Article 14 not as an isolated guarantee but as part of the structural commitments an amendment must respect. Sudhir Krishnaswamy, in Democracy and Constitutionalism in India (2009), reads the post Kesavananda case law as a continuing negotiation over the content of the basic structure, and the November 1975 opinions are the first entries in that negotiation: the moment the abstract noun acquired a docket number, a losing provision and a surviving election.

A distinction the verdict established deserves separate emphasis, because later controversy has often blurred it. Striking clause (4) did not rest on any finding that the election of 1971 was itself unfair, and upholding the election did not rest on any deference to the Emergency. The first limb policed the boundary between constituent power and adjudication. The second limb applied a statute that Parliament, acting within its ordinary legislative competence as the bench found it, had amended. A reader who collapses the limbs into a single gesture of rescue or resistance loses the case's teaching, which is that a constitutional court can refuse a legislature the power to decide a case while accepting the legislature's power to change the law the case is decided under. That separation, austere and even uncomfortable, is the operational content of the basic structure doctrine in its first application.

## Free and fair elections enter the basic structure

The most quoted doctrinal deposit of the November judgment is a phrase and the reasoning inside it. Before 1975, enumerations of the basic structure in the Kesavananda opinions had spoken of democracy, of the republican form, of the rule of law and of judicial review, but the conduct of elections had figured as an application of democracy rather than as a named structural feature with its own content. The November opinions changed the register. Justice Khanna's opinion reasoned from democracy to its necessary conditions: a democratic polity in which the choice of representatives cannot be tested for fairness by an independent forum has lost an essential part of what makes it democratic, and an amendment that withdraws the test withdraws the condition. Justice Mathew's opinion supplied the institutional corollary, treating the adjudication of election disputes as one of the processes by which a democracy continuously verifies its own legitimacy. Between them, the opinions installed free and fair elections in the structure not as a slogan but as a working standard against which a constitutional amendment had been measured and found wanting on a dated record.

The installation had practical consequences that unfolded across the following decades, and tracing them disciplines the pattern claim against exaggeration. The standard did not become a general licence for courts to supervise election administration; the Article 329 bar on mid process interference survived intact, and the Election Commission's autonomy under Article 324 developed on its own line of authority. What the 1975 installation supplied was a constitutional floor beneath the electoral process: legislation and amendment alike must leave standing a genuine possibility that an election's legality can be questioned before an adjudicator and decided on evidence. Later benches drawing on the basic structure in election adjacent cases, from the secrecy of the ballot to the disclosure of candidates' antecedents, wrote in a doctrinal environment in which the fairness of elections already carried structural rank because a bench in 1975 had put it there. The point can be put as a counterfactual. Had clause (4) survived, the first application of Kesavananda would have taught that an amendment may extinguish the judicial enforcement of electoral legality for the highest offices, and every later invocation of free and fair elections as basic structure would have had to climb over that precedent. The doctrine's content in 2020, when this article's locked date falls, descends from the answer given in 1975 as surely as its existence descends from the answer given in 1973.

There is a further, quieter deposit. The November opinions treated the equality of candidates and voters before election law as part of the same structural fabric, most explicitly in Justice Chandrachud's reasoning on the privilege clause (4) conferred. Election law in India had always assumed a formal equality among candidates, that the Prime Minister and the first time independent file nominations under the same Act and answer petitions in the same court. Clause (4) breached that assumption for two offices, and the bench's refusal to accept the breach constitutionalised the assumption. When later courts speak of a level field in electoral competition as a democratic value with legal force, they speak downstream of November 1975. The deposit matters to the operationalisation reading because it shows the first application doing generative work, adding content to the doctrine while applying it. A doctrine whose first use merely blocked a provision might have remained a negative veto, available for emergencies of constitutional design and silent in ordinary times. The November opinions instead supplied affirmative content, a named feature with a stated rationale, that later courts could reason from in cases the 1975 bench never imagined. That generativity is the quality that separates an operational doctrine from a rhetorical one, and it is measurable in the later reports, where free and fair elections performs analytical work in disputes over electoral rolls, candidate disclosure and the powers of the Election Commission, each time carrying the authority of its first enforcement behind it. A merely defensive judgment, one that struck the clause on the narrowest available ground and said nothing general, could not have seeded the later case law in this way. The 1975 bench, for all the constraints of its moment, wrote opinions meant to govern future amendments, and future amendments have in fact been governed by them.

## Triumph and accommodation: the scholarly argument adjudicated

No reading of the November 1975 verdict has commanded the field, and the disagreement among scholars is itself part of the case's record. The strongest form of the triumph reading runs as follows. A bench sitting during an Emergency, with the press censored and the opposition imprisoned, nevertheless told a Prime Minister commanding an overwhelming parliamentary majority that her constitutional amendment was void. The bench could have hidden behind clause (4) itself, treating the amendment as self executing and the appeal as abated. It instead asserted the power Kesavananda had declared and used it against the very provision designed to defeat the court's jurisdiction. Granville Austin's Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), the standard scholarly history of the Constitution's working during these decades, presents the verdict within its account of the Emergency period as evidence that the basic structure doctrine had acquired real restraining force, surviving its first contact with a determined government. On this reading the striking down of clause (4) is the durable event and the election's survival a footnote about a repealed statute.

The strongest form of the accommodation reading answers that the bench gave with one hand and returned with the other. The verdict's practical outcome left Indira Gandhi in office, her election validated, her disqualification erased and the Emergency undisturbed, at the very hour when the political system most needed a counterweight. Critics writing close to the events, among them Rajeev Dhavan in his 1977 study of the Supreme Court under strain, pressed the institutional question of what the court's assertions of power were worth when their immediate beneficiary was the government that had proclaimed the Emergency. The accommodation reading notes, further, that the amended election law the bench applied had been tailored retrospectively to the findings against one candidate, so that accepting the amendments as ordinary legislation drained the striking down of clause (4) of most of its consequence for the litigants. On this reading the verdict taught future governments a usable lesson: lose the constitutional clause, keep the statute, keep the office.

The adjudication this article offers weighs the two readings against the text of the judgment and the later record. The accommodation reading is correct about the immediate distribution of advantage, and any account that hides that fact fails the evidence. The triumph reading is correct about the doctrinal event, and any account that treats clause (4)'s fall as decoration fails the later case law, in which bench after bench cited November 1975 as the authority that amendments can fall. The framings fail only when either claims the whole case. The verdict was an accommodation in its second limb and a genuine assertion of constitutional power in its first, and the two limbs were connected by legal reasoning rather than by bargain, so far as the record shows: the same distinction between constituent power and legislative power that destroyed clause (4) required the bench to apply the amended statute it had upheld. The complication the brief for this case insists upon must also be faced squarely. The verdict's content was tailored to an immediate political context in a way that later doctrine was not; clause (4) itself existed only because of one woman's appeal. The pattern claim survives that complication because the pattern is visible in the sequel. Governments after 1977 did not again attempt to validate a named election by constitutional amendment, and benches after 1975 applied the two track method to amendments of general application, from the Forty-Second Amendment's clauses in Minerva Mills to the Ninth Schedule in I. R. Coelho. A pattern that outlives its originating context has earned its name.

The scholarly debate also has a quieter third strand, represented by T. R. Andhyarujina's The Kesavananda Bharati Case (2011), which treats November 1975 as the proof that Kesavananda's compromise was workable: the doctrine could invalidate without paralysing, because the same judgment that struck the amendment decided the underlying dispute under law. Read together, the three strands mark the points a serious account must hold simultaneously. The doctrine worked. The government survived. The method endured. The sections that follow trace the method through its named applications.

## The pattern after 1975: repeal, Minerva Mills, Waman Rao, Coelho and Puttaswamy

The first sequel came from the electorate and the Parliament it returned. The Emergency ended in March 1977, the Janata Party won the general election of that month, and Raj Narain defeated Indira Gandhi at Rae Bareli in the contest the courts had spent six years anticipating, a reversal recorded in [the 1977 Janata verdict explained](/2019/04/28/1977-janata-verdict-explained/). The Constitution (Forty-Fourth Amendment) Act 1978 then omitted Article 329A from the Constitution altogether, clearing away the surviving clauses of a provision whose heart the Supreme Court had already destroyed. The repeal matters to the pattern claim for a simple reason. Parliament, restored to plural occupation after 1977, did not seek to revive the device of clause (4) even for other purposes. The device had been marked, by judgment and then by repeal, as outside the repertoire of permissible amendment.

The second sequel came from the bench itself. In Minerva Mills v Union of India, decided on 31 July 1980, a Constitution Bench struck down clauses (4) and (5) of Article 368 as inserted by the Forty-Second Amendment, which had sought to bar judicial review of amendments and to declare the amending power unlimited. The Minerva Mills bench reasoned exactly as the November 1975 bench had reasoned: it measured an amendment against named basic features, judicial review among them, and destroyed the offending clauses while leaving the remainder of the constitutional text to operate. In Waman Rao v Union of India, decided on 13 May 1981, the Court sustained the architecture of Article 31B and the Ninth Schedule for laws inserted before the Kesavananda judgment of 24 April 1973, while confirming that laws inserted afterward remained open to basic structure challenge, a line drawn directly from the premise November 1975 had made practical, that amendments and amendment like instruments are examinable against the structure. A nine judge bench carried the logic to its conclusion in I. R. Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu on 11 January 2007, holding that Ninth Schedule laws placed there after April 1973 must answer to the basic structure, tested through the fundamental rights that form part of it. And in K. Puttaswamy v Union of India on 24 August 2017, another nine judge bench grounded privacy in the Constitution's structure of dignity and liberty, in a judgment whose treatment of precedent included the express overruling of the Emergency era habeas corpus decision. These later cases are named here as the trajectory the November verdict opened; their full arguments belong to their own place in the series, later than the date of this article, and are flagged as forward references rather than links.

The contrast case sharpens the pattern's outline. Five months after the November verdict, on 28 April 1976, a Constitution Bench decided ADM Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla and held, by four to one, that during an Emergency no person could move a court for habeas corpus against illegal detention, with Justice Khanna alone dissenting. The failure of that bench to bring constitutional structure to bear on executive action is examined in [the institutional-architectural-failure-pattern](/2020/08/08/adm-jabalpur-1976-habeas-corpus-case/). Placed side by side, November 1975 and April 1976 show the same court, in the same Emergency, wielding the basic structure doctrine against an amendment and declining to wield constitutional principle against detention. The pattern claim of this article is a claim about doctrine and method, not a claim about the court's uniform courage, and the ADM Jabalpur contrast is the standing reminder of that limit. What November 1975 established, ADM Jabalpur did not disturb: no later bench has questioned that a constitutional amendment clause can fall for damaging the basic structure, and the Forty-Fourth Amendment's repeal of Article 329A assumed the point.

### How did the November 1975 method travel into later cases?

The method travelled as a two track discipline. In Minerva Mills in 1980 the bench tested the Forty-Second Amendment's clauses against named basic features and struck them, as the 1975 bench had tested clause (4). Waman Rao in 1981, I. R. Coelho in 2007 and K. Puttaswamy in 2017 each repeated that measurement.

## Raj Narain: the petitioner who would not leave the courtroom

The case bears two names, and the second is too often treated as a cipher. Raj Narain was a socialist organiser from Uttar Pradesh whose political life ran through the movements and parties that contested Congress dominance from outside the ruling organisation: the socialist tradition of Ram Manohar Lohia, the Samyukta Socialist Party and the currents that later fed into the Janata coalition. He had sat in the Lok Sabha from Varanasi in the 1950s and had contested Rae Bareli in 1971 as the opposition's standard bearer against the Prime Minister on her own ground. His stated politics, expressed across decades of agitation, held that concentrated executive power was the central danger to Indian democracy, and his litigation was that politics continued by legal means. The petition he filed in April 1971 was not a lawyer's afterthought to a lost election. It was a deliberate wager that the republic's courts would apply the republic's election law to the republic's most powerful office holder, and he sustained the wager through four years of interlocutory warfare, a privilege battle that reached the Supreme Court under his name in January 1975, and a trial at which he sat through the Prime Minister's own testimony.

Raj Narain's conduct after the Allahabad judgment completes the symmetry that makes the case a study in institutional patience rewarded and then overtaken. He won his judgment on 12 June 1975 and watched its enforcement dissolve, first into a conditional stay, then into an Emergency that imprisoned his political allies, then into amendments that rewrote the law he had won under. His cross appeal in the Supreme Court failed in November 1975 along with his petition. Yet the same Raj Narain contested Rae Bareli again in March 1977, in a free election held after the Emergency's end, and defeated Indira Gandhi at the ballot box by a margin the constituency's voters delivered without judicial assistance, becoming Minister for Health and Family Welfare in the Janata government that followed. The sequence gives the operationalisation reading a human footnote. The courtroom route Raj Narain chose in 1971 produced, in the end, the doctrine rather than the seat. The electoral route he chose in 1977 produced the seat. The Constitution's design, which Article 329 expresses, always contemplated both routes running in parallel, petitions policing the legality of elections and elections policing the politics of governments. Clause (4) tried to close the first route for the highest office. Its destruction kept both routes open, which is why a defeated petitioner who never won his case still stands in the case's name.

## The five judges after November 1975

The bench that decided the case dispersed into the Emergency's remaining months and into the republic's memory along lines that later commentary has read back into the judgment, sometimes unfairly. Chief Justice A. N. Ray retired in January 1977, weeks before the Emergency ended, his tenure permanently associated with the supersession that began it and with the ADM Jabalpur majority he would lead in April 1976. Justice M. H. Beg succeeded him as Chief Justice, the second supersession of the decade, passing over Justice Khanna after the ADM Jabalpur dissent. Justice Khanna resigned on the day of that supersession in January 1977 rather than serve under it, and his resignation, with the dissent that provoked the punishment, fixed his place in the profession's regard as the judge who had twice, in Kesavananda's lineage and in the habeas corpus case, stated the limit that power least wished to hear. In the November 1975 case his contribution stands between those poles: the free and fair elections reasoning that destroyed clause (4) is the work of the same judge, applying in an election case the structural method he had announced in a property case. Justice K. K. Mathew retired in 1976 and later chaired commissions of inquiry and the Press Council, carrying into public life the democratic theory his opinion had stated. Justice Y. V. Chandrachud became Chief Justice of India in February 1978 and held the office until 1985, the longest tenure of the republic to that date, during which the court he led decided Minerva Mills and consolidated the doctrine his 1975 opinion had helped to operationalise, a continuity that links the November judgment to the post Emergency court's reconstruction more directly than any commentary can.

These afterlives matter to the case's assessment in one disciplined way. They caution against reading the November judgment backward from ADM Jabalpur, as though the bench of April 1976 simply revealed what the bench of November 1975 had concealed. The two benches shared four judges, and their products differ categorically: one destroyed a constitutional amendment on structural grounds, the other declined to protect personal liberty against executive detention. Indian constitutional history holds both facts without dissolving either, and the honest verdict on the institution across those five months is unevenness under pressure, not uniform capitulation or uniform heroism. The operationalisation claim is deliberately pitched at the level where the November judgment is strong: the level of doctrine, method and citation. At that level the judgment's authority has never since been displaced, and the judges who wrote it, whatever their other records, produced the text on which that authority rests.

## How the case entered the doctrine's working canon

A judgment's operational status is proved in the citations of its successors, and the November 1975 verdict accumulated those citations across every decade that followed. Minerva Mills cited it for the reviewability of amendments. The nine judge bench in S. R. Bommai v Union of India in 1994, reconstructing the basic features for the federal and secular questions before it, worked within a canon of features to which the 1975 case had contributed free and fair elections and the rule of law. Election jurisprudence absorbed the case's premise that the fairness of elections is a structural commitment rather than a statutory grace, a premise visible whenever courts describe the electoral process as part of the Constitution's democratic identity, including in the jurisprudence surrounding the Election Commission's independence under Article 324. Textbook enumerations of the basic structure, from the reports of the National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution in 2002 onward, reproduce lists in which the features named or confirmed in November 1975 appear as settled entries. The case also entered the classroom and the examination hall as the standard first illustration of the doctrine applied, the answer to the question students are always asked and governments occasionally forget: has any amendment ever actually been struck down on this ground. One amendment clause had been, in 1975, in a case about the Prime Minister's own election, and the illustration's force comes precisely from that conjunction of doctrine and stakes.

The documentary record supporting this afterlife is unusually complete, and its completeness is part of the case's teaching value. The Allahabad judgment survives as a full trial record with findings issue by issue. The amendment survives in the Gazette with its clauses numbered for destruction. The five November opinions survive as separate reasoned texts, cited as (1975) Supp SCR 1 and reported at AIR 1975 SC 2299, each available for the judge by judge reading this article has practised. Austin's history, Andhyarujina's participant account and Krishnaswamy's doctrinal study supply three independent scholarly frames, written in 1999, 2011 and 2009, through which the same texts can be re-read against the Emergency frame, the personality frame and the operationalisation frame. Few Indian cases offer a student so closed a circuit between event, text, judgment and commentary, and the circuit is a further reason the operationalisation reading can be tested rather than merely asserted: every proposition it advances can be carried back to a dated document and checked.

## The Kesavananda review that dissolved in the same month

Three days after the election judgment, the doctrine it had operationalised faced an attempt at removal that failed in open court, and the episode belongs in this case's record because it fixed the judgment's authority at the moment of its birth. On 10 November 1975 a bench of thirteen judges, constituted by Chief Justice Ray, assembled to hear a review of Kesavananda Bharati, on applications moved in pending land reform litigation from Tamil Nadu. No party had filed a review petition against Kesavananda in the ordinary course, and the constitution of the bench on the Chief Justice's initiative, days after a Constitution Bench had applied the doctrine against the government, drew the bar's immediate scrutiny. Nani Palkhivala, appearing in the review proceedings, argued for two days that the bench had been improperly constituted to rehear a decided question of that magnitude without a proper reference, and that the enterprise amounted to an attempt to undo by roster what could not be undone by argument. On 12 November 1975 the bench was dissolved, and the review was abandoned. T. R. Andhyarujina's participant account in The Kesavananda Bharati Case (2011) treats the dissolution as the moment the doctrine's survival ceased to be in question, because the attempt to reverse it had collapsed on procedural grounds the whole profession could see. For the election case the consequence was direct: the authority on which clauses (4) and (5) had fallen stood unreversed and unreviewed, and the November judgment entered the law reports as the doctrine's first and governing application rather than as an interim skirmish in an unfinished war.

## The judgment in the country between November 1975 and March 1977

The judgment landed in a public sphere that could not freely discuss it. Newspapers reported the operative order under censorship, opposition leaders who might have parsed its first limb in Parliament sat in detention, and the government's own publicity emphasised the second limb, the Prime Minister's exoneration, over the first limb's destruction of her amendment. The asymmetry of reception is itself evidence for the framings debate. Contemporaries living through the Emergency experienced the judgment primarily as an event in the struggle over Indira Gandhi's continuation in office, and by that measure the government had won the day. The legal profession read the same document differently. In the bar's journals and in the teaching that continued through the Emergency years, the striking down of clause (4) circulated as the datum that Kesavananda had teeth, and the case took its place in the small set of authorities every constitutional practitioner was expected to command. The two receptions coexisted because the judgment genuinely contained both contents, an outcome the government could use and a doctrine it could not escape.

The months that followed tested the judgment's practical weight and found its limits with precision. The government did not re-enact clause (4) in altered dress, nor did it appeal the doctrine by any of the routes a determined executive might have attempted; the Forty-Second Amendment of 1976, which rewrote large parts of the Constitution, attacked judicial review on other fronts rather than reviving the annulled device. At the same time the Emergency's machinery proceeded undisturbed by anything in the November judgment, detentions continued under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act, and the habeas corpus litigation that became ADM Jabalpur moved toward its April 1976 conclusion in the same court. The judgment had policed the boundary of constituent power. It had not policed, and its text did not purport to police, the executive's use of emergency power against liberty. Readers who ask why November 1975 did not prevent April 1976 are asking the judgment to have been a different document, addressed to a different case. The election appeal gave the bench an amendment to test. The detention appeals gave it a proclamation and a suspension order to test. It rose to the first test and failed the second, and the series' treatment of each case keeps the verdicts separate for that reason.

The reckoning arrived through the ballot. Indira Gandhi announced elections in January 1977, the Emergency was lifted in March, and the electorate delivered the verdict that the legal process had circled for six years: the Congress lost, the Janata Party formed the government, and Raj Narain took Rae Bareli. The Shah Commission, appointed in May 1977 under Justice J. C. Shah to inquire into Emergency excesses, assembled the documentary record of the period, including the circumstances in which the proclamation had been advised and the amendments of 1975 carried through a depleted Parliament. The Commission's record confirmed, from the government's own files, much of what the operationalisation reading treats as context: the haste, the detentions and the tailoring of law to the executive's peril. It could not and did not revise the November judgment's legal content, which by then belonged to the reports. The Forty-Fourth Amendment's repeal of Article 329A in 1978 closed the episode's textual history. The election case had begun as a petition about rostrums and a secretary's resignation date. It ended as the first working demonstration that the Constitution's basic structure is a limit courts will enforce, demonstrated in the hardest conditions the republic has offered a court, against the strongest government the republic had then produced.

## The cross appeal and the charges that failed twice

Raj Narain's cross appeal asked the Supreme Court to go further than Justice Sinha had gone, and its failure completes the picture of what the November judgment decided. The Allahabad judgment had rejected the charges concerning the use of Air Force aircraft and helicopters, the alleged excess of election expenditure, the alleged distribution of blankets and liquor, and the alleged appeals in the name of rival symbols. Raj Narain's cross objections pressed those charges again in the Supreme Court, seeking findings of corrupt practice that would have voided the election independently of the two findings the High Court had recorded. The bench declined to convert the rejected charges into findings. On the amended law and on the evidence as the trial court had assessed it, the cross appeal failed, and the failure matters for the judgment's balance. The same bench that destroyed the government's constitutional clause also refused the petitioner's invitation to widen the case against the Prime Minister beyond the record a trial judge had made. Neither side left the courtroom with the whole of its case, and the symmetry of the refusals is part of the evidence that the bench was applying discriminations of law rather than distributing favours.

The expenditure strand of the cross appeal carried a statutory history of its own that the judgment absorbed. In Kanwar Lal Gupta v Amar Nath Chawla, decided on 3 October 1974, the Supreme Court had held that expenditure incurred by a political party in connection with a candidate's election could count toward the candidate's statutory ceiling, a reading that exposed many candidates, including the Prime Minister, to petitions founded on party spending. Parliament answered with the Representation of the People (Amendment) Act 1974, which inserted an explanation to section 77 providing that expenditure by a party or by supporters, not authorised by the candidate, would not be deemed expenditure by the candidate. Raj Narain's challenge to that amendment formed part of the November hearing, and the bench upheld the 1974 amendment as a valid exercise of legislative power to define the statutory concept of election expenditure. The episode is a miniature of the case's larger pattern. Twice in the litigation Parliament changed election law in ways that benefited the appellant's position, once in 1974 before the Allahabad judgment and once in August 1975 after it, and twice the Supreme Court sustained the change as legislation. What the bench would not sustain, in 1975, was the third and different act: an amendment that reached past legislation into adjudication and closed the case by command. A reader who grasps why the 1974 amendment survived and clause (4) fell has grasped the judgment's whole legal logic, and has the sharpest available answer to the accommodation reading, since the distinction the bench enforced was stated in law reports before the Emergency, in Kesavananda's separation between amending the Constitution and exercising the powers the Constitution distributes.

The charges that failed twice also discipline the historical memory of the Allahabad verdict itself. Posterity, assisted by the Emergency that followed, has sometimes inflated the Allahabad findings into a general verdict on the Prime Minister's conduct of the 1971 campaign. The record does not support the inflation. Two statutory findings, each turning on the construction of section 123(7), each dependent on dates and on the duties of officers, constituted the whole of what was proved, and the balance of a large and aggressively pleaded petition was dismissed at trial and dismissed again on cross appeal. The operationalisation reading of November 1975 does not need the Allahabad case to have been larger than it was. The doctrine's first application arose from a small case correctly decided under the law of its day, in a court that kept its findings within its evidence, and the smallness of the originating case against the largeness of the doctrine it released is itself a recurring feature of constitutional development that this series has met at every pillar.

## Three framings weighed: why the operationalisation reading carries the case

The brief for this case names three framings and asks for adjudication among them. The Emergency context framing reads the verdict as an event inside the Emergency: a judgment delivered by a censored country's court, four and a half months into authoritarian rule, whose meaning lies in its timing and its immediate political effects. That framing captures the atmosphere of the hearing, the detention of the petitioner's political allies and the government's interest in the outcome, and it explains why contemporaries received the judgment as a political act whatever its legal content. Its weakness is that it cannot explain the judgment's afterlife. Courts cite texts, not atmospheres, and the text of November 1975 has been cited for four decades by benches deciding questions far removed from the Emergency, which suggests that the verdict's centre of gravity lies in its reasoning rather than in its moment.

The political personality framing reads the verdict as a chapter in Indira Gandhi's biography: the Prime Minister judged, stayed, rescued by amendment and finally confirmed in office by the court that refused her amendment. That framing captures the litigation's origin in one candidate's campaign and the amendment's tailoring to one appellant's peril, and it connects the case to [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) only by analogy, since the tensions of the founding settlement ran through this case in personal form. Its weakness is symmetrical to the first framing's weakness. A biographical reading cannot explain why the judgment's propositions about free and fair elections, judicial review and the separation of powers bind litigants who never heard Indira Gandhi's name in a courtroom, nor why the Forty-Fourth Amendment's framers treated clause (4)'s device as permanently discredited.

The operationalisation reading defended here absorbs what the rival framings establish and then goes further. It concedes the Emergency context and the personal tailoring, and it insists that the verdict's historically operative content is the working application of the basic structure doctrine to a constitutional amendment for the first time, with free and fair elections entering the protected structure and a two track method established for every later case. The reading is falsifiable in a way the others are not: it predicts that later benches would cite the case for propositions, that later Parliaments would avoid its condemned device, and that later doctrine would enlarge the list of features the case began. The record from Minerva Mills to Puttaswamy confirms each prediction. The regional and institutional settings of the case point the same way. Rae Bareli sits inside the electoral laboratory of [Uttar Pradesh as bellwether and laboratory](/2020/01/06/uttar-pradesh-bellwether-laboratory/), and the dispute's journey from a returning officer's declaration to a Constitution Bench illustrates the scrutiny architecture that the Election Commission's own history, traced in the pillar on elections, presupposes. For the student of the doctrine, the case remains the hinge. Kesavananda declared that the Constitution has a basic structure no amendment may damage. Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain was the first occasion on which that declaration decided what the law is, striking a clause from the Constitution while deciding, under the law that remained, that the Prime Minister kept her seat. Both halves are the verdict. Neither half is intelligible without the other.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain case?

Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain is the Supreme Court of India's judgment of 7 November 1975, reported at AIR 1975 SC 2299, in the election dispute between Prime Minister Indira Gandhi and her defeated Rae Bareli rival Raj Narain. A five judge Constitution Bench struck down clauses (4) and (5) of Article 329A, inserted by the Thirty-Ninth Amendment, as damaging the basic structure of the Constitution, in the first application of the Kesavananda Bharati doctrine to destroy an amendment provision. The same bench applied the Representation of the People Act as amended in 1975, set aside the Allahabad High Court judgment that had voided her election, and upheld her 1971 election.

### Q: What did the Allahabad High Court say in 1975?

The Allahabad High Court, through Justice Jagmohanlal Sinha, delivered judgment on 12 June 1975 in Election Petition No. 5 of 1971. The court held two corrupt practices proved under section 123(7) of the Representation of the People Act: Indira Gandhi had obtained the assistance of Yashpal Kapoor while Kapoor remained a gazetted officer in government service, and district officers at Rae Bareli had built rostrums and arranged loudspeakers for her election meetings. The court rejected the remaining charges, including those concerning aircraft, expenditure and distribution of goods. It declared her Rae Bareli election void and disqualified her for six years, while staying the judgment's operation for twenty days.

### Q: What is the 39th Amendment?

The Constitution (Thirty-Ninth Amendment) Act 1975, which received presidential assent on 10 August 1975 during the Emergency, inserted Article 329A into the Constitution. The amendment placed the elections of the Prime Minister and the Speaker of the Lok Sabha outside the ordinary election petition process, assigning them to a forum Parliament might create by law. Its clause (4) declared that such an election could not be deemed void on any ground, annulled court orders declaring such elections void, and caused pending petitions and appeals to abate. The Supreme Court struck clauses (4) and (5) down on 7 November 1975, and the Forty-Fourth Amendment omitted Article 329A in 1978.

### Q: What is Article 329A?

Article 329A was a provision inserted into the Constitution by the Thirty-Ninth Amendment in August 1975 to govern the elections of persons holding the office of Prime Minister or Speaker of the Lok Sabha. Its opening clauses withdrew such elections from the election law that applied to other members of Parliament and reserved them to an authority or forum that Parliament might establish by law. Clauses (4) and (5) went further, declaring such elections immune from being voided, voiding court orders to the contrary, and disposing of pending election petitions and appeals by constitutional command. The Supreme Court declared clauses (4) and (5) unconstitutional in November 1975, and the article was omitted by the Forty-Fourth Amendment in 1978.

### Q: Why was Section 4 struck down?

Section 4 of the Thirty-Ninth Amendment, which inserted Article 329A, was struck down in its operative clauses because the Supreme Court held that clauses (4) and (5) damaged the basic structure of the Constitution. The five opinions of 7 November 1975 grounded the result in named features: free and fair elections as an inseparable part of democracy in Justice Khanna's reasoning, the resolution of election disputes through judicial process in Justice Mathew's, the separation of powers and equality in Justice Chandrachud's, and the rule of law in Chief Justice Ray's. A clause that abolished the forum for testing the Prime Minister's election and declared the result by legislative command crossed the limit Kesavananda Bharati had set on the amending power.

### Q: Was Indira Gandhi's election upheld?

Yes. The Supreme Court's order of 7 November 1975 set aside the Allahabad High Court judgment of 12 June 1975 and upheld Indira Gandhi's election from Rae Bareli in the 1971 general election. The bench reached that result by applying the Representation of the People Act as Parliament had amended it through the Representation of the People (Amendment) Act 1974 and the Election Laws (Amendment) Act 1975, whose validity the bench sustained. Under the amended definition of candidate, the amended explanation fixing Yashpal Kapoor's resignation date by its Gazette notification, and the protection for assistance rendered in official duty, the two corrupt practices found at Allahabad could not be sustained in law.

### Q: When was the first basic-structure case?

The first case in which the Supreme Court actually struck down a provision of a constitutional amendment for damaging the basic structure was Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain, decided on 7 November 1975. The doctrine itself had been announced earlier, in Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala on 24 April 1973, where a thirteen judge bench held that Parliament's amending power under Article 368 cannot damage or destroy the Constitution's basic structure, although the amendments challenged in that case survived. The 1975 case is therefore described as the doctrine's first operational application: the first occasion on which the declared limit was enforced against a named clause, Article 329A clauses (4) and (5).

### Q: What did Article 329A clause (4) do to pending election appeals?

Clause (4) of Article 329A terminated pending election proceedings by constitutional command rather than by adjudication. It provided that an election of a Prime Minister or Speaker covered by the article would not be deemed void or ever to have become void on any ground, that orders of courts declaring such an election void would themselves be void, and that petitions and appeals pending before any court in respect of such an election would abate. Applied to the litigation of 1975, the clause would have ended Indira Gandhi's own Supreme Court appeal and Raj Narain's cross appeal without a hearing on the merits, converting the Allahabad judgment into a nullity and the contested election into a validated fact by legislative declaration.

### Q: What grounds did Justice Khanna give for striking down clause (4)?

Justice H. R. Khanna held that democracy is a basic feature of the Constitution and that free and fair elections form an inseparable part of democracy. Clause (4) of Article 329A, in his reasoning, destroyed the possibility of testing the Prime Minister's election, because it abolished the adjudicatory forum for such an election dispute while providing no substitute mechanism through which the fairness of the election could be examined. A right to challenge an election that no forum exists to enforce is, on this reasoning, no right at all. His opinion in the case continued the structural method of his Kesavananda Bharati opinion of 1973, in which he had held that the amending power stops at the identity of the Constitution.

### Q: What grounds did Justice Chandrachud give for striking down clause (4)?

Justice Y. V. Chandrachud held clause (4) of Article 329A invalid on grounds that included the separation of powers and equality. On separation, his opinion reasoned that the determination of a disputed election, by ascertaining facts on evidence and applying law to them, is a judicial function, and that the clause transferred that function to the legislature by declaring a pending election valid by constitutional text. On equality, the clause privileged the holders of two offices, the Prime Minister and the Speaker, by immunising their elections from the legal scrutiny that governed the elections of every other member of Parliament, without a justification the Constitution could accept. His reasoning also treated the rule of law as damaged when a litigant's majority could annul the court's order in its own cause.

### Q: Why did the amended definition of candidate matter to the Kapoor charge?

The amended definition mattered because the Kapoor charge depended on dates. Justice Sinha had found that Indira Gandhi obtained Yashpal Kapoor's assistance during January 1971, before Kapoor's resignation was notified on 25 January 1971, at a time when the unamended Act could treat her as a candidate held out to the constituency. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act 1975 redefined a candidate as a person who has been nominated, with retrospective effect. Indira Gandhi filed her nomination on 1 February 1971, so under the amended definition she became a candidate only on that date, and assistance obtained in January could not be assistance obtained by a candidate within section 123(7). The Supreme Court applied the amended definition, and the charge failed on that ground as well as on the Gazette explanation.

### Q: Why did Justice Sinha grant a twenty-day stay of his own judgment?

Justice Sinha granted the stay on the application of Indira Gandhi's counsel immediately after delivering judgment on 12 June 1975, and the recorded purpose was practical and constitutional at once. An order voiding a Prime Minister's election and disqualifying her for six years created an immediate vacancy at the head of the government, and the governing party required time either to choose a new leader or to test the judgment in appeal. The unconditional stay of twenty days kept the judgment's consequences suspended while preserving the judgment itself, so that the Supreme Court could decide the appeal with the political situation stabilised. The device was a standard judicial instrument for judgments with large public consequences, and its twenty day term set the calendar within which the appeal, the conditional stay of 24 June and the Emergency proclamation all followed.

### Q: What happened to Article 329A under the Forty-Fourth Amendment?

The Constitution (Forty-Fourth Amendment) Act 1978, enacted by the Janata Party government after the Emergency, omitted Article 329A from the Constitution altogether. By then the provision was already a shell: the Supreme Court had struck down its operative clauses (4) and (5) in November 1975, and the elections of the Prime Minister and the Speaker had in practice returned to the ordinary election petition process under the Representation of the People Act. The omission completed the repeal of the 1975 device at the level of constitutional text. Parliament did not attempt to reinsert any equivalent provision, and prime ministerial elections have since remained examinable by election petition in the High Courts like the elections of other members of Parliament.

### Q: How did Minerva Mills carry forward the pattern set in November 1975?

Minerva Mills v Union of India, decided on 31 July 1980, applied the two track method of November 1975 to the Forty-Second Amendment. A Constitution Bench measured clauses (4) and (5) of Article 368, which the Forty-Second Amendment had inserted to bar judicial review of constitutional amendments and to declare the amending power unlimited, against named basic features of the Constitution, judicial review among them, and struck those clauses down. The remainder of the constitutional text was left to operate, and questions arising under it were decided on the surviving law. The sequence replicated 1975 exactly: an amendment tested against the structure and destroyed in part, ordinary questions decided afterward under the law that remained, with Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain cited as the authority that amendments can fall.

### Q: What did Granville Austin argue about the November 1975 verdict?

Granville Austin, in Working a Democratic Constitution (1999), his scholarly history of the Indian Constitution's operation through the Emergency decades, treated the November 1975 verdict within the Emergency chapters as evidence that the basic structure doctrine had acquired genuine restraining force. Austin's account emphasises that a bench sitting under Emergency conditions, with the press censored and opposition leaders detained, nevertheless invalidated the central clause of the amendment enacted to defeat its own jurisdiction, rather than treating the amendment as self executing. His reading gives the verdict weight as an institutional event: the doctrine survived its first contact with a determined government commanding a large parliamentary majority, and the striking down of clause (4), rather than the election's survival under the amended statute, is the element his history carries forward.

### Q: What role did Shanti Bhushan play in the election case?

Shanti Bhushan was Raj Narain's counsel through the election litigation, at the Allahabad trial and in the Supreme Court. At Allahabad he cross examined Indira Gandhi when she gave evidence in March 1975, the first occasion on which a serving Indian Prime Minister testified in a court, and he built the petitioner's record on the Kapoor assistance and the rostrum arrangements that produced the two findings of corrupt practice. In the Supreme Court in the autumn of 1975 he led the constitutional attack on clause (4) of Article 329A, arguing that the clause damaged free and fair elections, offended equality and usurped the judicial function by deciding a pending case. The attack succeeded on the clause and failed on the election, which the amended statute saved.

### Q: How does the November 1975 verdict differ from ADM Jabalpur?

The two verdicts came from the same court during the same Emergency and point in opposite directions. In Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain on 7 November 1975 the Supreme Court applied the basic structure doctrine to strike down clauses of a constitutional amendment, asserting constitutional limits against the legislative power of a government with a large majority. In ADM Jabalpur v Shivkant Shukla on 28 April 1976 a Constitution Bench held by four to one that no person could move a court for habeas corpus against illegal detention while the Emergency lasted, with Justice Khanna dissenting alone. The first case policed the boundary of constituent power in an election dispute. The second declined to police executive detention. The contrast defines the unevenness of the court's Emergency record.

### Q: What is the triumph and accommodation reading of the verdict?

The triumph reading holds that the verdict of 7 November 1975 was a genuine assertion of constitutional power, because a bench sitting during the Emergency struck down the amendment clause designed to defeat its jurisdiction and made the basic structure doctrine work for the first time. The accommodation reading answers that the same judgment upheld Indira Gandhi's election under a retrospectively amended statute, left her in office and left the Emergency undisturbed, so that the government lost a clause and kept everything else. The scholarly debate between the readings, carried in works by Granville Austin in 1999, Rajeev Dhavan in 1977 and T. R. Andhyarujina in 2011, turns on whether the case is weighed by its doctrinal first limb or by its immediate political outcome, and the text supports elements of both.

### Q: Why did the Court uphold the Election Laws (Amendment) Act while striking down clause (4)?

The Court treated the two August 1975 measures as different species of power. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act was ordinary legislation within Parliament's legislative competence: it changed the definitions and explanations of the Representation of the People Act, and retrospective clarification of a statute is a recognised legislative act whose product courts apply in pending proceedings. Clause (4) of Article 329A was a constitutional amendment that did not change the law governing elections but decided a pending case, annulled a court's order and abolished the forum for a class of election disputes, which the bench held damaged the basic structure. The distinction between changing the law and deciding the case explains the split outcome: the statute survived scrutiny and governed the election, while the amendment clause fell.

### Q: Which basic features did the November 1975 verdict name or confirm?

The five opinions of 7 November 1975 named or confirmed free and fair elections as part of democracy, judicial review, the rule of law and the separation of powers as features of the basic structure that the amending power cannot damage. Justice Khanna's opinion placed free and fair elections inside democracy as a structural feature. Justice Mathew's opinion tied the judicial resolution of election disputes to the democratic structure. Justice Chandrachud's opinion worked through the separation of powers and equality, and Chief Justice Ray's through the rule of law. Later enumerations of the basic structure, including those in Minerva Mills in 1980 and in the nine judge decisions of the following decades, reproduced these features as settled entries whose first enforcement is traced to this case.
