Few labels in political argument do more work with less precision than the petty bourgeoisie and the middle class. A freelance designer is called petty bourgeois by one writer and working class by another. A hospital consultant earning a large salary is described as middle class in a newspaper profile, as a credentialed employee in a sociology paper, and as part of a professional managerial class in a polemical essay, and each description sounds plausible because each is measuring something different. A shopkeeper who employs two assistants, a tenant farmer, a senior manager with share options, a self employed electrician with a van and a full order book: ordinary language sweeps all of them into one broad middle, and then argument proceeds as though the sweep had settled a question rather than postponed it. This article postpones nothing. It separates the things that share a name, states the Marxist criterion exactly, tests the difficult cases one by one, and follows the strange career of a technical term that escaped the seminar room and became an insult.

![The petty bourgeoisie and the middle classes in Marx, shopkeepers, professionals, managers and three meanings - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The stakes are not merely terminological. Class vocabulary is used to explain elections, to predict political behaviour, to assign blame for cultural change, and to decide whose interests a policy serves. When the vocabulary is loose, the explanations built on it are loose in the same places, and the looseness is invisible precisely because the words feel familiar. Everyone knows what middle class means until two speakers discover, twenty minutes into an argument, that one of them means an income range and the other means a relation to the means of production. The first service a glossary article can perform is therefore negative: to show which questions the familiar words cannot answer. The second service is positive: to supply the distinctions that can answer them, drawn from Marx's own usage, from the three major attempts to repair that usage for a salaried age, and from the hard cases that break careless versions of all four schemes. Readers who want the wider vocabulary set out systematically can work through the [marxism glossary key terms](/2010/05/15/marxism-glossary-key-terms/), which owns the general definitions; this article owns the intermediate positions and the confusions that gather around them.

## The petty bourgeoisie as a position, not a mood

The petty bourgeoisie, in Marx's usage, is a class defined by a double relation to production. Its members own means of production, as the bourgeoisie does, and they work with those means themselves, as the proletariat does. The master craftsman with a workshop and tools, the peasant proprietor cultivating a family holding, the shopkeeper behind a counter, the small manufacturer employing a handful of hands while still labouring alongside them: each owns productive property, and each must personally work, because the property is too small to yield a living from ownership alone. That doubleness is the whole definition. Ownership without personal labour makes a capitalist, at whatever scale of comfort. Personal labour without ownership makes a wage worker, at whatever level of pay. The petty bourgeois is the figure in whom the two conditions coexist, and the coexistence is not a statistical midpoint between rich and poor but a distinct structural position with its own pressures, its own temptations, and its own characteristic politics.

Three consequences follow from defining the class by position rather than by income or lifestyle. The first is that wealth is irrelevant to membership at the margin. A prosperous master builder who owns equipment, employs three workers, and still spends each day on site is petty bourgeois even if the household income exceeds that of most salaried managers. A struggling barrister employed by a large firm, owning nothing productive beyond a laptop and a library, is not petty bourgeois in the strict sense even if the income is modest and the anxieties severe, because nothing is owned that could be worked independently. The second consequence is that the boundary is crossed by events, not by feelings. When the master builder stops working on site and lives from the labour of an expanding workforce, the position has changed into that of a small capitalist, whatever the family still calls itself. When the shopkeeper loses the shop and takes a wage in a supermarket chain, the position has changed in the opposite direction, whatever the memories and tastes retained. Class, on this scheme, is something that happens to people through changes in their relation to production, and identity trails behind it, sometimes by a generation.

The third consequence is the one that generates most of the confusion this article exists to clear. Because the petty bourgeois position is defined independently of income, the Marxist map and the income map of a society cut across each other at every level. There are poor petty bourgeois and rich wage workers, secure capitalists and precarious professionals, and no contradiction arises, because the schemes are answering different questions. The income map asks how much a household commands. The cultural map, which later sections examine, asks how a household lives, speaks, and is recognised. The Marxist map asks where a person stands in the process by which goods and services are produced and the surplus distributed: what is owned, whose labour is directed, and on what terms the person must work in order to live. A reader who keeps the three questions separate will find that most public argument about the middle class consists of answers to one question being offered as refutations of answers to another. That observation, developed fully below, is the article's central claim, and it has a name designed for citation: the three middle classes.

## The three middle classes

The three middle classes is the claim that the phrase middle class denotes three different things in three different registers of speech, and that arguments about it fail when the registers are mixed. In the first register, everyday and statistical speech, middle class names an income band: the households clustered around the median, neither rich nor poor, whose standard of living is taken as the social norm. In the second register, political journalism and cultural commentary, middle class names a formation of taste, education, accent, occupation, and aspiration: a way of living that can be recognised in a street, a school gate, or a shopping basket, and that persists across wide differences of income. In the third register, Marxist analysis, middle class, when it is used carefully at all, names an intermediate position in relation to production: the territory between the class that owns the means of production and lives from that ownership, and the class that owns nothing productive and lives by selling its labour power. The petty bourgeoisie occupies part of that territory by the classical definition. The salaried managers and credentialed professionals who dominate modern discussion occupy another part, and placing them required the theoretical repairs examined later in this article.

Each register has its own legitimate use, and none is a corruption of the others. The income band is the right tool for describing the distribution of living standards, for locating the households squeezed by a tax change, or for comparing prosperity across countries. The cultural formation is the right tool for explaining consumption patterns, educational strategies, and the texture of social recognition. The positional sense is the right tool for analysing who directs production, who appropriates surplus, and whose interests are engaged by a change in property relations. Error begins when a conclusion earned in one register is spent in another. A survey showing that most people identify as middle class, a cultural finding, settles nothing about the structure of ownership. A table showing that the median income has stagnated, a distributional finding, settles nothing about whether managers constitute a class. A Marxist demonstration that professionals sell their labour power, a positional finding, settles nothing about whether those professionals feel secure, vote progressively, or recognise themselves in the description. The registers answer to different evidence, and the discipline of keeping them apart is the precondition for using any of them well.

The phrase the three middle classes is offered as a mnemonic for that discipline. When an argument about the middle class begins, the first move is to ask which of the three is in play. When a statistic is produced, the first move is to ask which register the statistic belongs to and whether it can bear the weight the argument places on it. When a writer moves from the cultural sense to a positional conclusion, or from an income figure to a claim about class power, the move should be visible and defended, not smuggled through the ambiguity of a shared phrase. Most polemics on the subject cannot survive that audit, which is why the audit is worth performing. The remainder of this article works mainly in the third register, because that is where the petty bourgeoisie lives, but it returns to the other two registers whenever a confusion between them is doing damage, and its closing sections give rules for translating between registers without cheating.

### What does middle class mean in Marxist terms?

Not an income band. It refers to positions between the two main classes, chiefly the petty bourgeoisie, who own small means of production and work them themselves, and salaried employees with authority or credentials who own nothing productive yet are not simply selling labour power on ordinary terms. Position, not pay, decides.

That answer is the snippet form of the whole article, and every element in it earns a section. Positions, plural, because the intermediate territory contains more than one kind of location and the differences among them matter. The petty bourgeoisie first, because it is Marx's own category with a clear criterion. Salaried employees with authority or credentials second, because they are the population that broke the classical map and forced its successors to improvise. Ordinary terms last, because the phrase marks the exact point of difficulty: a senior manager and a hospital consultant both sell their capacity to work, yet the terms on which they sell it, the authority they exercise while working, and the assets their credentials represent make the plain category of wage labour feel inadequate to nearly every analyst who has examined them, including analysts who conclude that wage labour is where they belong. The feeling of inadequacy is data. The theoretical question is what to build from it.

## Petite, not petty: the word itself

The English form petty bourgeoisie invites a misreading that the French original does not. Petite bourgeoisie means the small bourgeoisie: small in scale of property, not small in character. The adjective classifies the size of the holding, as it does in petty commodity production, the standard term for production by independent producers owning their own means. English petty, by contrast, carries a moral charge of triviality and small mindedness, and generations of readers have absorbed the charge into the concept, treating petty bourgeois as a sneer at narrow horizons, suburban tastes, and anxious respectability. That sneer exists in Marx's writing, and sections below will meet it in his political journalism, where the class's outlook is anatomised with real contempt. But the sneer is an argument about the outlook generated by a position, not the definition of the position, and readers who start from the sneer cannot reconstruct the argument. A wealthy small proprietor of liberal views and cosmopolitan tastes remains petty bourgeois. A wage worker of narrow views and fierce respectability does not become petty bourgeois by temperament. The German Kleinburgertum, which Marx also used, makes the same classificatory point as the French: the small burgher stratum, defined by the modest scale of its property and its direct engagement in production or trade.

Translation history explains part of the damage. English editions of Marx long rendered the term in its French dress or in the anglicised petty form interchangeably, and popular usage settled on the form that sounded like an insult. Once a category sounds like an insult, it gets used as one, and its analytical content drains away. The drain is visible in ordinary political speech, where petty bourgeois functions as a synonym for smug, complacent, or hypocritically radical, applied to persons whose relation to production the speaker has not considered for a moment. A tenant activist denouncing a landlord with one rental flat and a professor denouncing a tradesperson who employs an apprentice are both called petty bourgeois in such speech, although their positions are structurally unlike: the first lives substantially from property, the second works daily with owned tools. Precision here is not pedantry. A term that once sorted these cases now lumps them, and the lumping serves rhetoric at the expense of the analysis the term was built to carry.

There is a reciprocal error worth naming. Because the insult usage is so widespread, some readers assume the analytical usage must be a later scholarly rescue, a polite fiction laid over an originally polemical word. The texts do not support that assumption. In the Manifesto of the Communist Party, in the historical writings on France, and in the economic manuscripts, the category does classificatory work: it picks out small manufacturers, shopkeepers, artisans, and peasant proprietors as a stratum with interests distinguishable from those of both great classes, and it predicts a trajectory for that stratum. The polemical colouring is real but secondary, an overlay on a working concept. The correct reading order is definition first, evaluation second, and the evaluation itself must be earned by the analysis of the position rather than imported from the adjective. Readers who reverse the order will find Marx merely abusive where he is in fact diagnostic, and will miss the diagnostic claims that later history put to the test.

## The wrong definitions that circulate

Four wrong definitions of the petty bourgeoisie circulate widely, and each survives because it captures a fragment of the truth. The first defines the class by income: the petty bourgeoisie as those earning a middling amount, more than manual workers and less than executives. This fails on the criterion itself, since income is a result that the position helps to explain and cannot serve as its definition without circularity, and it fails empirically at both ends, admitting rich wage workers and excluding poor proprietors. The second defines the class by occupation labels: shopkeepers, clerks, and small farmers as a fixed list inherited from the nineteenth century. Lists decay as economies change, and a definition that cannot classify a freelance software consultant or a franchise operator is a museum piece rather than a tool. Marx's criterion classifies both without strain, as later sections show, because it asks about ownership and labour rather than consulting a table of trades. The third wrong definition is the cultural one: the petty bourgeoisie as the stratum of a certain taste, prudish, acquisitive, and status anxious. Cultural portraits of the class exist in Marx and in the literature after him, and some are acute, but a portrait is a hypothesis about what a position does to outlook, not a test for membership. The fourth defines the class by self description: those who call themselves middle class are the middle class. Self description measures identification, which is a real social fact with real political effects, and class theory has a separate vocabulary for it, examined in the treatment of class in itself and class for itself. It cannot substitute for position without dissolving the distinction between what people are and what they take themselves to be, a distinction on which the entire analysis depends.

Each wrong definition also has a characteristic political use, and naming the use helps explain the persistence. The income definition serves governments, which prefer a middle defined by a band they can claim to be enlarging. The occupational list serves nostalgia, allowing a vanished economy of independent shops and workshops to stand in for the present one. The cultural definition serves contempt, licensing the speaker to sort acquaintances by taste while believing class analysis is being performed. Self description serves complacency, since majorities in many countries describe themselves as middle class, and a class to which nearly everyone belongs can be invoked for almost any policy and against almost any redistribution. Against all four, the positional criterion has one virtue that outweighs its difficulties: it can be wrong in an informative way. It makes claims about ownership, authority, and the sale of labour power that evidence can check, and where the evidence shows the criterion straining, as it does for salaried managers and professionals, the strain is visible and arguable rather than dissolved into a fog of impressions. The repairs considered later in this article are attempts to relieve exactly that strain, and their disagreements are disagreements about evidence and mechanism, which is what serious classification disputes should be.

### Is petty a term of contempt or a measure of scale?

It is a measure of scale. Petite in the French original means small, classifying the size of the property a proprietor works with, and Kleinburgertum in German makes the same neutral point. The English word petty drags a moral sneer into a classificatory term, and readers who start from the sneer misread the concept from the first line.

Holding that distinction steadily changes how the famous passages read. When Marx describes the smallholding peasants of France as a mass formed by simple addition, like potatoes in a sack, the image is contemptuous, certainly, but the contempt is doing analytical work: it expresses a claim about the mode of production, isolated family holdings whose occupants share conditions without entering into manifold relations with one another, and therefore cannot represent themselves as a class in the political sense. Strip the image away and the claim remains testable and interesting. Restore the image without the claim and nothing remains but a sneer at peasants. The same discipline applies to the shopkeeper and the artisan throughout the historical writings. Marx's judgements on the intermediate strata are severe, sometimes unfairly so, and the severity is part of the record. The record also contains a structural account of why a stratum placed between two great classes, fearing descent into the one and unable to rise into the other, develops the outlook he describes. A reader equipped with the definition can separate the account from the severity and judge each on its merits. A reader equipped only with the sneer can do neither.

## Marx's small proprietors in the texts

The category is present from the beginning of Marx's mature political writing. In the Manifesto of the Communist Party of 1848, the intermediate strata are named as the lower middle class: the small manufacturer, the shopkeeper, the artisan, and the peasant, each defined by modest property worked by its owner, and each said to fight against the bourgeoisie to save a threatened existence. The description is double. These strata are conservative, even reactionary, when they try to roll back the development that is destroying them, and they are revolutionary when they pass over to the standpoint of the class that is replacing them. What they cannot be, in the Manifesto's scheme, is stable. Modern industry and competition are presented as a mill grinding the small producer between the two great classes, throwing one fragment upward into the bourgeoisie and the mass downward into the proletariat. The prediction embedded in that image is examined in its own section below; the classificatory point here is that from 1848 onward the petty bourgeoisie is a stratum defined by property of a scale that still requires its owner's labour.

The French historical writings give the category its richest development, because France supplied the cases. In The Class Struggles in France and in The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, the smallholding peasantry appears as the largest intermediate mass in the society, and Marx's treatment turns on the gap between position and political capacity. The peasants share a mode of production and a set of interests, which makes them a class in the positional sense; but their isolation on scattered holdings prevents the organisation and self representation that would make them a class in the political sense, so their interests are represented for them by an executive power standing above society. The famous sack of potatoes image belongs to this argument and not to mere abuse, as the previous section insisted. Beside the peasantry, the urban petty bourgeoisie of shopkeepers and small masters receives a different treatment: organised enough to have deputies and newspapers, articulate enough to formulate demands, yet divided against itself because its members are creditors and debtors, landlords and tenants, employers and employed in miniature, so that their politics oscillates between the great classes whose programmes they alternately borrow. The oscillation is not a character flaw. It is the political expression of the double position, and Marx's narrative derives it from the position with a rigour that survives his partisanship.

In Capital the category changes register. The analytic works are concerned with the pure forms of capitalist production, and the petty producer appears there mainly as a vanishing premise: independent commodity production, in which the producer owns the conditions of labour, is the form from which capitalist production differentiates itself, and the historical sections on so called primitive accumulation narrate the separation of producers from their property that creates the two great classes of the mature system. Marx knew, and said, that the differentiation was incomplete and uneven, that small producers persisted within and alongside capitalist production, and that trades existed in which the independent producer was being subordinated to merchant and industrial capital without yet being dispossessed. His category for the subordinated but not yet dispossessed producer, the artisan working for a merchant who controls the market, anticipates later arguments about formal subsumption and about the disguised wage relations of putting out systems and platform work. The series treatment of [surplus value in modern firms](/2011/01/15/surplus-value-modern-firms/) takes up that subordination from the side of value production; here the point is historical. The classical texts give the petty bourgeoisie a clear criterion, a predicted trajectory, and a politics derived from position. What they do not give, because the phenomenon was still modest in Marx's lifetime, is an account of the salaried intermediate strata: the managers, administrators, and credentialed experts who own nothing and yet do not present as ordinary wage labour. That absence is the seed of the new middle class problem.

## The disappearance prediction, stated exactly

Precision about the prediction matters, because the verdict of history depends on what was actually predicted. Marx and Engels predicted that capitalist development would erode the intermediate strata of independent producers. Competition from large scale production, with its lower costs and greater resilience, would ruin small manufacturers and artisans in sector after sector; retail concentration would squeeze the small shopkeeper; agricultural production would be industrialised and the peasantry dissolved into rural capitalists and rural wage labourers. The ruined would descend into the proletariat, a fortunate few would climb into the bourgeoisie, and society would polarise toward the two class poles around which the theory's dynamics turn. The prediction is directional and tendential rather than dated: no deadline is given, and the theory's own logic allows counter tendencies, since new trades continually open niches for small producers and since capital itself sometimes prefers to leave risky, low margin, or labour intensive activities to nominally independent operators. Engels, writing later prefaces and supplements, acknowledged fresh evidence of peasant and small producer persistence and adjusted emphases without abandoning the tendency claim.

Stated that way, the prediction has three separable components, and they have fared differently. The first component is sectoral: in manufacturing, large scale production did displace the independent artisan across most branches, and the master craftsman employing journeymen became a marginal figure in the advanced economies. That component was borne out so thoroughly that its success is now hard to see. The second component is aggregate: the share of the labour force in self employment and small proprietorship fell steeply across the long twentieth century in the industrial countries, as wage and salary employment became the normal condition. That component was also broadly borne out, and any honest account must register the scale of the decline before turning to the complications. The third component is the strong one, the expectation of disappearance and polarisation, of an intermediate stratum shrinking toward insignificance and leaving a society of owners and workers facing each other without buffers. That component failed, and the manner of its failure is the subject of the next section. Readers should notice what this three way split achieves. It prevents the two lazy verdicts that dominate popular discussion: that Marx was simply right about the middle strata, which ignores the failed third component, and that he was simply wrong, which ignores the massive real decline the first two components describe. The record is a transformation misdescribed by its own theory as an extinction, and explaining the transformation required the successors to rebuild the middle of the map.

## What happened instead: persistence, decline, and return in new forms

Self employment declined, and then it stopped declining and in places grew again, in forms the nineteenth century would have found strange. The independent artisan gave ground to the factory, but the late twentieth century produced the freelance consultant selling expertise to former employers, the self employed tradesperson booked through a platform, the contractor who is a firm of one person for tax and liability purposes, the franchise operator running a branded outlet under tight contractual discipline, and the micro retailer trading through a marketplace owned by one of the largest companies on earth. Directional description is the honest register here, and the brief for this article forbids invented figures, so no percentages will be asserted. The pattern across advanced economies, as the comparative literature describes it, is a long fall in the self employed share driven by agriculture and small manufacturing, a floor reached in many countries, and a composition transformed: fewer farmers and shopkeepers, more professionals, contractors, and service providers operating alone. Whether a given country's share rose or fell in a given decade matters less for class analysis than the transformation of what the category contains. Self employment in Marx's century usually meant a workshop, a stock of materials, and a trade learned by apprenticeship. Self employment in the platform economy can mean a car, a phone, and an algorithmic dispatcher, which strains the old category in ways worth examining directly.

The strain is instructive because it reveals what the criterion actually tracks. Consider the driver who owns a vehicle and works through a platform that sets prices, allocates jobs, disciplines performance, and can exclude the driver at will. By the letter of the classical definition the driver is a petty commodity producer, an owner of means of production selling a service. By the substance of the relation the driver controls almost nothing that makes ownership economically meaningful: not the price, not the customer relation, not the terms of access to the market. Two serious readings compete, as the application literature on modern firms also notes. One treats the platform relation as a disguised wage relation, ownership of the car notwithstanding, because the platform performs the essential functions of an employer. The other treats it as petty production subjected to rent extraction, in which a genuine independent producer pays for access to infrastructure the way a market trader pays for a pitch. The readings disagree about classification while agreeing about the facts, and the disagreement is productive: it forces analysts to specify which incidents of ownership matter, control over price, over the labour process, over the surplus, rather than invoking ownership as a talisman. That specification problem is not a defect imported by careless followers. It is the classical criterion meeting a form of production designed, in part, to blur it.

Agriculture and retail, the two classical heartlands of petty production, tell a parallel story of transformation rather than simple extinction. The family farm persists in many economies, but increasingly as a node in chains dominated by processors, supermarkets, and input suppliers, its independence qualified by contracts that fix prices, varieties, and methods from outside. The independent shop persists in niches where locality, specialisation, or personal service resists standardisation, while the centre of retail gravity moved first to chains and then to platforms. In both sectors the surviving small producer is real, and classifying the survivor as petty bourgeois remains defensible on the criterion; but the survivor's dependence on large capital for market access, credit, and inputs means the position no longer carries the autonomy the nineteenth century associated with it. A category can remain correct as classification while the social weight of what it classifies changes beyond recognition. That is the precise sense in which the disappearance prediction failed: not that the stratum vanished on schedule, and not that it endured unchanged, but that development dissolved the old petty bourgeoisie while continuously generating new small scale and dependent forms that the theory must classify without the comfort of a predicted end point. The broader question of whether wage labour has become the universal condition is owned by the analysis of [whether the working class still exists](/2011/11/01/does-the-working-class-still-exist/), which readers should consult alongside this one; the present article's concern is the territory at the boundary, where the classification work is hardest.

### How does ownership divide the shopkeeper from the manager?

Ownership is the dividing line. The shopkeeper owns the premises, stock, and goodwill whose use constitutes the business, and works them in person, standing inside production as a small proprietor. The manager owns none of the assets being managed, however large the salary or authority, and stands inside production as an employee directing assets owned by others.

That contrast, simple to state, dissolves a surprising amount of confusion, because salary size and workplace authority both fail as dividers where ownership succeeds. The manager of a large plant directs hundreds of workers, controls budgets exceeding the turnover of most small firms, and may earn several times the income of the shopkeeper on the same street, yet the positional map separates them completely: one sells labour power under terms shaped by scarcity and organisational trust, the other sells goods produced or traded with owned means. Everyday speech resists the separation, because everyday speech sorts by income and visible command, and on both measures the manager outranks the shopkeeper. The resistance is the three middle classes confusion operating in miniature. In the income register and the cultural register the manager is plainly the grander figure. In the positional register the shopkeeper is the proprietor and the manager is the employee, and neither register's verdict cancels the other's. Arguments that proceed by insisting the manager must be the more bourgeois of the two because of pay, or the shopkeeper because of property, without saying which register is in use, are not disagreements about the world. They are two monologues sharing a vocabulary, and the vocabulary is the problem this article exists to repair.

## The new middle class problem

While the old petty bourgeoisie was declining and mutating, a different intermediate population was growing at a pace no nineteenth century theory had anticipated: salaried employees who own no productive property, yet whose pay, authority, autonomy, and security separate them sharply from the wage workers beneath them in the same organisations. Managers at every level, engineers, doctors employed by hospitals, university teachers, senior administrators, designers, accountants, lawyers in firms, officers of the state bureaucracy: the roster is heterogeneous, and its heterogeneity is the problem. These people cannot be assigned to the bourgeoisie, because they own no capital and can be dismissed. They sit awkwardly in the proletariat, because they direct the labour of others, or command scarce certified expertise, or identify their interests with the organisations that employ them, and because their material condition often resembles the petty bourgeois comfort the theory associated with property. Twentieth century Marxism inherited a map with two great territories and a shrinking strip between them, and found the strip repopulated by a salaried multitude the original survey had not charted. The name given to the difficulty, the new middle class problem, distinguishes it from the old petty bourgeoisie precisely: old meant the small proprietors Marx described, new meant the credentialed and managerial employees he largely did not.

Three features make the new strata hard to place, and each feature generated a different theoretical repair. The first feature is authority. A line manager hires, disciplines, schedules, and appraises; a senior executive allocates capital and closes plants. Whatever their contractual status, such employees exercise functions that classical theory assigned to capital, and an analysis that files them as ordinary workers misses the function, while an analysis that files them as capitalists misses the contract. The second feature is credentials. A licensed profession controls entry to its own labour market through examinations and accredited training, and its members sell not generic labour power but a certified scarcity, which supports incomes, autonomy, and bargaining power unlike those of the uncredentialed. The credential is an asset in a loose economic sense, yielding a return across a career, yet it cannot be sold, bequeathed as capital, or used to employ others without further steps, which blocks any simple assimilation to property. The third feature is function in reproduction. Teachers, administrators, media workers, and the caring professions do not merely produce commodities; they reproduce the skills, norms, health, and organisational knowledge the whole system runs on, and a theory of class that ignores the reproductive function will misjudge both their numbers and their allegiances. The three repairs that follow can be read as three decisions about which feature to make decisive. Poulantzas made the boundary of productive labour decisive, and ideology with it. The Ehrenreichs made the reproductive function decisive. Wright made authority and credentials into dimensions of a location that could be contradictory rather than assignable to one side. The [marxist theory of class guide](/2011/09/01/marxist-theory-of-class-guide/) sets out the underlying criterion against which all three repairs are attempts to cope, and readers who keep that criterion in view will see that the quarrel among the repairs is a quarrel about which strain in the evidence deserves to govern the classification.

It is worth pausing on why the problem could not be solved by ignoring it. One tradition within Marxism did attempt something close: if class is defined strictly by ownership, then all non owners who must work are workers, the salaried strata are a privileged layer of the working class, and the matter is closed. The closure is purchased at a price. It leaves the theory without resources to explain why managers reliably act for capital against workers in disputes they do not own a stake in, why credentialed professions defend entry restrictions with a tenacity indistinguishable from property defence, or why the political behaviour of the salaried strata diverges from that of manual workers in study after study. A definition may be internally consistent and still fail as an instrument if the distinctions it erases are the ones that predict conduct. The rival error, multiplying classes until every occupation has one, dissolves the theory's explanatory ambition entirely. The three serious repairs all steer between those failures, which is why they remain the reference points. Each is now taken in turn, stated in the form its author intended, before the comparison table sorts their verdicts on identical cases.

## Poulantzas and the new petty bourgeoisie

Nicos Poulantzas, writing in the mid 1970s, most fully in Classes in Contemporary Capitalism, proposed the boldest solution: the salaried intermediate strata are a new petty bourgeoisie, a class distinct from both the bourgeoisie and the working class, assigned by a combination of economic, political, and ideological criteria. The economic criterion turns on the distinction between productive and unproductive labour developed elsewhere in the series. Wage labour that produces surplus value for capital belongs to the working class on Poulantzas's scheme; wage labour that does not, however skilled or well paid, does not. Commercial employees, most state employees, supervisors whose work is the surveillance and discipline of other workers, and the broad ranks of service and administrative labour fall on the unproductive side of the line, and therefore outside the working class, their lack of property notwithstanding. Supervisory authority adds a political criterion: those who exercise the powers of capital within the labour process occupy the place of capital in that relation, whatever their payslips say. Ideology adds the third strand, since the new petty bourgeoisie is said to share the individualism, careerism, and aspiration toward the bourgeoisie that marked the old, importing its outlook into salaried form.

The proposal has real strengths, and they should be registered before its difficulties. It explains, as the strict ownership criterion cannot, why the salaried strata so often align with capital in practice and why their organisations differ from unions of productive workers. It takes the productive and unproductive distinction seriously as a class boundary rather than a technicality, connecting the class map to the theory of value in a way that gives the classification work to do. And it predicts the political volatility of the stratum, its susceptibility to authoritarian appeals and to meritocratic ideologies, from structural features rather than from distaste for its tastes. The difficulties are equally real. The productive boundary is itself contested, and building a class frontier on it imports every dispute about which labour produces surplus value into the foundations of the class map, so that the classification of a nurse or a teacher changes with one's position in a separate theoretical controversy. The ideological criterion threatens circularity: a stratum is assigned to a class partly by its outlook, and its outlook is then explained by its class assignment. And the resulting working class is narrow, a minority of the employed population in advanced economies, which sits strangely with the political role the theory assigns it. Sympathetic critics, Wright among them, concluded that Poulantzas had identified genuine dividing lines but drawn them in the wrong place, converting gradients of authority and function within the wage earning population into a frontier between classes. The criticism matters because it shaped the third repair, which keeps Poulantzas's phenomena and redraws the geometry.

## The Ehrenreichs and the professional managerial class

Barbara Ehrenreich and John Ehrenreich formulated the professional managerial class in an essay of 1977, written for a radical audience trying to understand why the educated salaried strata had disappointed the hopes placed in them. Their definition is functional rather than positional in the narrow sense. The professional managerial class consists of salaried mental workers who do not own the means of production and whose major function is the reproduction of capitalist culture and class relations: teachers and professors reproducing skills and deference, social workers and administrators managing the poor, engineers and designers organising production, media and advertising workers manufacturing consent and desire, managers coordinating the whole. The class is defined against both poles. Against capital, because its members are employees dependent on salaries and often in genuine conflict with owners over budgets, autonomy, and professional standards. Against the working class, because its daily work is the direction, instruction, and supervision of others, and because its material comfort and cultural formation separate its interests from those of the people it manages, teaches, and treats.

The original argument had a specific polemical target that later usage has forgotten, and recovering it is one of this article's services. The Ehrenreichs were not coining a sneer at affluent liberals. They were explaining a structural antagonism: the new left of their generation, drawn heavily from the professional strata, repeatedly collided with working class constituencies whose lives the professionals administered, and the collision could not be understood as misunderstanding or bad faith on either side. The professional managerial class had interests of its own, in the expansion of professional employment, in credentialed authority over clients, and in the cultural prestige of expertise, and those interests could conflict with owners above and workers below. The essay also carried an internal warning, developed in Barbara Ehrenreich's later work on the fear of falling: the class's position is insecure, its comforts dependent on organisational budgets and credential values that can be cut, standardised, or automated, and its politics are marked by anxiety about descent quite as much as by confidence in command. Read whole, the thesis is an essay in self criticism by members of the stratum it describes, and its tone is diagnostic rather than denunciatory.

The objections arrived promptly and remain the standard criticisms. The functional definition gathers occupations with little in common beyond salary and education, and critics asked whether a hospital nurse, a corporate lawyer, and a schoolteacher share enough to constitute a class rather than a statistical aggregate. The reproduction function proved elastic: almost any mental labour reproduces capitalist relations in some sense, so the boundary of the class expands or contracts with the analyst's ingenuity. And the thesis struggled with the strata's internal conflicts, since professionals employed by the state, by corporations, and by small organisations face different pressures and often oppose one another. Defenders reply that the same objections, pressed hard enough, would dissolve any class concept, including the classical ones, since the working class of the classical texts also spans occupations with divergent cultures and interests. The reply has force, and the honest summary is that the professional managerial class names a real formation with contested boundaries, most illuminating when used to analyse the antagonism between credentialed administrators and the administered, and least illuminating when used as a demographic label for everyone with a degree and a salary above the median.

## Wright and contradictory class locations

Erik Olin Wright's solution, developed from the late 1970s onward in works including Class, Crisis and the State and the comparative project summarised in Classes, refuses the premise that every position must belong wholly to one class. Capitalist societies, on Wright's account, are structured by several dimensions of exploitation and domination at once, and a position can be located differently on different dimensions, which yields contradictory class locations: positions that share the interests of opposed classes simultaneously. The manager is the paradigm. As an employee without significant ownership, the manager stands with workers against owners on the dimension of property. As the holder of organisational assets, the delegated control over plant, budgets, and the labour of others, the manager stands with owners against workers on the dimension of authority. The supervisor, the semi autonomous professional, and the small employer each occupy other contradictory or intermediate cells in a map built from three assets: means of production, organisational control, and scarce skills or credentials. Credential holders exploit, in Wright's later formulation, through the restriction of access to skills, a mechanism distinct from the exploitation exercised through property, and the professional's location is contradictory because the same person is exploited as an employee and advantaged as a credential monopolist.

The virtues of the scheme are its honesty and its testability. It does not pretend the salaried strata away, and it does not annex them to a class whose defining criterion they fail. It generates hypotheses that survey research can examine, and Wright spent decades examining them, measuring how class locations, so defined, correlate with income and with attitudes, and revising the scheme when the correlations disappointed. The fullest treatment of that research programme belongs to the series article on [Erik Olin Wright's class analysis](/2020/02/15/erik-olin-wright-class-analysis/), which owns the detailed exposition; here the scheme matters as the third member of a comparison. Set against Poulantzas, Wright keeps the phenomena of authority and credentials but treats them as dimensions along which wage earners differ, rather than as frontier posts between classes. Set against the Ehrenreichs, he declines the functional definition and the cultural reproduction story, preferring measurable assets to a stratum's alleged mission. The cost of the scheme is its loss of narrative simplicity. A map with twelve locations does not march through history the way two great classes do, and critics from several directions have asked whether a location that is contradictory in structure can ground the collective action the classical theory expects from classes at all. Wright's answer, that interests can be genuinely divided and action correspondingly ambivalent, is less a refutation than a redescription of the difficulty, and the ambivalence of the salaried strata in actual politics gives it a certain empirical dignity.

What follows for a reader choosing among the three is a division of labour rather than a coronation. Poulantzas is strongest on the political and ideological behaviour of salaried strata and on the class significance of unproductive labour. The Ehrenreichs are strongest on the lived antagonism between professionals and their clients and on the cultural work the strata perform. Wright is strongest where measurement matters, in sorting actual populations and testing whether the sorting predicts anything. The intermediate positions table below applies all three to the same eight cases, because the surest way to understand a classification scheme is to watch it classify, and to notice where the schemes agree, which is often, and where they split, which is where the theoretical choices live.

### What distinguishes a credential from a means of production?

A means of production can be owned, sold, rented out, and used to employ the labour of others while its owner withdraws from work. A credential cannot be sold or bequeathed, yields income only when its holder works, and commands a premium by restricting entry to an occupation rather than by organising production. Scarcity is shared; alienability is not.

The distinction carries more weight than its brevity suggests, because a large share of confusion about professionals reduces to treating the credential as a small piece of capital and its holder as a small capitalist. The analogy tempts because both assets yield returns above the wage of unskilled labour, both require investment to acquire, and both are defended by their holders with property like vigilance. The differences decide the classification. Capital employs others; the credential employs only its holder. Capital survives its owner's idleness and death; the credential dies with the career. Capital's return is profit, a share of surplus produced by others; the credential's return is closer to a rent on artificially restricted supply, paid as salary by an employer who still appropriates the surplus of the organisation as a whole. That is why the strict criterion places the employed professional among wage labour, why Wright treats skill assets as a separate dimension rather than a species of capital, and why Poulantzas, reaching a different destination, must travel by the road of unproductive labour and ideology rather than by property. Readers who master this one distinction can follow all three repairs; readers who collapse it will find the debate among them unintelligible, since the debate is largely about what to do with an asset that behaves like property in the labour market and like nothing at all in the market for firms.

## The intermediate positions table

The intermediate positions table is this article's findable artifact. It takes eight real positions and records, for each, the facts that the rival schemes treat as decisive: whether the person owns means of production, whether the person exercises authority over the labour of others, whether the person holds a credential that restricts entry to the work, and then the classification each of the three theoretical solutions delivers. Read across a row and the schemes can be compared on identical evidence. Read down the classification columns and the character of each scheme becomes visible: Poulantzas sorts by productive function and supervision, the Ehrenreichs by reproductive role, Wright by combinations of assets that are allowed to contradict one another.

Three lessons fall out of the table, and each is worth stating slowly. The first is that the schemes agree more often than their polemics suggest. The shopkeeper and the tradesperson are petty bourgeois on every scheme that retains the classical category, because the classical criterion was built for them. The line manager is intermediate on every scheme, because authority without ownership is the case no scheme can file at a pole. Disagreement concentrates on the credentialed professionals, and it concentrates there because the schemes weigh the credential differently: irrelevant beside unproductive function for Poulantzas, constitutive for the Ehrenreichs, a distinct asset for Wright. A reader who wants to know where a real dispute lies should watch the doctor and the academic, not the shopkeeper. The second lesson is that classification follows the decisive fact the analyst has chosen, and the choice is theoretical, not clerical. Nothing in the raw description of the salaried doctor forces a verdict; the verdict arrives when a scheme declares which fact about the doctor shall govern. Honest analysis names its governing fact. The third lesson concerns the landlord, who exposes a gap in schemes built around employment. A small landlord owns property that yields income without the owner entering production at all, which is neither petty production nor management nor credentialed labour. Marxist analysis handles the case through rent rather than through the production centred categories, and the table leaves the case visible precisely so that readers notice the boundary of the instrument: not every income from property is profit of enterprise, and not every proprietor is a producer.

## Sorting the hard cases

Tables classify types; judgement classifies persons, and persons arrive mixed. The franchise operator is the standard modern mixture. The operator owns or leases the outlet's equipment, hires and directs the staff, and bears local risks, which are the marks of a small employer and petty proprietor. The operator also works under a franchisor's manual that fixes the product, the prices within limits, the suppliers, the branding, and the inspection regime, pays royalties calculated on turnover, and can lose the licence for deviations, which are the marks of a subordinate in someone else's production system. The classification that respects both sets of facts treats the franchise operator as a petty proprietor under formal subordination to large capital: genuinely an owner and employer at the point of production, genuinely dependent at the point of market access. The verdict will not satisfy anyone who wants a single word, and that dissatisfaction is the correct response to a genuinely double position. Wright's vocabulary handles the case most gracefully, since a contradictory location is designed for it, but the older language reaches the same place by distinguishing the operator's local function from the system's commanding heights.

The professional in private practice poses the opposite mixture. A solicitor who owns a practice and employs clerks and junior lawyers has crossed from credentialed employee to small proprietor, and the classification follows the practice, not the diploma. The same solicitor employed by a large firm remains a seller of labour power whose credential scarcity raises the price, which is why the question are doctors and lawyers petty bourgeois has no occupational answer: the answer tracks the practice structure, and it changes across a single career as juniors become partners and partners become owners. Partnership itself is a graded case, since the equity partner owns a share of the firm and appropriates part of the surplus produced by its employees, while the salaried partner in some modern firms holds a title without ownership. The lesson generalises. Occupations are not classes. Positions within occupations are classes, and the same occupational label can span the proletarian, the contradictory, and the properly bourgeois locations as ownership and authority vary beneath it. Every lazy use of the vocabulary, in journalism and in polemic, begins by forgetting that sentence, and every competent use begins by remembering it.

The small employer deserves a paragraph of its own, because the boundary between petty bourgeois and capitalist runs through the category and the boundary is functional rather than numerical. No count of employees settles it, although textbooks sometimes offer one. The governing question is whether the owner must work in production for the enterprise to function and live. Where the owner's labour remains necessary, where the books are kept at midnight and the van is driven by the proprietor on Saturdays, the position remains petty bourgeois even with a dozen employees. Where the enterprise runs under managers and the owner lives from its surplus while performing at most the functions of ownership, the position is capitalist even with a modest headcount. Marx's own treatment of the transition, in the discussion of the minimum sum of capital and the metamorphosis of the master into a capitalist, turns on exactly this release of the owner from direct labour. The boundary is real, its location is a matter of evidence about who does what, and disputes along it are the healthy kind: arguments about facts under a shared criterion.

### When does self employment stop being independent production?

Self employment shades into dependence when the nominally independent producer loses control of the decisions that make ownership meaningful: the price charged, the customers served, the pace and method of work, and access to the market itself. A platform driver bound on all four counts keeps the legal form of independence while its substance migrates to the platform.

That answer deserves expansion because so much modern small scale work sits in the shaded zone. The market trader who rents a pitch but sets prices, chooses stock, and builds a personal clientele is an independent producer paying for a service, and the rent paid no more abolishes independence than a shopkeeper's rent does. The courier whose rates are set by an application, whose jobs arrive by allocation rather than by custom, whose performance is scored by metrics the courier cannot inspect, and whose access can be withdrawn without process, occupies a different position wearing similar legal clothes. Between the trader and the courier lies a spectrum of dependence, and the analytical task is to locate cases on it by examining control, not to force a binary verdict from the contract's label. Legislatures and courts face the same spectrum when they decide employment status, and their tests, control, integration, and economic reality, are close cousins of the class analyst's questions, which should raise confidence that the questions are the right ones. For the class map, the shaded zone is where the petty bourgeoisie of the platform age is being formed, and its members' double character, owners of tools and vehicles, subjects of an employer's discipline in everything but name, reproduces in digital form the subordination of the artisan to the merchant that Marx observed in the putting out system. History does not repeat, but relations of dependence have a limited repertoire, and recognising an old relation in new dress is among the oldest skills this tradition teaches.

## One more family of hard cases: portfolios, pensions, and shares

Modern compensation has invented positions that trouble the ownership criterion from the employee side, and the article's discipline requires meeting them rather than waving at the boundary. The senior employee paid partly in shares, the manager whose pension fund holds equities across the whole market, the professional whose retirement depends on the performance of companies never visited: each owns fragments of the means of production, and polemic from several directions has tried to make those fragments do classificatory work, either dissolving the working class into a nation of tiny capitalists or dismissing the fragments as irrelevant. The criterion, applied with its usual attention to function, gives a calmer verdict. What matters is whether ownership yields a living and confers control. A pension entitlement yields neither during the working life it punctuates: it cannot be directed by its holder, cannot be lived on while the salary is still needed, and arrives, when it arrives, as deferred wages administered by institutions the holder does not govern. Share based pay is closer to the line, since a sufficiently large award can free an executive from the need to work, and at that point the position does change, which is why the senior executive row of the table sits nearest the bourgeoisie. The line is crossed by magnitude translated into independence, not by the mere presence of equity in a pay packet, and the analyst's task is to ask the functional question of each case: could this person stop selling labour power and live from property? Where the answer is no, the classification stands, however diversified the portfolio. Where the answer is yes, it changes, however modest the lifestyle the person continues to lead. The test is crude, and like all the tests in this article it is meant to be: crude tests, openly stated, are what keep a vocabulary honest between the hard cases that refine it.

## The professional managerial class as an insult

The reception history of the professional managerial class is the strangest part of this article's subject, and the part with the widest practical use, because the term now circulates as a political epithet in places its authors never imagined. The Ehrenreichs coined the category in 1977 as insiders analysing their own stratum for a movement readership, and for two decades it lived the quiet life of a sociological term, cited in debates about class structure and largely unknown outside them. Its second life began when commentators across the political spectrum discovered its convenience as a name for a resented figure: the credentialed administrator, the human resources officer, the diversity consultant, the public health official, the journalist, the academic, gathered into a single acronym, PMC, and blamed for the cultural tone of institutions. Writers on the populist right use the term to name the class they hold responsible for progressive orthodoxy in universities and media. Writers on the left use it to name the stratum whose material interests, on their account, divert movements toward cultural politics and managerial reform and away from redistribution. The two usages share the acronym and almost nothing else, and both have largely shed the original definition, in which the class was specified by a function in reproduction and set in determinate relations to owners and workers.

Three features of the migration explain its success, and each is a small lesson in how concepts travel. First, the term names a genuine experience. Many people do encounter credentialed authority mainly as friction: as the administrator who refuses, the consultant who restructures, the official who explains. A vocabulary that dignifies that friction as class analysis will be taken up whatever its accuracy. Second, the acronym detaches the term from its argument. PMC can be deployed by speakers who could not state the Ehrenreichs' definition, in the way that petty bourgeois is deployed by speakers who could not state Marx's, and the parallel between the two careers is exact: a positional category decaying into a term of abuse as it travels. Third, the insult usage is unfalsifiable in a way the original was not. The original category made claims about function and interest that could be checked against the behaviour of the strata it named. The insult merely assigns disliked cultural traits to a class presumed to hold them, and since the class is identified by the traits, no evidence can disturb the assignment. The circuit is the same one this article found in the cultural misdefinition of the petty bourgeoisie, and recognising it in both places is the beginning of immunity.

What should a careful writer do with the term now? The advice parallels the treatment of its older cousin. Used with its definition attached, the professional managerial class remains a serviceable category for the stratum of salaried reproducers of culture and organisation, and the series article on [Marxism in political discourse](/2022/02/01/marxism-in-political-discourse/) tracks how such terms fare in public argument more generally. Used as an acronym of contempt, it tells the reader about the speaker's enemies and nothing about the structure of society. The distinction is not between politeness and rudeness. Marx's own prose is frequently rude about classes he had defined with care. The distinction is between a term that sorts the world and a term that merely boos it, and a writer who cannot say which of the two is being done in a given paragraph has lost control of the instrument. The Ehrenreichs themselves supplied the test of responsible use: they specified the class's function, its boundaries, its internal anxieties, and its conflicts with both neighbouring classes, and they wrote as members examining their own position. Polemic that cannot do as much is not continuing their argument. It is spending their coinage after melting it down.

### Why did the professional managerial class become an insult?

Because the term names a real friction, the encounter with credentialed authority that administers, instructs, and refuses, while its acronym lets speakers assign disliked cultural traits to a whole stratum without stating the original definition. Detached from the Ehrenreichs' functional argument, the category decayed from analysis into a label for enemies, as petty bourgeois had before it.

The decay has a cost beyond accuracy, and the cost falls on the very arguments the insult is meant to serve. A critic who believes that credentialed administrators have bent an institution toward their own interests has the makings of a strong case: the interests can be specified, the decisions traced, the beneficiaries named. The insult forecloses that work by supplying a ready made villain whose class membership explains everything in advance. The critic is left weaker, not stronger, and the institution's actual governors, the owners and senior executives who set the budgets within which administrators administer, escape attention entirely. There is an irony here that Marx would have recognised, since his complaint against the moralising socialism of his own day was identical in form: denunciation of persons and strata as a substitute for analysis of relations, satisfying to deliver and useless for understanding. The remedy is also his. Define the position, derive the interest, check the derivation against conduct, and let the severity of the conclusion be what the analysis earns. Severity so earned is formidable. Severity borrowed from an acronym is noise.

## Near neighbours: the terms it is confused with

Glossary precision is mostly boundary work, and the petty bourgeoisie has four boundaries that are habitually blurred. The first is the boundary with the bourgeoisie proper, crossed when ownership expands until the owner is released from personal labour and lives from the surplus of an enterprise run by others. Scale matters here only through function: a proprietor of three shops who still opens one of them each morning stands on the near side of the line, and the heir who inherits the same three shops and merely collects their profits through a manager stands on the far side, although the property is identical. The second boundary is with the proletariat, crossed downward when property is lost or becomes nominal, as with the platform driver whose vehicle is owned but whose market, price, and discipline belong to another. The third boundary is with the lumpenproletariat, Marx's category for the declassed strata outside regular production: the boundary is productive engagement, since the petty bourgeois works a property within the economy's ordinary circuits, while the lumpenproletariat in the classical texts subsists at the margins of those circuits, a distinction about relation to production and not, again, about respectability, however moralised the older prose can sound. The fourth boundary is the least discussed and the most useful: with the labour aristocracy, the privileged layer of the working class itself, better paid and more secure through craft organisation or imperial advantage on the traditional account. The labour aristocrat owns nothing and directs nobody, and belongs positionally to the working class, while enjoying a condition that cultural and income registers rank with the lower middle class. Four registers, four different placements, one person: the case is a compact demonstration of why the registers must be announced before they are used.

Household and family complicate every boundary, because petty production is characteristically family production. The classical smallholding and the corner shop alike run on the labour of spouses and children whose work is unwaged and often invisible in employment statistics, and the proprietor's position cannot be understood without the household that subsidises it with unpaid hours, flexible availability, and a standard of living that absorbs bad months. Class analysis that counts only the titled owner understates the labour the enterprise consumes and misdescribes the unit that actually occupies the petty bourgeois position, which is the family firm in the literal sense. The complication cuts the other way for the salaried strata. A household containing a manager and a nurse, or a teacher and a tradesperson, pools positions that the schemes classify separately, and the household's income, culture, and politics are products of the pool rather than of either position alone. Survey research classifies households by a dominant earner for practical reasons, and the convention is defensible, but readers should know a convention is operating. Much of the apparent softness of class boundaries in lived experience is household pooling seen from outside: positions are sharp, households are mixtures, and individuals experience the mixture. A theory that keeps the levels distinct can explain the softness without surrendering the sharpness, which is another instance of the discipline this article has recommended throughout: say which unit, which register, and which criterion is in play, and most mysteries of the middle dissolve into bookkeeping.

## Productive, unproductive, and the salaried boundary

The distinction between productive and unproductive labour runs underneath the new petty bourgeoisie debate and must be handled carefully, because it decides Poulantzas's frontier and influences everyone else's. In the Marxist usage, productive labour is labour that produces surplus value for capital: the criterion is the social relation, not the material character of the output, so the teacher in a commercial school chain may be productive while the teacher in a public school, doing indistinguishable work, is not, since the first is employed by capital for profit and the second by the state out of revenue. Unproductive labour, on this usage, is not useless labour, and the vocabulary's apparent insult is another translation trap of the kind this article has repeatedly flagged: surgery, teaching, and administration are unproductive in the technical sense wherever they are funded from revenue rather than advanced as capital, and the classification carries no judgement about their necessity or worth. The full development of the distinction, with its application to modern firms, belongs to the series treatment of [surplus value in modern firms](/2011/01/15/surplus-value-modern-firms/), already cited for the platform case; the point here is its class consequence.

The consequence is a genuine fork in the theory, and readers should see both roads. If the productive boundary is made a class boundary, following Poulantzas, then the great majority of state employees and service workers stand outside the working class, and the class map acquires a salaried class nearly as large as the proletariat it flanks. If the productive boundary is treated as a distinction within the wage earning class, marking different relations to surplus production among people who all sell their labour power, then the map keeps two great classes and a gradient, and the phenomena Poulantzas noticed, the distinct politics and organisation of the unproductive strata, must be explained as layer effects rather than class effects. Wright's scheme can be read as an attempt to decline the fork, since his dimensions let a state professional be a wage earner on the property axis and advantaged on the credential axis without any verdict being required on the productive question. No road is costless. The first multiplies classes and narrows the proletariat past the point where its assigned historical role remains plausible. The second risks explaining away real divergences of interest as mere shading. The third purchases flexibility at the price of a map too complex for the narrative uses to which class theory is usually put. A glossary article cannot settle the fork, and this one does not pretend to. It can insist that the fork be recognised where it occurs, so that two writers who place the public sector nurse in different classes are understood to be applying different theories rather than making rival factual claims about nursing.

## The political behaviour of the intermediate strata

Marx's political writings return obsessively to the intermediate strata because those strata kept deciding events his two class scheme could not settle alone. The French peasantry's support delivered France to Louis Bonaparte. The urban petty bourgeoisie's vacillation, in his narratives, opened and closed the revolutionary possibilities of 1848 in turn. The pattern he derives from the position has three elements, and they recur too often in later politics to be dismissed as period detail. The first element is oscillation. A stratum that owns a little and fears losing it, that employs a few and fears becoming employed, has interests pulling toward both great classes and a programme identical with neither, so its allegiance swings with circumstances: toward the workers when the great bourgeoisie threatens its existence through competition, taxes, or credit, and toward order and property when the workers threaten the little it has. The second element is representation by others. Strata too fragmented to organise a national programme of their own repeatedly find their interests voiced by leaders, parties, and executives who stand outside them, a pattern Marx anatomised in the Bonaparte case and that later analysis of populism, in several traditions, has rediscovered under other names. The third element is intensity disproportionate to size. Because the intermediate strata experience development as a direct threat to a valued and precarious independence, their political mobilisation can be fierce, local, and sudden, flaring around taxation, regulation, rents, and the terms of credit, the precise points where large capital and the state touch the small enterprise.

Later history supplied confirming and complicating evidence in roughly equal measure, and the honest treatment gives both. Small proprietors and farmers have indeed been available for authoritarian and reactionary mobilisations in many countries, and the social base of interwar fascism in parts of Europe included large petty bourgeois elements fearing both big capital and organised labour, which is the classical oscillation resolved rightward under crisis conditions. The same strata have also supplied recruits and strongholds to agrarian populisms, cooperativist movements, and left coalitions, particularly where credit cooperatives and marketing boards converted their grievances against merchants and banks into organisation. The salaried new strata diverge from the old in a way the repairs help to explain. Credentialed professionals in many countries have trended toward cultural liberalism and public sector defence, a pattern the Ehrenreich framework anticipates from the stratum's reproductive employment, while managers and executives align predictably with the organisations that employ them, as Wright's organisational asset would suggest. None of this amounts to a law, and the article's discipline requires saying so. What the positional analysis yields is not a prediction of votes but a map of pressures: which threats a stratum will feel first, which alliances are available to it, and which appeals are likely to find the seam between its fears. Political actors who read the map well have repeatedly mobilised the middle strata more effectively than actors who read only income tables or cultural stereotypes, which is the practical vindication of the positional register, offered with the caution that maps of pressure are not destinies.

The political importance of the petty bourgeoisie in particular is easily understated by a theory whose attention belongs to the great classes, and Marx himself supplies the correction, since his most famous political narrative is substantially a story of intermediate strata. A class need not be the future's protagonist to be the present's pivot. Small proprietors vote, organise locally, staff the lower reaches of parties, and transmit the common sense of whole neighbourhoods; their defection or mobilisation has settled more elections and more street contests than their share of national income would suggest. The salaried strata have added a second pivot in the administrative state and the knowledge economy, since the people who operate the institutions through which policy reaches citizens are themselves a class force with interests in the institutions' expansion and defence. Any politics that treats the middle of the map as a residual, a leftover to be divided between the poles at the end of the analysis, will be surprised by events at regular intervals. The two class model earns its keep as an account of the system's driving relation. It fails, and has always failed, as a complete census of the forces on the field, and Marx's practice as a political journalist, whatever the Manifesto's simplifications, never made that mistake.

## How the salaried middle was made

The new middle class problem has a history, and the history disciplines the theory by showing that the salaried strata were built by identifiable processes rather than secreted by the concept of capital in the abstract. The first process was the growth of the large organisation itself. As firms expanded beyond the scale an owner could supervise personally, layers of management, accounting, engineering, and sales administration became necessary, and the functions of capital were delegated to employees in a hierarchy that reproduced, inside the firm, the shape of a class structure in miniature: direction at the top, routine execution at the bottom, and a graduated middle whose members both gave and took orders. The second process was the expansion of the state, above all in education, health, and welfare, which created vast salaried professions employed out of revenue and oriented to public provision rather than to profit, a fact that matters for every scheme, since Poulantzas's frontier and the Ehrenreichs' reproductive core both draw their largest populations from this expansion. The third process was credentialling itself: the long campaign by which occupations from medicine to accountancy secured legal or customary monopolies over entry, converting skill into a licensed asset and binding their members' fortunes to the institutions that certify them. The fourth was mass higher education, which transformed the credential from a rarity into a normal expectation for the children of the salaried strata and thereby made the strata self reproducing in a recognisably classlike way, with inheritance running through school fees, tutoring, and cultural preparation rather than through deeds and shares.

Seen historically, the three repairs are responses to different phases of that construction. Poulantzas wrote when the state expansion and the political turbulence of salaried employees were the salient facts, and his new petty bourgeoisie is recognisably the stratum the expanding state and the large firm had jointly produced. The Ehrenreichs wrote from inside the American version of the same stratum at the moment its postwar confidence was ending, and their stress on insecurity and reproduction reflects that moment. Wright's programme belongs to a later phase in which the strata were established, internally differentiated, and available for measurement, and in which the urgent question had become empirical: do the locations a theory proposes actually predict the incomes and attitudes they are meant to explain? The sequence is a small history of Marxist class analysis maturing under evidence, and it cuts against the lazy portrait of a doctrine repeating nineteenth century formulas. The formulas changed because the world they classified changed, and the changes were argued about in the open, with named opponents and checkable claims. Whether the changes suffice is the subject of the objection that follows; that they were genuine theoretical work is not in dispute among those who have read them.

The historical view also clarifies the relation between the old and new intermediate strata, which polemics habitually confuse. The new strata did not descend from the old, and in the main they did not replace it person for person. The children of shopkeepers became, in large numbers, teachers, technicians, officials, and managers, so the generations are continuous even where the positions are not, and family memory transmits the culture of independence into salaried lives that no longer contain it. That transmission explains a recurring datum that purely positional accounts stumble over: the persistence of petty bourgeois outlook, the reverence for property, self reliance, and educational striving, among employees who own nothing. Ideology has a longer half life than position, a point Marxist cultural analysis has always granted in principle and class analysis forgets in practice. Wright's attitudinal research found habitually messy correlations between location and outlook, and the mess is intelligible once households, family histories, and the second and third registers of the three middle classes are admitted into the account. Position constrains; it does not ventriloquise. The distinction is the difference between a theory of class that can survive evidence and one that survives by explaining its failures away.

## The objection: a residual bin that swallows the model

The strongest objection to everything in the preceding sections must now be stated at full strength, in the form its most serious critics would choose. The objection is that the proliferation of intermediate categories is an admission of failure wearing the dress of refinement. A theory that divides society into two great classes derives its force from the parsimony of that division: the whole dynamic of exploitation, accumulation, and crisis is meant to flow from a single relation. When the census is taken, however, the two classes of the model turn out to share the society with small proprietors who will not disappear, managers who fit neither pole, professionals whose credentials behave like property, officials, supervisors, contractors, and hybrids of every description, until the intermediate territory rivals the poles in population and exceeds them in variety. Each awkward population receives a category: a new petty bourgeoisie for the salaried, contradictory locations for the managers, a professional managerial class for the reproducers. The critic's charge is that this is not explanation but accommodation, the sociological equivalent of adding an epicycle for every planet that strays. A model that can absorb any observation by minting a category for it has ceased to risk falsification, and a theory that risks nothing explains nothing. Worse, the minting is not governed by a shared rule: Poulantzas, the Ehrenreichs, and Wright sort the same nurse into three different locations by three different decisive facts, and the tradition offers no tribunal to decide among them. What presents itself as a science of class begins to resemble a taxonomy contest, in which the winner is the scheme whose author argues most ingeniously rather than the scheme the evidence selects.

The objection gains strength from the theory's own texts and history. Marx predicted the simplification of class structure as capital developed, and the tradition that claims his authority has spent a century documenting complication instead. A prediction is not honourably saved by redefining its terms until the record fits, and the repairs can be read, unkindly but not absurdly, as exactly that operation performed with great sophistication. The objection also gains strength from the political disappointments attached to the categories. The new petty bourgeoisie did not behave with the uniformity its assignment implied; the professional managerial class fragmented along lines of sector, nation, and generation that the functional definition could not anticipate; contradictory locations predicted attitudes weakly enough that their own author revised the instruments repeatedly. A critic may fairly ask what work the categories do that a plain description of occupations, incomes, and authorities would not do more honestly. Underneath all of these points sits the deepest one. Class for Marx is not one variable among others but the structuring relation of the society, and a structuring relation whose map requires this much patching may simply be the wrong structure: perhaps authority, expertise, and organisational position are independent axes of inequality that no derivation from property will ever unify, and the honest conclusion is pluralism about stratification rather than a repaired monism. That conclusion has distinguished defenders in the Weberian tradition and beyond it, and any reply that does not feel its weight has not understood the objection.

## The reply, and what must be conceded

The reply begins by separating two kinds of category multiplication, because the objection's force depends on running them together. A category is ad hoc when it is introduced for one awkward case, explains nothing beyond that case, and connects to no mechanism in the theory. A category is principled when it is derived from the theory's own dimensions, applied consistently to every case, and made to yield claims that could turn out false. By that standard the serious repairs are not epicycles. Wright's locations are generated by crossing a small set of assets, and every position in an economy is assigned by the same operation, the nurse and the executive by one rule rather than by ingenuity per case. Poulantzas's frontier, whatever its other faults, applies a single distinction, productive and unproductive labour, with supervisory and ideological corollaries stated in advance, and it entailed the risky claim that the salaried strata so defined would display a distinctive politics, a claim evidence could and did test. The Ehrenreichs' category is the most vulnerable of the three to the ad hoc charge, since its functional boundary is elastic, and the reply should concede that vulnerability rather than paper over it: used loosely, the professional managerial class does become a bin. Used with its function specified and its internal conflicts admitted, it remains a claim about a determinate stratum and its characteristic antagonisms, and claims of that shape can be wrong in ways that teach something. The general point survives the concession. A theory may grow categories the way a map grows detail as the survey improves, and the growth is a vice only when the new detail is unconstrained by the theory's principles or untestable in principle.

The historical point also has a reply, though a partial one. The prediction that failed was the disappearance of intermediate positions; the relation the two class model describes, the appropriation of surplus by owners from non owners, was not thereby falsified, because the new strata were absorbed into the wage relation even as their internal differentiation grew. A society in which nearly everyone is an employee is, in the respect the model cares most about, more polarised than the society of 1848, not less, and the repairs are attempts to chart differentiation within the employee condition rather than refutations of the condition itself. That defence is strong for Wright's version, weaker for Poulantzas's, since a scheme that expels most employees from the working class cannot also claim their absorption into wage labour as vindication, and the inconsistency should be marked. On the tribunal problem, the honest reply is that the tribunal exists and is slow: comparative research, of which Wright's programme is the exemplar, tests whether proposed locations predict income, attitudes, and conduct better than rivals, and categories that predict nothing have in fact been abandoned by the tradition, which is why the literature's active vocabulary is narrower than its historical one. Pluralism about stratification remains a live alternative rather than a refuted error. The Marxist claim is not that authority and expertise are unreal, but that they are systematically entangled with property: credentials are distributed by institutions funded from surplus, authority in firms is delegated by owners and revocable by them, and organisational assets exist only inside enterprises someone owns. The entanglement claim can be examined case by case, and where it fails, where a credential or an office commands resources genuinely independent of property, the theory should lose. A reply that specifies its own defeat conditions is not an epicycle. It is the objection's demand, accepted.

What must be conceded, finally, should be conceded without varnish. The number of competing solutions to the new middle class problem is itself evidence that none is satisfactory: three generations of able analysts, working from the same texts and the same censuses, produced three incompatible maps, and a problem with three solutions of that quality usually has none. The classical prediction of disappearance was wrong in the form in which it was made, and the tradition does itself no credit when it redescribes the failure as a tendency still in transit after a century and three quarters. And the everyday success of the vocabulary, petty bourgeois and PMC alike circulating as insults among people innocent of the definitions, is evidence that the categories answer a polemical need more reliably than an analytical one in their public life. None of these concessions dissolves the subject. The positions are real, the sorting matters for explanation and for politics, and the choice is not between the repaired maps and a pristine two class purity that the record refuted long ago. The choice is among imperfect instruments, used with their limitations announced. That is the spirit in which this article has presented them, and it is the only spirit in which class analysis deserves to be read.

## The income band and the cultural formation, taken seriously

Nothing in the positional argument licenses contempt for the other two registers, and a section that takes each seriously will protect readers against a vulgar Marxism as narrowing as the confusions it corrects. The income band is the register of material security. Where a household sits in the distribution decides whether illness is an inconvenience or a catastrophe, whether a child can be supported through a long education, whether old age is a prospect or a dread, and those facts are class facts in the plain human sense whatever vocabulary a theory prefers. Political argument addressed to the squeezed middle speaks to real squeezing, and analysts who reply that the squeezed are positionally heterogeneous have answered a question nobody asked. The band also has a dynamics of its own: median incomes can stagnate while positional structure transforms, or rise while security collapses through the withdrawal of collective provision, and a complete social description tracks the band alongside the map. What the band cannot do is explain the mechanisms that move it. Incomes are outcomes of bargaining power, property, credentials, and state policy interacting, which is to say outcomes of the positional structure among other things, and explanation runs from positions to bands more fruitfully than the reverse. The registers are complements in a division of labour, and the three middle classes claim is a plea for the division, not a coup by one register against the others.

The cultural formation deserves the same respect, together with one warning. As a description of how people live and recognise one another, middle class culture is a genuine object of study: the educational strategies by which salaried and proprietor families transmit advantage, the tastes that mark belonging, the moral vocabulary of respectability, effort, and independence that descends, as the historical section noted, from the old petty bourgeoisie into the salaried strata that inherited its children. Cultural analysis explains identification, and identification is politically real: the worker who identifies as middle class and the manager who identifies as working class both act on their identifications, and a politics that ignores identity in the name of position will be perpetually astonished. The warning concerns direction of explanation. Cultural portraits of the petty bourgeoisie, in Marx and after him, constantly tempt writers to treat outlook as the essence and position as its illustration, reversing the theory's causal claim. The theory's claim is austere: a stratum placed between ownership and labour, dependent on a small property it must work and defend, develops characteristic anxieties and ambitions because of the placement, and would develop them in any costume. The claim may be wrong; the evidence of outlook surviving position, examined above, shows it is at least incomplete. But the claim is the theory, and cultural description that floats free of it is journalism, valuable as journalism and mislabelled as class analysis. The boundary is worth patrolling in both directions, against economists who think culture is decoration and against cultural critics who think position is a metaphor.

One further register occasionally intrudes and should be refused a seat. Political speech sometimes uses middle class to mean the virtuous public, the taxpayers, the ordinary families in whose name a speaker claims to act, a purely rhetorical sense with no descriptive content at all. The three middle classes are at least answerable to evidence. The rhetorical middle is answerable to nothing, expanding to include every listener and contracting to exclude every opponent, and its function is to borrow the respectability of the other senses for conclusions none of them supports. Readers trained by this article to ask which sense is in play will find the rhetorical sense easy to spot, since it is the one whose boundaries shift mid sentence. The correct response is not pedantry but a simple request: name the band, the culture, or the position, and proceed. Arguments that survive the request were probably sound. Arguments that cannot were probably the point of the exercise.

## Credit, rent, and the quiet subordinations of small property

The classical image of the petty proprietor suggests independence, and the reality, in Marx's own analysis and in the record since, is a web of dependence running through credit and rent. The smallholding peasant of the French writings is enmeshed with the mortgage holder and the usurer, so that a share of the holding's surplus is drained away as interest before any question of profit arises, and Marx treats capital in its usurious and mortgage forms as a power over small production that can dominate without organising production at all. The shopkeeper's landlord stands in an analogous relation, with commercial rent absorbing location value the tenant's labour helped to create, and the periodic renegotiation of the lease functioning as a recurring expropriation point in the small firm's life. The artisan supplying a merchant, the farmer contracted to a processor, the franchisee paying royalties on turnover: the forms multiply, and the pattern is one, the extraction of a share of the petty producer's surplus by owners of assets the producer must access, money, premises, market, brand. The pattern matters for classification because it disciplines the word independence. Petty producers are independent in the labour process, where they direct their own work, and dependent in circulation, where the terms of sale, finance, and occupancy are set by larger capitals. Both halves belong in the description, and schemes that record only the first half flatter the position, while schemes that record only the second dissolve it into disguised wage labour too eagerly.

The political consequences of the credit relation deserve the emphasis Marx gave them. Indebtedness is the daily experience through which abstract structures reach the small proprietor: the bank's terms, the supplier's prices, the landlord's increase, the tax demand arriving in a bad season. Grievance against those pressure points explains the characteristic targets of petty bourgeois politics, which are as often finance and monopoly as they are labour, and which can therefore be recruited by movements against banks and chains as readily as by movements against unions. Analysts who expect the stratum's politics to track a single axis, friend or enemy of the working class, will misread episode after episode. The stratum's structural situation supplies grievances in several directions at once, and which grievance organises its politics in a given conjuncture depends on which pressure is sharpest and which movement is present to name it. That is the durable insight inside the oscillation thesis, and it survives the failure of the disappearance prediction intact. A class can persist, mutate, and still be understood through the relations that define it, provided the analyst follows the surplus: who produces it in the small enterprise, and through which channels, rent, interest, royalty, and unequal exchange, the larger economy draws its share away. Following the surplus is the method of the whole series in miniature, and the intermediate strata reward it exactly because their subordination is real but partial, inscribed in flows rather than announced in titles.

## Correct usage: a summary for writers

The closing summary gathers the article's rules into a form a writer can apply at the desk. Say petty bourgeoisie only of those who own means of production and work them: small proprietors, artisans, peasant proprietors, independent tradespeople, and remember that petite measures the property and not the person's worth. Say small capitalist where the owner has been released from necessary personal labour and lives from an enterprise worked by others. Say working class, with whatever layer distinctions the argument needs, of those who own nothing productive and sell their labour power, including the highly paid, the credentialed, and the secure, unless the scheme in use is announced and its different verdict defended. For the salaried intermediate strata, name the scheme: Poulantzas's new petty bourgeoisie, the Ehrenreichs' professional managerial class, or Wright's contradictory locations, and accept the obligations of the scheme chosen, its decisive fact and its known weaknesses. Keep the three middle classes apart: income band for distribution, cultural formation for identity and lifestyle, position for the analysis of production and power, and mark every crossing between them. And when a term arrives as an insult, PMC in an acronym or petty bourgeois in a sneer, ask for its definition before granting it a hearing, since the request alone dissolves most of the usage it will meet.

Readers assembling notes across the class cluster will find the distinctions easier to hold if each scheme is kept with its decisive fact and its test cases attached, the kind of organised note making that [VaultBook's study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) are built for, with the intermediate positions table preserved beside the definitions it arbitrates. The cluster's other articles supply the surrounding structure: the criterion in the class guide, the boundary census in the working class article, and Wright's research programme in its own exposition, each linked from the sections above. What this article adds, and what its readers should carry away, is a habit rather than a verdict: the habit of asking, whenever the middle of society is invoked, which middle is meant, by what criterion it is bounded, and what the speaker gains by leaving the question unasked. The petty bourgeoisie has survived a century and three quarters of predicted disappearance, theoretical repair, and casual abuse, and it survives because the position it names keeps being recreated by economies that generate small property and dependent labour in the same motion. A vocabulary adequate to that persistence is not a luxury for specialists. It is the minimum equipment for arguing about the society that exists.

## Education as the sorting machine of the new middle strata

If property was the inheritance mechanism of the old middle class, education is the inheritance mechanism of the new, and following its operation explains features of the salaried strata that purely workplace analysis misses. The old petty proprietor transmitted position by transmitting the shop, the holding, or the workshop, together with the skills practised inside them. The salaried professional transmits position by transmitting the capacity to acquire credentials: the fees where education is purchased, the tutoring and examination preparation where it is nominally free, the cultural equipment of books, accents, and expectations that schools reward, and the knowledge of which qualifications carry scarcity value and which have been inflated into mere entry tickets. The transmission is less secure than the deed to a shop, since the child must still perform in examinations and the credential must still hold its value against expansion, and that insecurity is written into the stratum's politics and psychology. Educational expansion therefore cuts both ways for the new middle strata. Each widening of access fulfils the stratum's professed values and threatens the scarcity on which its premiums rest, and the resulting ambivalence, public enthusiasm for opportunity joined to private ferocity in defence of advantage, is among the best documented regularities in the sociology of these classes. A class analysis that ignored the school would miss the machine by which the stratum reproduces itself, which is why the Ehrenreichs placed teachers at the reproductive core and why credential assets in Wright's scheme behave so persistently in his data.

The sorting machine also governs the boundary traffic between the strata and their neighbours, and the traffic runs in both directions. Downward mobility for the children of professionals arrives as credential failure, the degree not completed or the profession not entered, and it is experienced as a fall precisely because the family position was held in the form of expectations rather than property that could cushion the descent. Upward mobility for the children of workers and small proprietors runs through the same gates, and the scholarship boy and girl of an older literature are the ancestors of the first generation professional whose class location the schemes must classify without reference to origin. Location is present tense: the first generation consultant and the third generation consultant occupy the same cell, although their security, their networks, and their relation to the culture of the stratum differ in ways that attitudinal research keeps detecting. Schemes classify positions; biographies distribute people among positions with different equipment for holding them. Confusing the two levels produces the familiar puzzle of the professional who feels like an impostor and the proprietor's child who feels born to the salaried world, and the puzzle dissolves when position, origin, and identity are kept as separate entries in the account, which is, once more, the three registers discipline applied within a single life.

There is a consequence for the disappearance debate that this perspective makes visible. The old petty bourgeoisie reproduced through property and was therefore vulnerable to the concentration of property; ruin the small holding and the class position died with it. The new strata reproduce through institutions, schools, licensing bodies, and employing organisations, which no concentration of industrial capital dissolves, and which expand with the complexity of the economy itself. A stratum whose reproduction is institutional rather than proprietary cannot be ground down between the millstones in the manner the Manifesto imagined for the artisan, because its millstones are also its parents. That is a structural reason, deeper than the mutation of self employment, why the middle of the map repopulated: the economy that destroyed independent production simultaneously built the credentialled and managerial apparatus that staffed its own coordination. Whether one calls that apparatus a class, a set of contradictory locations, or a layer of the wage earning population, its mode of reproduction guarantees its persistence in a way the workshop's mode never could, and any future version of the disappearance thesis will have to reckon with schools and licences as it once reckoned with looms.

## How the schemes have been tested

Class schemes are usually debated as philosophies, and it is a merit of the later literature that some of them were treated as instruments and tested. The tests take three forms. Income tests ask whether the locations a scheme proposes actually sort people into the earnings bands the scheme implies, so that contradictory locations fall between the poles they contradict and credential assets command the premiums the theory of skill exploitation predicts. Attitude tests ask whether locations predict opinions and identifications, on redistribution, on workplace authority, on the legitimacy of unions and owners, in the directions the theory of divided interests suggests. Behavioural tests, the hardest to run, ask whether locations predict conduct where it costs something: voting in polarised contests, striking or refusing to strike, crossing or honouring picket lines, joining the organisations of one class or the other. Wright's comparative project is the standing example of the first two forms pursued systematically across countries, and its results were mixed in an informative pattern: the locations sorted incomes robustly, sorted attitudes genuinely but modestly, and sorted them best precisely where the theory of contradictory interests said the divisions should bite, among managers whose attitudes fell between those of owners and workers on the questions that divide owners from workers. A scheme whose central novelty survives partial testing in that pattern has earned more than dismissal and less than triumph, which is the correct epistemic temperature for most of social science.

The tests also expose what the schemes cannot do, and the exposure is useful. None of the three schemes predicts individual conduct, and none was built to: class locations are claims about the pressures on averages of thousands, and the manager who devotes a life to union organising and the proprietor who gives the shop to its workers are not refutations but reminders of the level at which the claims operate. Nor can the schemes settle their own boundary disputes empirically in every case, because the measurements inherit the definitions: a survey that classifies the public sector nurse by Poulantzas's rule and finds distinctive attitudes has confirmed the rule's usefulness, not its truth against Wright's rival placement, which the same data can be made to serve under its own classifications. The tribunal of research, invoked in the reply to the objection, is therefore real but slow and partial, better at eliminating schemes that predict nothing than at crowning one among schemes that predict differently. Traditions outside Marxism live under the same conditions, and the class schemes of mainstream sociology, which sort by occupation and employment status into their own contested maps, face the tribunal with no clearer verdicts. The comparison is worth making because the objection presented earlier sometimes proceeds as though taxonomic untidiness were a Marxist peculiarity. It is the common condition of class analysis in complex societies, and the Marxist schemes are unusual mainly in stating their decisive facts openly enough to be argued with, which returns the discussion to this article's refrain: an instrument whose governing criterion is visible can be criticised, repaired, or replaced, while an instrument whose criterion is hidden in common sense merely reproduces the confusions of the three middle classes in academic dress.

## The service economy and the changing composition of the middle

The growth of services altered the intermediate strata from within, and attending to the alteration prevents a common anachronism: reasoning about the middle of the map from manufacturing cases in an economy where most employment serves, cares, teaches, codes, sells, and administers. Services divide in a way that reproduces the class problem at a finer grain. Some services are organised as classical petty production, the independent plumber, the family restaurant, the corner repair shop, where ownership and labour unite in the old form. Some are organised as mass wage labour with thin margins and close supervision, the call centre and the warehouse, where the positional verdict is plain. Between them lies the characteristic service middle: the employed professional whose work is relational and judgement laden, the clinician, the teacher, the social worker, the designer, whose output resists the measurement and pacing applied to the warehouse and whose employers purchase precisely the discretion that resists it. That purchased discretion is an economic fact before it is a cultural one. It rests on credentials that certify judgement, on clients who cannot evaluate the service without trusting the provider, and on tasks whose standardisation would destroy the value being bought. The stratum's autonomy at work, so often described as a middle class lifestyle trait, is grounded in this structure of the labour process, and its defence of professional autonomy is a defence of the conditions under which its labour power commands its price. Poulantzas could not see the point in these terms because his frontier ran elsewhere; the Ehrenreichs saw it as reproduction; Wright's skill assets capture its bargaining core. The case is a fair test of the schemes, and the honest result is that each captures a facet, which is the state of the art this article has reported throughout.

Services also changed the relationship between the middle strata and the working class beneath them, in both directions. Personal services create daily contact across the class boundary, the professional treating, teaching, and advising working class clients, a contact that can generate solidarity or friction depending on whether the encounter is structured as service or as supervision. The Ehrenreich analysis was built on that ambiguity, and it remains the sharpest account of why encounters with the professional middle so often carry a charge that encounters with distant owners do not: the owner is an abstraction, the administrator is a face. At the same time, the expansion of routine service employment enlarged the working class in forms whose conditions resemble the insecurities once assigned to the petty strata, irregular hours, income volatility, dependence on customer goodwill, without any property to show for them. The boundary populations of an economy are always where its categories strain, and the service economy manufactures boundary populations industrially: the employed therapist with a private sideline practice, the retail manager with a market stall, the teacher selling courses through a platform in the evenings. Each hybrid is a small argument for Wright's dimensional patience over any single frontier, and a reminder that the map's job is to locate pressures, not to issue everyone a single identity card.

## Small proprietors and the authoritarian temptation, examined carefully

No part of this subject attracts more careless writing than the association between the petty bourgeoisie and authoritarian politics, and a glossary article owes its readers the careful version. The careless version runs in two directions. One treats the small proprietor as a born authoritarian, reading a politics off a position as though fear of descent mechanically produced a demand for a strongman. The other, embarrassed by the first, denies the association altogether and treats every inquiry into it as prejudice against small business. The evidence supports neither. It supports a conditional claim: strata whose position is squeezed between large capital and organised labour become available for authoritarian mobilisation under specifiable conditions, when the squeeze is acute, when established parties have failed to answer their grievances, when an authoritarian movement addresses those grievances by name, against banks, chains, bureaucrats, or foreigners, and when the labour movement appears as a threat to property rather than as a shield against the squeeze. Where the conditions fail, the same strata have sustained democratic, cooperative, and even revolutionary movements, and the comparative record contains both histories in abundance. Marx's French analysis, so often quoted as though it convicted the peasantry of a permanent politics, in fact models the conditional form: the smallholders' Bonapartism is explained by their isolation, their debts, their tax burdens, and the absence of any organisation of their own, a set of circumstances, not an essence.

The salaried strata complicate the pattern in ways the repairs help to organise. The authoritarian movements of the interwar period drew not only on small proprietors but on officials, teachers, and white collar employees whose status anxieties and resentments a purely proprietary analysis cannot reach, which was one historical pressure behind the later invention of categories for the salaried middle. The lesson for classification is that political availability is a property of strata in conjunctures, not a deduction from locations, and any scheme that promises to read votes off positions directly will be refuted by the next election. What positions supply is a field of pressures and resentments; what conjunctures supply is the organisation that collects them. The distinction protects the theory from its friends as much as from its enemies, since the friend who treats the petty bourgeoisie as a permanent reactionary bloc makes the same error as the enemy who treats it as a prejudice, and both errors are dissolved by the conditional form. Analysis earns its authority by specifying conditions, and on this subject the specification is the whole of the wisdom available.

## Petty production beyond the advanced economies

The categories of this article were formed on European material, and their travel to the rest of the world is a further test of the criterion over the occupational list. In economies where wage employment has never become the majority condition, the independent producer is not an intermediate residue but a central figure: the peasant proprietor, the market trader, the artisan, the transport owner operator, and the vast informal sector of own account workers who own tools, stalls, and vehicles of every description. Applying the positional criterion in such settings classifies by ownership and labour as usual, and it immediately reveals the scale problem in the disappearance framework, since the predicted transition to a society of owners and wage workers describes, at most, one region's trajectory generalised without warrant. Development practice spent decades rediscovering the point under the heading of informality, classifying by registration and firm size while the producers in question were sorted, by any positional analysis, into petty commodity production subordinated to merchants, moneylenders, and platforms in patterns Marx's treatment of formal subsumption anticipates with uncomfortable accuracy. The criterion travels; the timetable does not, and the distinction between a portable criterion and a parochial timetable is the same distinction this article drew within the European record.

The salaried strata travel differently, since states in developing economies are often the principal employers of the credentialed, and the new middle class problem arrives there wearing the uniform of the civil service, the public university, and the state enterprise. Poulantzas wrote with such formations partly in view, and his assignment of state employees has been debated most fiercely where the state is the commanding employer. The professional strata in such settings can be at once the modernising elite of nationalist imagination, the clientele of the ruling party, and the sharpest critics of its failures, a combination the contradictory locations vocabulary handles with its usual patience. For the glossary's purposes the point is modest and general. The three middle classes confusion is not a European local difficulty: income bands, cultural formations, and positions diverge in every society that has all three, and arguments that import classifications across contexts without re-examining the underlying ownership and authority relations reproduce the confusion at a larger scale. Precision is a portable discipline even where the map must be redrawn, and redrawing it by the criterion, case by case, is the work the framework exists to make possible.

## How censuses and surveys draw the middle

Between the theory and the reader stand the official classifications, and a short account of how they work explains why public numbers about the middle class so rarely answer theoretical questions. Statistical agencies classify workers by occupation and by employment status, employee, employer, or own account worker, categories built for administrative use and revised at intervals as economies change. The employment status trichotomy is the closest official cousin of the positional criterion, and its own account category is a rough census of petty production, but it is rough in instructive ways. It counts the incorporated consultant and the street trader in one column, misses the unwaged family labour inside the family firm, and records the franchise operator and the platform driver by their legal form rather than by their control over price and market. Sociological class schemes built for research, of which the British tradition of occupational classifications is the most elaborate, refine the occupational side into grades of employment relation, distinguishing service relationships, with salary, autonomy, and career prospects, from labour contracts, with wages, supervision, and insecurity. That distinction is a genuine insight purchased from the same phenomena the Marxist repairs address: the service relationship is the administrative shadow of the credentialled and managerial middle, and its presence in mainstream schemes is evidence that the new middle class problem is a problem of the world and not of one doctrine. None of these instruments is wrong. Each measures what it was built to measure, and the recurrent public confusion is to read them as censuses of Marxist classes or of income bands according to the needs of the headline. A reader equipped with the three registers can place each instrument where it belongs: employment status approximates position with legal noise, occupational grades approximate the employment relation, income tables measure the band, and identification surveys measure the culture. The map of the middle that public discussion actually uses is a composite of all four, assembled without a key, and supplying the key is a service no single statistic can perform for itself.

The measurement discussion also returns a dividend to the theory, because it shows what a positional census would require and why none exists in pure form. To count classes as Wright defines them, a survey must ask about ownership, about authority over others and over budgets, and about credentials, of the same respondents, and then classify households whose members occupy different locations. Such instruments have been built for research and their results inform the testing section above, but no state runs one, since states need administrable categories rather than theoretical ones. The absence is worth a moment's reflection. Public argument about class proceeds almost entirely on proxies, and the choice of proxy quietly decides the conclusion: use income and the middle is vast and squeezed; use identification and it is vaster and contented; use employment status and the petty strata shrink to a rump; use credentials and the new middle swells. Writers who announce findings about the middle class without naming their instrument are asking readers to trust a composite assembled in private. The demand this article has made throughout, name the register, applies with equal force to numbers: name the instrument, and let the reader judge what its reading is evidence about. Journalists inherit most of their class figures from such instruments at second or third hand, stripped of the definitions that produced them, and the figures harden in transmission into facts about a middle class that no instrument ever counted. Restoring the definition to the figure, in a clause or a parenthesis, costs a sentence and buys the reader the ability to disagree intelligently, which is the most that honest numbers can offer and considerably more than the composite middle of public discussion provides.

## A note on scale: the very small, the middling, and the almost large

Scale deserves a final precision, because the vocabulary of smallness hides three different conditions. At the bottom stands the own account worker without employees, the purest petty producer, whose position is the most exposed and whose income often falls below the wages of the employed, a fact that embarrasses every income based reading of the term middle. The condition is nonetheless distinct from wage labour in the respects the criterion tracks: the producer owns the tools, chooses the work within the market's constraints, and pockets or loses the whole proceeds, so that risk, autonomy, and appropriation sit in one pair of hands. In the centre stands the small employer, whose position the boundary discussion above resolves by function: still working, still necessary to the enterprise, the proprietor belongs to the petty stratum even as a workforce gathers, and the gathering itself creates the daily experience of class from both sides, employing in the morning and labouring in the afternoon, which no other position in the economy quite reproduces. At the top stands the almost large, the firm whose owner has withdrawn from production but whose scale remains provincial, a capitalist by every functional test whose social world, clubs, schools, and politics, may still be that of the local petty bourgeoisie. Cultural registers file the third figure with the second; the positional register separates them absolutely, and the separation is not a quibble, because their interests diverge exactly where it matters: the working proprietor gains from cheaper credit and lower rents in the currency of survival, the withdrawn owner from the appreciation of an asset that can be sold. Insolvency statistics, succession disputes, and the politics of small business taxation all turn on the difference, and a vocabulary that cannot state it cannot describe the constituency that lobbies under the name of small business, which is a coalition of all three conditions pretending, for political purposes, to be one. Naming the pretence is not hostility to the coalition. It is the precondition for understanding its internal quarrels, which surface whenever a policy benefits owners as asset holders at the expense of proprietors as workers, or the reverse, and which commentators register as inconsistency only because their category has no joints where the strain appears.

## What the middle strata mean for the two class model

A synthesis is owed, because a glossary article that leaves its subject in fragments has failed its readers. The two class model and the crowded middle are not rivals in the way polemic supposes. The model is a claim about the engine of the system: that the production and appropriation of surplus by owners of the means of production, from workers who must sell their labour power, is the relation that drives accumulation, shapes the state, and sets the terms on which every other position exists. The crowded middle is a claim about the census: that between and around the poles stand positions, petty proprietors old and new, managers, professionals, officials, hybrids, whose conduct cannot be read off the engine diagram and whose weight in events is out of proportion to their place in the theory of value. Marx's own practice held both claims at once, the Manifesto's polarisation alongside the French histories' teeming intermediate world, and the tension between them is not a scandal to be confessed but the theory's working condition. Trouble begins only when either claim is inflated into the other: when the census is denied in the engine's name, producing a politics perpetually surprised by its allies and enemies, or when the census is used to dissolve the engine, producing a pluralism that can describe every stratum and explain the motion of none. The repairs examined here are the tradition's attempts to hold both claims with the joints showing, and their imperfections are preferable, on the evidence assembled, to either inflation.

For the general reader, the yield of the whole inquiry can be carried in a few sentences. The petty bourgeoisie is a real position, defined by small property personally worked, predicted to vanish, and instead transformed, persisting in dependent and platform mediated forms its theorist half foresaw. The new middle strata are a real population, defined by salary, authority, and credentials rather than by property, and the three schemes for placing them are instruments with named decisive facts and known failure modes, to be chosen by the question being asked rather than by allegiance. Middle class is three phrases wearing one coat, and the first move in any argument that uses it is to say which phrase is speaking. Armed with those sentences, a reader can follow public argument about class with the confusions stripped out, which was the promise of the glossary framework and the purpose of this article. The terms will go on being abused; terms that sort power usually are. They will also go on being needed, because the positions they name go on being recreated, and a reader who can use them precisely owns a piece of equipment that no amount of acronym shouting can supply.

## For students and exam candidates

The standard examination treatment of this topic asks how Marxism handles the middle class, and the table in this article is the answer's skeleton. Open by separating the three meanings of middle class, since markers reward the separation immediately. Define the petty bourgeoisie by its double character, ownership plus personal labour, and note the disappearance prediction with its divided record. Then present the new middle class problem and at least two of the three repairs, Poulantzas and Wright being the most examinable pair, with the decisive fact of each stated in one sentence. Close by evaluating: the proliferation objection, the reply that the serious schemes are principled rather than ad hoc, and the concession that three incompatible solutions signal an unsolved problem. Answers that define by income, or that treat petty as an insult, lose the marks this structure gains.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The income versus position confusion is the misconception to surface first, and the reliable instrument is a sorting exercise. Give the class eight brief cases, a shopkeeper, a salaried surgeon, a platform driver with a financed car, a franchise operator, a professor, a foreman, a landlord with one flat, and a freelance designer, and have students classify each twice, once by income and cultural markers and once by the positional criterion, before any theory is taught. The disagreements between the two sorts generate the lesson: students discover that their arguments were cross register, and the three repairs can then be introduced as attempts to handle the cases the positional sort left strained, the surgeon and the foreman above all. Set Poulantzas against Wright on the same case rather than in separate summaries, since the schemes reveal themselves in collision, and finish with the acronym exercise: a paragraph of polemic using PMC as an insult, rewritten by the class into an argument with the definition restored. Students who can perform that rewrite have understood the article.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

This vocabulary is used constantly in commentary and almost always loosely, and the working rules for professional use are strict but simple. In copy, announce the register: income band, cultural formation, or position, and do not let a statistic from one register carry a conclusion in another. Treat the professional managerial class as a term with an origin, the Ehrenreichs' functional argument of 1977, and either use it with that definition in view or expect informed readers to notice its absence; the acronym deployed without the argument is a marker of polemic, and editors should treat it as such. For historians, the intermediate strata are the pivot of the French narratives and of most real politics since: follow the surplus through rent, interest, and royalties before assigning the small proprietor an independence the flows contradict, and remember that households, not titled individuals, are the units that occupy petty positions. For researchers, Wright's testing programme remains the methodological benchmark, and its mixed results are the honest state of the field. In every genre, the question that disciplines the prose is the one this article has repeated: which middle, bounded by what criterion, on what evidence?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the petty bourgeoisie?

The petty bourgeoisie is the class of small proprietors who own means of production and work them themselves: artisans with workshops, peasant proprietors on family holdings, shopkeepers, and small masters employing a few hands while still labouring alongside them. The definition is double, ownership plus personal labour, and each half excludes a neighbour. Ownership without personal labour makes a capitalist, once the enterprise runs under others and the owner lives from its surplus. Personal labour without ownership makes a wage worker, whatever the pay. Petite in the original French measures the small scale of the property, not the worth of the person, and the English word petty misleads readers who hear contempt in a classificatory term.

### Q: What does middle class mean in Marxist terms?

In Marxist terms middle class does not name an income band, a lifestyle, or the statistical middle of a distribution. It names intermediate positions in relation to production, the territory between the class that owns the means of production and lives from ownership and the class that owns nothing productive and lives by selling labour power. The classical occupant of that territory is the petty bourgeoisie. The harder occupants are salaried managers and credentialed professionals, who own nothing yet exercise authority or hold scarce certified skills, and whose placement required the later schemes of Poulantzas, the Ehrenreichs, and Wright. This article calls the confusion among the income, cultural, and positional senses the three middle classes.

### Q: Is a small business owner working class or capitalist?

It depends on the owner's relation to the work, not on the size of the turnover or the number of employees alone. Where the owner must labour in the business for it to function, keeping the books at night, serving customers, driving the van, the position is petty bourgeois, the intermediate case the question usually has in mind. Where the business runs under managers and the owner lives from its surplus while performing only the functions of ownership, the position is capitalist, however modest the firm. And an owner in name only, whose prices, customers, and methods are dictated by a platform or franchisor, may stand closer to dependent labour than to either proprietor or capitalist. The criterion asks who works, who owns, and who decides.

### Q: Did Marx predict the petty bourgeoisie would disappear?

Yes, as a tendency of capitalist development. The Manifesto expects competition and large scale production to grind the small manufacturer, shopkeeper, artisan, and peasant between the two great classes, throwing a few upward into the bourgeoisie and the mass downward into the proletariat. The record divides into three parts. In manufacturing and in the aggregate share of self employment the decline was real and large. In the strong form, disappearance and polarisation, the prediction failed: self employment stabilised and mutated into freelance, contract, franchise, and platform forms, while salaried intermediate strata grew beyond anything the prediction allowed. The precise verdict is transformation misdescribed as extinction, and honest treatments keep the prediction and the record as separate items.

### Q: What is the professional managerial class?

The professional managerial class is the category formulated by Barbara Ehrenreich and John Ehrenreich in an essay of 1977 for salaried mental workers who own no means of production and whose major function is the reproduction of capitalist culture and class relations. Teachers, social workers, engineers, managers, administrators, and media workers are its core examples: employees, yet set against the working class by their work of directing, instructing, and supervising others, and against capital by their dependence on salaries and their professional standards. The essay was self criticism from inside the stratum, not a sneer at it, and the acronym PMC, under which the term later travelled as an insult, shed the functional definition that gave the category its content.

### Q: Are managers part of the capitalist class?

Not by the ownership criterion, since managers as such own no productive capital and can be dismissed, and not simply workers either, since they exercise capital's functions of direction, discipline, and allocation. The schemes divide on the case. Poulantzas assigns supervisory employees to a new petty bourgeoisie by their function and their place in the labour process. Wright places managers in contradictory class locations, employees on the property dimension and aligned with owners on the organisational dimension. Senior executives who allocate capital and hold substantial share based pay stand nearest the bourgeoisie in function and reward, while the foreman stands nearest the shop floor. The graded answer is the informative one: managers occupy the boundary, and the interesting question is which dimension of their position governs in a given dispute.

### Q: What is the new petty bourgeoisie?

The new petty bourgeoisie is Poulantzas's category, set out in the mid 1970s, for the salaried intermediate strata of advanced capitalism: commercial and administrative employees, supervisors, technicians, and most state employees, whom he assigned outside the working class. The assignment rests on the distinction between productive and unproductive labour, since wage labour that does not produce surplus value falls, on his scheme, outside the proletariat however it is paid, reinforced by the political criterion of supervisory authority and by the ideological traits he held the stratum to share with the old petty bourgeoisie. The category explains the strata's alignment with capital and their distinctive politics, at the price of a narrow working class and a frontier built on a contested distinction.

### Q: Are doctors and lawyers petty bourgeois?

Occupation alone cannot answer, because the classification tracks the structure of practice rather than the diploma. A doctor who owns a practice and employs staff is a small proprietor for the hours that ownership governs, and a lawyer who is an equity partner shares in a firm's surplus in a proprietor's way. The salaried hospital consultant and the employed solicitor, by contrast, sell labour power, at a price raised by credential scarcity, and the strict criterion places them among wage earners, with Wright's contradictory locations available for those who find the placement incomplete. The same career can cross the boundary several times, from employee to partner to owner, which is why the question dissolves once positions rather than professions are classified.

### Q: Why does the everyday meaning of middle class differ from the Marxist one?

Because the two usages answer different questions that share a phrase. Everyday speech sorts people by income, security, education, and style of life, producing a broad middle between rich and poor that most people in prosperous societies claim to inhabit. Marxist analysis sorts people by their relation to production, what they own, whose labour they direct, and on what terms they must work, producing a positional map that cuts across the income order at every point: rich employees, poor proprietors, powerful managers who own nothing. Neither usage is a corruption of the other. The damage begins when a finding from one is spent in an argument about the other, a cultural majority invoked against a positional claim, or an income table offered as a verdict on class power.

### Q: Is the petty bourgeoisie politically important?

Yes, out of proportion to its size, and Marx's own political writings are the first evidence, since the French narratives turn on the peasantry's support for Bonaparte and the urban petty bourgeoisie's vacillation in revolution. The importance has structural sources. The stratum is locally rooted, organisationally present in every town, and electorally pivotal between the great classes. Its grievances arrive through many channels at once, rent, interest, taxation, and competition, so it can be mobilised in several directions, and its fear of descent gives its politics an intensity that larger and safer classes rarely match. A stratum that cannot rule in its own programme can still decide who rules, which is the form its importance usually takes.

### Q: How does a franchise owner complicate the petty bourgeois category?

The franchise operator satisfies half the classical definition splendidly and violates the other half in spirit. The operator owns or leases equipment, hires and directs staff, bears local risk, and works in the business, which are the marks of the small proprietor. Yet the franchisor fixes the product, constrains prices, dictates suppliers and methods, inspects for compliance, and takes royalties on turnover, so the operator's independence stops at the boundary of the brand's system. The case forces the analyst to specify which incidents of ownership matter, control of the labour process on one side, control of market access and the surplus on the other, and it is best described as petty proprietorship under formal subordination to large capital, a genuinely double position rather than a puzzle with a hidden single answer.

### Q: What should a teacher separate first when students use middle class loosely?

The teacher should separate the senses before correcting any single claim, because most loose usage is not wrong within its own register but confused across registers. The first separation is between income and position: ask whether the sentence under discussion is about how much a household earns or about what its members own and direct. The second is between identity and location: what people call themselves is evidence about identification, not about structure. A sorting exercise makes both separations tangible, since students classifying a surgeon and a shopkeeper twice, once by income and once by the positional criterion, discover the divergence themselves. Only after the registers are apart do the schemes of Poulantzas, the Ehrenreichs, and Wright have anything to attach to.

### Q: Why can professionals sell expertise without becoming petty bourgeois in the strict sense?

Because the strict sense requires ownership of means of production that are worked by their owner, and expertise is not such a means. A credential cannot be sold, rented out, or bequeathed, yields income only while its holder works, and appropriates no surplus from the labour of others. What the professional sells is labour power of a scarce and certified kind, commanding a premium closer to a rent on restricted entry than to a profit on capital. The employer, a hospital, a firm, a university, still owns the organisation within which the expertise is exercised and appropriates its surplus. Scarcity raises the price of the commodity the professional sells; it does not change what the commodity is, and the classification follows the commodity.

### Q: How does the professional managerial class thesis differ from Wright's contradictory locations?

The two schemes differ in their decisive fact and in their geometry. The Ehrenreichs define their class by function: salaried mental workers whose work reproduces capitalist culture and class relations form a distinct class with its own interests, set coherently against owners above and workers below. Wright refuses a single decisive fact, building locations from several assets, property, organisational control, and credentials, and allowing a position to fall on opposed sides of different dimensions at once, so the manager is a contradictory location rather than a member of a third class. The practical difference appears in hard cases. The Ehrenreich scheme gathers the teacher and the executive into one formation by their reproductive role; Wright's scheme separates and grades them by the assets each actually holds.

### Q: What makes the petty bourgeoisie swing politically in Marx's historical writings?

The swing is derived from the position, not from fickleness of character. The small proprietor employs labour and fears the claims of the employed; owns property and fears the competition, credit power, and taxation of the great owners; and holds too little to be safe from descent in either direction. Circumstances decide which fear governs. When large capital presses hardest, through monopolies, banks, or rents, the stratum leans toward the workers and their movements. When the workers press hardest, through wages, hours, or the threat to property itself, it leans toward order. Fragmentation completes the pattern, since a stratum that cannot organise a national programme of its own repeatedly accepts representation by leaders standing above it, as the French peasantry accepted Bonaparte.
