The German Romeo and Juliet that German readers, actors, and schoolchildren have quoted for more than two centuries is the work of one hand, and the hand does not belong to the man whose name stands second on its spine. August Wilhelm Schlegel translated the play himself. He printed it first in Schiller's journal Die Horen in 1796 and issued it in book form in 1797 as the opening play of his Shakspeare's dramatische Werke, published by Johann Friedrich Unger in Berlin. Ludwig Tieck, whose name the finished German Shakespeare would carry beside Schlegel's, translated none of this play. The double name is the brand of a completed set, finished between 1825 and 1833 by other hands, chiefly Wolf Heinrich Graf von Baudissin and Dorothea Tieck. The brand has done its work so well that the shorthand Schlegel-Tieck is spoken everywhere as if it named a partnership at a single desk. It did not, and the distinction matters, because the credit structure of the most influential translation in German literary history rests on getting the hands right.

A second verdict concerns the text itself. Schlegel's rendering is the strongest documented case in European literature of a translation becoming a classic inside the receiving language on its own merits. The publisher's description of the standard Reclam edition records that the version grew, across the nineteenth century, into an independent German classic, and German stages have spoken its lines, cut or reworked, ever since. The price of that naturalisation is visible on the page. Schlegel's Romeo and Juliet is smoother than Shakespeare's: more lyrical in diction, more regular in music, and quietly relieved of certain knots, the astrology of the Prologue, the medlar obscenity in Mercutio's wit, the plague in Mercutio's curse, that the English text refuses to surrender. The German classic was bought with smoothness, and the purchase can be audited line by line.

A translation that enters another nation's canon raises the question of ownership alongside the question of accuracy. Germany did not merely receive the play. It adopted the author, calling him unser Shakespeare, our Shakespeare, and the adoption was executed through this version's language, through the literary German of the Weimar classical age in which Schlegel chose to write. The sections that follow separate the hands that made the German Shakespeare, test the method Schlegel announced against the verse he printed, weigh the celebrants of the naturalisation against the auditors of the text, and deliver a verdict on what the German classic cost the English play. The complication runs through every stage of the story: a translation this successful is an act of appropriation, and the appropriated object is changed by the taking.

The method of the article matches the nature of its question. Claims about a translation's fidelity collapse into taste unless they are tested passage by passage, so the passages come first in what follows: the German text quoted against the English at the Prologue, the feast, the balcony, the duel, and the death speeches, with the losses and survivals recorded in each case. Only when the textual evidence is on the table do the celebrants and the auditors get their hearing, because their quarrel, which has run for two centuries, is at bottom a quarrel about what those passages show.

The play is the right test case for a further reason. No other Shakespeare tragedy puts a translator's programme under equal strain from so many directions at once: formal lyrics that must stay formal, prose that must stay quick, obscenity that must decide whether to stay obscene, and a metaphysics of stars and plague that must decide whether to stay literal. A version that survives Romeo and Juliet has survived the whole range of the original's demands, and a version that fails here fails informatively, at identifiable joints. Schlegel's version did both, which is why it, rather than any safer play, became the foundation of the German Shakespeare and the standing evidence in every German argument about what translation is.

The stakes extend past scholarship. Every literature that reads Shakespeare in translation, which is to say most of the world, reads him through a local bargain of the Schlegel type, struck by translators whose names their readers rarely know, at prices their readers never see itemised. The German case is the discipline's best-documented bargain, the one where the programme was declared in advance, the text survives for inspection, the receipts were kept by two centuries of auditors, and the adopting nation recorded its satisfaction in a possessive pronoun. To audit this Romeo is to learn how to audit all the others, which is the use of a type specimen, and the reason this play opens the account of its life in other languages.

## The Wrong Name on the Spine: Schlegel, Tieck, and the Hands Behind the German Romeo and Juliet

The bibliographical record is exact, and it assigns the play to one man. Schlegel's Romeo und Julia appeared first as an advance printing in Die Horen in 1796, then in 1797 at the head of the first volume of Shakspeare's dramatische Werke from Unger's Berlin house. The translator was twenty-nine, not yet the professor of literature he became at Jena in 1798, a chair he owed in large part to the reputation these translations were building. The Romeo stood near the start of his engagement with Shakespeare rather than at its summit. He had made trial renderings with Gottfried August Bürger as early as 1789, working on A Midsummer Night's Dream, and had been labouring on Hamlet from 1793. The tragedy of Verona was among the first fruits of a young man's decade of immersion, and its quality set the terms on which the whole enterprise would be judged.

The enterprise widened fast. Between 1797 and 1810 Schlegel published nine volumes containing seventeen plays in his own translation, the standard count given by the reference record, and those volumes remade the German idea of Shakespeare. Where German readers had previously met the plays in prose renderings that treated verse as packaging, Schlegel gave them the plays as poems, in German verse that moved as the English moved. The Romeo and Juliet of 1797 was the foundation stone: the play through which a generation of German readers learned that Shakespeare could sound, in their own language, like a classic rather than like a translated curiosity.

The enterprise also had a hidden hand. Caroline Schlegel, the translator's wife, took part in the work of preparation and revision that stood behind the published volumes, and the scale of her share has been argued from documentary evidence since Hermann Conrad published his findings in 1911. The title pages name one translator. The workshop held at least two people, and the arguments over the second person's contribution belong to the same pattern that governs the whole Schlegel-Tieck story: the printed credit is a simplification of a messier collaborative reality, simplified in the direction of the famous name.

### When did Schlegel's Romeo and Juliet first appear in print?

The play appeared twice: as an advance printing in Schiller's journal Die Horen in 1796, and then in book form in 1797, opening the first volume of Shakspeare's dramatische Werke, published in Berlin by Unger. The journal appearance came first, so 1796 is the translation's true birth year, with 1797 marking its arrival as a book.

The series stopped in 1801. The immediate cause on record is a quarrel with the publisher Unger, which ended the publishing arrangement with eight of the projected plays still undone and the set incomplete. Biographical accounts add a domestic cause: the collapse of Schlegel's marriage to Caroline, whose departure removed the workshop's second pair of hands. Schlegel never resumed the work. He lived for more than four further decades, lectured across Europe, became the most famous German critic of his age, and left his Shakespeare unfinished. The seventeen plays stood as a torso, admired and incomplete, for a generation.

Completion came from another household. In 1819 Schlegel entrusted the continuation to Ludwig Tieck, the novelist and dramatist who had championed Shakespeare in Germany since the 1790s and who shared the Romantic circle's reverence for the plays. Tieck accepted the editorship and, with it, the obligation to supply the nineteen missing plays. His own share of the translating, however, was small. The labour fell to Wolf Heinrich Graf von Baudissin, a diplomat and man of letters who rendered thirteen of the missing plays, and to Tieck's daughter Dorothea, who rendered five by the count most bibliographies give, with some accounts crediting her with six. Tieck's role was that of editor, adviser, and presiding name. The finished set of 1825 to 1833 therefore carries two names on its title pages, Schlegel as translator and Tieck as the man who supplemented and annotated the work, and neither name belongs to the two people who did most of the completing.

For Romeo and Juliet the arithmetic is simpler and the correction sharper. The play is Schlegel's alone, from the first scene to the last, printed before Tieck had any editorial connection with the enterprise at all. When German audiences wept over the balcony scene in Schlegel's verse through the nineteenth century, they were hearing a twenty-nine-year-old's work of 1796 and 1797. The double name on the spine describes the history of a set of books. Applied to this play, it is a misattribution that two centuries of habit have hardened into common knowledge.

The Jena setting explains the enterprise's confidence. Schlegel worked at the centre of the early Romantic movement, in the circle that included his brother Friedrich, Novalis, and the young Tieck, for whom Shakespeare was not a foreign author to be imported but the presiding genius of the literature they intended to found. Translation, in that circle, was a creative act of the first rank, the means by which German letters would absorb the world's poetry and be remade by the absorbing. The Shakespeare volumes were the circle's largest single undertaking, and the Romeo, as the first play of the first volume, carried the manifesto's weight: if the method could make this play live in German, the method was proved.

The seventeen plays that followed over the next thirteen years mixed histories, comedies, and tragedies without completing any single sequence, a torso whose very incompleteness testified to the labour each play demanded. Schlegel's fame in his lifetime rested at least as much on criticism, the Vienna lectures on dramatic art and literature carried his reading of Shakespeare across Europe, but posterity reversed the emphasis: the lectures are studied, the translations are still read, staged, and quoted. The young translator of 1796 built more durably than the famous lecturer of 1808, and the Romeo and Juliet stands at the head of the durable work, the first brick of the edifice that German culture would spend a century finishing, furnishing, and claiming as its own.

The choice of Die Horen as the launch vehicle was itself a statement about the intended audience. Schiller's journal was the leading literary periodical of the German republic of letters, the organ in which the classical age announced itself, and an advance printing there placed the translation before the nation's critics and poets before it reached the bookshops. The Romeo arrived, therefore, not as a publisher's speculation but as a literary event with a manifesto attached, reviewed and argued over by the very readership whose language it proposed to use. A prose Romeo issued cold in a bookshop might have been filed as another Wieland. A verse Romeo launched from the pages of Die Horen, with the translator's Shakespeare essay in the same journal as its escort, demanded judgement as a programme, and received it.

## The Prose Century: Wieland, Eschenburg, and the German Stage Before Schlegel

Schlegel did not bring Shakespeare to Germany. He brought Shakespeare's verse, and the difference defines his achievement. German readers had known the English dramatist, in fragments and adaptations, for more than half a century before the Romeo of 1797. Caspar Wilhelm von Borck had published the first exact German translation of any Shakespeare play, a Julius Caesar, in 1741. A Swiss rendering of Romeo and Juliet in blank verse by Simon Grynaeus had appeared in 1758, proof that the play's German life began decades before its German classicism. What Germany lacked was not acquaintance but a text that carried the poetry.

Christoph Martin Wieland supplied the acquaintance at scale. Between 1762 and 1766 Wieland translated twenty-two plays into German prose, Romeo and Juliet among them in 1766, and his versions were the channel through which the German reading public, Goethe included, first absorbed Shakespeare whole. Wieland's prose was a deliberate concession: he judged German verse unequal to the task and chose intelligibility over music. The concession had a cost that this play paid in full. A tragedy built from sonnets, rhymed couplets, and lyrical duets became, in Wieland, a story told in paragraphs. The plot survived the crossing. The poem did not.

Johann Joachim Eschenburg completed and revised the prose Shakespeare between 1775 and 1782, giving Germany its first full set and correcting Wieland from the English texts with a scholar's care. Eschenburg's was the standard German Shakespeare of the late eighteenth century, respectable, complete, and prosaic. Meanwhile the German stage made its own arrangements. Christian Felix Weiße's Romeo und Julie of 1767 and 1768, a free adaptation, ruled German theatres for two generations, and Georg Benda's Singspiel Romeo und Julie of 1776, with a libretto by Friedrich Wilhelm Gotter, let the lovers live, opera taking the licence that spoken tragedy had also been taking. The Germany of 1790 knew the lovers of Verona well. It knew them in prose, in adaptation, and in song. What it had never possessed was the play itself, entire, in German verse that answered the English verse form for form.

Eschenburg's labours deserve their due before the prose century is dismissed, because his edition was a scholarly instrument of a new kind. Working from the English texts with a care Wieland had not attempted, he corrected his predecessor's errors, filled the gaps in the canon, and surrounded the plays with the apparatus of a literature being taken seriously. What his Shakespeare lacked was not learning but music, and the lack was decisive for this play above all: a tragedy whose lovers speak in sonnets cannot survive as an argument in paragraphs, however accurate the paragraphs are. The German record thus presents a clean sequence of partial solutions before 1796, adaptation without the text, the text without the verse, the verse attempted without the register. Each predecessor solved the problem the previous one had exposed, and Schlegel's synthesis closed the sequence by refusing to choose among text, verse, and register at all.

Weiße's long stage reign carries a lesson the audit should not waste. His Romeo und Julie succeeded for two generations not because German audiences were undiscriminating but because adaptation was the period's honest technology for importing plays: the adapter owned his changes openly, signed them, and answered for them. The prose translators likewise owned their method. Schlegel's revolution was to change the technology while keeping the candour, announcing a programme of formal fidelity and submitting to be judged by it. The smoothing documented later in this article is therefore not a return to the adapters' licence but a shortfall against a declared standard, which is a more serious and more interesting charge, and the only one the evidence supports.

That vacancy is the context that makes Schlegel's programme legible as an intervention rather than a preference. The prose century had treated Shakespeare as a mine of plots and characters, a storyteller whose language was an accident of his foreignness. Schlegel's generation, the Jena Romantics, held the opposite doctrine: the language was the work, the verse was the meaning's body, and a translation that discarded the form had discarded the play. The Romeo of 1796 and 1797 was the demonstration piece of that doctrine, chosen, one suspects, because no play in the canon makes the doctrine harder or more necessary. A prose Romeo and Juliet is a summary. A verse Romeo and Juliet is the thing itself, or it is nothing.

The critical ground had been prepared by a generation that learned to prize Shakespeare precisely for the qualities French classical rules condemned. Gotthold Ephraim Lessing's Hamburgische Dramaturgie, written in the seventeen-sixties, had argued Shakespeare into German critical respectability as the model of a drama answerable to nature rather than to rule, and Johann Gottfried Herder's essay of 1773 had hailed him as a world-historical genius whose plays were events of nature and history rather than artefacts of a school. By the seventeen-seventies Friedrich Ludwig Schröder's Hamburg productions had made the plays a living stage repertory in adapted form. Germany thus arrived at 1796 loving Shakespeare ardently and possessing him inadequately: the criticism was fervent, the stage versions were free, and the texts were prose. The fervour created the demand that only a poet-translator could supply.

The operatic branch of the story shows how far adaptation could go. Benda's Romeo und Julie of 1776, the Singspiel with Gotter's libretto, let the lovers live, resolving the tragedy into the rescue patterns of opera, and its success across German stages demonstrated that the story's hold on German audiences was independent of the story's ending, let alone of Shakespeare's language. Weiße's spoken adaptation achieved the same hold by similar liberties. A public that wept for the lovers in versions that spared them, in prose that summarised them, was a public ready to be given the actual play. Schlegel's intervention succeeded because the preparation had been so thorough and the possession so incomplete: the nation had been taught to revere a poet it had never been allowed to read.

## The Translation Programme: Verse for Verse and Prose for Prose

Schlegel announced his method before he fully practised it, in the essay of 1796 printed in Die Horen under the title Etwas über William Shakespeare bei Gelegenheit Wilhelm Meisters, something on William Shakespeare on the occasion of Wilhelm Meister. The essay used Goethe's novel, with its famous Hamlet discussions, as the platform for a translator's manifesto. Its standard, as the reference summaries of Schlegel's career record it, was functional equivalence: the German text should produce the effect of the English, and it should do so by fidelity to form. Verse was to be answered with verse in the same metres. Rhyme was to be answered with rhyme. Prose was to remain prose. The translator's task was not to explain the play to German readers but to rebuild the play in German materials, joint for joint.

The programme was radical against the prose century precisely because it treated form as content. A sonnet in the English play was an event, two lovers composing a poem with their mouths at first meeting, and only a German sonnet could stage that event. A rhyming couplet at a scene's close was a door shutting, and only a German couplet could shut it. Schlegel's Romeo and Juliet puts the programme to work on the most form-saturated play Shakespeare wrote, a play containing a sonnet for a prologue, a sonnet shared between the lovers, songs, riddles, rhymed laments, and prose that ranges from servants' banter to Mercutio's fireworks. The play is a complete test bench for a theory of translation, and Schlegel knew it: his own essay on the play, Über Shakspeares Romeo und Julia, printed in Die Horen in 1797, presents the translator's reading of the work he had just recreated. The reading that Schlegel later built into his Vienna lectures, and the German Romantics who absorbed that reading, are traced in [the German Romantics and Schlegel's reading of the play](/2026/10/30/schlegel-german-romantics-reading/).

### What did Schlegel promise his method would preserve?

Schlegel promised to preserve the play's formal body entire: verse answered by verse in the same metre, rhyme answered by rhyme, and prose left as prose, so German readers met the same kind of object English readers met. His standard was effect through form, a rebuilt play rather than a report about one.

The era supplied a frame for judging how far the promise went. In 1813 Friedrich Schleiermacher delivered his Academy lecture on the different methods of translating, and its famous dichotomy divides all translation into two roads: the translator either leaves the reader in peace and moves the author toward the reader, or leaves the author in peace and moves the reader toward the author. Schlegel's programme, announced seventeen years before the lecture named the choice, takes the harder road in form. German readers were to be moved toward Shakespeare's metres, his sonnets, his couplets, his alternation of verse and prose. Yet the dichotomy has a second axis, diction, and on that axis Schlegel travelled the other road. The German he wrote was the polished literary language of Weimar classicism, the language formed in the wake of Goethe and Schiller, regular, elevated, and closed against roughness. The author was brought to the reader in vocabulary while the reader was brought to the author in form. That double movement, foreign in structure and domestic in surface, is the key to everything that follows: to the version's triumph, and to the smoothing that the audit of the text lays bare.

The essay on the play itself, Über Shakspeares Romeo und Julia of 1797, belongs to the same enterprise viewed from the other side. A translator who publishes his reading of the work alongside his rendering is making a claim about what translation is: not a mechanical transfer but an interpretation executed in verse, answerable to a stated understanding of the whole. Schlegel's reading treated the play as a unified poem rather than a string of beauties, and the translation enacts the reading, holding the play's tones in a single register as an interpretation holds its object in a single view. The point matters for the audit. Some part of the famous uniformity of the German text is not carelessness or prudery but doctrine: a critic-translator who prized the artwork's unity above its local variety would smooth as a matter of principle, and did.

The split that Schleiermacher's frame reveals, formal fidelity against lexical domestication, is therefore not an accident of Schlegel's practice but its deepest structure. Two fidelities were on offer in the period's theory, fidelity to the letter's shape and fidelity to the receiving language's genius, and Schlegel's programme seized the first with both hands while quietly serving the second. His metres are Shakespeare's; his words are Weimar's. Every virtue and every defect catalogued in the passage audit descends from that division of loyalty, and the division explains why the argument about the version has never settled: celebrants and auditors are both describing the same text accurately, one party attending to its skeleton and the other to its skin.

The programme's influence ran beyond Germany, and the export matters for judging its stature. The Schlegel model, a complete Shakespeare rebuilt in the receiving language's verse with form held constant, became the ambition that nineteenth-century translators across Europe set themselves, the standard against which national Shakespeares elsewhere were planned and measured. Translation theory's historians treat the German enterprise as the type case of a literature importing a foreign classic so thoroughly that the import reorganises the home tradition. That is the largest frame in which the Romeo of 1797 sits: not a German curiosity but the founding artefact of a European practice, the demonstration that a translated author could be naturalised at the level of the line. The naturalisation's costs, the smoothing and the annexation, were exported with the method, and every later national Shakespeare inherits the bargain along with the ambition.

## The German Verse Under the Lens: Prologue, Sonnet, and Balcony

The programme survives inspection at the level of architecture. Take the Prologue. Shakespeare casts the argument of the play as a sonnet, fourteen lines in which the Chorus compresses two households, one feud, two lovers, and a double suicide into the tightest form English poetry owns. Schlegel's German Prologue is likewise a sonnet, fourteen lines, rhymed, turning on the same joints: Zwei Häuser waren, gleich an Würdigkeit, two houses, equal in dignity, set in Verona, where the action sticks. The rhyme scheme carries the argument in blocks as the English does, the couplets close where the English couplets close, and the final promise to the audience, that what is wanting in the tale the players' toil will supply, lands in the same position. A German hearer in 1797 received the same formal experience as a London hearer in 1595: a poem that is also a plot summary, offered as a pledge of workmanship.

The lovers' shared sonnet at the Capulet feast passes the same test. Shakespeare's boldest formal stroke in the play, the first dialogue of Romeo and Juliet shaped as a single sonnet that the two speakers build between them, survives in Schlegel as a rhymed German sonnet, its pilgrim and saint figures intact, its negotiation of a kiss conducted inside the form rather than around it. The quatrain structure, the turn, the closing couplet: all stand. This matters beyond ingenuity. The shared sonnet is the play's thesis about its lovers, that their speech naturally harmonises into one poem, and a translation that loosened it into ordinary dialogue would have lost the thesis while keeping every fact. Schlegel keeps the thesis.

The shared sonnet repays a slower look, because its mechanics are the scene. Shakespeare builds the poem in stages: Romeo's opening quatrain casts his hand as a pilgrim at a shrine, Juliet's answering quatrain corrects the pilgrim's theology with a wit that grants the suit while refining its terms, and the two voices then interlock through the sestet toward the kiss the form has been negotiating. Schlegel preserves the staging, the pilgrim and the saint, the hand and the lips, the prayer granted by being answered, inside a German sonnet whose rhymes fall where the English rhymes fall. The scene's erotic theology, desire conducted as liturgy, survives because its form survives, and the form survives because the translator's programme recognised it as the meaning rather than as decoration on the meaning.

The Friar's entrance speech extends the demonstration from lyric to meditation. Shakespeare gives Lorenzo a chain of rhymed couplets on the paradox of the earth, nature's mother and her tomb, the womb that is a grave, and on the opposed virtues lodged in plants and in the human breast. Schlegel's German keeps the couplet chain and the paradoxes in step: Die Mutter der Natur, die Erd, ist auch ihr Grab, und was ihr Schoß gebar, sinkt tot in ihn hinab, the mother of nature, the earth, is also her grave, and what her womb bore sinks dead back into it. The womb and tomb wordplay, which turns on an English rhyme, is rebuilt as a German conceptual rhyme, Schoß and Grab locked in the couplet's close. A translator working from a prose crib could not have found that equivalence; it is the find of a poet reading the original's figure and answering figure with figure.

The balcony scene shows the method at line level. Romeo's opening, Doch still, was schimmert durch das Fenster dort? Es ist der Ost, und Julia die Sonne, answers But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? It is the east, and Juliet is the sun, beat for beat, image for image, the German blank verse flexing to hold the English line's contents without spilling. The moon speech follows with its mythology intact: the envious moon, her vestal livery sick and green, rendered as the pale, sick vestal dress that only fools wear. Even the Friar's entrance in the third scene of the second act, Shakespeare's rhymed couplets for the Friar's herbal meditation, comes over into German rhymed couplets, Der Morgen lächelt froh der Nacht ins Angesicht, the morning smiling on the night's brow, couplet answering couplet for the length of the speech.

The scene's close supplies the audit's most quoted German couplet. Shakespeare's parting, good night, good night, parting is such sweet sorrow that I shall say good night till it be morrow, becomes Nun gute Nacht! So süß ist Trennungswehe, ich rief wohl gute Nacht, bis ich den Morgen sähe: good night, so sweet is parting's pain that good night would be called until morning saw the lovers again. The oxymoron of sweet sorrow survives as süßes Trennungswehe, sweet parting-pain, compressed into a single German compound, and the couplet clicks shut on the rhyme as the English clicks. Immediately before it stands the bird speech, Juliet imagining Romeo as her pet bird let out on a silken thread and pulled back, Gleich einem Armen in der Banden Druck, und dann zurück ihn zieht am seidnen Faden, like a poor prisoner in his fetters, then drawn back by the silken thread, image for image, cruelty and tenderness in their English proportions. The parting is the scene German quotation loves best, and the passage audit shows why: at the lovers' most lyrical, the translation loses least, because lyricism was the receiving language's home ground.

### How close does the German Prologue stay to the English sonnet?

Formally, the German Prologue stays exact: fourteen lines, rhymed as a sonnet, with the argument's joints in the same places and the closing promise to the audience in the closing position. Both audiences receive the same formal event. The closeness is architectural before it is verbal, and the verbal departures belong to a different ledger.

Juliet's great question receives the rendering that German quotation has made canonical. Shakespeare's wherefore, which asks not where Romeo is but why he is Romeo, why he must carry the name that makes him her enemy, becomes O Romeo! Warum denn Romeo? Verleugne deinen Vater, deinen Namen: O Romeo, why then Romeo? Deny thy father, thy name. The warum preserves the true sense against the perennial misreading of the line as a question about place, and the rose speech that follows is carried entire: Was ist ein Name? Was uns Rose heißt, wie es auch hieße, würde lieblich duften, what is a name? That which we call a rose, by any other name, would smell sweet. Generations of German readers learned the philosophy of the scene in these lines, and learned it accurately.

Mercutio's Queen Mab speech, the play's most virtuosic set piece, stands complete in the German, opening Nun seh ich wohl, Frau Mab hat Euch besucht, Mercutio's conclusion that Queen Mab has been visiting, and running its catalogue of dreams, courtiers, lawyers, ladies, and soldiers at full length. The speech is a test no prose translator could pass and no adapter would attempt, an aria of prose fantasy whose meaning is its momentum. Schlegel carries the momentum. Whatever the audit finds elsewhere, the German Romeo and Juliet never flinches from the play's set pieces. Its sonnets are sonnets, its couplets couplets, its great speeches entire. The architecture of the English play stands in German, load-bearing walls and ornamental plaster alike, and that achievement, against the background of the prose century, was revolutionary enough to explain a reputation.

The enjambment tells the same story at the level of the breath. Shakespeare's balcony verse runs over its line endings as thought outruns measure, and Schlegel preserves the run-overs rather than tidying each line into a sealed unit: Denn über meinem Haupt erscheinest du der Nacht so glorreich lets the sentence pour across the break exactly where the English pours. A translator tidying for elegance would have closed those lines; the German verse keeps them open, because the programme understood that the verse's gait, not merely its contents, was part of the meaning. Where the German text betrays the English, it betrays lexically, in single words and images, almost never in the movement of the verse itself.

## The Verse Mechanics: How the German Line Carries the English

The technical basis of the achievement deserves a section of its own, because Schlegel's formal fidelity was not a slogan but a set of solutions to mechanical problems that had defeated his predecessors. German and English iambic verse differ in their raw materials. German, an inflected language, rhymes easily and sometimes too easily, its grammatical endings offering rhymes that cost no thought, while its compound nouns lengthen lines and crowd the pentameter's frame. English blank verse in Shakespeare's hands is supple with enjambment, feminine endings, and inverted stresses. A German translator promising the same metres was promising to make a heavier language dance at an English tempo, and the promise, kept across seventeen plays, is the labour for which the reference tradition honours Schlegel as the creator of the German Shakespeare's verse form.

The couplets demonstrate the craft in its most exposed form, since a couplet either snaps shut or sags. The Friar's herbal meditation in the second act holds its rhyme across the whole speech, and the Prince's judgement in the third act shows the form doing judicial work in German: Er hat Mercutio, ihn Romeo erschlagen; wer soll die Schuld des teuren Blutes tragen, he slew Mercutio, Romeo slew him; who bears the guilt of the precious blood, and the sentence closes with Wenn Gnade Mörder schont, verübt sie Mord, when mercy spares murderers, it commits murder, answering Shakespeare's mercy but murders, pardoning those that kill, point for point. Benvolio's long narrative of the duel, which Shakespeare loads into couplets of reportage, keeps its rhymes in German for nearly forty lines without the sense buckling. The door-shutting effect of the English couplet, verdict, narrative, and moral all closing with a click, survives the crossing intact.

Juliet's epithalamium in the third act offers the sterner test of sustained blank verse under classical machinery. Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds, towards Phoebus' lodging invokes the sun god's chariot and Phaethon's reckless driving as the machinery of a young wife's impatience, and Schlegel carries the whole apparatus into German without apology: Hinab, du flammenhufiges Gespann, zu Phöbus' Wohnung, down, flame-hooved team, to Phoebus' dwelling, with Phaethon named in the following lines as the whip that would drive the sun to its rest. A domesticating translator of the prose school would have paraphrased the mythology into a remark about the slow sun. Schlegel keeps gods, chariot, and myth entire, trusting the German line to bear the learned freight. The passage is a useful control on the smoothing thesis: where the English is elevated, allusive, and difficult, the German is all three in answer.

The prose mechanics cut the other way, toward speed. Shakespeare's prose in this play runs on interruption and riposte, and the German preserves the tempo: Mercutio's wound exchange with Romeo, Ja, ja, geritzt, geritzt, ay, ay, a scratch, a scratch, keeps the doubled beat of the English joke even as its content, the minimising of a mortal wound, darkens. The servants' dialogue in the first scene, examined in the smoothing audit for its re-punned jokes, also keeps its stichomythic pace, insult answering insult at the English rhythm. Prose fidelity is easier to overlook than verse fidelity because it leaves no formal fingerprint, but the programme's prose clause was the one Wieland's generation would have found strangest, and its observance is why the German play reads as a play rather than as a poem with dialogue interruptions. The mechanics, taken together, describe a translator whose formalism was total in intention: every stratum of the original's verbal organisation, sonnet, couplet, blank verse, and banter, was to have its German counterpart, and in the event nearly every stratum got one.

One further mechanical habit deserves recording, since it governs the texture German audiences actually heard. Shakespeare's blank verse habitually ends lines on unstressed syllables, lets speeches trail into shared lines split between speakers, and varies the caesura's position to keep the verse breathing. Schlegel's German reproduces these irregularities rather than regularising them into the marching iambics that a lesser formalism would have imposed: the lovers' exchanges split lines between the two voices at the same joints as the English, the dying Mercutio's prose interrupts the verse fabric at the same places, and the Prince's couplets close scenes whose interiors have been allowed their English looseness. The formalism, in short, was a formalism of the whole verbal surface, attentive to the original's freedoms as well as its rules. That attentiveness is the strongest technical defence the version possesses against the audit's findings, and it should be entered in the record plainly: whatever Schlegel smoothed, he did not simplify the verse's behaviour, and the German play moves, breathes, and interrupts itself like the English one.

## What the Translation Smooths Away: Stars, Medlars, and Plagues

Against the architecture stands the surface, and on the surface the audit finds a pattern. Begin again with the Prologue, this time at the level of the word. Shakespeare's sonnet names the lovers as a pair of star-cross'd lovers who take their life, and calls their love death-mark'd. The astrology is the play's metaphysics in miniature: the stars cross these lives, the mark of death is on the love from the first line. Schlegel's German sonnet contains no stars. Aus dieser Feinde unheilvollem Schoß das Leben zweier Liebender entsprang: from these foes' ill-fated loins the life of two lovers sprang. The self-killing, the lovers taking their own life, dissolves into a life springing, and death-mark'd love becomes todgeweihte Liebe, love consecrated to death, in which the mark, a brand of fate, is elevated into a consecration, a religious solemnity. The sonnet's frame stands; its cosmology has been planed smooth. A German audience met destiny as solemnity where an English audience met it as astrology and doom.

The medlar passage shows the same hand at work on comedy. In the second act's first scene Mercutio conjures the absent Romeo and imagines him sitting under a medlar tree, wishing his mistress were that kind of fruit, and the speech ends in the famous obscenity of the open-arse and the poperin pear, a pun that depends on the medlar's folk names and carries the scene's sexual frankness to its peak. Schlegel gives three decorous lines in which Romeo sits leaning against a tree and wishes his sweetheart were the ripe fruit that fell into his lap. Tree, fruit, and lap survive; the medlar, the open fruit, the pear, and the joke's whole anatomy are gone. The pun was untranslatable in its English form, since the slang lives in English fruit names, but the replacement declines even a substitute grossness. Where the knot could not be untied, Schlegel cut it, and chose the cut that preserved the scene's respectability.

Mercutio's dying curse undergoes the same laundering. A plague o' both your houses sounds three times in the English scene, the word plague gathering force with each repetition and forging a link to the plague that will later seal Friar John's letter and kill the lovers by quarantine. Schlegel's Mercutio cries Zum Teufel beider Sippschaft, to the devil with both clans, then Hol der Henker eure beiden Häuser, may the hangman take both your houses, and finally Zum Teufel eure Häuser. The word Pest, plague, never sounds. Imprecation replaces disease, and the curse loses its thematic echo inside the play's machinery of death. In the same speech the grave man pun, ask for me tomorrow and you shall find me a grave man, becomes einen stillen Mann, a quiet man: the stillness of the corpse survives translation, the tomb inside the word grave does not. The edition itself confesses this class of loss elsewhere, printing a bracketed note at Juliet's ay, I, and eye wordplay in the third act to explain that a pun on three identical sounds exists in the English, rather than counterfeiting a German equivalent. The honesty is admirable. It is also an admission that the method has a floor, below which the English play's verbal life simply stops crossing.

### What happens to Mercutio's medlar joke in the German text?

The joke is removed, replaced by a decorous wish. Schlegel keeps Mercutio imagining Romeo beneath a tree, wishing his mistress were ripe fruit fallen into his lap, but the medlar tree, the open-arse, and the poperin pear vanish. The pun is not recreated in German; the passage shrinks to an image a polite audience could hear without a smirk.

The counter-evidence matters, because the smoothing was selective rather than prudish in any simple sense. The opening scene's bawdy, the servants' talk of maidenheads and standing, survives in Schlegel with its sexual threat intact, though the diction is veiled: Sampson will rob the Montague maids of their maidenly heads, take their maidenhood, take it in either sense. The crude wordplay of coals and colliers is re-punned in German materials, pocket and pickpocket, Taschenspieler, doing the work of the English chain, and the thumb-biting insult becomes the German gesture of boring a donkey, einen Esel bohren, a native insult substituted for a foreign one. Mercutio's conjuration catalogue in the second act, with its inventory of Rosaline's body down to the region that lies near the thighs, stands unexpurgated. Schlegel was no bowdleriser. His cuts fall where the English is untranslatable or where its machinery is cosmic rather than comic: the stars, the medlar, the plague. The pattern points to a poetics, not a censor. The German text raises the play's diction to a single high register, the literary language of German classicism in the wake of Goethe and Schiller, as the Reclam description puts it, and achieves what that description calls a poetic translation work of great linguistic closedness. Geschlossenheit, closedness, uniformity: the word names the smoothing from inside the tradition that prizes it. Shakespeare's English is a mixed fabric, high lyric and low pun, cosmology and obscenity, verse and prose. Schlegel's German is a woven cloth of one fine thread, and the uniformity that made it a classic is the same uniformity that planed the knots away.

The edition's own apparatus shows the translators' circle knew exactly where the floor lay. At Juliet's frantic wordplay in the third act, where she cries that a single syllable, ay, must decide her joy or pain, and Shakespeare rings the changes on ay, I, and eye, three words with one sound, the German text prints the speech and appends a bracketed note: Ein Wortspiel mit den Wörtern aye (ja), I (ich) und eye (Auge), die alle gleich ausgesprochen werden, a wordplay on the words aye, I, and eye, which are all pronounced alike. The note is scholarship standing where poetry failed: an honest label on an empty space. Few translations confess their losses in their own margins, and the confession strengthens rather than weakens the version's authority, since a text that marks its gaps can be trusted where it claims success.

The oxymoron chain in the same scene supplies the control experiment. Juliet's discovery that Romeo has killed Tybalt detonates Shakespeare's most concentrated burst of paradox: beautiful tyrant, fiend angelical, dove-feather'd raven, wolvish-ravening lamb. Schlegel answers figure for figure: Holdselger Wütrich! Engelgleicher Unhold! Ergrimmte Taube! Lamm mit Wolfesgier! lovely tyrant, angel-like fiend, enraged dove, lamb with a wolf's greed, preceded by the serpent heart hidden in the flowering face, O Schlangenherz, von Blumen überdeckt. Nothing is planed, nothing elevated, nothing spared: where the English effect is lyrical paradox, the German matches it at full strength, epithet for epithet. The contrast with the medlar and the stars is the audit's most instructive result. Schlegel's smoothing was never a general softening of intensity. It struck at machinery that would not cross, astrology and plague, and at filth that could not cross politely, while the play's rhetorical violence, its oxymorons, curses against fortune, and laments, crossed at full voltage. The German play is not a gentler play. It is the same play with its cosmology and its obscenity selectively dimmed, and its lyricism, if anything, burnished brighter than the original's.

The Prologue's missing stars matter beyond their line, because the stars are the keynote of the play's fortune system. Shakespeare's tragedy sounds the note again at every crisis: Romeo calls himself fortune's fool over Tybalt's corpse, defies the stars when the false news from Mantua arrives, and reads his ending in a cosmos whose machinery the Prologue has already described. A German reader meeting the Prologue without stars receives the tragedy's events under a vaguer heaven, as solemn destiny rather than as astral mechanics, and the recurrences of the fortune theme later in the play lose the anchor the first fourteen lines should have set. Smoothing at a keynote is not a local loss. It retunes the whole instrument, and the German play's famous atmosphere of consecrated, lyrical doom, doom as a religious weather rather than a mechanical one, is the retuned sound. Critics who praise the German text's unity of tone are praising, without always knowing it, the acoustics of that first excision.

The Nurse's lament in the same act tests the prose at the opposite social pole, and the result rounds out the picture. Shakespeare's Nurse brings the news of Tybalt's death in a wail of repetition, the tolling he is dead misheard by Juliet for her husband, and Schlegel's prose keeps the repetitions hammering: Daß Gott erbarm! Er ist tot, er ist tot, er ist tot! Wir sind verloren, Fräulein, sind verloren, God have mercy, he is dead, he is dead, he is dead, we are undone. Nothing here is elevated, nothing classical in the Weimar sense, and the refusal to elevate is itself the fidelity: the programme's prose clause protected the low voices from the register that governs the lovers' verse. The smoothing, in other words, had a class boundary. It planed the cosmology above the characters and the obscenity inside their jokes, while the servants, nurses, and clowns kept their repetitions, their oaths, and their grief at full vernacular force. A reader auditing only the famous lyrics would miss the boundary, and with it the translation's actual ethics, which reserved its classicism for the passages German classicism was built to carry.

## Unser Shakespeare: How the Translation Became a German Possession

The phrase unser Shakespeare, our Shakespeare, compresses a national claim into two words. It does not say that Germany admired Shakespeare, or studied him, or staged him more faithfully than other nations. It says that Shakespeare had become, by right of labour and love, partly German: a poet whose true textual home, many Germans argued, was the German language, since German translators had given him the care, the complete editions, and the reverent stages that England had denied him. The claim is extravagant, and it was made in earnest across the nineteenth century by critics, societies, and schoolmasters. Georg Gottfried Gervinus, whose four-volume Shakespeare commentary began appearing in 1849, argued the naturalisation at scholarly length, treating the English poet as a possession of German cultural life. The tercentenary year of 1864 gave the claim an institution: the Deutsche Shakespeare-Gesellschaft, the German Shakespeare Society, founded at Weimar, made the guardianship of Shakespeare a standing German enterprise, seated in the town of Goethe and Schiller as if to file the Englishman between them.

The vehicle of the adoption was the translation. A nation cannot adopt a poet it reads in summaries, and after 1797 Germany read Shakespeare in Schlegel's verse, a verse written in the high literary language of the Weimar classical age. The sociological consequence followed the literary one. To German ears the plays sounded classical, sounded like Goethe and Schiller sounded, and a classic that sounds like the national classics slides, by an easy acoustics, into the national pantheon. Goethe himself, who directed the Weimar court theatre from 1791 to 1817, prepared an adaptation of Romeo and Juliet for that stage, and Goethe's lifelong engagement with the play is a documented scholarly subject in its own right. The Weimar connection mattered symbolically beyond any single production: the town that embodied German classicism took the English tragedy into its repertory and its pantheon in the same gesture.

Editions carried the version into every stratum of reading Germany. The completed Schlegel-Tieck set went through edition after edition, a second in 1839 and 1840 and a third in 1843 and 1844, while cheap formats multiplied the text's reach. Reclam's Universal-Bibliothek, the pocket library that put the classics into railway carriages and school satchels, carried Romeo und Julia in Schlegel's version from the eighteen-seventies, an 1876 Reclam printing standing in the catalogues, and the Reclam text became the form in which school generations met the play. The classroom completed what the theatre began: the play's place in lessons far beyond Germany is mapped in [the play in global classrooms](/2030/06/28/play-in-global-classrooms/), and within Germany the Schlegel text was simply the play, the wording from which quotations were drawn and against which all rivals would be measured. A student setting Schlegel's couplets beside the English in parallel columns, in [a free online presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) or in the facing pages of a bilingual edition, handles the two texts that this long adoption fused into one cultural object.

### Did German readers treat Shakespeare as a foreign author at all?

Increasingly, they did not. The formula unser Shakespeare, our Shakespeare, states the outcome: a poet naturalised by translation, claimed as a German possession on German labour, editions, and stages. Foreignness drained out of the plays as Schlegel's German became the sound of Shakespeare's name for German readers, until the English original could seem the foreign text.

The adoption thesis needs its complication stated plainly, because the same evidence supports a cooler reading. Naturalisation is a compliment that conceals a takeover. Germany's Shakespeare was Schlegel's Shakespeare, metrically faithful and lexically smoothed, a poet more uniformly lyrical than the English original, and the nation that adopted him adopted, in part, its own reflection: Weimar classicism wearing an English name. The strongest form of the celebrants' claim, that Germany understood Shakespeare better than England did, therefore proves less than it announces. Germany understood a Shakespeare who had been partially remade in Germany's image, and loved him, understandably, as one of its own. The translation's triumph and the translation's infidelity are not two facts about the Schlegel text. They are the same fact, read from opposite banks of the Rhine.

German usage eventually fixed the adoption in a formula of rank. The standing commonplace of German cultural talk set Shakespeare beside Goethe and Schiller as the third of the national classics, a ranking so familiar that it passed without argument, and the tercentenary society gave the sentiment a bureaucracy: the Shakespeare-Gesellschaft's Jahrbuch, its yearbook, became an engine of German Shakespeare scholarship, an annual proof that the guardianship unser Shakespeare proclaimed was being exercised in print. Guardianship of this kind is a one-way ratchet. Each generation of German scholars, editors, and schoolmasters that worked on the plays added its labour to the original claim, until the claim rested less on Schlegel's achievement alone than on the accumulated industry of a nation that had made the foreign poet a department of its own literary state.

The translation also fed back into the German language itself. Schlegel's solutions for Shakespeare's images set precedents that later German translators inherited as defaults, his phrases passed into the quotation collections from which German speakers draw their literary small change, and the balcony scene's wording became the form in which the culture phrases first love's defiance of a name. A translation that supplies a language with its quotations has passed beyond literature into the tongue's furniture. That is the depth of naturalisation the phrase unser Shakespeare records, and it is why rival translators in later centuries faced not merely a competing text but an installed idiom: to displace Schlegel, a challenger had to displace phrases that German speakers experienced as their own words rather than as a translator's choices.

The tercentenary year itself showed the adoption operating as public ritual. In 1864 German towns marked the three-hundredth anniversary of Shakespeare's birth with performances, speeches, and festivities on a scale no other foreign author received, and the founding of the Society at Weimar in that year converted the festival's sentiment into a permanent institution with officers, membership, and a yearbook. Sentiment became administration. The schoolroom then performed the adoption's quiet final stage: generations of German pupils met the play in Schlegel's wording as a set text, memorised the balcony scene as German poetry, and carried the phrases into adult quotation without any sense of handling a translation at all. A classic that children memorise in school has completed the journey from foreign work to national furniture, and the Schlegel Romeo completed it earlier and more thoroughly than any other translated play in the language.

The claim eventually crossed back into English as a commonplace in its own right. Anglophone writing on Shakespeare repeats, usually with a mixture of pride and ruefulness, that the Germans made Shakespeare a classic of their own literature, and the repetition concedes the naturalisation from the losing side. A claim that the dispossessed party itself repeats has passed beyond polemic into the record. The ruefulness is worth parsing, since it identifies what England believed it had lost: not the plays, which remained in every English library, but the exclusive ownership of their meaning, the right to say what Shakespeare finally was. Germany's translation had demonstrated that a sufficiently devoted foreign readership could hold a share in that right, and unser Shakespeare was the share certificate. Translations since have been measured, in every receiving culture, against the German proof that such a share can be earned.

## Completing the Edition: Tieck's Name, Baudissin's Labour, Dorothea's Hand

The title pages of the completed edition state the division of labour with a precision that later usage blurred. The set, issued by G. Reimer in Berlin between 1825 and 1833 in nine volumes, names the work as Shakspeare's dramatische Werke, translated by August Wilhelm von Schlegel, supplemented and annotated by Ludwig Tieck: ergänzt und erläutert von Ludwig Tieck. Translator, singular, Schlegel. Supplementation and annotation, Tieck. The hyphenated brand Schlegel-Tieck, which sounds like a writing partnership, is a later shorthand for a publishing arrangement, and the shorthand has systematically misled readers about who wrote what. For the seventeen plays of 1797 to 1810 the answer is Schlegel. For the nineteen completions the answer is chiefly Baudissin and Dorothea Tieck. For Romeo and Juliet the answer is Schlegel alone, unassisted by the completion machinery that did not exist until the play was nearly thirty years old in German.

The completion's internal history is a small drama of delegation. Schlegel entrusted the continuation to Tieck in 1819. Tieck began with energy, and the first three volumes appeared in 1825 and 1826, but then the editor stalled, and the enterprise passed in practice to the two workers: Baudissin, methodical and productive, and Dorothea Tieck, whose five plays in the set, or six in some accounts, were rendered under her father's roof and published under the edition's collective credit. Her Macbeth of 1833 closed the edition, the last translation in the famous set, as the bibliographical apparatus records. A woman's hand thus finished the most famous translation enterprise in German letters, inside a set whose public names were two men's. The pattern of the spine, famous names in front and working hands behind, holds at every level of the story.

Tieck's editorial hand reached backward as well as forward. In his correction sessions, the Corrigirstunde held with his collaborators, the texts were retouched, and Schlegel's own plays did not escape the treatment, to Schlegel's indignation, as the scholarly tradition records. The founder's texts were revised by the completer's circle in the founder's lifetime, without his blessing. The episode completes the portrait of the edition as a collective, evolving artefact rather than a single act of authorship: conceived by one young man in Jena, sustained by a wife's uncredited labour, abandoned in a quarrel, resumed under a famous editor, executed by a count and a daughter, and corrected in sessions its originator resented. Schlegel-Tieck names all of that. It is the name of an institution, and institutions, unlike translators, do not hold pens.

### Who finished the German Shakespeare after Schlegel stopped?

The finishers were Wolf Heinrich Graf von Baudissin, who translated thirteen of the missing plays, and Dorothea Tieck, who translated five, or six in some accounts, under her father Ludwig Tieck's editorship; Schlegel had entrusted the continuation to Tieck in 1819. The edition appeared from Reimer between 1825 and 1833, and Dorothea's Macbeth closed it.

What the institution produced, for this play, was stability. The German Romeo and Juliet of the completed editions is the text of 1797, carried through the century with the light retouching of the correction sessions rather than wholesale revision, and that stability was itself a canonising force. A classic is a text that stops changing, and Schlegel's Romeo stopped changing early. School editions, stage prompt books, and quotation all drew on the same wording for generations, fixing phrases in the national memory: the Warum denn Romeo of the balcony scene became the German form of the question, as fixed in German ears as the English line in English ones. Rival versions would later challenge the text, the Voß family's among them in the nineteenth century and new translations in the twentieth, and the challenges would be measured against a wording that had a century's head start in the nation's ear.

The hyphenated shorthand itself has a history worth one paragraph, since the brand did cultural work the title pages never authorised. Booksellers, scholars, and theatre bills needed a short name for the standard German Shakespeare, and Schlegel-Tieck, welding the founder to the completer, supplied it. The weld flatters both parties symmetrically and misleads both ways: it lends Tieck a translator's share he never claimed on the title page, and it lends Schlegel an editorial completion he spent years resenting. Whole plays changed perceived authorship inside the brand, none more consequentially than this one, whose German readers for two centuries have praised or blamed a partnership for the work of a twenty-nine-year-old working alone in the seventeen-nineties. Bibliographical precision is a dull virtue with sharp consequences, and the correction of the brand is the smallest and most portable finding of the entire audit: the German Romeo and Juliet has one author after Shakespeare, and his name is August Wilhelm Schlegel.

## The Challengers: Voß, Fried, and Günther Against the Installed Text

No classic goes unchallenged, and the Schlegel text's challengers map the changing conscience of German translation. The first serious rivalry came from the Voß family, the sons of Johann Heinrich Voß, the great translator of Homer, whose competing Shakespeare applied a sterner literalism and asked why the smoother version kept its prestige against a more exact one. The question has been part of the German record ever since, and the prestige's survival is itself evidence: the installed text held the stage and the schoolroom not because no alternative existed but because generations, offered alternatives, kept choosing the wording they knew. Installed idiom beats philological virtue in the marketplace of quotation, a law the Schlegel text illustrates from the winning side.

The twentieth century's challengers worked from the opposite bank. Erich Fried, the Austrian-born poet and translator, retranslated the play in the century's later decades as part of a Shakespeare rendered with a poet's ear for the original's grain. A passage-by-passage comparison of the two German versions, across the opening prose, two blank-verse passages, and the lovers' shared sonnet, credits Fried with the closer hold on the form and content of the English and Schlegel with the stronger pull toward the receiving language. The comparison's distribution of praise is the old split in modern dress: the challenger wins the audit, the incumbent keeps the audience, and the choice between them is a choice about what a translation is for.

Frank Günther built the challenge into a life's work and, unusually, into a public record of its own reasoning. His bilingual, commented Shakespeare translations have appeared with dtv since 1976, and Romeo und Julia stands among the early volumes of his complete edition from ars vivendi, catalogued as its fifth volume, furnished with a line-by-line commentary, an introductory essay by Christa Jansohn, and Günther's own Bericht aus der Übersetzerwerkstatt, a report from the translator's workshop, in which the decisions are confessed rather than concealed. His declared standard was performability: a present-day German audience should receive an impression answering to the one the original made on its first hearers. Where a pun could not cross, Günther sometimes invented replacement dialogue outright and documented the invention in his notes. The method is Schlegel's smoothing turned honest and systematic, the same willingness to rebuild rather than transcribe, practised under a duty of disclosure that the earlier classic never owed its readers. Günther died in 2020 with the edition's Sonnets volume unfinished, twenty-two sonnets rendered and the remainder completed by Christa Schuenke on his recommendation. The challenger's workshop, unlike the incumbent's, shows its scaffolding, and the contrast between the two texts' paratexts, silence in the one and confession in the other, is a small history of translation ethics in itself.

The challengers' collective failure to displace the incumbent is itself a datum in the audit, and it cuts in an unexpected direction. If the Schlegel text's smoothing were merely a defect, a century of rivals armed with the defect's exposure should have routed it; Fried's closer fidelity and Günther's playability each won their critical arguments and neither won the culture. The explanation returns the argument to the naturalisation's depth: the incumbent's advantage was never only textual. It was the accumulated capital of school memorisation, stage habit, quotation, and the sheer familiarity of phrases that German speakers mistake for the language's own property. A translation that has become idiom cannot be defeated by a better translation, only outlived, and the challengers' real achievement was to change the terms on which the incumbent is read: after Fried and Günther, no informed German reader meets Schlegel's smoothness as innocence. The classic survives, but it survives as a documented classic, its price list attached, which is the condition in which this article has presented it throughout.

## The German Stage and Its Romeo: From Dresden to a Berlin Musical

A translation becomes a national classic in the study, but it keeps the title in the theatre. The literature of the completed edition records the stage fact in one line: in Germany the plays are still performed largely in the Schlegel-Tieck translation, cut or reworked. The qualification matters as much as the claim. Acting texts have always cut the play, German acting texts among them, and the Schlegel wording that audiences heard across two centuries arrived filtered through prompt-book scissors. The classic's stage life is an adaptation chain laid over a stable page text, each generation of directors cutting the same revered wording to the measure of its own stage.

Tieck himself stands at the head of that documentary record. His long letter to Friedrich von Raumer describes a production at the Dresden court theatre in 1824 that attempted the then unusual discipline of retaining as much of the original play as possible, a landmark in the German movement away from the free adaptations of the Weiße era toward the text itself. The letter survives as evidence of the moment when fidelity became a stage programme rather than a study virtue. Later in the century Franz Dingelstedt planned editions of the plays prepared for stage practice from 1858 onward, an institutional admission that the classic text and the playable text were cousins rather than twins. The German stage's Romeo was Schlegel's, but Schlegel's as edited by the practical needs of companies, which is to say the German stage practised on its classic the same smoothing, by excision, that Schlegel had practised by diction.

The chain runs unbroken into the commercial theatre of the twenty-first century. The Berlin musical Romeo and Julia, Liebe ist alles, by Peter Plate and Ulf Leo Sommer, which opened at the Theater des Westens in 2023 and tours onward, speaks its dialogue in the historical Schlegel translation, according to the production's own materials. A pop score thus carries verse of the seventeen-nineties to a mass audience, and the producers report audiences past four hundred thousand by September 2025. No other Shakespeare translation in Europe has a comparable stage biography: born in a journal in 1796, enthroned at the classical court theatres, carried by school editions into the national memory, and still earning its keep in a commercial musical house. The stage record is the strongest external evidence for the celebrants' side of the argument. Whatever the auditors find on the page, the German theatre voted for Schlegel's text with two centuries of performances.

The cut deserves a moment's analysis, because it qualifies the fidelity story in a way the page audit cannot. Schlegel's programme promised the whole formal body of the play, and the page delivered it; the stage then amputated scenes, speeches, and subplots from the delivered body as freely as the prose-century adapters had done, differing only in keeping Schlegel's wording for whatever survived the scissors. German audiences therefore received the classic in two states at once: entire in the study, excerpted in the theatre, and the reverence attached to the wording survived the excerpting because the wording, not the wholeness, was what the culture had canonised. Dingelstedt's planned stage-practice editions, prepared from 1858, acknowledged the two states officially, offering the classic pre-cut for companies: the institution adapting its own monument to use. A text that can be cut for two centuries without losing its authority has the kind of classicism that lives in phrases rather than in structures, which is precisely the classicism the smoothing created, since a uniform lyrical surface yields detachable beauties more readily than a jagged one.

Tieck's Dresden letter rewards a closer reading, because it shows the fidelity programme migrating from the study to the stage under the editor's own hand. The 1824 court production he describes attempted to retain as much of the original play as the stage would bear, a deliberate break with the adaptation culture that had governed German Shakespeare since Weiße, and Tieck reported the experiment to Raumer as a matter of principle rather than of taste. The significance is structural: the man whose name completed the edition understood his office as a guardianship of the text against the theatre's habits, even while his own correction sessions retouched that text in the study. Fidelity, in the Schlegel-Tieck institution, was always a negotiated quantity, defended against adapters, adjusted by editors, and preached by both. The letter is the negotiation's most candid surviving document, and its survival in the record, through Siegfried Jacobsohn's later printing, lets the negotiation be audited like everything else in this history.

The musical datum should be weighed with the same care as every other exhibit, since a production's own materials are advocacy as well as evidence. What the Berlin production proves is narrower than its publicity suggests and still remarkable: a commercial creative team, free to commission any modern German text or write dialogue from nothing, chose verse from the seventeen-nineties as the spoken fabric of a show aimed at a general audience, and the choice survived contact with that audience at a scale the producers quantify in hundreds of thousands. Teams do not make such choices from piety alone; they make them because the old wording still plays, still lands its jokes and its grief at first hearing, in a way that flatter modern dialogue did not promise to do. The datum is thus a market test of the programme's central wager, that form carries effect across centuries, run under the least sentimental conditions imaginable, the box office. Schlegel's text passed the test in 2023 as it had passed the different tests of 1824 and 1864, and an audit that has recorded the version's losses all the more scrupulously should record this survival with equal precision: the classic is not merely revered, it is still, in the plainest commercial sense, usable.

The profile measures two variables separately: the survival of form, meaning metre, rhyme, sonnet architecture, and the prose and verse distribution; and the survival of content, meaning imagery, wordplay, obscenity, and metaphysical machinery. The distinction is the article's whole argument in miniature, since the profile's rows show the two variables behaving in opposite ways.

The eight passages were not chosen at random, and their selection follows a rule worth stating. ## The Verdict: A National Classic Bought with Smoothness

The critics divide into three camps, and the evidence assigns each camp its portion of the truth. The celebrants of the naturalisation, Gervinus at their head from 1849, claimed that Germany had made Shakespeare its own, and as a statement about cultural history the claim is simply correct: the adoption happened, the society was founded, the editions multiplied, the stages obeyed, and unser Shakespeare named a real possession. The auditors, whose classic statement in English is Margaret E. Atkinson's monograph August Wilhelm Schlegel as a Translator of Shakespeare of 1958, and whose work continues in the reassessment edited by Christa Jansohn and Stefan Knödler in 2023, examined the text line by line and found the smoothing that the profile records: elevated diction, regularised music, knots cut where the English is roughest. As a statement about the page, the audit is also correct, and the passages quoted in this article let any reader verify it. The practitioner Frank Günther, whose own bilingual German Shakespeare has been appearing since 1976 and whose Romeo und Julia stands among the early volumes of his complete edition, applies a third standard, playability for a present audience, and documents his own departures from the letter in a workshop commentary, conceding the purist case in his notes while rejecting its premise in his text.

Schleiermacher's dichotomy delivers the adjudication. Schlegel moved the reader toward the author in everything structural, and moved the author toward the reader in everything lexical, and the German classic is the product of that precise mixture. The celebrants mistook the mixture's success for fidelity entire. The auditors mistook the lexical smoothing for the whole story, underrating the formal courage that made the version revolutionary against the prose century. Günther's standard exposes what both camps miss: Schlegel's text played supremely for its audience because it was written in that audience's classical tongue, and the smoothing was not a failure of the playing but its mechanism. The verdict follows. The Schlegel Romeo and Juliet is the strongest historical case that a translation can become a national classic in its own right, and it bought that status with the original's roughness: the stars, the medlar, and the plague were the price, and German literature, which gained a classic, and English literature, which lost nothing it could not spare elsewhere, both profited by the exchange. The appropriation was real, and so was the gift.

The pattern is not Germany's alone. Wherever a national literature adopts this play, a translator's bargain with the receiving language decides what the nation will love, as [Pasternak's Russian Romeo and Juliet](/2030/05/03/pasternak-russian-translation/) shows in a sterner century, and as [Tsubouchi's Japanese translation](/2030/05/10/tsubouchi-japanese-translation/) shows where the translator became a national institution in his turn. Germany's case remains the type specimen because its documents are the fullest and its classic the longest-lived. The German Romeo and Juliet is a classic twice over, once as Shakespeare's play and once as Schlegel's poem, and the second classicism was purchased by planing the first. Two names stand on the spine, and the record corrects them both: the poem is one young man's, the purchase was a nation's, and the price list is printed, for any auditor to check, in the passages where the stars went out.

The predecessors supply a control experiment that sharpens the verdict's causal claim. Germany ran the alternatives. Grynaeus offered verse without the classical register in 1758, and his blank-verse Romeo founded no tradition. Wieland offered the play's matter in distinguished prose, and his Romeo was superseded the moment a verse rival appeared. Weiße offered the story adapted to the stage's taste, and his Romeo ruled only until a text worth ruling arrived. Schlegel's combination, verse form wedded to the Weimar classical register, is the single variable that distinguishes the version which became a classic from the versions which became footnotes, and the natural experiment licenses the conclusion the celebrants never quite stated: the smoothing was not an incidental blemish on the naturalisation but its enabling condition. A rougher, more foreign German Romeo would have been a better mirror and a smaller classic.

The appropriation frame, finally, should be carried through without flinching, since the article's subject is a canon changing hands. Germany's gain was real and so was the taking: a national literature enriched itself by annexing a foreign poet, translating him into its own classical idiom, and then citing the annexation as proof of its own universality. The 2023 reassessment edited by Jansohn and Knödler, with its attention to the early-Romantic programme behind the translations and to the differences between Schlegel's procedures and the Tieck circle's, shows the annexation still being unpacked by the culture that performed it. None of this diminishes the gift the version made to German readers, who received a great play in great German verse when their alternative was prose. It merely prices the gift correctly. The German Romeo and Juliet is what a translation looks like when it wins completely: the original survives, the receiving literature is enlarged, and the border between the two becomes, for most readers on the winning side, invisible.

One further counterfactual belongs to the English side of the ledger, and it disciplines any impulse to pity the original. English readers sometimes speak as if a great translation were a tax levied on the source, something the source survives diminished. The German case shows the opposite flow. Shakespeare's European stature, the very idea of Shakespeare as world literature rather than as a national dramatist, was built substantially in Germany, in Schlegel's verse and the criticism it licensed, and the prestige reimported itself into the English-speaking world's own valuation of its poet. The original gained a continent by being smoothed. Whatever the stars and the medlar were worth, and the audit has priced them honestly, they were spent on an appreciation of the whole that no unsmoothed crossing of the period could have purchased, and the appreciation compounded for two centuries in both languages.

The case finally instructs beyond itself, because it isolates the variable that most translation debates leave entangled. Fidelity is not one quantity but a portfolio, formal, lexical, tonal, functional, and a translator who maximises one holding must spend from another. Schlegel's portfolio is legible because his programme declared the weightings in advance: form held at par, lexis spent on elevation, obscenity and cosmology written down where they blocked the transfer. Later ages reweight the portfolio, Günther toward the functional, Fried toward the lexical grain, and call the reweighting progress, but the structure of the choice is the one Schlegel's practice first made visible at this scale. The German Romeo and Juliet endures as the discipline's clearest exhibit because its accounts were kept so well: a declared programme, a checkable text, two centuries of audits, and a nation that recorded, in the phrase unser Shakespeare, exactly what it thought it had bought.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which hand wrote the German Romeo and Juliet, Schlegel's or Tieck's?

Schlegel's hand alone wrote it. August Wilhelm Schlegel translated the play at the start of his Shakespeare enterprise, printing it in Schiller's journal Die Horen in 1796 and in book form in 1797 as the opening play of his Shakspeare's dramatische Werke. Ludwig Tieck's connection with the German Shakespeare began only in 1819, when Schlegel entrusted him with completing the set, more than two decades after the Romeo existed in German. Tieck translated none of this play, and the completed edition's title pages say as much, crediting Schlegel as translator and Tieck as the man who supplemented and annotated the whole. The double name describes the set, not this play. A further consequence follows for citation: quoting the German Romeo as Schlegel-Tieck, as habit does, credits an editor for a translator's work and should be reserved for the completed edition as a whole, while the play itself belongs in references to Schlegel alone.

### Q: How does the German Prologue handle the phrase star-crossed lovers?

The German Prologue drops the stars entirely. Shakespeare's Chorus calls the pair star-cross'd lovers and their love death-mark'd, building the play's astrology into its first fourteen lines. Schlegel's sonnet keeps the fourteen lines and the rhymes but renders the lovers' origin as a life springing from the foes' ill-fated loins, Aus dieser Feinde unheilvollem Schoß das Leben zweier Liebender entsprang, and turns death-mark'd into todgeweiht, consecrated to death. Fate survives as solemnity rather than as astrology. The excision is the smoothing thesis in miniature: the form of the English sonnet is preserved with scrupulous care while its cosmic machinery, the crossed stars that govern the tragedy, is quietly removed. The loss propagates, since the play's later defiance of the stars loses the keynote the Prologue was meant to strike.

### Q: Does the Schlegel text keep the lovers' shared sonnet as a sonnet?

It keeps the shared sonnet as a complete rhymed sonnet. The first exchange between Romeo and Juliet at the Capulet feast, which Shakespeare shapes as a single sonnet built by two speakers, stands in Schlegel's German as a rhymed sonnet with its pilgrim and saint figures, its quatrain structure, and its closing couplet all intact. The fidelity is the point of Schlegel's announced programme, verse answered by verse in the same form, and the shared sonnet is the programme's hardest test in this play, since loosening the exchange into dialogue would have preserved every fact while destroying the scene's meaning, which is that the lovers' speech harmonises into one poem at first contact. The survival is the more telling because sonnet form in German, with the language's easy inflectional rhymes, tempts a translator toward facility; Schlegel's sonnet keeps the English poem's difficulty as well as its shape.

### Q: How does Juliet's wherefore line read in German?

Juliet's line reads O Romeo! Warum denn Romeo? Verleugne deinen Vater, deinen Namen. The warum preserves the true sense of wherefore, which asks why Romeo must be Romeo, why he carries the enemy name, and not where he is standing. Schlegel compresses the doubled Romeo of the English into a single call and folds refuse thy name into the denial of the name itself, but the philosophy of the scene survives entire, including the rose speech that follows: what is a name, and that which we call a rose would smell sweet under any other name. The German wording became canonical in its turn, the form in which German quotation remembers the scene. The rendering also settled a scholarly point in advance: generations of German readers, meeting warum, were never tempted by the vulgar error that reads wherefore as a question about where Romeo is hiding.

### Q: What did Schlegel do with the servants' prose in the opening scene?

Schlegel kept the servants' exchange as prose and re-punned it in German materials. Shakespeare's Sampson and Gregory open the play with a chain of wordplay on coals, colliers, choler, and collars that cannot cross languages mechanically. The German text rebuilds the jokes from German stock: pocketing insults becomes Taschenspieler, pickpocket play, and the leather jerkin, the Koller, takes over the collar's duties, while the thumb-biting challenge becomes the native German insult of boring a donkey. The method is the programme's prose clause in action, prose for prose, with the wordplay replaced rather than translated. The register survives the crossing even where the individual jokes are new inventions in the receiving language. The scene therefore tests the programme's prose clause exactly as the sonnets test its verse clause, and it passes on both counts: German servants banter in prose, at pace, with jokes that work in German rather than footnotes that explain English ones.

### Q: How many plays did Baudissin translate for the completed edition?

Baudissin translated thirteen plays for the completed edition. Wolf Heinrich Graf von Baudissin, a diplomat and man of letters, was the completion's chief workhorse after Schlegel entrusted the enterprise to Ludwig Tieck in 1819 and Tieck's own translating proved small in quantity. Dorothea Tieck supplied five plays, or six in some accounts, and Tieck presided as editor, so the nineteen missing plays divide as thirteen to Baudissin, a handful to Dorothea, and the remainder to the editorial circle. The count matters because the completed Shakespeare of 1825 to 1833 is remembered under two famous names, Schlegel and Tieck, while its largest single block of new translation after Schlegel's own is Baudissin's thirteen. The count also corrects the spine's implication in the other direction: Tieck, the edition's second name, personally translated fewer of the completions than either of the two workers his editorship employed.

### Q: What was Tieck's correction hour, and why did it anger Schlegel?

The correction hour, the Corrigirstunde, was the working session in which Ludwig Tieck and his collaborators retouched the edition's texts, and it angered Schlegel because its revisions reached into his own plays. The scholarly tradition records that Schlegel's translations did not escape the correction, to his indignation: the completer's circle was polishing the founder's verse in the founder's lifetime, without his consent. The episode exposes the completed edition's real nature as a collective, evolving artefact rather than a fixed monument. Schlegel had surrendered the enterprise in 1819, but surrendering the continuation was one thing and watching his Romeo and his Hamlet adjusted by another household's taste was another, and the record keeps his resentment. The retouching was light, and the completed editions carried his texts substantially as he left them, which is why the grievance is remembered as a footnote rather than a scandal.

### Q: What does the phrase unser Shakespeare claim?

The phrase claims ownership. Unser Shakespeare, our Shakespeare, asserts that the English poet had become a German possession by right of labour: German translators had given him complete and careful texts, German editors had annotated him, German stages had honoured him, and German readers had made him a classic, while England, in the claim's polemical form, had neglected its own. Critics such as Gervinus argued the naturalisation at length from 1849, and the German Shakespeare Society, founded at Weimar in 1864, institutionalised the guardianship. The claim's vehicle was Schlegel's translation, which made the plays sound like German classics. The compliment conceals a takeover, since the Shakespeare Germany owned was partly remade in Germany's classical image. The phrase's grammar makes the point by itself: unser is a possessive, and a nation does not use possessives about property it regards as borrowed.

### Q: Why did Schlegel stop translating Shakespeare in 1801?

The documented immediate cause is a quarrel with his publisher, Johann Friedrich Unger, which ended the arrangement in 1801 with the set incomplete. Biographical accounts add the collapse of Schlegel's marriage to Caroline, whose preparatory and revising hand had stood behind the enterprise from its early years and whose departure dismantled the workshop. Schlegel lived until 1845, lectured across Europe, and never resumed the translations, leaving seventeen plays as a celebrated torso. The continuation waited until 1819, when he entrusted it to Ludwig Tieck, and the completed edition of 1825 to 1833 was executed chiefly by Baudissin and Dorothea Tieck. The stop is therefore twofold in the record: a publishing rupture, and a household's dissolution behind it. Neither cause was artistic, which is the point the record supports: the greatest German Shakespeare was abandoned for money and marriage, not for any failure of the work, and its completion had to wait for another household's patience.

### Q: How does the German text render Mercutio's plague on both houses?

The German text renders the curse without the plague. Shakespeare's Mercutio cries a plague o' both your houses three times, and the word binds his death to the plague that later seals Friar John's letter and destroys the lovers' rescue. Schlegel's Mercutio cries Zum Teufel beider Sippschaft, to the devil with both clans, then Hol der Henker eure beiden Häuser, may the hangman take both your houses, and finally Zum Teufel eure Häuser. Devil and hangman replace disease, and the repetition survives while the referent changes. The substitution is characteristic smoothing: the imprecation keeps its dramatic force and loses its thematic wiring, the verbal echo between the curse and the quarantine that the English text builds into its catastrophe. German productions inherit the weakened echo, one reason the German play's catastrophe can feel more like fate's solemnity than like the English play's machinery of accident and disease.

### Q: What is lost in the German version of Mercutio's grave man pun?

The tomb is lost, and the stillness remains. Shakespeare's dying joke, ask for me tomorrow and you shall find me a grave man, fuses grave the adjective, meaning serious, with grave the noun, meaning tomb, so that the jest predicts the corpse. German cannot fuse the two senses in one word, and Schlegel chooses einen stillen Mann, a quiet or still man. The choice keeps the death inside the line by implication, since the still man of tomorrow is the dead man, but the pun's mechanism, one word carrying wit and burial together, stops at the border. The case is the method's floor in miniature: where the English effect depends on an accident of English vocabulary, the German text can gesture at the meaning and cannot rebuild the machine. Later German translators faced the identical wall at the identical line, which acquits Schlegel of idiosyncrasy: the loss belongs to the language pair, and only its handling, quiet here, is his.

### Q: Did the German translation bowdlerise the opening scene's bawdy?

The opening scene's bawdy survives, veiled rather than removed. Sampson's threats against the Montague maids keep their sexual content in Schlegel: he will rob them of their maidenly heads, take their maidenhood, take it in either sense, and the boasting about standing and being a pretty piece of flesh runs its full course in German. The diction is decorous where the English is blunt, jungfräuliche Häupter for maidenheads, but the exchange's meaning is intact and its threat unmistakable. The finding disciplines the smoothing thesis: Schlegel's cuts fall on the untranslatable pun and the cosmic machinery, not on bawdy as such. Mercutio's conjuration catalogue of Rosaline's body likewise stands unexpurgated in the second act, a further control on any simple prudery charge. The accurate charge is narrower and more interesting: the translation protects the audience from the cosmos and from untranslatable filth, while trusting the audience with every bawdy passage whose sense can be rebuilt in German materials.

The profile tests the verdict by making its prediction checkable passage by passage. ### Q: How does Frank Günther's German Romeo and Juliet differ in aim?

Günther's version aims at playability for a present-day German audience. Where Schlegel wrote in the classical literary language of his own age, seeking the effect of a classic, Günther, whose bilingual commented Shakespeare translations have appeared since 1976 and whose Romeo und Julia stands among the early volumes of his complete edition, seeks the effect the original had on its first audiences: immediacy in the theatre. For wordplay that cannot cross, he sometimes invents replacement dialogue and documents each decision in his commentary and workshop report, an openness about departure that contrasts with the silent smoothing of the earlier text. The difference is one of contract with the audience: Schlegel promised a monument, Günther promises a performance, and each text keeps its own promise. Günther's edition also differs in apparatus, printing the English facing the German with a commentary and a workshop report, so that the reader can audit the departures Schlegel's readers had to take on trust.

### Q: What role did the Reclam editions play in the translation's reach?

The Reclam editions carried the translation from the library into the satchel and the railway carriage. Reclam's Universal-Bibliothek, the cheap pocket series that standardised the German classics, issued Romeo und Julia in Schlegel's version from the eighteen-seventies, with an 1876 printing standing in the catalogues, and the Reclam text became the default form in which school generations and general readers met the play. Cheap editions are canon machines: a text priced for students gets quoted, memorised, and taught until its wording feels like the work itself. The publisher's description of the standard Reclam edition records the outcome, a version that grew across the nineteenth century into an independent German classic, and the classroom reach of the pocket format is a large part of how the growing happened. Price is a canonising force in its own right: the cheapest text becomes the quoted text, and the quoted text becomes the work.

### Q: Does any German stage production still speak Schlegel's words?

A Berlin musical speaks them nightly. Romeo und Julia, Liebe ist alles, by Peter Plate and Ulf Leo Sommer, opened at the Theater des Westens in 2023 and tours onward, and according to the production's own materials its dialogue is spoken in the historical Schlegel translation, the verse of 1796 and 1797 carrying the spoken scenes between a pop score's songs. The producers report audiences past four hundred thousand by September 2025. The datum closes the stage argument of two centuries: the completed edition's literature already recorded that German stages perform the plays largely in the Schlegel-Tieck wording, cut or reworked, and the musical extends that record into the commercial present, with a mass audience hearing a young Jena translator's lines as the natural voice of the lovers. A pop audience humming the score is quoting the seventeen-nineties without knowing it, which is what an installed classic means.

### Q: What did Schleiermacher's 1813 lecture say a translator must choose?

The lecture said a translator must choose between two roads and cannot travel both. In Über die verschiedenen Methoden des Übersetzens, on the different methods of translating, delivered to the Berlin Academy in 1813, Friedrich Schleiermacher argued that the translator either leaves the reader in peace and moves the author toward the reader, naturalising the foreign work, or leaves the author in peace and moves the reader toward the author, educating the reader into the foreignness. Schlegel's Romeo, published sixteen years before the lecture, enacts the choice split down the middle: its forms move the reader toward Shakespeare, sonnet for sonnet and couplet for couplet, while its diction moves Shakespeare toward the reader, into the polished classical German of the Weimar age. Schleiermacher, who knew the translations, supplied the theory for a practice that had preceded it, and the theory remains the sharpest instrument for grading what Schlegel did.

### Q: Why does the German Romeo and Juliet read more smoothly than the English?

The smoothness is the product of a diction chosen for uniformity. Schlegel wrote the play in the literary language of German classicism in the wake of Goethe and Schiller, as the Reclam description states, a high, regular, lyrically closed register, and he held that register across the whole play, where Shakespeare's English mixes high lyric with low pun, cosmology with obscenity, and verse with prose. Three kinds of knot disappear in the levelling: untranslatable wordplay such as the medlar, indecorous matter that the replacement renders polite, and cosmic machinery such as the Prologue's stars and Mercutio's plague. Margaret Atkinson's 1958 monograph audited the result line by line, and the 2023 reassessment edited by Christa Jansohn and Stefan Knödler continues the audit. The classic's famous Geschlossenheit, its linguistic closedness, is the smoothing viewed as a virtue. The smoothness also had a selection effect on the play's German afterlife: passages that survive levelling, the balcony lyricism above all, became the culture's chosen quotations, while the levelled passages dropped out of the national memory altogether.

### Q: Is the Schlegel-Tieck Romeo and Juliet a German classic or an English one?

It is both, and the doubleness is the answer rather than a hedge. As Shakespeare's play, the text is English in origin, plot, and architecture, and Schlegel's formal fidelity, sonnet for sonnet and prose for prose, keeps that identity intact on the German page. As Schlegel's poem, the text is a German classic in its own right, written in the classical language of the Weimar age, naturalised by two centuries of German stages, schools, and quotation, and smoother, more lyrical, and more uniform than its original. The German Shakespeare Society, Gervinus's commentaries, and the phrase unser Shakespeare all register the second identity. A translation that a nation adopts as its own classic has crossed the border it was built to cross, and this one crossed it more completely than any rival in Europe. The border, in this case, can be located precisely: it runs between the title page, which stayed English in its facts, and the German ear, which stopped hearing a foreigner.
