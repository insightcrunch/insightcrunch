Postcolonial theory and Marxism are usually presented as rival answers to a single question, as though a reader had to pick a side before opening a book. That presentation is the first error this article exists to correct. The two bodies of work are not competing explanations of the same thing. They study different things that happen to share a setting, the way a hydrologist and a historian of religion might both study a river: one measures flow, silt, and the labour that dug the canals, while the other asks how the river was represented, worshipped, and written into the identities of the people along its banks. Neither study refutes the other, because their objects differ even where their territory overlaps. Postcolonial theory takes as its object the discourse of colonialism: the representations, categories, and forms of subjectivity through which colonised peoples were made knowable and governable, and through which they came to know themselves. Marxist approaches take as their object the social relations of colonialism: who owned the land, who laboured and under what compulsion, how surplus was extracted and where it flowed, and what classes the process created and destroyed.

The difference can be made concrete with a single document, before any names are introduced. Consider a colonial settlement report, the kind in which a revenue officer classified the villages of a district by soil, tenure, and the customs he attributed to their inhabitants. A Marxist reading of that report asks what the settlement did: how the assessment fixed a money demand on the cultivator, how it converted customary rights into a legible property regime, which groups gained a saleable title and which lost the commons, and how the resulting pressures sorted a peasantry into landlords, tenants, and labourers. A postcolonial reading of the same report asks what the settlement said: how its categories manufactured the objects it claimed merely to describe, how the figure of the timeless village and the fanatical tribesman were assembled from administrative need, and how colonised readers later inherited those categories as the vocabulary of their own self-understanding. Both readings are about colonialism. Neither, by itself, answers the questions the other poses. Much of the quarrel this article reconstructs comes from mistaking that division of labour for a contradiction, and from each side periodically claiming that its object is the only real one. The reader who finishes this article should be able to state the difference of objects precisely, to identify the one determinate historical claim on which the sharpest Marxist criticism actually fastens, to explain why a single historiographical collective is claimed by both traditions as its own, and to judge what a combined account would have to accomplish. Those are the four pieces of equipment the comparison requires, and they are delivered in that order.

## Postcolonial theory and Marxism: two objects, not two answers

The cleanest way to hold the distinction is to notice that each tradition has a characteristic question it asks of any colonial scene, and that the questions do not compete. The Marxist question is genetic and structural: how did this arrangement arise, whose labour sustains it, and what does it do to the distribution of property and power? The postcolonial question is representational and subjective: how was this arrangement described, legitimised, and internalised, and what kinds of person did its descriptions call into being? A plantation can be interrogated either way. One line of inquiry follows the wage, the barracoon, the export ledger, and the laws that made labour coercible. The other follows the planter's memoir, the missionary's ethnography, the novel that taught metropolitan readers to feel the tropics as a theatre of their own virtue or decay. The scene is identical; the object extracted from it is not. When polemicists announce that one side has refuted the other, inspection usually finds that an answer about the ledger has been waved at a question about the memoir, or the reverse. The verdict of this article, stated at the outset so that the evidence can be weighed against it, is that the two approaches take different objects, that the strongest criticism each side makes of the other lands against a specific degenerate form of the other rather than against its legitimate object, and that the ferocity of the dispute is explained less by logic than by a trajectory: the collective at the centre of the quarrel changed its mind over two decades, and both camps have been arguing with different phases of its work while believing they were arguing about the whole.

### What is the difference between Marxism and postcolonial theory?

chiefly the object and the method. Postcolonial theory analyses colonialism through discourse, representation, and subjectivity. Marxist approaches analyse it through production, class, and the world market. They also disagree about whether capitalism universalises social relations across the colonised world, and about what drives historical change.

That answer deserves one immediate refinement, because the word object can mislead. The claim is not that the two traditions look at different empires or different centuries. Both read the same archives, walk the same districts, and cite many of the same witnesses. The claim is that each tradition constitutes its object differently out of that shared material, in the way that a chemist and a cook constitute different objects out of the same fish. For the Marxist line of work, colonialism is a phase and instrument of a world economy: an arrangement for moving land, labour, and raw material into circuits of accumulation centred elsewhere, maintained by states whose coercive apparatus was part of the economic machinery rather than an ornament upon it. For the postcolonial line, colonialism is a regime of knowledge as much as of extraction: a vast machinery for producing authoritative descriptions of the colonised, descriptions that outlived the flags and continued to organise scholarship, literature, and selfhood long after formal independence. It follows that the two traditions also disagree about causation. Where the Marxist account runs from material relations to ideas, treating representation largely as the legitimation or the lived texture of an underlying structure, the postcolonial account insists that representation is itself a material force, that empires could not have ruled populations they had not first rendered thinkable as subjects of rule. These are genuinely different theories, and the sections that follow give each its full strength before any grading begins. But different theories of different objects can clash only at the points where their objects touch, and one task of a careful comparison is to find those points and refuse to manufacture others.

A second preliminary distinction protects the whole discussion. Postcolonial theory is not a single position, and neither is its Marxist interlocutor. The label gathers literary scholars working on the novel, historians working on colonial archives, philosophers working on the fate of universal categories, and political theorists working on nationalism, bound by a family resemblance rather than a creed. Some of its canonical figures began inside Marxist projects and never entirely left them; some Marxist critics of the field are themselves colonial historians whose own work attends closely to representation. Any summary that begins by treating either camp as a bloc has already surrendered the precision the subject requires. The article therefore works with named texts and dated arguments throughout: a founding study of 1978, an essay of 1988 that posed the question of the subaltern voice, a body of concepts from the following decade concerned with hybridity and mimicry, a historiographical collective founded in South Asia in the early 1980s, a critique of 1992, an essay of 1994, and a book-length critique of 2013 whose replies occupy a full section of what follows. Where a claim cannot be attached to a named author and a dated work, it is not made. That discipline matters most at the points of highest temperature, because the dispute has generated a folklore of attributed positions, phrases everyone is said to hold and no one can be found holding, and the folklore serves only the polemicists. The reader is entitled to better, and the better is available: these were writers, and their books can be opened.

## The objects and methods grid

Comparisons of this kind fail most often by drift: terms slide between one paragraph and the next until the two approaches have been silently redefined into whatever makes the verdict easy. A grid prevents drift by fixing the dimensions of comparison in advance and holding both sides to them. The grid below is the findable artifact of this article, designed to be lifted out and used. It sets five dimensions side by side: the object of study, the method, the treatment of capitalism, the account of the agent who makes history, and the explanatory reach of each approach, including what lies beyond it. A final column states what a combined account would require on that dimension, which converts the grid from a scorecard into a specification: it shows that combination is an engineering problem with named requirements, not a mood of goodwill between camps. The rows are then examined one by one in the sections that follow, because a grid asserts in compressed form what prose must earn at length. Readers in a hurry can work from the table alone; readers who stay for the argument will find the table's cells becoming less comfortable and more useful as the qualifications accumulate. That is the intended experience. A comparison instrument that never becomes uncomfortable is flattering somebody.

| Dimension | Marxist approaches | Postcolonial theory | What a combined account would require |
|---|---|---|---|
| Object of study | Social relations of production: property, labour, extraction, class formation | Discourse and representation: categories, texts, subjectivity, the colonised as known | A scene analysed at both levels, with the relations between levels stated rather than assumed |
| Method | Historical materialism: archival and economic reconstruction of structures over time | Discourse analysis and close reading: the archive read as a producer of its objects | Reading the same documents twice, once for what they did and once for what they made thinkable |
| Treatment of capitalism | A world system that tends to universalise its relations, unevenly and violently | A system whose universals arrive fractured by colonial difference and are never complete | A defended position on how far universalisation proceeds and where it is blocked, with evidence |
| Account of the agent | Classes and organised collectivities as the bearers of historical change | Subjects formed within discourse, whose agency appears as negotiation, mimicry, and refusal | Agents who are both positioned by structures and formed by representations, with neither reduced to the other |
| What each can and cannot explain | Explains extraction, crisis, and the material interests of empire; weaker on meaning, identity, and the inner life of domination | Explains how rule was legitimised and internalised and how categories persist; weaker on extraction, accumulation, and material causation | An account that loses neither the ledger nor the memoir, and says which is doing the work in each episode |

The first row does the heaviest lifting, and its formulation was chosen with care. To call the Marxist object social relations is to refuse the vulgar version in which the object is a pile of wealth or a trade statistic; relations between people, organised around property and labour, are what the tradition claims to reconstruct, and its best practitioners read texts as evidence for relations they cannot observe directly. To call the postcolonial object discourse is equally to refuse a vulgar version, in which discourse means mere words floating free of institutions; in the serious literature the term names the regulated production of statements by archives, universities, armies, and administrations, backed by the power to make the statements consequential. A census category is discourse, and it could get a village taxed, conscripted, or disarmed. The grid's first row therefore does not oppose the material to the immaterial. It opposes two registers of the material: the register of who labours and who appropriates, and the register of who is described and who describes. Colonial rule operated in both registers continuously, which is why single-register accounts keep generating the sense, accurate as far as it goes, that something central has been left out. The sections on each object now take them in turn, at the length their literatures deserve.

A note on the fourth column is warranted before the detail begins. Combination is the standing temptation of every comparison, and it is usually proposed in a form that costs nothing: take the economics from one side and the sensitivity to culture from the other and stir. The grid refuses that version by attaching requirements to each row. A combined account of the agent, for instance, cannot be assembled by declaring that class and subjectivity both matter; it must specify how a person formed by colonial categories comes to act as a member of a class, and how class position inflects the categories available to her, in a particular episode with evidence. Where scholars have attempted that specification, the results are among the most valuable work in the whole field, and the section on combined accounts below returns to them. Where the specification is missing, combination is a compliment the two traditions pay each other at conferences while continuing to work exactly as before. The grid is printed here so that readers can test any proposed synthesis, including the ones this article admires, against requirements rather than against rhetoric.

## Objections to the comparison itself: is the verdict too peaceful?

A comparison that concludes with different objects owes its readers a hearing for the suspicion that the conclusion is a sedative: that declaring the two approaches sovereign over separate territories merely licenses each to ignore the other, and that the history of thought knows no such tidy partitions. The suspicion has force and should be met head on. The grid's claim is not that the territories are separate by nature. It is that the two literatures, as actually constituted, take different objects, and that most of their celebrated refutations fail for that contingent, historical reason. Where the objects genuinely overlap, on the crowd, on the nation, on the category of caste, the article has insisted on real competition and real stakes, and the universalisation quarrel survives the grid precisely because it occurs at an overlap: both traditions claim the same phenomenon, capitalism's career in the colonies, and describe it incompatibly. The verdict is therefore not peace but triage. It dissolves the battles that were never joined and concentrates attention on the few that were, and it is falsifiable in a way peace treaties are not: a critic who can show a major celebrated refutation that does join, on the same object with the same evidence, has damaged the verdict at its centre, and the article invites the attempt.

### Is the different objects verdict an evasion?

It would be an evasion if it dissolved every disagreement, and it does not: it dissolves the refutations that were aimed across registers, and it leaves standing, deliberately, the disagreements where both traditions claim the same phenomenon, above all the career of capital in the colonies, where the verdict sides with neither and specifies the evidence instead.

A second objection runs deeper and should also be heard before the detail resumes. It holds that the objects themselves are artefacts of the dispute: that there was no natural joint in colonial reality where discourse ended and production began, and that the two fields constitute their objects precisely in order not to meet. The observation is correct and does not damage the comparison, because the comparison's premise is identical. Objects in the human sciences are constituted by questions; the settlement report does not arrive labelled with its registers. What the grid compares is not slices of a given reality but two acts of constitution, and its judgement is that the acts, having been performed, generated literatures whose claims mostly fail to intersect, whatever one thinks of the carving. The deeper objection then converts into a question the article is content to leave open, since it is the question every practitioner of either field should carry: whether the carving itself, the separation of meaning from material life into different professions, is the colonial university's most durable bequest, more durable than any category it installed overseas. The founding phase of the collective, which read a police report for an insurgent's soul, and the Marxist historiography that read a rent roll for a civilisation, were both, in their beginnings, attempts to refuse the carving. That they ended as the two halves of a grid in a comparison article is a fact about institutions, and the reader is free to find it melancholy. The grid's defence is modest: it maps the carving so that those who wish to refuse it can see where the cuts were made.

## The first object: colonialism as discourse, representation, and subjectivity

Postcolonial theory, stated in its strongest form, begins from an observation that is difficult to deny once it has been pointed out: the colonised world was described before it was administered, and the descriptions did administrative work. European knowledge of the Orient, of Africa, of India, was not a neutral stock of facts that conquerors happened to consult. It was produced inside institutions of rule, financed by them, and organised by their needs, and its products bore the marks of that origin: the industrious and the indolent races, the martial and the effeminate peoples, the fanatical believer and the childlike peasant, each figure arriving with a policy already implied. To analyse colonialism through discourse is to treat that machinery of description as a primary object of study, on the grounds that rule over millions requires more than garrisons. It requires clerks who believe the categories on their forms, officers who perceive the populations in front of them through the figures their training supplied, and, most consequentially, colonised elites who learn to argue for their own freedom in the vocabulary the descriptions left available. The claim is not that representation replaced extraction, a claim almost none of the serious literature makes. The claim is that extraction was organised, legitimised, and prolonged by representations that must therefore be studied as part of the machinery, not as its decorative superstructure. A reader who grants only that much has already granted the field its object.

### What does it mean to study colonialism as discourse?

It means treating the authoritative descriptions of colonised peoples, produced by scholarship, administration, literature, and law, as a working part of colonial rule itself: a machinery that classified populations, licensed policies, and formed the self-understanding of rulers and ruled, and that therefore has to be analysed as a cause and not merely as a commentary on causes located elsewhere.

Three subordinate claims give the object its contours, and separating them prevents the usual caricature. The first concerns knowledge: colonial scholarship is studied as a corpus with rules of formation, deciding what could count as a fact about the colonised, which texts were canonical, which informants were credible, and which questions were askable at all. The second concerns the subject: colonised people were not only governed but invited, compelled, and educated to understand themselves through the categories supplied to them, so that resistance itself often spoke in borrowed grammar, claiming the rights of the citizen in the idiom of the empire or the purity of a tradition the ethnographers had helped to codify. The third concerns persistence: the representational machinery outlasted the administrations that built it, surviving into the literatures, curricula, and national mythologies of the successor states, which is why a field concerned with aftermaths gives texts such extended attention. Each claim can be examined separately, and each has been examined, often critically, from within the field itself. What they share is a refusal to treat ideas about the colonised as a transparent window onto a reality that can be studied without them. The window, on this account, was ground in a particular workshop, and the grinding is part of the history.

The method follows from the object. If discourse is the object, then the instruments are those of the reader: close attention to genre, trope, citation, and silence; the recovery of what an archive cannot say; the tracking of a figure, the sensual East or the martial race, as it migrates from a traveller's tale into a recruitment manual and from there into a census schedule. Literary training dominates the field's founding generation for the unmysterious reason that these instruments were sharpened in literature departments, and the migration of the questions into history, anthropology, and political theory carried the instruments along. This disciplinary fact explains several features of the field that its critics find exasperating and its practitioners find indispensable: the preference for the exemplary text over the statistical series, the willingness to let a novel bear evidentiary weight about a mentality, the suspicion of any aggregate that has not first been interrogated for the categories composing it. Whether those preferences are virtues depends on the question being asked, which is precisely the grid's point. Put to a passage of the colonial novel, the method yields knowledge obtainable no other way. Put to a famine, it must borrow or fall silent, a limitation the honest wing of the field states plainly and its critics state relentlessly.

## The second object: colonialism as production, class, and the world market

The Marxist object is older, larger, and organised around a single insistent thought: colonialism was a way of rearranging the material life of the colonised world so that its land and labour fed accumulation elsewhere, and everything else about it, its ideologies included, must be explained in relation to that rearrangement. The conquest of the Americas, the Atlantic slave trade, the deindustrialisation debates around Indian textiles, the plantation complexes, the mining concessions, the forced cultivation systems, the hut taxes designed to drive subsistence producers into wage labour: these are the tradition's home exhibits, and its categories were built to handle them. Production names the process by which a society reproduces itself materially; class names the positions that process assigns, owner and labourer, landlord and tenant, creditor and debtor; the world market names the arena in which formally separate territories were bound into a single division of labour, so that a decision in a Manchester mill or a London discount house could close a weaving town in Bengal or open a copper belt in central Africa. To analyse colonialism in this register is to ask, of every institution the empire built, what it did to the flow of labour and surplus, and to treat the answers as the explanation to which other accounts must be added rather than the backdrop against which they float.

### What does a Marxist explanation of colonialism have to identify?

It has to identify the property relations the conquest installed, the labour it mobilised and the coercion that mobilisation required, the surplus it extracted and the circuits through which surplus moved, and the classes those processes created, destroyed, or froze in place, because on this account a colony's politics and culture are unintelligible until its material constitution is reconstructed.

Two features of this object deserve emphasis because they are routinely missed by its critics. The first is that the Marxist object includes the colonised as makers of their own history in a specific and demanding sense: classes form, combine, and fight, and the tradition's historiography is full of strikes, insurgencies, and evasions that altered the terms of extraction, recorded because the theory says such action is where change comes from. Agency here is collective and interested rather than expressive; a crowd appears in this literature not as a voice to be interpreted but as a force to be reckoned with, whose composition, organisation, and demands explain what happened next. The second feature is the world market's role as a leveller in the theory's imagination. Because capital seeks cheaper labour and wider markets everywhere, the tradition expects it to dissolve local peculiarities over time, battering down tariff walls and customary tenures alike, and that expectation of universalisation is the precise point at which the postcolonial rejoinder fastens, as the later sections show in detail. The disagreement is therefore not a misunderstanding to be cleared up by goodwill. At the level of what capitalism does to difference, the two traditions make rival claims about the world, and the quarrel over universalisation is a real quarrel, with evidence on both sides and a named battlefield in the historiography of one South Asian collective. Everything else in the dispute is negotiable around that kernel; the kernel is not negotiable, because each side's wider architecture leans on its answer.

It is worth pausing on the evidentiary style that belongs to this object, since the comparison of methods recurs throughout the later sections. The Marxist historian's characteristic unit is the series: prices, wages, revenue assessments, trade volumes, the changing composition of a workforce across decades, from which structures are inferred that no participant could have seen whole. The archive is used against its grain in a particular direction, reading the administrator's tables for what they reveal about the relations the administrator was managing, while discounting his interpretations as the ideology of his office. Interpretation itself is not despised; it is assigned a place, downstream of reconstruction. This ordering, structure first and meaning second, is the methodological signature of the tradition, and its collision with the opposite ordering in the rival tradition accounts for a surprising share of the mutual incomprehension the dispute exhibits. Each side experiences the other's ordering as a dereliction: to begin with representation looks, from the structural side, like studying the menu while the kitchen burns; to begin with structure looks, from the representational side, like trusting the arsonist's own inventory of the house. The grid's method row holds both orderings still long enough for the reader to see that they are orderings, choices about where explanation starts, rather than discoveries about where reality starts.

## The dependency detour: the world market theorised before the quarrel

The structural column of the grid had its own great age of colonial theory before the postcolonial field existed, and the comparison is incomplete without it, because the postcolonial turn defined itself against this inheritance as much as against stagist orthodoxy. In the decades after the Second World War, a body of Marxist and Marxisant work centred increasingly outside Europe reconstructed the world economy as a single system of centres and peripheries: Andre Gunder Frank's thesis that development and underdevelopment are two faces of one process, the metropolis developing by underdeveloping its satellites; Samir Amin's account of accumulation on a world scale and of the peripheral formations it produced; Immanuel Wallerstein's world systems analysis, with its core, semiperiphery, and periphery exchanging roles across centuries. Whatever their differences, these theories shared the grid's second object at maximum scale. Colonialism in their pages is a transfer machine, and its explanation is complete when the flows of surplus, the specialisations imposed, and the classes thereby deformed have been traced. Their achievement was real: they destroyed the modernisation story in which each nation recapitulated a common path at its own speed, and they made the poverty of the periphery an effect of the wealth of the centre rather than a survival from before it. Their characteristic blindness was the reciprocal of the literary field's: culture, representation, and subjectivity appear in this literature, when they appear at all, as the local colouring of positions in the world division of labour, and a reader formed by it can explain why a colony exported copper for a century without once asking how its people were taught to describe themselves.

### What did the dependency theorists explain, and what did they miss?

They explained the periphery's poverty as a product of the same world process that enriched the centre, tracing the flows of surplus and the specialisations empire imposed; they missed, or treated as colouring, the representational machinery through which the colonised were classified, governed, and taught to understand themselves.

The postcolonial turn can be reread, with this inheritance in view, as an internal correction that changed address. The generation that formed the field had been educated in the dependency decades and found its explanatory satisfaction and its human thinness in equal measure; the colonised of the dependency texts are positions, balances, and classes, rarely speakers, and the experience of being classified, converted, schooled, and imagined, the daily texture of colonial subjection, had no theoretical home in the transfer machine. The linguistic turn offered the home. That the correction overshot, in the manner the first wave of criticism documented, abandoning the flows for the texts almost as completely as the flows had abandoned the texts, is the dialectic the grid exists to interrupt. It also explains a peculiarity of the dispute's geography: the argument was bitterest precisely where the dependency inheritance was strongest, in South Asia and Latin America, because there the abandonment was felt as the desertion of a going concern rather than as a change of fashion, and the internal dissents surveyed later in this article, Sarkar's above all, were written by men who had expected the collective to enrich the structural account with the insurgent's voice, not to trade the one for the other. The reader should hold the dependency detour in mind when the verdict distributes praise: the structural tradition arrived at the comparison with an economics the postcolonial field never replaced, only vacated, and the field arrived with a phenomenology of subjection the structural tradition never built, only presupposed. Each side's founding generosity and founding theft are the same events, and the grid's fourth column is the invoice for both.

## One document read twice: a settlement report under two instruments

The abstraction of two objects becomes usable only when it survives contact with a single piece of evidence, so consider the settlement report again, at greater length. Such reports were the characteristic literary product of British revenue administration in India: district by district, an officer toured the villages, measured and classified the soils, inquired into tenures, recorded the customs he attributed to castes and communities, and fixed the state's demand for a term of years. The document is thick, statistical, and drenched in judgement, and it sustained two empires of interpretation. Read with the Marxist instrument, it is a record of primitive accumulation conducted by ledger. The classification of soils is the calibration of extraction; the inquiry into tenure is the manufacture of a property right that can be sold for arrears, which is to say the conversion of a cultivator's customary standing into a landlord's asset or a moneylender's security; the fixed demand, payable in cash on dates indifferent to the harvest, is the mechanism that binds the village to the market and the migrant labour stream to the plantation and the mine. On this reading the report's judgements about indolence or improvidence are noise, the self-exculpation of the extracting power, to be discounted as one discounts any interested testimony. What the officer thought he was doing is the least important thing about what he did.

Read with the postcolonial instrument, the same report is a machine for producing the colonised as an object of knowledge. Its tables do not find the castes and tribes of the district waiting in nature; they select from a fluid social world the identities that administration requires, freeze them into schedules, and return them to the population as the terms in which petitions must be written, quotas claimed, and honour defended. The officer's ethnographic asides, his confidence that he knows what a community immemorially is, are not noise in this reading but signal: they are the point at which power becomes knowledge and knowledge becomes the self-understanding of the governed, who learn to present themselves to the state, and eventually to one another, in the state's vocabulary. What the officer did to the revenue is not denied; it is bracketed, because the reading is after a different effect, the report's long career as a template for how a society could be imagined. Neither reading is a distortion of the document. Each is a decision about which of the document's causal careers to follow, the career through property and hunger or the career through category and selfhood. The tragedy of the wider dispute is how often each side has insisted that its career is the document's only one, and the promise of the grid is that a reader equipped with both can refuse the insistence without splitting any difference: the report did both things, on different timescales, and a history of the district that omits either career is partial in a specifiable way.

The example also locates the genuine friction points with precision, and they are fewer than the polemic suggests. Friction arises first over causation: does the category drive the extraction, the extraction the category, or each the other in sequence, and with what weights in a given district? That is an empirical question, decidable in principle by evidence, and good work on both sides has treated it as one. Friction arises second over the status of the officer's prose: the Marxist habit of discounting it as ideology and the postcolonial habit of enthroning it as the event itself are methodological commitments that precede evidence and survive it, and on these the disagreement is philosophical and probably permanent. Friction arises third, and most consequentially, over what happens next in the story: whether the categories the report installed were destined to be eroded by the market relations the report also installed, as the universalisation thesis expects, or whether colonial difference was self-reproducing, stamped into the successor state's own machinery, as the rival thesis holds. That third friction is the one the famous quarrel is actually about, and it cannot even be stated until the two objects have been distinguished. Readers who have followed the settlement report this far now possess the distinction in working form, and the history that follows, of founding texts, a wandering collective, and a celebrated fight, can be read as the working out of these three frictions in institutional time.

## The founding text: how the Orient was made into an object

The field acquired its founding text in 1978, with Edward Said's Orientalism, a study of how European scholarship and literature constructed the Orient as an object of knowledge and of rule. The argument, compressed to its skeleton, is that the West's Orient was not a place its scholars discovered but a representation they produced and reproduced across centuries: a theatre of sensuality, despotism, backwardness, and splendour, assembled from travellers' tales, philology, administrative reports, and novels, and governed by rules that decided in advance what the Orient essentially was. Knowledge of this kind was not innocent of power. It was generated inside the institutions that governed the East, it certified the governors' competence to govern, and it taught generation after generation of administrators, novelists, and readers to perceive actual peoples through a ready-made figure. Said drew the instruments for this analysis from two sources whose presence in the book matters for the comparison at hand: from Michel Foucault, the notion of discourse as a regulated system for producing statements and objects; and from Antonio Gramsci, the notion of hegemony, the organisation of consent through culture, which Said used to explain how a representation becomes common sense. The founding text of postcolonial theory is therefore, in part, a Gramscian book, a fact the later dispute repeatedly forgets and the trajectory explanation restores to the centre. The study of colonial discourse began not as a rejection of the Marxist tradition but as an application of one of its heterodox instruments to an object the tradition had neglected.

### What did the founding study of the field actually argue?

It argued that European writing about the Orient formed a single, durable discourse that produced the Orient it claimed to describe: a representation governed by institutional power, largely impervious to correction by its ostensible object, and serviceable to rule because it certified the described peoples as requiring the describers' government, tutelage, and interpretation.

Two features of the book's reception belong in any exact account. The first is the scale of its migration. Written by a professor of comparative literature about a scholarly tradition, it reorganised research programmes far from its home discipline: anthropologists re-examined their fieldwork writing, historians returned to their archives with new suspicions, scholars of other empires asked what the equivalent representation had been for Africa, for Latin America, for the internal others of Europe itself. A concept of discourse that could travel that far had plainly found a real object, and the fertility of the research it licensed remains the strongest pragmatic argument for the field's existence, one its Marxist critics have rarely engaged directly. The second feature is the ambiguity Said left at the book's centre, which his critics and successors exploited in opposite directions. The study oscillates between two claims: that Orientalist representation systematically distorted a real Orient that better knowledge could reach, and that the Orient of the discourse was a fabrication with no corresponding reality behind it at all. The first claim is realist and invites correction by better scholarship; the second is constructivist and dissolves the object the first claim wants to recover. Said never fully adjudicated between them, and the field he founded inherited the oscillation, its realist wing continuing to write correctively about misrepresentation while its constructivist wing pursued the stronger and stranger thesis. Much of what looks from outside like the field's incoherence is this founding ambiguity reproducing itself in new material, and much of the Marxist criticism, at its most precise, is an attempt to force the adjudication Said declined: if representations answer to a reality they can misdescribe, then material history retains its court of appeal, and the discourse is one object among others rather than the whole of the real.

The ambiguity also explains the book's strange double position in the quarrel. Marxist critics could attack it for dissolving material history into text, while other Marxists could and did use it as a weapon against imperial ideology, since the exposure of legitimating representation had been a Marxist genre since the critique of ideology began. Said himself complicated both readings by insisting, against his constructivist admirers, on the reality of the peoples represented and on the humanist commitments of his own critical practice, and by the debts he acknowledged across the traditions: to Gramsci for hegemony, to the philological tradition for its attention to texts, to the historians of empire whose factual work he relied upon even while arraigning their frame. The founding text is thus a poor mascot for any camp. It is better used as the field's first exhibit of its own central problem: how to grant representation the causal weight the evidence shows it carrying without surrendering the world the representation is about. Every later position in the dispute can be stated as an answer to that problem, and the answers diverge at exactly the point where Said left the two claims unadjudicated. The grid records this as the method row's open wound, and the sections on the later critique show the wound being probed by name.

## The founding text in the historians' court

The founding study's passage through the discipline of history was rougher than its passage through literature, and the friction is instructive for the whole comparison. Professional historians of empire raised two objections that have never entirely gone away. The first was evidentiary: a discourse, they argued, that is defined as impervious to its object cannot be corrected by the object, yet the historical record contains Orientalists who learned the languages, corrected their predecessors, disagreed violently with one another, and sometimes produced knowledge that the colonised themselves used against the empire, a record that sits awkwardly with a machinery pictured as seamless and self confirming. The second objection was causal: even granting the representation everything the study claimed for it, the historians asked how the representation explained the timing and geography of conquest, the selection of some territories for settlement and others for extraction, the fiscal crises and military decisions through which empires actually expanded and contracted. A polemic of 1982, in which the Princeton historian Bernard Lewis attacked the book and its author replied, fixed the terms of this quarrel early and personally, and both sides of it have partisans still. The exchange matters here because it exhibits, at the field's birth, the grid's two objects in open competition for the same archive, before the competition had acquired its later theoretical costume.

### What did historians object to in the founding study?

They objected that a discourse pictured as seamless could not explain its own internal quarrels and corrections, and that representation, however powerful, could not by itself explain the timing, geography, and fiscal machinery of conquest, so that the study's object, however real, could not bear the weight of a general theory of empire.

The field's considered reply to the historians developed over the following decades and deserves the same precision. The reply concedes the study's rhetorical overreach, its tendency to write of the discourse as a single subject with a single will across two centuries, and relocates the claim: discourse names not a conspiracy or a monolith but a distribution of the sayable, the range within which disagreement itself had to occur, such that two Orientalists could quarrel bitterly over a reading while agreeing on the questions, the authorities, and the figure of the Oriental that structured their quarrel. On causation, the reply pleads the object: the study never claimed to explain why a fleet sailed in a given year, and judging it for failing to do so is the category error this article exists to prosecute, the grading of one object by another's standards. That plea is strong, and the reader should notice its limits, which are the limits of the plea wherever it is entered. A field cannot forever claim the territory of empire as its subject and then decline, as a category error, every question about empire's material operations that its rivals put to it. At some point the plea of the object becomes a refusal of the world the object inhabits, and the Marxist critics of the following sections were not slow to identify the point and to name it. The historians' quarrel thus stands as the dispute in embryo: a real object, a powerful method, an overreaching claim, a legitimate defence, and a boundary dispute about the defence's expiry date, all present in the first five years. Everything after is elaboration at higher altitude and with better footnotes.

One further consequence of the founding study's reception should be registered because it governs the field's institutional shape, and institutional shape is a theme the article will meet again in the first wave of criticism. The study's home was comparative literature, and its natural heirs were readers: scholars of the novel, the travel account, the colonial romance. Its questions migrated into history and anthropology, but its instruments travelled with them and stamped the receiving disciplines' versions of the field. When anthropologists took up the critique of their own writing, in the celebrated reckoning with ethnographic authority of the middle nineteen eighties, they did so as readers of their discipline's texts; when historians took it up, they did so, at their most characteristic, as readers of archives rather than as reconstructors of the economies the archives administered. The result is the asymmetry the grid's method row records: a field magnificently equipped to interrogate any document's way of meaning, and dependent, for the reconstruction of what the document's world materially was, on borrowing from the very historiography its founding gesture had arraigned. Symbiosis was always available. The polemic, on both sides, preferred a border war, and the border war's first skirmish was the reception of one book by the historians who felt, not without reason, that their life's work had been pronounced a branch of fiction by a professor of literature.

## The contrapuntal turn: the founding author returns to empire

The founding author's own sequel complicates every camp's portrait of him, and a comparison that omitted it would be grading a phase while preaching against the practice. In Culture and Imperialism, published in 1993, Edward Said widened the founding study's lens from the scholarly construction of the Orient to the culture of empire as a whole, and changed its method in a way his critics rarely register. The book's central readings are of the metropolitan novel, Austen, Dickens, Kipling, Conrad, read contrapuntally, in the musical figure the book made famous: the metropolitan text is heard together with the colonial history it presupposes and suppresses, the Antigua estate that funds the Mansfield Park whose order it never enters, the empire that is the condition of the domestic novel's world and the silence at its centre. The method is an instruction to read the two archives, the literature of the centre and the history of the periphery, as one text with two staves. Whatever its differences from the Marxist instrument, contrapuntal reading is not a flight from material history; it is a demand that literary pleasure answer for its material conditions, addressed to readers who had been trained to take the pleasure neat. The book also engaged, directly and respectfully, the anti-colonial resistance cultures the founding study had been taxed with omitting, and its author spent the same years as the most visible intellectual defender of a colonised people's national claim, a biographical fact that sits awkwardly with the folklore of a field indifferent to politics.

### What is contrapuntal reading?

It is the practice, named by the founding author in his study of culture and imperialism, of reading a metropolitan text together with the colonial history it presupposes and suppresses, hearing the literature of the centre and the experience of the periphery as two staves of a single score, so that neither is intelligible in the isolation polite reading prefers.

The sequel's reception by the dispute's parties is itself diagnostic, and the trajectory explanation predicts it exactly. The first wave's critique, published a year before the sequel, had fixed the field's image as the founding study's most constructivist pages; the sequel's engaged, contrapuntal, and frankly nationalist argument did not fit the effigy and was correspondingly under-read by the critics, while the field's own celebrants, invested in the theoretical radicalism of the earlier book's method, often treated the sequel as a falling off into humanist orthodoxy. Both responses valued the effigy over the author. In this article's economy the sequel matters for a plainer reason: it demonstrates that the founding intelligence, given fifteen years, moved toward the combination the grid specifies, holding the novel and the estate, the representation and the revenue, in a single reading, and stating the traffic between them as the critic's explicit task. The movement was not a conversion and was never announced as one; it is visible in the work, which is where movements of this kind live. Readers who want a single exhibit against the claim that the two objects cannot be held together by one mind could do worse than the Mansfield Park chapter, where the silence of a novel about the source of its heroine's world is made to testify about both the literature and the sugar economy at once, and where the testimony convicts neither of being the other's illusion.

## The literary object defended: the novel as colonial evidence

A reader who has followed the comparison this far may still harbour the suspicion that the representational tradition's home evidence, the novel, is too slight a thing to bear the weight placed on it, and the suspicion deserves a direct answer, because the field's literary core is not an eccentricity but a wager about where a society's self understanding is manufactured and stored. The wager's classic statement is the argument, developed by the founding author and codified in the 1989 survey The Empire Writes Back by Bill Ashcroft, Gareth Griffiths, and Helen Tiffin, that the empire's literatures are doubly evidentiary: the metropolitan canon shows the colonising society imagining its possession, in the great house novel's unexamined estates and the adventure tale's emptied continents, while the literatures of the colonised, seizing the coloniser's language and forms, show the imagination being repossessed, bent, and remade. The exemplary career is that of the Indian novel in English after 1981, when Salman Rushdie's Midnight's Children demonstrated that the imperial language could be made to carry a subcontinent's history in a grammar the metropole had to learn to read, and the generation of writers who followed made the demonstration into a literature. On the wager, these are not decorations upon the political history of decolonisation. They are among its primary documents, the record of what the colonised could imagine themselves to be at the moment of imagining, which is a fact about them as real as any in the revenue series and considerably harder to recover by other means.

### Why do the field's critics and defenders fight over novels?

Because the novel is the wager in its purest form: the defenders hold that a society's self understanding is manufactured in its stories, so that the stories are primary evidence about power; the critics reply that a method tuned to stories will find story shaped causes, and mistake the empire of signs for the empire.

The Marxist rejoinder to the wager is the comparison's oldest transaction, and it should be stated in its strongest form rather than its rudest. The rude form says that novels are not evidence. The strong form says that novels are evidence whose causal weight must be established rather than assumed, that the reading public for the imperial romance was a fraction of the population whose taxes and sons sustained the empire, and that the imagination of a class fraction, however brilliantly recovered, is not yet an account of the order that fraction ruled. The rejoinder draws strength from the comparison's own patron: Gramsci's notes on literature are notes on the national popular, on what a people actually reads and what it means that intellectuals do not write it, and they are models of suspicion toward the critic who generalises from the shelf he happens to inhabit. The field's best answer concedes the demand and meets it selectively, by tying the text to its institutional machinery, the school syllabus that made the canon compulsory from Lagos to Lahore, the examination that certified the colonial clerk by his Shakespeare, the publishing economy that decided which of the colonised would be read. Where the tie is made, the wager pays out in the comparison's hardest currency, a specified traffic between the registers, and the novel becomes evidence in a combined account rather than a substitute for one. Where the tie is not made, the reading floats, and the critics' rudeness begins to look like a public service. The section's lesson generalises the grid's: every instrument in this dispute earns its keep exactly where it consents to be audited by the other's evidence.

## The subaltern voice: a question about representation

In 1988 the field's second canonical intervention posed its most famous question. Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak's essay "Can the Subaltern Speak?", published in a volume on Marxism and the interpretation of culture, took the category of the subaltern, which a South Asian historiographical collective had borrowed from Gramsci to name those subordinated by class, caste, gender, and office, and asked whether such a figure can appear as a speaking subject within the structures that represent her. The essay's engine is a distinction between two senses of representation that English collapses into one word: representation as proxy, speaking for another in the political sense, and representation as portrayal, re-presenting another in the aesthetic or philosophical sense. Spivak's charge was that radical intellectuals habitually slide between the two, assuming that to portray the oppressed accurately is to let them speak, and that this slide conceals the intellectual's own constitutive role in manufacturing the voice it claims merely to transmit. Her exemplary case was the subaltern woman, and specifically the colonial archive's record of widow immolation, in which the woman's own testimony is structurally absent: the colonial text speaks of rescue, the patriarchal text of devotion, and between the two sentences the person whose death is at issue does not appear as a subject at all. The essay's notorious conclusion, that the subaltern cannot speak, was a claim about representational structures, not about the empirical silence of the oppressed, who speak constantly; it asserted that within the circuits through which speech becomes audible as speech, the subaltern woman's utterance is not registered, and that intellectuals who announce her voice are usually ventriloquising their own.

### What question did the essay of the subaltern voice pose?

It asked whether the subordinated, and above all the subordinated woman, can appear as a speaking subject inside representational structures built by colonial and patriarchal power, or whether every apparent recovery of her voice is a portrayal by intellectuals that substitutes their representation for her speech while claiming merely to have relayed it.

The essay matters to the comparison for three reasons beyond its fame. First, it was aimed inward: its sharpest criticisms were reserved for Western radical theory and for the historiographical collective's own early confidence that the subaltern's consciousness could be recovered from the archive as an intact presence, a confidence Spivak charged with essentialism, the positing of a pure subaltern subject outside the representations the historians themselves deplored. The field's most celebrated text is thus a critique of the field's own founding historical project, delivered from a position formed by deconstruction, and it is one of the hinges on which the collective's trajectory, examined below, actually turned. Second, the essay is a work of Marxist formation as well as deconstructive method: its author has consistently described her project in relation to Marx's texts, her reading of the proxy and portrayal distinction runs through the Eighteenth Brumaire's famous remark about those who cannot represent themselves, and the essay's publication venue was a landmark volume of Marxist cultural theory. The binary the polemic requires, Marxism here and postcolonial theory there, cannot survive ten minutes with the essay's own bibliography. Third, the essay fixed the field's characteristic difficulty in its sharpest form. If the subaltern cannot speak within representation, then the politics of the field cannot consist in amplifying her voice, and critics have asked, from several directions, what it can consist in: vigilance about one's own representations, the unlearning of privilege, the patient reading of the archive's silences. Whether those practices amount to a politics or to an ethic in politics' clothing is the question the Marxist critique of 1992 pressed hardest, and it is a fair question, which the field's subsequent history has answered more by institutional success than by refutation.

A caution about the essay's afterlife completes the account. Its conclusion was widely circulated in a flattened form, as though it asserted that oppressed people are mute or that their struggles are fictions, and its author spent later years glossing, qualifying, and regretting the slogan her sentences had become, clarifying that the claim concerned the impossibility of unmediated retrieval within specific structures of hearing, not the existence of subaltern speech and resistance, which her own work documented. The flattening is instructive for the comparison's purposes. A field that studies representation discovered that it could not control the representation of its own texts, and the slogan's career, like the career of the settlement report, forked into a scholarly life and a polemical one. The Marxist critique sometimes engaged the scholarship and sometimes the slogan, and part of the trajectory explanation's work is to keep the two apart, since verdicts earned against the slogan are often banked against the scholarship. Readers are advised to hold the essay's actual argument, the distinction between the two senses of representation and the structural absence it diagnoses, as the position to be evaluated, and to treat the slogan as one more exhibit of the phenomenon the essay describes: a statement about a woman, or about the subordinated generally, circulating in her place.

## Hybridity and mimicry: the concepts of the following decade

The field's third canonical stratum formed in the work of Homi K. Bhabha, collected in The Location of Culture in 1994, whose concepts of hybridity, mimicry, and the third space gave postcolonial theory its most portable vocabulary. Mimicry names the colonial demand that the colonised become like the coloniser, reformed, recognisable, a subject of a difference that is almost the same but not quite, and the menace that demand generates for authority when the copy is imperfect: the mimicking subject repeats the coloniser's signs with a slippage that reveals those signs to be repeatable, hence not natural, hence not destiny. Hybridity names the condition produced where cultures meet under domination, not a fusion of two purities but a third space of enunciation in which the purity of either original is shown to be a retrospective fiction and in which new positions, unauthorised by either tradition, become speakable. The concepts are instruments for reading colonial encounters at the level of the sign and the psyche, and their strength is the recovery of a kind of agency that structural accounts systematically miss: the agency of the colonised subject working inside the coloniser's forms, neither simply resisting from an intact outside nor simply submitting, but displacing the forms' authority by inhabiting them wrongly. Ambivalence, the simultaneous attraction and repulsion that Bhabha locates in the colonial relation itself, is the theoretical price of that recovery: authority that must constantly repeat its stereotypes confesses, in the repetition, that they never quite take.

### What is mimicry in the vocabulary of hybridity?

Mimicry is the colonial production of a reformed, recognisable copy of the coloniser, almost the same but not quite, whose very imperfection menaces the authority it was meant to extend, because the copy demonstrates that the coloniser's identity is a performance that can be repeated, and therefore misrepeated, by those it was meant to exclude.

The concepts travelled extraordinarily well, and their travel is itself evidence in the comparison. Hybridity became, for a period, the field's master key, applied to literatures, diasporas, cuisines, and identities on every continent, until some of its own practitioners began to complain that a concept designed to unsettle purity had hardened into a celebration of mixture that presumed the purities it denied and floated free of the material asymmetries that had made the encounter colonial. That internal complaint matters, because it shows the field policing its own inflation with arguments that sometimes sound remarkably like the external critique: that the celebration of hybridity is easiest for those whose material circumstances are already secure, and that a migrant intellectual's experience of betweenness is not the experience of a plantation labourer. The Marxist criticism of the following sections makes that argument from outside, attributed to its authors; the field's internal version of it should be registered first, so that the external version is not mistaken for a discovery. What distinguishes the positions is not the observation but the remedy. The internal remedy is finer reading and renewed attention to location; the external remedy is a change of object, from the sign back to the structure. The grid exists to make that difference of remedy visible, and to prevent the shared observation from being mistaken for agreement.

Bhabha's work also supplies the comparison with its clearest case of a methodological commitment generating both a literature's power and its characteristic blindness. The commitment is to read colonialism in the scene of address: the proclamation, the classroom, the conversion, the moment where authority speaks and a subject is formed in hearing it. Read that way, colonial power appears anxious, repetitive, and vulnerable at its point of application, and an entire archive of colonial texts yields meanings invisible to the ledger. The blindness is symmetrical: at the level of the scene of address, the extraction that the scene administers tends to recede into a backdrop, and a reader formed only by these instruments can finish an education in the field knowing a great deal about the anxieties of district officers and very little about the revenue demand that structured their districts. No serious practitioner denies that both the scene and the demand exist. The question the comparison must keep open, because the evidence keeps it open, is the question of weight: which level explains more of a given outcome, and whether a theory of the whole can be built by adding the levels or whether one level must be granted priority and the other derived. On that question the traditions divide along the line the grid's treatment of capitalism already marked, and the historiographical collective to which the article now turns is where the division ceased to be abstract, because the collective tried, across twenty years, to live on both sides of it.

## The field's instruments: where the method came from

The method row of the grid conceals a genealogy that repays excavation, because a field's instruments determine what it can see, and postcolonial theory's instruments were not forged for the colonial archive. They were imported, in the main, from two sources in French thought. From Michel Foucault came the concept of discourse itself, with its attendant archaeology: the claim that what can be said in a period is governed by rules of formation that no individual speaker authors, that knowledge and power are not external to one another but mutually constituting, and that the archive is therefore to be studied as a system of production rather than consulted as a warehouse of facts. From Jacques Derrida came deconstruction, the practice of reading a text for the oppositions that organise it, the privileged term and its subordinated other, civilised and savage, West and East, and for the moments where the text's own logic unsettles the hierarchy it asserts. Said took the first instrument and, by his own account, declined the second's full rigour; Spivak, Derrida's translator, wielded both; Bhabha fused them with psychoanalysis. The collective in South Asia arrived at adjacent instruments by a different road, through Gramsci and through the practical demands of reading police prose, and only later converged with the Franco metropolitan toolkit. Two arrivals at similar methods from different directions is a fact the trajectory explanation uses: the method was in the air of the humanities, a general turn toward language and representation across disciplines, and the colonial archive was one of its proving grounds rather than its cause.

### Why did literary scholars lead a field about colonialism?

Because the field's founding questions were questions about texts, their genres, tropes, and authority, and the instruments for answering such questions, close reading, discourse analysis, and deconstruction, had been sharpened in literature departments, whose scholars therefore set the field's agenda even where its evidence lay in historians' archives.

The genealogy carries a consequence that both the field's critics and its honest practitioners have noted, in different idioms. Instruments imported from the study of European philosophy and literature bring their native problems with them. Foucault's discourse was theorised against the archives of European medicine, punishment, and sexuality; Derrida's oppositions were excavated from European metaphysics. Carried to the colonial archive, the instruments disclosed real structures, as the settlement report example showed, but they also predisposed their users toward certain findings: the text that deconstructs itself, the authority that betrays its anxiety, the binary that inverts. A method that reliably finds ambivalence will find it, and the question of whether the colonial record is unusually ambivalent or the instrument unusually sensitive to ambivalence is not one the instrument itself can answer. The Marxist tradition has a symmetrical vulnerability, since its instruments, forged in the analysis of European industry, reliably find classes, fractions, and the tendential fall of whatever they are pointed at, and are likewise unable, from inside, to distinguish the world's structure from the kit's. The comparison therefore issues in a methodological humility that neither camp's polemics encourage: instruments are wagers, their findings are joint products of the world and the wager, and the only known corrective is the deliberate confrontation of one instrument's findings with another's on the same evidence. The grid is that confrontation, frozen into a table. The reader who has grasped the genealogy will also grasp why the confrontation is so rarely staged in the literature itself. Instruments are also identities and livelihoods; a scholar is her training; and the staging of a fair confrontation requires each party to treat its own identity as a hypothesis, an exercise for which the academy provides neither time nor reward. The exceptions, again, are the working historians and anthropologists of particular places, whose evidence keeps breaking their instruments and who therefore hold them more loosely than the theorists do.

There is a final twist in the genealogy that the dispute's folklore obscures and that a careful comparison should restore. The French instruments were themselves, in significant part, products of the colonial world's pressure on European thought. Foucault's generation was formed in the shadow of the Algerian war; Derrida was an Algerian born Jew whose relation to French universality was biographically double; the critique of European reason that the field imported was already, in its origins, partly the colonised world's return into the metropole's philosophy. The Marxist objection that postcolonial theory deploys Parisian fashions against material history thus simplifies the intellectual geography twice over: the fashions were tailored partly from colonial cloth, and their tailors included men for whom the colony was not an abstraction. None of this validates any particular application of the instruments, and the objection's institutional core, that the applications flourished in metropolitan universities under metropolitan reward structures, survives the correction intact. But the correction matters to the verdict's calibration. A field whose tools have colonial fingerprints on them is not adequately described as the metropole theorizing its other for its own amusement, and a Marxism that cannot register the complexity will keep winning arguments against a caricature while the actual literature, hybrid in its own genealogy, proceeds elsewhere.

## Knowledge factories: the survey, the society, and the census

Both registers of the grid meet in a class of institutions that neither tradition can monopolise, because they were at once extraction machinery and description machinery, funded from revenue and staffed by scholars: the learned societies, surveys, and censuses through which the colonial state knew its possession. The Asiatic Society, founded in Calcutta in 1784 by the judge and philologist William Jones, organised the recovery of Sanskrit learning under Company patronage, and its labours produced both a genuine revolution in European knowledge, the recognition of the Indo-European language family, and a serviceable past for the governed, a classical golden age administered by Brahmin informants whose selection of texts and customs the new rulers then codified as the law of the land. The Great Trigonometrical Survey, begun in 1802 and carried across the subcontinent over decades at staggering cost in money and in the lives of its survey parties, measured the territory into a single geometric frame, the precondition alike of revenue settlement, military movement, and the cartographic imagination in which India became one object because it had been drawn as one. The census, attempted across India in the early eighteen seventies and conducted synchronously from 1881, enumerated the population into castes, religions, and occupations whose hardening into the master categories of Indian politics is the standing exhibit of the representational argument. Bernard Cohn, the anthropologist whose essays taught both traditions to read these institutions, catalogued the modalities by which the colonial state investigated its world: the historiographic, the observational, the survey, the enumerative, the museological, and the surveillance modality, each a machinery for converting a society into administrable knowledge.

### What do the knowledge factories show about the two objects?

They show the objects interlocked in brick and budget: the same institutions that extracted the revenue also manufactured the descriptions, the same survey that fixed the tax demand drew the map that made a single governable territory imaginable, so that neither register can be told as the other's afterthought.

The factories are the comparison's best physical evidence because their budgets refute the lazy version of both traditions at once. Against the lazy structural reading, in which knowledge is a reflex of extraction, the factories show the state spending treasure on descriptions with no immediate revenue yield, pursuing the classification of castes and the measurement of mountains with an appetite that extraction alone cannot timetable, because rule over strangers required a world made legible before it could be made profitable, and the legibility project had its own momentum, its own careers, its own truths. Against the lazy representational reading, in which discourse floats upon its own rules, the factories show every description wired to a use: the philology decided which law a court would enforce, the survey decided where a regiment could march and a canal could run, the census decided the units in which representation, recruitment, and eventually electorates would be counted. Knowledge and power in these institutions are not two things in alliance. They are one budget, and the historian's task is to follow the money through the modality and the modality through the money, which is the combined account again, this time with an address and an accounts department. The collective's founders knew these institutions intimately, since the colonial archive they read against the grain was largely the factories' output, and the postcolonial field's theorisation of the archive descends from the encounter. What the factories add to the comparison is scale and stone: a reminder that discourse, in the colonial case, was not a metaphor for influence but a civil service, with salaries, and that production, in the same case, was never conducted in silence but always in a voice that described what it was doing to the people it was doing it to. A reader who keeps the factories in view will find it hard to surrender either register, which is precisely the condition the grid was built to produce.

## The bridge built from the other side: cultural studies and the Marxist theory of representation

The dispute's standard map, in which a Marxism of structures faces a postcolonialism of signs across an empty strait, omits the bridge that Marxists themselves built across it a generation earlier. British cultural studies, formed at Birmingham's Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies after 1964 and intellectually armed by Raymond Williams and Stuart Hall, was a Marxist project devoted from its origins to representation: to the press, the school, the advertisement, the police photograph, as machinery for the manufacture of consent and the definition of the real. Williams had supplied the tradition with its most durable correction, the insistence that culture is ordinary and material, a whole way of life rather than a decoration upon one, and the concept of structures of feeling with which to catch experience before it hardens into doctrine. Hall, the centre's director through its most influential years, took Gramsci as the bridge's chief engineer: his analyses of Thatcherism as an authoritarian populism, constructed in the terrain of popular common sense before it was ratified at elections, remain the exemplary demonstration that a Marxist analysis can grant representation full causal weight without surrendering the analysis of the state, the economy, and the class alliances the representations organise. Hall's famous formula for his own position, Marxism without guarantees, named the bridge's toll: determination retained as a pressure and a limit, abandoned as a script.

### What did cultural studies contribute to this comparison?

It demonstrated, from inside the Marxist tradition and a generation before the famous quarrel, that representation could be granted full causal weight within a materialist analysis: that consent is manufactured in texts and institutions, that common sense is a terrain of class struggle, and that none of this requires dissolving the economy into discourse.

The bridge's existence reframes the dispute in a way neither camp's folklore encourages. If a Gramscian Marxism had already theorised hegemony as the organisation of consent through culture, and had practised the reading of popular texts as political evidence at the highest level, then the postcolonial field's founding gesture was not the discovery of representation but its relocation: from the metropole's own class order to the colonial relation, and from a problematic in which representation served hegemony to one in which representation threatened to become the whole of the real. Hall himself watched the relocation with a godfather's ambivalence, welcoming the colonial turn of the questions his generation had asked about race and the West, the field's founding figures acknowledged the debt explicitly in Said's case, and registering, in essays of the nineteen nineties, his unease at a theoretical style that seemed to him to dissolve the political into the textual at the moment of its triumph. The unease, from that source, cannot be filed as an outsider's incomprehension, and it gives the comparison its most authoritative internal witness: the man who had done most to build representation into Marxist analysis judged that the postcolonial ascendancy had kept the representation and mislaid the analysis's other half. His judgement is an argument, not a verdict, and the field's practitioners have answered it by pointing to their own political engagements and to the structural work in their penumbra. But the witness's standing explains the peculiar intimacy of the whole dispute, its quality of a family quarrel conducted at civilisational volume. The parties share ancestors, Gramsci above all, and a quarrel over an inheritance is always the bitterest kind, because each side experiences the other's reading of the will as a theft of something that was also theirs.

## A collective in South Asia: history from below

In 1982 a group of historians, most of them working on South Asia and gathered around the editor Ranajit Guha, launched a journal series under the title Subaltern Studies and a manifesto, "On Some Aspects of the Historiography of Colonial India", that announced a quarrel with every established way of writing the subcontinent's past. The enemy was elitism in two uniforms. Colonialist historiography credited the Raj with whatever modernity India possessed; nationalist historiography credited a nationalist elite with the freedom struggle and treated the masses as its foot soldiers or its mob. Against both, the manifesto proposed that the politics of the people constituted an autonomous domain, neither derivative from elite initiatives nor a mere reflex of economic grievance, and that this domain had its own traditions, organisation, and consciousness, recoverable from the archive if the archive were read against its grain. The category under which the enterprise marched, the subaltern, was borrowed from Gramsci, in whose prison writings it named the subordinate groups whose history is fragmented, episodic, and intertwined with the history of civil society and the state. The borrowing was deliberate and the problematic was Gramscian through and through: hegemony, the relation between domination and consent, and the famous problem of a class that dominates without leading. For the comparison this article is conducting, the founding facts are the load-bearing ones, and they should be fixed before the later story blurs them: the most contested body of work in postcolonial theory began as a self-consciously Marxist historiographical project, South Asian in location, archival in method, and aimed at writing history from below within a problematic supplied by the most heterodox of Marxist theorists. The series' own account of Gramsci's category and its receptions is given in the companion treatment of [gramsci reception and misreadings](/2015/05/01/gramsci-reception-and-misreadings/), which the reader should consult for the lineage in full.

### Where did the category of the subaltern come from?

It came from the prison notebooks of Antonio Gramsci, where the subaltern groups are those subordinate strata whose history is fragmented and episodic, bound up with the history of the state and of civil society, and whose autonomous initiatives the historian must reconstruct from records written by their rulers.

Guha's own first great exhibit of the method, Elementary Aspects of Peasant Insurgency in Colonial India, appeared in 1983 and showed what the manifesto promised: a reconstruction of more than a century of revolts from the records of the power they attacked, extracting from police reports and official correspondence the insurgent's own codes, his signs, his inversions of the marks of subordination, his religious idiom used as an instrument of mobilisation. The book is a semiotic performance, reading the prose of counter-insurgency as an inverted transcript of the consciousness it tried to crush, and it remains the founding phase's most impressive achievement. Two things about it matter for the sequel. Its subject is collective action in the material world: peasants seizing grain, destroying debt bonds, attacking the moneylender and the landlord, and its method, however literary in technique, is put to a historian's question about what people did and why. And its central claim is the autonomy of that doing: peasant politics was not the nationalist movement's echo nor the economy's reflex, but a domain with its own grammar, which the historian insults when he treats it as a stage on the way to someone else's politics. The scholars who shared the enterprise, among them Shahid Amin, David Arnold, Partha Chatterjee, David Hardiman, and Gyanendra Pandey, produced in the early volumes a shelf of work on forests, famine, crime, labour, and communal violence that permanently altered South Asian historiography. Whatever the collective later became, it became it from this: a Marxist-inflected history from below, in open polemic against both imperial and nationalist elites, with the peasant insurgent as its hero and the colonial archive as its ambivalent source.

The regional setting amplified the project's significance, and the series' treatment of [marxism in india complete guide](/2017/12/01/marxism-in-india-complete-guide/) supplies the wider scene. India possessed, and possesses, one of the world's strongest indigenous traditions of Marxist scholarship, with communist parties, Marxist historians of international standing, and a fierce internal debate about the character of Indian society and its colonial transformation. The collective did not enter an empty field; it entered an argument, challenging an established Marxist historiography on its own ground for having reproduced, in nationalist dress, the elitism it condemned in imperial dress. That origin explains a feature of the later global dispute that outsiders consistently misread: the quarrel between the collective and its Marxist critics was, at its source, a family quarrel among historians of South Asia about evidence, method, and the reading of one archive, conducted by people who had read the same footnotes. Its transformation into a global allegory of two civilisations of thought, Marxism and postcolonial theory facing off across a seminar table, happened later and elsewhere, as the texts travelled to literature departments on other continents and shed their footnotes. The trajectory explanation, when it arrives, will insist on this itinerary, because the itinerary is the explanation: a Delhi and Calcutta argument about peasant insurgency became, by stages, a metropolitan theory of representation, and the arguments of each stage were aimed at targets belonging to that stage.

## The established adversary: Marxist historiography in India

To see what the founding manifesto was against, the adversary it named as elitist in its Marxist uniform must be given its due, because it was formidable. Indian Marxist historiography by the early nineteen eighties commanded achievements on which the collective itself silently relied. D. D. Kosambi, mathematician and historian, had pioneered a materialist reading of ancient India that treated myth as sedimented social fact and set a standard of interdisciplinary audacity neither camp has consistently matched. A generation of historians centred on Delhi and Aligarh had reconstructed the agrarian order of Mughal and colonial India in granular detail, measuring revenue systems, tracing the commercialisation of agriculture, and arguing, in one of the fiercest scholarly debates of the nineteen seventies, over the mode of production in Indian agriculture: whether the countryside was semi-feudal, capitalist in formation, or colonial in a sense that required its own category. The debate, conducted in the pages of the Economic and Political Weekly and at conferences across the subcontinent, was political economy at full stretch, with tenancy records and wage series as its ammunition. A parallel debate over Indian feudalism asked whether the early medieval order met the concept's terms. These were not scholastic exercises. Their participants understood the classification of the countryside as a strategic question for movements then contesting it, and the scholarship's quality was a direct expression of the stakes. The collective's founders were formed inside this world, cited it constantly, and aimed their manifesto not at its existence but at its characteristic blindness: for all its agrarian precision, the established historiography had remarkably little to say about the consciousness of the people whose rents it measured, treating popular politics as the reflex of economic position or the property of the national movement's narrative, precisely the elitism the manifesto charged.

### What had Marxist historiography in India already established?

It had established the material constitution of the colonial countryside: the revenue systems and tenures through which surplus was extracted, the commercialisation of agriculture, the debates over feudal and capitalist relations in the village, all reconstructed from records at a standard the collective inherited even while charging that historiography with silence about popular consciousness.

The fairness of the charge can now be graded, and the grading matters because the Marxist side of the later global dispute sometimes writes as though the collective had attacked a straw man. The charge was half fair. The mode of production debate had indeed proceeded, in its dominant register, as though classification exhausted explanation: once the countryside was assigned its mode, the politics of its inhabitants were presumed to follow, and insurgency appeared in the literature chiefly as evidence for or against a classification rather than as a politics with its own grammar. Guha's insurgents, with their codes and inversions, their refusal to behave as the classification predicted, were a genuine discovery against that register, and the historians of the established school who reviewed the early volumes knew it. The charge was unfair in its other half, insofar as the manifesto's rhetoric implied a historiography without people, whereas the established tradition included labour history, histories of peasant movements written from within the communist movement's own memory, and Kosambi's example of a materialism saturated with culture. The collective's polemic, like most founding polemics, simplified its parent to sharpen its own outline. But the simplification had a productive consequence that justifies this section's place in the comparison: it defined, with perfect clarity, the two objects whose competition this article tracks, inside a single national historiography, a decade before the competition was theorised globally. In India in 1982 the objects were already distinct and already rivalrous: the rent roll and the insurgent's consciousness, the mode of production and the prose of counter-insurgency. The global dispute between Marxism and postcolonial theory is, in considerable part, this Indian argument with its luggage unpacked on other continents, and readers who see the origin see why the dispute's participants so often seem to be arguing about the same archive: at the start, they literally were.

## The founding phase: an explicitly Gramscian project

The early collective's theoretical commitments can be stated as three connected theses, each traceable to its Gramscian inheritance and each destined to become, in altered form, a bone of the later contention. The first thesis concerned the colonial state: it was, in Guha's eventual formulation, a dominance without hegemony. Where the metropolitan bourgeoisie of the European nineteenth century had, on the standard account, supplemented coercion with leadership, educating the subordinate classes into a national culture that made its rule appear as the general interest, the colonial bourgeoisie, foreign and racially marked, ruled a society it could not lead. Its dominance rested on coercion disproportionately, and the culture of the rulers remained an enclave. The second thesis concerned the colonised elite: the indigenous bourgeoisie had likewise failed to achieve hegemony over the nation it claimed to represent, speaking for the people in a nationalist idiom the people did not author, so that the nation's history split into two domains, the elite's and the subaltern's, whose disjunction was the master key to the colonial past. The third thesis was methodological: the subaltern domain, being unrecorded in its own voice, had to be reconstructed from the prose of those who policed it, read with a disciplined inversion, so that the archive of domination became, under the right method, the archive of resistance.

### How did the founding phase read a peasant revolt?

It read the official record against its grain, treating the police report and the magistrate's despatch as an inverted transcript in which the insurgent's codes, signs, solidarities, and aims could be recovered from the language of the power that suppressed him, and it insisted the revolt's politics were the peasants' own, neither borrowed from elites nor discharged automatically by hunger.

Held up against the grid, the founding phase sits in an interesting position: its object was closer to the Marxist column than to the postcolonial one. It studied collective action, material grievance, and the state; its discourse analysis was a technique for getting at action, not yet a theory that discourse was the whole of the real. Even the dominance without hegemony thesis, for all its Gramscian vocabulary, is a claim about the structure of a state and the failure of a class project, the kind of claim that can be wrong in the way historical claims are wrong, by evidence. That is precisely why the book-length critique of 2013 could aim at it so exactly, and why the critique's target had to be this phase's theses rather than the later work's reading practices. The phases must not be conflated, because the verdicts differ: a refutation of the dominance without hegemony thesis, if it succeeded, would damage the founding project and leave the later discourse analysis untouched, while a vindication of the later work's readings of colonial texts would leave the historical thesis exactly as exposed as before. The polemical habit of scoring points against one phase and banking them against the whole is the dispute's most reliable generator of heat, and the reader now has the means to detect it: ask, of any celebrated blow, which phase's claim it actually strikes. Very often the answer reveals that the blow and the applause were aimed at different books.

The founding phase also contained, from the start, the seed of its own transformation, and honesty requires the seed to be identified in the phase itself rather than blamed on later corruption. The method of reading against the grain placed the historian's attention on texts, on the rhetoric of reports, on signs and codes, and the longer that attention was sustained, the more the text threatened to become the topic rather than the instrument. The claim of subaltern autonomy, meanwhile, carried an unexamined essentialist passenger: the autonomous domain was spoken of as though its consciousness existed whole, prior to its representations, awaiting recovery by the adequately sympathetic reader. Both vulnerabilities were named from inside the wider project within a few years, by Spivak's essay among other interventions, and the naming began the turn. It is therefore too simple, as the next section argues, to narrate the trajectory as fidelity followed by betrayal. The turn was the founding phase's own problems being worked on by its own members with new instruments imported for the purpose. Whether the instruments solved the problems or merely changed the subject is the real question the dispute should be about, and it is a better question than the one the polemic usually asks.

## History from below before the manifesto: the Thompson inheritance

The collective's founding gesture, the recovery of the people's politics from beneath the narratives of their rulers, had a distinguished Marxist ancestry that its own rhetoric of novelty tended to minimise, and restoring the ancestry clarifies both the achievement and the quarrel. E. P. Thompson's The Making of the English Working Class, published in 1963, had rescued the English crowd from what its author called the enormous condescension of posterity, reconstructing Luddites, Methodists, and weavers as the makers of a class and of themselves, in a history where experience, culture, and organisation are the very substance of class formation rather than its superstructure. Thompson's class is not a thing but a relationship and a happening, made in common struggle and in the languages, chapels, and rituals through which the struggle was understood. A generation of historians across the world, the collective's founders among them, learned their trade in that book's school, and the line from Thompson's crowd to Guha's insurgents is direct: the refusal of the reflex theory of revolt, the insistence that the people's politics has its own rationality and its own archive, the reading of hostile records for the presence they were written to deny. The collective's originality was not the gesture. It was the gesture's relocation to the colonial archive and its weaponisation against a nationalist historiography that Thompson's heirs in India had themselves built.

### What did the collective take from the older history from below?

It took the founding refusal: that the politics of the subordinate is neither a reflex of economic grievance nor a rehearsal for elite leadership, but a domain with its own rationality, recoverable from the records of the power that policed it, provided the historian reads those records against their grain and their contempt.

The inheritance cuts the dispute at an angle its folklore does not expect, because it makes the founding phase the continuation of Marxist historiography's most honoured internal tradition rather than its repudiation, and it relocates the turn's significance accordingly. What changed, across the nineteen eighties, was not that historians discovered the people's consciousness, which their Marxist grandfathers had pursued with distinction. What changed was the theory of where consciousness lives and how it can be reached: from experience, Thompson's category, formed in struggle and recoverable by sympathetic reconstruction, toward representation, the later field's category, formed in discourse and reachable only through the critique of the forms that mediate it. Experience promises the historian a presence behind the text; representation denies that any presence is available except through texts and their systems, so that the recovery the grandfather promised becomes, for the grandchildren, a methodological romance to be suspected. Spivak's essay is the precise hinge, since its argument about the subaltern voice is, among other things, an argument against the Thompsonian recovery conducted by a scholar who shared the recovery's politics. The Marxist criticism of the turn is therefore best understood, at this depth, as a defence of experience against its deconstruction, mounted by a tradition that contains both terms: Thompson's own late polemics against the structuralist dissolution of the subject were fought on exactly this ground, inside Marxism, before the postcolonial field had a name. The comparison draws the moral it has drawn at every level: the dispute's deepest lines do not run between the camps. They run through them, and the camps are the temporary institutional forms the lines assume. A reader who sees this is finally immune to the folklore, because no camp can recruit her to a war whose front she knows to be elsewhere.

## The turn: from insurgency to representation

Across the later nineteen eighties and the nineteen nineties the collective's centre of gravity moved, volume by volume and recruit by recruit, from the reconstruction of subaltern action toward the analysis of colonial representation. The signs of the movement are bibliographic before they are doctrinal. The early volumes are dominated by historians writing on peasants, workers, forests, and crime; the later volumes admit more literary scholars, more work on texts, gender, and the colonial construction of knowledge, and the project's centre of institutional gravity drifts from South Asian history departments toward a diaspora located increasingly in the universities of North America. New recruits brought new instruments: the anthropologist Bernard Cohn's work on the colonial state's knowledge practices had already shown how censuses, surveys, and museums manufactured the India they administered; Gayatri Spivak's interventions pressed the collective on its essentialism and on the gendered subaltern its volumes had largely missed; Dipesh Chakrabarty, himself a labour historian of the founding generation, began the long meditation on historical time and European categories that culminated in the pivot examined in the next section. By the time the project acquired a Latin American counterpart group and a global readership in cultural studies, its characteristic questions had altered. The prose of counter-insurgency remained a touchstone, but the emphasis had migrated from the insurgent behind the prose to the prose itself, its genres, its silences, its construction of the objects it policed. The subaltern, invoked ever more widely as the field expanded, underwent a corresponding migration: from a Gramscian designation of subordinate groups in a definite social formation to something nearer a general figure for the marginalised voice, a usage against which several of the founders themselves protested without regaining control of the word.

### What changed when the collective turned toward discourse?

The object changed: where the founding project had used the reading of prose as an instrument for recovering what subaltern people did, the later work increasingly treated the prose itself, its categories, genres, and silences, as the reality to be explained, and the subaltern migrated from a designation of subordinate groups toward a figure for the voice that representation excludes.

Two judgements about the turn must be kept apart, because conflating them is the second most reliable generator of heat in the dispute. The first judgement concerns intellectual necessity. The turn answered real problems: the essentialism charge was well founded, the founding phase had underattended to gender and to the textual machinery its own method kept discovering, and the instruments imported for the purpose, deconstruction, discourse analysis, the critique of historicism, genuinely disclosed features of the colonial archive that the older method had stepped over. A project that had refused to change under those criticisms would have deserved the charge of dogma. The second judgement concerns cost. The turn coincided with, and plausibly abetted, a wider displacement in the humanities: from political economy to culture, from structures to texts, from the explanation of material arrangements to the interpretation of their meanings, at exactly the decades in which material arrangements were being violently reorganised across the postcolonial world by structural adjustment and market integration. Whether the collective led that displacement or merely surfed it is disputed, and the class analysis of the displacement, offered by its critics in 1992 and 1994, is examined in the sections that follow with the care the brief requires: as a move in the literature to be described and weighed, not as a verdict this article adopts about scholars' motives. What can be said neutrally is that the timing made the turn legible as a symptom, and that the collective's own members were not naive about the legibility. Chatterjee and Chakrabarty both wrote, in different registers, about the implicatedness of the postcolonial intellectual in the institutions that circulate the critique, and the field's best work often carries that self-suspicion as a visible seam. The seam does not settle the argument. It does disqualify the lazy version of it, in which one side alone possesses self-knowledge.

## The nationalist question: a discourse derived, a nation fragmented

The turn's most consequential single body of work, and the bridge between the collective's founding phase and its pivot, is Partha Chatterjee's interrogation of nationalism, begun in Nationalist Thought and the Colonial World of 1986 and completed in The Nation and Its Fragments of 1993. The first book's argument gave the literature one of its durable phrases: nationalist thought as a derivative discourse. Anti-colonial nationalism, Chatterjee argued, opposed the colonial power politically while remaining imprisoned within the thematic the coloniser's knowledge had set, accepting the essentialist opposition of East and West, the state's form, the narrative of progress, and contesting only the personnel of rule. The nationalist elite's claim to speak from an autonomous cultural essence was, on this reading, made inside a framework whose deepest premises were borrowed, so that independence changed the rulers faster than it changed the categories of rule. The second book complicated its own thesis in the direction of the evidence. Nationalism, it argued, had divided the world into an outer domain of economy and statecraft, where Western superiority was conceded and imitated, and an inner domain of language, family, religion, and culture, where sovereignty was asserted earliest and defended longest, and where the nation was already imagined as itself while the state remained alien. The fragments of the title are the peoples the nation's story could not digest: women, castes, peasants, minorities, whose claims the inner domain sheltered and whose subordination it also sanctified.

### What does it mean to call nationalist thought derivative?

It means that anti-colonial nationalism, on Chatterjee's argument of 1986, fought the colonial power inside a framework of knowledge the colonial order itself had supplied, accepting its essential divisions and its narrative of progress while contesting only who should rule, so that political independence risked reproducing the categories of the rule it expelled.

The women's question essay, published in 1989 and among the most reprinted texts the collective produced, shows the method at its most fertile and its most controversial. Nationalism, the essay argued, resolved the question of women's emancipation by relocating it to the inner domain: the new woman of the nationalist ideal was to be educated, refined, and visibly modern in spirit while remaining the guardian of the home's spiritual essence, distinguished at once from the Western woman and from the women of the lower orders, so that the markers of subordination were recoded as the signs of sovereignty. The argument disclosed, in a single case, the whole machinery this article has been describing: a representation doing material work, constituting a subject, the nationalist woman, whose formation organised real lives, schools, households, and laws. It also drew the standing objection in its sharpest domestic form, that the reading of prescriptive texts tells the historian what women were enjoined to be and not what they were, and that the inner domain's supposed sovereignty looks different from the standpoint of the women whose subordination it spiritualised. Both the achievement and the objection travelled with the collective into its later phase, and both are rehearsed here because Chatterjee's trajectory is the trajectory in one career: the historian of peasant and national politics who became the theorist of the political society of the governed, keeping the founding phase's enemy, elitism, in view while migrating from the archive of insurgency to the analysis of the categories through which populations are governed. The book-length critique of 2013 aimed one of its principal charges at this career's claims about capital and difference, and the reply of 2013, encountered in its section below, is unintelligible without the nationalism work in hand: the rule of colonial difference was first theorised here, as a claim about how the colonised were constituted as subjects of a distinct political order, before it was ever a claim about what capital could or could not do.

## The subaltern in the factory: a labour history that broke the sequence

The pivot book grew from a labour history, and the labour history deserves its own account because it is the collective's most complete attempt at the combination the grid specifies, executed before the combination had a name. Rethinking Working-Class History, Dipesh Chakrabarty's study of the Bengal jute mill workers published in 1989, began as a work of the founding phase's kind, an archive based reconstruction of a workforce's world: the mills' labour regimes, the sardari system of recruitment and command, the workers' neighbourhoods, festivals, and solidarities. Its argument, however, turned against the historiography that had formed its author, including the Marxist historiography of India itself. That historiography, Chakrabarty charged, measured the jute workers against a script derived from the European working class's supposed maturation, from pre-political crowd to trade union to class consciousness, and recorded the workers' actual culture, hierarchical, communal, saturated with deference and devotion, as a failure to become what the script promised: a bourgeois and pre-bourgeois residue that history had neglected to clear. The study refused the measurement. The workers' culture was not an immature version of a European destination but a different organisation of life, in which relations of command and community that capital found useful, and in part created, were lived from inside as the fabric of the world. Capital in the mills did not encounter a blank labour power awaiting its universal form; it encountered, hired, disciplined, and depended upon persons whose gods and patrons came with them through the factory gate.

### What did the jute mill study refuse to say about its workers?

It refused to say that their hierarchical and devotional culture was a failure to become a proper working class on the European script: it treated that culture as a contemporary organisation of life that capital used and depended upon, not as a survival awaiting dissolution, and it charged the historiography that measured them so with historicism.

The study's importance for this comparison is out of proportion to its fame outside South Asian history, because it contains, in working form, every element of the subsequent quarrel at the scale of one industry. The universalisation question is present as an empirical finding: the capital relation was installed in the mills completely, wages, shifts, fines, and the world market for jute all present, and simultaneously the relation was clothed in, and operated through, forms that the cultural version of the thesis says should have dissolved. The waiting room is present as the historiographical habit under indictment. The categories question is present as the book's method: class retained, but forced to describe a workforce for whom the abstract individual of the labour contract was a legal fiction everyone conspired to maintain and no one inhabited. Even the pivot book's signature distinction is here in embryo, History 1 in the mill's accounts and History 2 in the bustee's festivals, eleven years before the terms were coined. A reader who masters the jute study possesses the later philosophy as a set of footnotes to an archive, which is the healthiest form in which to possess any philosophy. And the Marxist reader who dismisses the pivot while honouring the labour history, a common distribution of respect, should be asked to say where the archive ends and the error begins, since the philosophy's author has always presented it as the archive's lesson. The book-length critique, to its credit, engages the labour history as part of its target's foundation rather than pretending the philosophy fell from the sky; the replies, to theirs, insist that the lesson of the mills is what the philosophy says it is. The mill, like the settlement report, is a document both instruments must read, and the two readings are still being written.

## The pivot: provincialising Europe

The book that fixed the collective's later position, and that the trajectory explanation treats as the pivot of the whole migration, is Dipesh Chakrabarty's Provincializing Europe, published in 2000 after a gestation in essays through the preceding decade. Chakrabarty had been a labour historian, a student of the Calcutta jute workers, and the book's argument grows from that archive's resistance to its categories. Its central charge is aimed at historicism, the habit of historical thought that arranges societies along a single timeline of development, such that Europe's past becomes every other society's future and the colonised are consigned to what the book memorably calls the waiting room of history: not yet ready for self-rule, modernity, or the full humanity of the historical subject, always becoming and never arrived. Against that timeline the book sets a distinction between two histories of capital. History 1 names the narrative capital tells about itself and the analyst can reconstruct from its logic: the universalising drive that posits labour, value, and the commodity form as the deep grammar of modern life everywhere. History 2 names the lifeworlds that capital encounters, inhabits, and depends upon without ever fully translating into its own grammar: relations of kinship, devotion, caste, and community that are not capital's precursors, not survivals scheduled for dissolution, but contemporaries that interrupt and modify its rule from within the present. To provincialize Europe is to demote the categories generated from Europe's History 1, historicism's stages and transitions, from universal theory to what they are: indispensable and inadequate, powerful provincial instruments that must be used and simultaneously watched for the worlds they cannot see.

### What does provincialising Europe ask historians to do?

It asks them to use the categories of European historical thought, which remain indispensable, while refusing their claim to universality: to treat those categories as provincial products of a particular history, to register the lifeworlds that interrupt capital's universal narrative from within the present, and to write history without assigning the colonised to a waiting room of perpetual not-yet.

The book's position on the question that divides the traditions is exact, and exactness matters because the position is persistently misreported by both camps. Chakrabarty does not deny that capital universalises. History 1 is his own name for that drive, and his account of the jute mills presupposes the world market's real abstractions at every step. His claim is that the universalisation is never complete or self-sufficient: capital lives in History 2 even as it subordinates it, the worker who sells labour power also inhabits relations capital did not create and cannot digest, and a history that narrates only History 1 mistakes capital's self-image for the world's constitution. Nor does the book reject universal categories wholesale, a misreading its author has repeatedly and explicitly denied: the argument is that the universals of European thought are simultaneously necessary and insufficient, to be translated and contested rather than either enthroned or discarded. Readers should register how much of the subsequent quarrel depends on these two qualifications. The book-length critique of 2013 reads the collective, Chakrabarty included, as denying that capital universalises social relations in the colonial world and as abandoning universal categories for critique; the book's own text, on the pages where History 1 is defined, concedes the universalising drive and retains the categories under a warning label. Whether the critique's reading is a misunderstanding or a deliberate sharpening is itself disputed, and the replies section gives the dispute its due. For the comparison, the operative fact is that the pivot book is a dialectical object, holding the universal and its interruption in one frame, and that dialectical objects attract monocular readings the way bright objects attract moths.

Provincializing Europe also completes the collective's migration in a way its author stated with unusual candour: the project that began as history from below within a Marxist problematic had become a critique of the historical discipline's own categories, addressed less to the peasantry of the archive than to the historians of the present, and its politics had migrated accordingly, from the side of a class whose action was to be recovered to the side of a vigilance about the concepts through which any recovery proceeds. That migration is the trajectory. The collective's name for itself stayed constant; its object, its methods, its institutional home, and its imagined reader all changed. A dispute that treats the collective as a fixed quantity will therefore talk past itself with perfect confidence forever, each side citing genuine texts against genuine texts and wondering at the other's blindness. The next section states the trajectory explanation as the article's namable claim and draws the consequences for how the famous critiques should be read. After that, the critiques themselves, and the replies, can be presented in their proper phases, and the reader can grade them with the phases in view.

## The governed: the collective's later phase meets the postcolonial state

The trajectory did not end at the pivot, and a short account of the collective's most influential later development protects the comparison against treating 2000 as a terminus. In The Politics of the Governed, published in 2004, Partha Chatterjee turned the collective's instruments on the postcolonial state itself and produced the distinction between citizens and populations that has since travelled through the study of democracies everywhere. Civil society, on this account, is the preserve of the proper citizens, the propertied and accredited, who deal with the state through rights and law; the great majority of the governed appear to the state as populations, categories of classification and welfare, to be enumerated, targeted, and managed, and their politics, the politics of the governed, proceeds by negotiation with that machinery: the illegal settlement that bargains its illegality into services, the squatters who convert a census category into a claim. The framework is Foucauldian in its machinery and Gramscian in its sympathies, and it is unmistakably a product of the trajectory: the subaltern of the founding phase, whose autonomous domain was to be recovered from the colonial archive, reappears as the governed of the democratic present, whose politics is to be read in the files of the welfare office with the same against the grain discipline the insurgent's historian brought to the police report.

### What distinguishes populations from citizens in the later framework?

Citizens, in Chatterjee's account, are the accredited members of civil society who meet the state as bearers of rights under law; populations are the classified categories through which the state actually governs the majority, by enumeration, welfare, and exception, and whose politics consists in negotiating with that governmental machinery.

The framework matters to the verdict for two reasons. First, it is evidence against the folklore that the later collective had no politics but textuality: the politics of the governed is a theory of how the poor of the postcolonial world actually engage power, written by a founder of the project in its fifth decade of life, and it has been taken up by scholars and activists who would not describe themselves as postcolonial theorists at all. Second, it exhibits the trajectory's characteristic double movement in its mature form. The categories of the analysis, governmentality, population, biopolitics, are the metropolitan instruments of the turn; the sympathy and the archive are the founding phase's own; and the object is neither the insurgent's consciousness nor the coloniser's text but the contemporary state's paperwork, read for the politics it administers and the politics it provokes. A Marxist reader will note what the framework brackets: the accumulation that the governmental machinery serves appears as context rather than as engine, and the populations' negotiations are rarely connected to the circuits that decide what there is to negotiate about. A postcolonial reader will note what it achieves: an account of actually existing democracy in the postcolony that neither celebrates the citizen form nor waits for its completion, the waiting room refused in the analyst's own practice. The grid grades the framework as the later phase's best answer to the charge of political evacuation, and as a standing demonstration that the trajectory, followed past the point where the polemics stopped watching, bends back toward the material world by a road its founders paved with other men's concepts and their own archive discipline.

## The trajectory explanation

The trajectory explanation can now be stated in its full form, since the evidence for it has been laid out in the order of its occurrence. The historiographical collective at the centre of the dispute between Marxism and postcolonial theory began, in 1982, as a Gramscian project: South Asian historians applying the category of the subaltern and the problematic of hegemony to write history from below, in polemic against elitist historiography and in productive quarrel with the established Marxist historiography of their own region. Over the following two decades the project changed its object, from subaltern action to colonial representation; its methods, from archival reconstruction assisted by reading against the grain to discourse analysis assisted by deconstruction; its personnel, as literary scholars joined and then multiplied; its institutional home, from South Asian history toward the metropolitan humanities; and its characteristic product, from the recovered insurgent to the interrogated category, with the book of 2000 as the pivot on which the change can be dated in a single career. Both traditions therefore claim the collective, and both claims are documented. Marxists claim the founding phase because it was theirs: its problematic, its personnel's formation, its enemy. Postcolonial theory claims the later phase because it is theirs: its canonical texts, its exported vocabulary, its institutional triumphs. Arguments about the collective are consequently, very often, arguments about different phases conducted as though about a single position, and the failure to specify the phase is the dispute's master defect. The explanation earns its name by converting a hundred scattered confusions into one diagnosable error, and it yields a rule of reading the reader can apply to every subsequent claim in this article and in the literature beyond it: of any statement about what Subaltern Studies held, ask when.

The rule's power can be shown on the dispute's three most recycled exchanges. The charge that the collective abandoned political economy for text is true of the migration's direction and false of its origin, since the founding texts are saturated with political economy and even the pivot book retains capital's logic as History 1; the charge describes a tendency of the later phase inflated into an essence of the whole. The defence that the collective never rejected Marxism is likewise true of the personnel's formation and false as a description of the later work's relation to historical materialism, whose categories the pivot book explicitly brackets as provincial. And the claim that the collective proved capital did not universalise in the colonies dissolves, under the rule, into two claims of different dates: Guha's historical thesis about the colonial state's dominance without hegemony, a founding-phase claim about politics and class formation, and Chakrabarty's philosophical thesis about the incompleteness of capital's translations, a pivot-phase claim about categories and lifeworlds, related by descent but not identical, and not refutable or vindicable in a single motion. The book-length critique of 2013 will be seen, when it arrives in the argument below, to target the first of these with its heavy artillery while announcing the second as its quarry, and the replies will be seen answering for the second while the first takes the fire. Neither side is lying. Both are mis-specifying the phase, and the mis-specification is not accidental: the critique gains scope by treating the collective as one thing, and the defence gains depth by the same treatment. The trajectory explanation is this article's refusal of the convenience, offered in the belief that a dispute correctly localised is already half resolved, because each local question, the historical thesis, the philosophical thesis, the methodological drift, admits of evidence in a way the civilisational standoff does not.

One more consequence deserves drawing before the critics enter. The trajectory was not a fall from an origin, and the article does not so present it. The founding phase contained the turn's seeds, in its textual method and its essentialist temptation; the later phase retained the founding phase's enemies, elitism and imperial common sense, and much of its moral energy. Nor was the migration unique to this collective: it recapitulates, in one institutional biography, the migration of the Western humanities as a whole across the same decades, from the social history and political economy of the postwar settlement toward the cultural and linguistic turns, and the collective's distinction is that it performed the migration in public, in numbered volumes, with its changes of mind preserved in print where they can be audited. That auditability is a gift to the comparativist. Where other fields changed by generational replacement, leaving the transformation to be inferred, this one changed by argument among named people whose earlier and later positions can be set side by side. The dispute about it is correspondingly better evidenced than most disputes of its kind, which is a further reason to regret the polemical habit of phase-blindness: the materials for a precise quarrel exist, in a way they rarely do, and the precision is there for the taking by any reader willing to date the claims before grading them.

There is a corollary for the two traditions' wider self understanding, and it should not be softened. If a Marxist historiographical project could become, by its own internal logic worked out over twenty years, the foundation of a field routinely described as Marxism's opposite, then the boundary between the traditions is not a wall but a membrane, crossed repeatedly by people carrying concepts in both directions and being changed by the carrying. The traffic was never one way. The collective took Gramsci from the Marxist inheritance and returned the subaltern to the world's vocabularies, where Marxists now use it, often without citation, to name the very subjects their older categories missed. Postcolonial theory took the critique of Eurocentrism to the metropolitan disciplines and returned a generation of historians unable to read an archive innocently, including historians who grade the return as an unmixed gain for materialist scholarship. An inheritance quarrel presupposes a family, and the trajectory explanation's final service is to restore the family to view: not to reconcile it, since the disagreements catalogued in this article are real and some are irreconcilable, but to make its members answerable to their actual kinship, which the folklore of two civilisations exists to deny. The reader who sees the family sees also why the quarrel will not end. Families do not conclude their arguments about an estate while the estate is still being lived in, and this estate, the interpretation of the colonial past and of the capitalism that made it, is lived in by everyone the argument concerns.

## The first wave of Marxist criticism: classes, nations, literatures

The first full-dress Marxist response to the new field arrived in 1992, Aijaz Ahmad's In Theory: Classes, Nations, Literatures, a book whose targets were the emergent orthodoxy of postcolonial theory in metropolitan literary studies and several of its presiding figures, examined at length and without diplomatic softening. Ahmad's argument has three layers, and the layers must be separated because the replies engage them unevenly. The first layer is literary-critical: against the celebration of hybridity, migration, and exilic sensibility as the truth of the postcolonial condition, Ahmad insists that most of the colonised world does not migrate, that its literatures are written overwhelmingly in its own languages for its own readers, and that a theory generalising from the migrant intellectual's bookshelf mistakes a stratum's experience for a civilisation's. The second layer is political: the category of the Third World, and the nationalism that was its historical bearer, are defended against a criticism that, in Ahmad's account, dissolves collective anti-imperial politics into textual performance at exactly the moment when the material offensive of capital against the postcolonial states was intensifying, leaving the critic exquisitely equipped to deconstruct a nationalism and unequipped to organise a defence. The third layer is the one the brief for this article instructs be handled as a move in the literature rather than adopted: Ahmad locates the enabling condition of postcolonial theory in the class position of its producers, intellectuals of postcolonial origin installed in Western universities, whose professional rewards flow toward the deconstruction of collective projects and away from the unfashionable labours of political economy. That location argument is presented here as Ahmad's, attributed and dated. It is a claim about the sociology of a field, of the same genre as claims this series has examined about other intellectual formations, and it stands or falls on evidence about institutions and incentives, not on the character of any individual scholar, which this article neither impugns nor needs.

### What was the charge in the study of classes, nations, and literatures?

The charge, laid by Aijaz Ahmad in 1992, was that the emergent postcolonial theory of the metropolitan universities had substituted textual politics for political economy: generalising the migrant intellectual's experience into the condition of the postcolonial world, dissolving nationalism and collective resistance into discourse, and abandoning the material analysis of an imperialism that had not obligingly become textual.

The book's treatment of the field's founding text illustrates its method and its limits together. Ahmad's chapter on Said arraigns Orientalism for its oscillation, the same unadjudicated pair of claims identified in the section above: Said cannot consistently hold both that Orientalism falsified a real Orient and that the Orient was its fabrication, and Ahmad presses the contradiction with genuine philosophical force, arguing that Said's humanism and his Foucauldian inheritance pull the book apart. The pressing is one of the critique's durable achievements, and later scholarship of all camps has had to route around it. Yet the same chapter illustrates the method's characteristic risk: a critic determined to locate a field's political function can slide from the analysis of an argument to the assignment of its author to a position in a global drama, and readers have differed ever since over where the analysis ends and the assignment begins in Ahmad's pages. The field's defenders answered with counter-charges of their own, that the book's socialism was of a declamatory kind, long on the naming of the correct politics and short on engagement with the literary and historical detail the field had actually produced, and that its picture of metropolitan academe as a machine for manufacturing consent ignored the real institutional marginality of the work it attacked. Both the critique and the counter-charges remain in print and in use, and the comparison's obligation is to register the exchange at full strength rather than to crown a winner three decades on, in a dispute whose terms the trajectory explanation has already reframed: Ahmad was shooting, largely, at the later phase in its metropolitan form, while the founding phase in its South Asian form, whose historians shared many of his commitments, was mostly outside his field of fire. His book says comparatively little about Guha's insurgents. It says a great deal about what their grandchildren's vocabulary was doing in English departments. The phases govern even here.

## The aura charge: criticism in the age of global capitalism

Two years later the argument about the field's social location received its most quoted formulation. Arif Dirlik's essay "The Postcolonial Aura", published in Critical Inquiry in 1994, characterised postcolonial criticism as the ideology of a globalised intellectual stratum: a body of thought whose categories, the celebration of hybridity, the suspicion of totality, the relocation of politics into representation, answered to the situation of intellectuals circulating within a capitalism that had itself become global, flexible, and contemptuous of the national containers in which older anti-imperial politics had been organised. The essay's sting was its reversal of the field's self-image. Where postcolonial criticism understood itself as the opposition to a global order, Dirlik proposed that its characteristic moves were the order's own ideology in oppositional dress: difference without totality mirrors a market that sells difference; the dissolution of collective subjects mirrors a production regime that dissolves collectivities; the arrival of the postcolonial intellectual in the metropolitan university is not the infiltration of the centre by its margins but a personnel flow of the new order itself. The essay, like the location argument in Ahmad, is described here as a position in the literature, attributed to its author and date, and not adopted as this article's account of any scholar's motives or worth. It is the strongest form of a genre of argument, the sociology-of-knowledge indictment, and its strength and its dangers are the same: it explains a field's shape by its conditions of production, and it licenses the reader to stop engaging the field's arguments once the explanation is in hand. The field's replies made exactly that point, objecting that the indictment refuted nothing in Said, Spivak, or Bhabha by name, and that an intellectual formation can be both the product of an order and that order's genuine critic, as the entire Marxist tradition, itself produced inside bourgeois universities, ought to remember.

The two essays of the first wave share an architecture that is worth naming, because the second wave, the book of 2013, breaks with it deliberately. Both Ahmad and Dirlik argue from the outside in: from the field's institutional location and social function toward the character of its ideas, treating the ideas largely as symptoms. The approach has an honourable Marxist pedigree, the critique of ideology, and a known defect, which is that symptom-reading is unfalsifiable in the hands of a skilled practitioner, since any argument can be redescribed as the expression of the position it is said to symptomise. The replies exploited the defect effectively. But the first wave also identified, beneath the sociology, a substantive absence that no amount of rejoinder about genre could dissolve: the field, in its metropolitan ascendancy, had remarkably little to say about the capitalism of its own moment, the structural adjustments then being imposed across Africa, Asia, and Latin America, the recomposition of labour, the new enclosures. A reader could complete a distinguished education in postcolonial studies, a defender of the critique might say, without once encountering the balance of payments. That observation survives every counter about motives, because it is a claim about a syllabus, checkable against syllabuses, and it is the observation the second wave's author took up and converted into a different and more dangerous kind of argument: one that grants the field its texts and contests them on the evidence of history itself.

## Dissents from within the archive: the historians who stayed with the documents

Between the first wave and the second stands a set of criticisms that the standard telling, with its two camps, tends to lose, because their authors were neither metropolitan theorists of capital nor converts to discourse: they were historians of South Asia who had been inside the collective's own world and dissented from its trajectory on evidentiary grounds. The most consequential was Sumit Sarkar, a historian of Bengal who had contributed to the collective's early volumes and broke with it publicly, charting what he called the decline of the subaltern in Subaltern Studies in an essay collected in his Writing Social History of 1997. Sarkar's charge was precise and documentary. The early project, on his account, had achieved real discoveries about popular politics; the later project, under the influence of the linguistic turn and of a certain reading of the postcolonial moment, had progressively abandoned the subaltern as a historical subject, replacing the reconstruction of consciousness and action with the analysis of elite texts about the subaltern, until the collective named for history from below was producing, in his judgement, a sophisticated history of representations from above. The trajectory explanation recognises Sarkar's essay as its own kind of evidence: a participant's log of the migration, kept by a man who stayed behind at the archive. His dissent cannot be dismissed as an outsider's ideology critique, and the field's engagement with it has been correspondingly uneasy.

### What did the member who walked away object to?

Sumit Sarkar objected that the collective he had helped to found had, under the turn toward discourse, abandoned its subject: the subaltern whose consciousness and action the early volumes recovered from the archive was being replaced later by elite representations of the subaltern, so that a project of history from below had become a history of texts from above.

An earlier dissent had anticipated the methodological core of Sarkar's complaint. In 1988, the same year as Spivak's essay, Rosalind O'Hanlon published "Recovering the Subject", an examination of the collective's founding ambition from the standpoint of a historian sympathetic to its aims. O'Hanlon's question was whether the subaltern subject the project promised to recover was recoverable in the form the promise assumed: a coherent, self-present consciousness, standing outside the relations and representations that constituted it, awaiting the historian's sympathetic retrieval. Her answer was sceptical, and it came from inside the Marxist historiographical tradition rather than from deconstruction: the subject of history, she argued, is not found but made, in struggle and in discourse together, and a project that posits the subaltern's intact consciousness reproduces, in inverted form, the humanist essentialism of the elitist historiography it opposes. The essay is a gift to the comparativist because it falsifies the simple map twice over. It is a criticism of the collective's founding phase on grounds nearly identical to Spivak's, delivered by a Marxist historian in a journal of Asian studies, and it shows that the founding phase's problems were visible to its friends in its own decade, in its own idiom. The turn, on this evidence, was not a corruption imported by literary theorists in the nineteen nineties; it was one resolution, among several available, of difficulties the founding generation had already diagnosed in itself. O'Hanlon's own resolution pointed elsewhere, toward a social history in which subjects are analysed as constituted within relations of power without surrendering the analysis of the relations. That road, less travelled by the collective, is one of the routes the combination section below must reckon with, since it represents a synthesis attempted from within historiography rather than announced from the podium of theory.

The internal dissents matter to the verdict this article eventually reaches for a further reason: they discipline the calibration of praise and blame. When the book-length critique of 2013 arrived, it was received in the metropolitan arena as a thunderbolt, an external army breaching the walls. In the longer record it looks more like the most systematic of a series of reckonings that the collective's own world had been conducting for twenty five years, by Sarkar on the trajectory, by O'Hanlon on the subject, by Spivak on representation, and by others on gender, caste, and the archive's limits. The collective's achievement and its vulnerability are the same fact: it argued with itself in public, at a level of quality that attracted the world's attention, and the world's polemic simplified the argument into a war between two armies that neither camp's best members would recognise as their own. The reader who keeps the internal dissents in view is inoculated against the simplification, and is ready for the second wave's arrival in its true proportions: a formidable book, aimed at a determinate target, whose author knew exactly which historiographical claims he intended to destroy.

## The detailed critique: the specter of capital

In 2013 the dispute acquired the text around which it has since organised itself: Vivek Chibber's Postcolonial Theory and the Specter of Capital, the most detailed Marxist critique the field has received, detailed in a specific and consequential sense. Where the first wave had argued from the field's location to its ideas, Chibber argues from the field's texts to the record of history, granting the work its scholarship, reconstructing its claims in their strongest form, and then submitting a small number of determinate claims to the evidence. The book announces its target with unusual precision, and precision about the target is the first thing a fair account must preserve: the target is the Subaltern Studies collective's mature theoretical framework, in the work of Guha, Chatterjee, and Chakrabarty, and through it the wider postcolonial theory that drew on that framework. It is not, and its author insists it is not, an attack on the study of colonial discourse, on Said's exposure of Orientalist representation, or on attention to culture as such, none of which the book disputes. The claims selected for destruction are three, and each is a claim about history rather than about reading. First, Guha's thesis that the colonial state was a dominance without hegemony because the bourgeoisie in the colony failed to universalise its rule, failing where its European counterpart had succeeded in making its dominance into leadership. Second, the associated thesis, developed in Chatterjee's work on the politics of the governed and the rule of colonial difference, that capital in the colonial world governed through the preservation of difference because it had failed to carry through the homogenising, universalising transformation it achieved in the West. Third, Chakrabarty's framing, in which capital's universal categories are shown to be interrupted by History 2, read by Chibber as the philosophical generalisation of the first two claims: the doctrine that capital's universalisation, real in Europe, misfired in the colony, and that social theory must therefore abandon or radically relativise its universal categories east of Suez.

### What exactly did the book-length critique set out to prove?

It set out to prove that the collective's central historical claim is false in both its halves: that capital did not fail to universalise relations in the colonial world in the manner alleged, and that the European bourgeoisie, the standard of successful universalisation, was neither the liberal nor the universalising force the claim requires it to have been.

The demolition proceeds by refusing the comparison's implied baseline. On the European half: the bourgeoisies of the metropolitan nineteenth century, examined without their own mythology, did not rule primarily by consent, did not extend liberal rights universally, and did not homogenise their own societies into a uniform citizenship; they governed through restricted franchises, coerced labour regimes at home and abroad, and a cultural leadership that was partial, contested, and often enough a veneer over coercion. If dominance without hegemony describes rule that does not secure the active consent of the ruled, then it describes most bourgeois rule most of the time, and its presence in the colony cannot mark the colony's exceptional failure. On the colonial half: capital's universalising drive, properly defined, was never a drive toward cultural homogenisation or liberal politics. It is the drive to subsume labour under the capital relation, to generalise market dependence, to make the reproduction of life everywhere pass through the wage and the commodity, and that drive operated in the colony with exactly the ruthlessness the theory predicts, entirely compatible with ruling through racial hierarchy, communal categories, and preserved custom where those served accumulation. Capital does not need its subjects to be liberal individuals; it needs them to be exploitable, and it is promiscuously indifferent to the cultural forms under which exploitation proceeds. The collective's error, on this account, was to measure capital by a liberal yardstick capital itself never used, and then to record the shortfall as a discovery about the East. The political moral completes the argument: universal categories are not the enemy of critique but its only weapons, since the interests that exploitation violates, in bodily integrity, in subsistence, in resistance to domination, are universal interests, recognisable in the behaviour of peasants and workers on every continent, whose ubiquitous resistance to being squeezed is unintelligible if their worlds are sealed in incommensurable difference. To abandon universals, the book concludes, is to disarm the colonised in the name of respecting them, and to leave local dominations, of caste, gender, and community, without a tribunal.

The book's force, which the replies section must not be allowed to diminish by summary, lies in three qualities rarely combined. It reads the collective's texts closely and quotes them at length, so that its reconstructions can be checked. It specifies falsifiable claims and marshals comparative evidence, from the history of European labour and franchise to the political economy of colonial states, in a form historians can grade. And it explains, rather than merely denouncing, the appeal of the position it attacks, rooting the universalisation error in a defensible observation, that colonial rule really was saturated with difference, which the collective then misattributed to capital's failure rather than to capital's method. Readers who dissent from its conclusions have rarely been able to dismiss its questions, and the subsequent debate, in journals, symposia, and review essays, has been conducted at an altitude the first wave never reached. It is also, in the trajectory explanation's terms, a book about the founding phase's theses armed as a book about the whole field: its historical artillery is trained on Guha's claims about the colonial state, and its philosophical chapters generalise the victory to the pivot book's distinction, a generalisation the replies contest at exactly the joint where phase passes into phase. That contest is the subject of the next section, and it is given equal length by design, because the brief of this article and the evidence of the exchange alike require that the replies be heard at the same strength as the charge.

## The folklore of the demolition

Before the replies are given their section, the book's reception requires a word of its own, because the reception generated a folklore that the comparison must clear away if the replies are to be heard at their true weight. In the folklore's heroic version, a lone materialist entered the temple of postcolonial theory in 2013 and destroyed it, the field's subsequent silence or rout attesting the kill. In the folklore's counter version, a crude polemicist ignorant of the literature he attacked was repelled without difficulty, the replies having left nothing standing. Both versions are circulation products, manufactured by the same economy of academic reputation that the first wave's sociology described, and both are false in the way of circulation products: by the substitution of a verdict on a career for an engagement with claims. The documentary record is less dramatic and more interesting. The book was reviewed, debated, and assigned; its targets replied in print within the year; symposia were convened; the field's syllabuses absorbed it as an antagonist, which is how fields honour the criticisms they survive; the collective's members continued their projects, several of which took up the critique's themes of capital and its forms explicitly. A field is not a building, and books do not demolish fields; they redistribute standing within them, forcing restatements, taxing the careless formulations, and retiring the arguments that cannot be restated. By that measure the book achieved a great deal, and by that measure also its achievement has a definite boundary, which the replies mark.

### Did the book-length critique end the field it attacked?

No. It redistributed standing within the field, forced its historical claims into refutable form, and retired some formulations that could not be restated, but the field's projects continued, its principals replied in print within the year, and its syllabuses absorbed the book as an antagonist rather than expiring under it.

The folklore matters beyond hygiene, because it replicates, in the dispute's own reception, the pathology the trajectory explanation diagnosed in the dispute itself. Just as the polemic attributed one phase's claims to a whole career, the folklore attributes a whole field's fate to one book, because a demolition is a better story than a redistribution and because camps recruit with stories. The reader who has followed the argument this far is equipped to resist both versions with the same instruments used throughout: ask which claims, whose, of what date, and what the replies actually said. Folklore cannot survive those questions, which is why folklore prefers not to be asked them. There is a further asymmetry worth noting for the sake of the younger scholars who inherit this dispute. The book's author wrote as a Marxist sociologist intervening in a neighbouring field's journals and conferences, an act of intellectual trespass that the receiving field could have ignored at no professional cost; that it instead argued, at length and at the highest level its principals could command, is evidence about the field's seriousness that its detractors' folklore cannot accommodate, just as the book's own close reading of the collective's texts is evidence about its author's seriousness that the counter folklore cannot accommodate. The exchange, taken whole, is the two traditions at their best, which is precisely why its simplification into a demolition, in either direction, should be refused by anyone who proposes to learn from it. The replies now follow, and they should be read as what they were: the considered answer of a research tradition to the most exacting external audit it had received, delivered by the very scholars whose claims were under audit.

## The replies: the collective answers its critic

The replies came from the collective's members and from scholars across the field, in review essays, in a published exchange in the Economic and Political Weekly, in journal symposia devoted to the book, and in the subsequent work of the principals, and they converge on four arguments while differing in emphasis and temper. The first argument is that the critique misidentifies the claim it destroys. Partha Chatterjee's reply, "Subaltern Studies and 'Capital'", published in the Economic and Political Weekly in 2013, states the point with the authority of a founding member: the collective's claims about the colonial state and about capital were claims about power and political modernity, about the specific forms through which colonial rule was exercised and legitimised, not a denial that labour was subsumed, markets generalised, or exploitation organised on an expanding scale. The rule of colonial difference named a technique of government, the constitution of the colonised as a different order of being requiring a different order of rule, and that technique is fully compatible with, indeed was an instrument of, the universalising drive the critique defends. Capital universalised, on this reply, precisely by differentiating; the homogenisation the critique acquits capital of intending was never the phenomenon the collective was describing.

### How did the collective's members answer the book-length critique?

They answered that the critique mistook its target: the claims attacked concerned the forms of colonial rule and their categories, not a denial that capital subsumed labour and generalised markets; capital's universalisation proceeded through, not despite, the manufacture of colonial difference; and the pivot book's distinction was an argument about categories, not a report of capital's failure.

The second argument concerns the European baseline from the other side. If the metropolitan bourgeoisie was indeed as coercive and its hegemony as thin as the critique demonstrates, the replies ask, then what work is the concept of hegemony doing in the critique's own history, and has the critique dissolved a real distinction along with a false application? The colonial state's difference from the metropolitan, the repliers insist, was not a shortfall on a shared scale but a constitutional feature: race as an organising principle of law, the formal division of the population into citizens and subjects, the acknowledged permanence of separate political orders, features with analogues in Europe's treatment of its own subordinated populations but not with equivalents in its self-understanding as a political community. To grade both states as dominance without hegemony may equalise the score while erasing the phenomenon, the phenomenon being how the difference was produced, justified, and lived. The third argument defends the pivot book on its own ground. Chakrabarty's replies, in the exchange and in his subsequent restatements, return to the definitions: History 1 and History 2 are not two regions or two stages, the first European and the second colonial; they are two registers present wherever capital rules, the register of its self-expanding logic and the register of the lifeworlds that logic presupposes, encounters, and never exhausts. A critique that reads the distinction as a claim about capital's geographical failure has, on this reply, mistaken an analytic distinction for an empirical border, and has then refuted the border nobody drew. The universal categories, the reply continues, were never abandoned: they were shown to be double, at once indispensable to critique and inadequate to the worlds critique addresses, and a Marxism that cannot hold that doubleness has mistaken its instruments for the world. The fourth argument is methodological and returns the trajectory explanation's compliment with interest. The critique, the repliers observe, treats Subaltern Studies as a single doctrine across three decades, flattening a collective that argued with itself continuously, attributing to all members the theses of whichever text is under fire, and ignoring the project's internal critics, its transformations, and its considerable body of work that fits neither the charge nor the stereotype. A materialism so attentive to history in its objects, the reply suggests, might extend the same attention to the history of its targets.

Beyond the four arguments, the exchange generated a penumbra of assessment from scholars attached to neither camp, and a fair account should register its balance. The critique was widely credited with restoring political economy and comparative evidence to a debate that had drifted into exegesis, and with forcing the field to state its historical claims in refutable form, a service even some of its targets' defenders acknowledged. It was also widely faulted for the ferocity of its simplifications at the philosophical joint, for a dismissiveness toward entire literatures it did not engage, and for a confidence about the universality of interests that sits uneasily beside the critique's own demonstration that bourgeois universals were so often counterfeit in practice: if the proclaimed universals of the nineteenth century were ideology, the repliers' sympathisers asked, by what test are the critic's universals certified genuine, and the exchange's most durable residue may be that question, which neither side has fully answered. The honest summary is that the book landed its heaviest blows on the founding phase's historical thesis, where the evidence genuinely is difficult for the thesis in its strong form, and that its philosophical generalisation, the claim to have dissolved the pivot book's framework and with it the field's warrant, remains contested by replies that the critique's partisans have not persuaded the uncommitted to dismiss. That distribution of verdicts is, in miniature, the verdict of this whole article, and it was invisible before the phases were distinguished. The reader has now seen the dispute's loudest battle fought with the phase rule in hand, and can perform the same operation on the counter-charge to which the article turns: the postcolonial indictment of Marxism, which likewise strikes a phase of its target and is routinely banked against the whole.

## The Latin American mirror: coloniality and the view from another periphery

The dispute's geography has a third continent that its standard telling, organised around South Asia and the Anglophone academy, tends to forget, and the forgetting conceals a natural experiment. Latin America received the collective's example directly: a Latin American Subaltern Studies Group was founded in 1992 by scholars of the region's literatures and histories, issued its own founding statement, and set out to reread the continent's past against its creole elites. Within a decade the experiment had reproduced, in compressed form, the whole trajectory this article has traced in the South Asian original: an early phase of archive and insurgency, a turn toward discourse and the colonial text, and an internal reckoning in which historians charged the literary wing with abandoning the social, followed by the group's dissolution amid mutual reproaches that a reader of Sarkar will find eerily familiar. Independent of the group, and partly against it, the region's own theorists developed the concept of coloniality, above all in the work of the Peruvian sociologist Aníbal Quijano: the matrix of racial classification and labour control installed by the conquest of the Americas, which survived formal colonialism to structure the republics, and which Quijano, formed as a Marxist of the dependency generation, theorised as constitutive of capitalism from its first American century rather than as its feudal prelude.

### What did the Latin American debate add to the comparison?

It added a replication and a rival: the replication of the collective's whole trajectory within a decade, suggesting the migration belongs to the enterprise rather than to one continent's failings, and the rival concept of coloniality, which fused the classification of peoples with the organisation of labour in a single matrix.

The experiment's results bear on the verdict in both directions. The replication supports the trajectory explanation's institutional reading: given a founding problematic about subalternity, a metropolitan readership hungry for theory, and the reward structure of the humanities in the nineteen nineties, the migration toward discourse occurred on schedule in a different language and a different archive, which is difficult to explain by the personal failings of any group of scholars and easy to explain by the forces the first wave's sociology identified. The coloniality concept supports something else: the demonstration, from within a Marxist formation, that the racial classification of peoples and the organisation of their labour can be theorised as one matrix rather than traded between two fields as separate objects. Quijano's matrix is, in the grid's terms, a combined account announced at the level of principle, and its career exhibits both the promise and the standing difficulty of such announcements: it reorganised a research programme and generated a school, the modernity and coloniality programme, whose own internal critics have charged its later exponents with the reciprocal overshoot, a culturalism of the colonial difference in which the labour half of the matrix fades behind the classification half. The pattern is by now familiar enough to state as the comparison's melancholy law: every synthesis, carried into institutions, decays toward the register its carriers were trained in, and the grid's fourth column has to be rewon by each generation at the level of the episode. Latin America's mirror shows the law operating outside the dispute's home territory, which is what makes it evidence rather than anecdote.

## The counter-charge: what postcolonial theory says Marxism gets wrong

The postcolonial indictment of Marxism has five counts, and they should be stated as counts, separately, because they have different strengths and different targets. The first is Eurocentrism: the tradition's master narrative was built from European materials, its sequence of modes of production generalised from a European path, its universal subject, the proletariat, modelled on a European class, so that the rest of the world enters the theory as a delayed or deficient instance of a story already complete elsewhere. The second is stagism, the operational form of the first: history arranged as a staircase every society must climb, with the colonised assigned to the lower steps and their own forms of life classified as survivals, obstacles, or raw material for a transition whose direction is known in advance. The third is the textual count, Marx's own colonial writings, in which the journalist of 1853 could describe the destruction of Indian society as history's necessary solvent; the textual basis of the charge is examined in its own article in this series, [marx on colonialism debate](/2017/03/01/marx-on-colonialism-debate/), and is cross-referred rather than retried here. The fourth is the charge of the waiting room, Chakrabarty's name for the political consequence of the first two counts: a theory that knows the future assigns the colonised to anticipation, their present judged as an immature version of someone else's past, their politics perpetually premature or belated. The fifth is the quietest and in some ways the deepest: that categories travel badly, that class, civil society, the state, and the individual, forged in the analysis of European formations, deform the societies they are carried to, where kinship, caste, devotion, and community do the work those categories divide among themselves, so that applying the European kit unmodified manufactures the very backwardness it then diagnoses. Each count names a real feature of some Marxist writing. None names a feature of all of it, and the calibration of that sentence is the work of the next two sections.

### Which Marxism do the postcolonial objections actually hit?

They hit, with full force, the stagist orthodoxy that arranged all societies on a staircase and assigned the colonised to its lower steps, the version that dominated official Marxism for a long middle period; they graze the broader tradition where its categories travel unexamined; and they miss the multilinear reading of Marx's later work, which the next section states.

It is part of a fair hearing to note which of the counts the Marxist side has substantially conceded from within. The stagist staircase, in its rigid form, was already an embarrassment to serious Marxist historiography before the postcolonial critique generalised the charge: historians of India, of Africa, of Latin America had spent decades complicating the sequence under the pressure of their archives, and the series' treatment of [indian marxist historians](/2018/03/01/indian-marxist-historians/) documents a tradition arguing with its own scheme in print. The deepest version of the argument is therefore not whether stagism existed and did damage, which is common ground, but whether stagism is Marxism's essence or its degeneration: whether the staircase is the theory itself or a codification imposed on a body of work whose own development, especially in the founder's final decades, was moving against it. That is an interpretive and historical question with texts on both sides, and it is the question the strongest objection presses to its limit. The objection deserves, and by the standing rule of this series receives, a section of its own, at full strength, attributed, and answered without evasion, including the concession the evidence requires.

## The strongest objection, stated in full and answered

The strongest postcolonial objection to Marxism is not the charge of European birth, which would convict every modern theory including postcolonial theory itself, whose instruments were forged in Parisian seminars. It is the charge, stated most systematically in Chakrabarty's Provincializing Europe, that Marxist categories are not merely from Europe but structured by a European historical experience in a way that survives their transport: they presuppose a sequence, the dissolution of precapitalist relations, the formation of classes on the European pattern, the emergence of the abstract citizen and the abstract labourer, and when the historian carries them to a colonised society she finds herself narrating that society as a failure to complete the sequence, its present read as Europe's past not yet overcome, its difference converted into backwardness. The objection is methodological before it is political. It alleges that the very instruments of Marxist analysis encode a philosophy of history, historicism, in which some peoples are the subjects of History and others are its laggards, and that this encoding reproduces, inside the critique of empire, the empire's own temporal arrogance: the coloniser also said the colonised were behind, also promised arrival, also treated the present of the colony as the past of the metropole. At full strength the objection adds that the reproduction is not an accident of application but a property of the categories, since categories like capital, abstract labour, and the bourgeois state were theorised from formations in which the sequence had run, and carry its shape in their definitions. A Marxism that answers this objection by citing the good intentions of Marxists, or the errors of a few stagist textbooks, has not understood it. The objection is about the grain of the tools.

The answer begins by granting the objection its true target and its historical extent, because the concession is large and must not be rationed. The stagist version of Marxism was, for a long period, the dominant one. Codified in the Second International's evolutionary confidence, hardened in the official doctrine of the communist states, where a five stage sequence was promulgated as law and applied to every society on earth with the confidence of a railway timetable, and reproduced in countless textbooks and party schools, stagism was what most of the world, colonised and coloniser alike, encountered under Marxism's name. Generations of anti-colonial militants were instructed by their own movements to ally with a national bourgeoisie destined to complete a stage, to postpone social questions until the sequence permitted them, to read their own societies' forms as survivals awaiting dissolution. The postcolonial objection against that Marxism is not merely available; it is documented in the colonised world's own experience of its would-be liberators, and any defence of the tradition that skips the concession is worth nothing. The answer then proceeds to the part of the record the objection leaves out, which is the founder's own trajectory and the reading of it that the objection must, and largely does not, engage. The series' account of the instrument at stake is set out in the [historical materialism complete guide](/2010/01/15/historical-materialism-complete-guide/), and its relevant finding can be summarised plainly. In his final decades Marx was dismantling the staircase in his own workshop: studying the Russian commune and concluding, in the letter and drafts to Vera Zasulich of 1881, that it might pass to a collective order without traversing the capitalist stage, provided the surrounding conditions allowed; filling notebooks on non-European societies whose communal forms he increasingly treated as contemporaries with their own dynamics rather than as fossils; and insisting, against his own Russian disciples' impatience, that his sketch of Western European development was not a master key for every people. The multilinear reading of that late work, defended by a substantial line of scholarship, holds that the sequence was a regional finding illegitimately promoted, first by epigones and then by states, into a universal law, and that the theory's own resources, its attention to concrete formations and combined development, contradict the promotion.

The answer concludes by weighing what survives of the objection after the multilinear correction, because something does survive and honesty requires its inventory. The categories remain European in their formation even when the sequence is surrendered, and their transport remains a skilled operation rather than a mechanical application: the analyst of a caste order or a kinship economy must still ask, as the objection teaches, whether her instruments are finding the society's joints or imposing the kit's. Marxist historiography of non-European societies at its best does exactly that asking, and produces, in the work on Indian modes, African labour, and Latin American dependency that the tradition houses, analyses in which the categories are modified by their objects rather than merely applied to them. The objection, therefore, lands in graded form. Against stagist Marxism it is decisive, and stagist Marxism was for decades the Marxism that ruled states and movements, a fact no exegesis of the founder's notebooks can wish away. Against Marxism as such, as a method for analysing production and class in any formation, it reduces to a discipline, a permanent warning about categories and their travel, which the tradition ignores at its peril and which its own best practice had already internalised before the warning was systematised from outside. The strongest postcolonial criticism, like the strongest Marxist criticism surveyed earlier, turns out to strike a determinate form of its target with full force, and the target as a whole only in the diminished form of a caution. Symmetry of that kind is not a diplomatic split of the difference. It is what the evidence, organised by the grid and the trajectory, actually shows.

## Caste: the case neither kit was built for

Every comparison benefits from a test case chosen to be maximally unfair to both sides, and for this comparison the case is caste. Caste is a system at once material and representational in proportions neither tradition's kit was designed for: a division of labour and an assignment of dignity, a regime of land, occupation, and hereditary exclusion enforced by violence and endogamy, and simultaneously a cosmology, a ritual order of purity and pollution that its subjects internalised as the structure of the moral world. The Marxist kit encounters in caste a division of labour that does not behave as class should: it is ascriptive where class is positional, sanctified where exploitation is supposed to be legible, and it persists across changes of mode, land regime, and market depth that ought, on a simple reading, to have dissolved it, sorting workers within the same factory and the same union into ranked communities whose solidarity it fractures from within. The postcolonial kit encounters the reciprocal embarrassment: a discourse so thoroughly materialised in bodies, marriages, wells, and wages that treating it as representation seems to describe everything about it except its weight, while the colonial construction thesis, true in part, meets the awkward fact that the colonised possessed, in B. R. Ambedkar, a critic of caste more radical than any the empire's categories produced, whose Annihilation of Caste of 1936 arraigned caste as a division of labourers, a hierarchy of graded inequality that made solidarity structurally improbable, in an idiom owing little to colonial ethnography and much to an insider's fury.

### Why is caste the hardest test for both approaches?

Because caste is indivisibly a material division of labour and a sanctified order of representation: the structural kit must explain why it survives the transformations that dissolve other relations, and the representational kit must explain how a cosmology acquires the force of land, wages, marriage, and violence.

Ambedkar's presence in the argument is the standing rebuke to both camps' self-sufficiency, and the comparison should record his use of each side's resources without conscription into either. To the Marxists of his own India he posed the question their kit least wanted: why a movement of the exploited should be led, in its theory and its personnel, by the very castes that monopolised literacy, and why class solidarity should be expected from workers whose religion instructed them in their graded places; his answer, that caste must be annihilated as a religious and social order before class politics could begin, reversed the sequence the staircase prescribed and anticipated, from 1936, the postcolonial objection about categories and their travel, since he had watched European class vocabulary arrive in India and sit, unexamined, atop a social formation it could not describe. To the representational tradition he poses the reciprocal question: caste was indeed codified, enumerated, and hardened by the colonial census, but the order the census found was no administrative fiction, and the colonised's own most searching critic treated it as a material system of graded exploitation whose abolition required the destruction of the scriptural authority that sanctified it, an abolition he pursued through law, conversion, and constitutional engineering rather than through the reading of texts. The collective itself was slow on caste, its founding volumes centred on class and community in registers where caste appeared more often as context than as problem, and its critics within India, including historians of the Dalit movements that Ambedkar founded, taxed it with reproducing, in radical dress, the upper caste academy's field of vision. That taxation belongs in this article's record because it is the category travel objection arriving from inside the colonised world, addressed to the postcolonial tradition itself, and it demonstrates the objection's universality: every kit deforms some object, and the only defence is the discipline of letting the object answer back. The best work on caste in the decades since has been done by scholars who hold both kits at once and trust neither, reconstructing caste's political economy and its cosmology in the same argument, which is the combination problem posed by the hardest case the subcontinent offers.

## The waiting room: stagism and its long dominance

The waiting room deserves a page of its own because it is the point where the postcolonial critique touches not merely texts but the political experience of the colonised inside the movements that claimed them. Historicism's consignment was not an abstraction to the Indian communist told that his country's bourgeois stage must mature under a national leadership he distrusted, or to the African nationalist informed that tribal forms were survivals his party must patiently outgrow, or to the peasant organiser whose constituency's devotion was classified as the opium of a premodern stratum. The staircase had administrators. Its costs were borne by real movements in real time: alliances contracted with classes that used the alliance to consolidate and then to crush the left that had midwifed them; social questions deferred until the deferral became the regime; indigenous and communal forms, which might have been materials for a different modernity, treated as rubble to be cleared for a future drawn in another continent's image. The postcolonial literature's insistence on this record is its most concrete political service, and it is notable that the service is performed with historical tools, names, dates, congresses, and resolutions, that any Marxist historian can verify and many have. When the critique confines itself to this record, its Marxist readers are found, increasingly, on its own side of the line.

The corresponding service on the other flank is performed by the recovery of the late Marx, and it also deserves statement without varnish, because the recovery is sometimes oversold into a legend of the founder's posthumous innocence. The multilinear texts are real but they are fragments: a letter with three drafts, notebooks published a century late, a correspondence in which the anti-stagist position is argued with disciples who mostly declined to be persuaded. The tradition that actually governed the twentieth century took the staircase and left the notebooks in the drawer, and it is a nice question whether a theory is responsible for its dominant institutional form or only for its best textual one. The postcolonial objection assumes the former; the Marxist defence assumes the latter; and the reader should notice that both assumptions are themselves theoretical commitments, about what a tradition is, that no archive can settle directly. What the archive settles is the distribution: for the long middle of the twentieth century, the staircase ruled, the notebooks slept, and the colonised encountered the staircase. A verdict that grades the tradition by its best texts will acquit it of the waiting room. A verdict that grades it by its operative history will convict it, and will then owe an account of why a theory so little stagist in its fragments proved so stagist in its institutions, an account that leads back, by a road the postcolonial critic rarely travels, to the sociology of parties and states that the Marxist tradition itself supplies. The dispute at this depth becomes genuinely joint property, and the comparison can leave it there, marked as the deepest open question the two traditions share.

## Does capital universalise? The question underneath the quarrel

Strip the dispute of its personalities and one empirical-theoretical question remains standing, the question on which the grid's third row turns: does capital universalise social relations, and if it does, what exactly is universalised? The quarrel persists partly because the disputants answer different versions of the question. Distinguish three. In the strong cultural version, universalisation means homogenisation: capital dissolves local forms and replaces them with a uniform civilisation of markets, individuals, and secular citizenship, so that difference is a residue in transit to extinction. In the strong relational version, universalisation means subsumption: capital makes the reproduction of life depend on the market and the wage everywhere it reaches, whatever cultural forms that dependence wears, so that difference is the clothing of a single relation. In the incomplete version, universalisation names a tendency that is real but constitutionally unfinished: the relation spreads, and the worlds it enters answer it, modify it, and persist within it, so that the outcome is neither uniformity nor untouched plurality but a contested compound in which the analyst must always specify which level she is describing. The collective's critics defend the relational version and convict the collective of denying it. The collective's defenders reply that they denied only the cultural version, and hold something like the incomplete one. The founding phase's historical thesis, Guha's dominance without hegemony, can be restated in these terms as the claim that in the colony the relational drive itself was politically cut short, its bearer class unable to perform the cultural and political universalisation that in Europe had accompanied it; and the book-length critique's counterclaim is that the accompaniment was a European myth, so that nothing was cut short. The reader now sees why the argument is so hard to close: each side's evidence is real, and each side's question is slightly different, and the difference between the questions is itself the residue of the two objects with which this article began.

### What would count as capital universalising a social relation?

It would count if the reproduction of life in a society came to depend, for the generality of its members, on the sale of labour power and the purchase of necessities through the market, so that land, labour, and subsistence were organised by the capital relation whatever cultural and political forms clothed the dependence.

The evidence, arranged by version, is instructive. For the relational version the record is overwhelming and neither camp's serious scholars deny it: by the middle of the twentieth century there was scarcely a society on earth whose reproduction did not pass through world markets, wage labour in some proportion, and states fiscally bound to accumulation, and the colonial period was precisely the violent installation of that dependence, as this series' account of the economic framework in the [marxism and imperialism guide](/2017/02/01/marxism-and-imperialism-guide/) sets out in full. For the cultural version the record is equally clear in the opposite direction: no such homogenisation occurred, the secular individual citizen remained a minority form even in the metropoles, and colonial difference, far from dissolving, was administratively deepened and then inherited by successor states whose politics still runs through the categories the empires installed. The incomplete version is where the scholarly frontier actually lies, and its research programme is the most demanding of the three: to trace, in a given formation, how the capital relation and the lifeworlds it subsumes modify each other over time, which requires the practitioner to work in both registers of the grid at once and explains the programme's slow advance relative to its manifestos. The practical upshot for the comparison is bracing. On the question that can be answered by evidence, the camps largely agree, whatever their rhetoric; on the question that divides them, the division is over the cultural version, which the Marxist side's own best theory never required and the postcolonial side sometimes attacks as though it were the whole of Marxism. The universalisation quarrel, correctly specified, is real but narrow, and its narrowness is the trajectory explanation's final vindication: only a polemic that mistakes phases and versions for essences could have made it look like a war over everything.

## Universal categories: abandonment, defence, and translation

The second kernel beneath the quarrel concerns the fate of universal categories themselves, and it is here that the misreadings are densest, so the positions must be pinned with unusual care. The postcolonial position, in the pivot book's exact form, is not that universal categories are false or dispensable. It is that the universals of European thought arrive double: as genuine instruments of critique, forged in struggles against real dominations and indispensable to any further struggle, and as provincial artefacts, bearing the marks of the particular history that produced them, liable to mistake that history's path for the world's destination. To provincialize is to hold both truths at once, using the categories while declining their innocence, translating them through the worlds they are carried into and letting the translation change them. The Marxist objection, in the book-length critique's exact form, is not that categories must be innocent. It is that critique stands in need of some universals that are not merely provincial products held under erasure: the interest of every labouring person in not being dominated, in subsistence, in the integrity of the body, without which exploitation has no definition and resistance no rationale, and whose universality is vouched not by European philosophy but by the behaviour of the exploited on every continent, who resist their exploitation in idioms that presuppose exactly those interests. Each position, pinned exactly, is formidable. The first defends the doubleness of every inherited concept; the second defends the floor beneath which doubleness must not be allowed to dissolve the ground of criticism itself.

The distance between the pinned positions is real but much smaller than the distance between their slogans, and the residual disagreement can be named. It concerns the status of the floor. The defence treats the universal interests as bedrock, prior to translation, identical in the Bengali mill and the Lancashire one, and is content that the idioms differ so long as the interest is honoured. The provincializing position suspects bedrock talk, having seen bedrock invoked for the staircase and the civilising mission, and prefers to treat even the floor as a universal that must be continually re-won through translation, never simply possessed. This is a genuine philosophical difference about the epistemology of critique, and it is unlikely to be settled by the evidence of any archive, because both sides can narrate the same resistances in their own grammar. What can be settled, and what the comparison should insist on, is the misreading that has done the practical damage: the claim that provincialising Europe means rejecting universal categories entirely. Its author denies it, in terms, repeatedly, in the book and after it; the categories are to be retained and deprovincialised by use, not burned. Polemic that continues to attribute the rejection to him after the denials is not engaging the position, and the same discipline applies in reverse to the slogan that Marxism must always consign the colonised to a staircase: the multilinear texts deny it, in terms, and engagement begins after the denials are registered. A reader who enforces this rule, attributing to each side only what it avows, will find the dispute smaller, harder, and more interesting than its reputation, which is the most useful condition in which a comparison can leave its subject.

## The agent: classes, subjects, and the crowd

The grid's fourth row, the account of the agent, records a difference so deep that it explains much of the dispute's emotional register, since a theory's agent is also its hope. In the Marxist register, history is made by classes: collectivities constituted by their position in production, whose interests are legible from that position, whose organisation converts position into force, and whose action, strikes, insurgencies, revolutions, parties, is the engine the narrative follows. The agent is plural, interested, and embodied; its consciousness matters enormously, but as a variable to be explained and organised, the famous passage from class in itself to class for itself, rather than as the medium in which the whole drama occurs. In the postcolonial register, history is made, insofar as the idiom permits the phrase, by subjects: persons formed within discourse, whose agency appears not as the pursuit of a legible interest from a known position but as negotiation within the categories that form them, the mimicry that unsettles, the fragment that refuses the nation's story, the subaltern whose speech the structures cannot register. The agent is singular before it is plural, formed before it acts, and its action is characteristically a displacement of meaning rather than a seizure of the means of production. Each register finds the other's agent unconvincing at exactly the point of its own confidence. The structural eye sees the negotiating subject and asks what it eats, who armed the scene of its negotiation, and why the negotiation's outcomes track the distribution of property so faithfully if property explains nothing. The representational eye sees the class and asks who told it that it was one, in what vocabulary its interest became legible to itself, and why classes sharing a position have narrated themselves into nations, castes, and faiths more readily than into the solidarity the theory assigned them.

### Who makes history, on each account?

On the structural account, classes make it: collectivities formed by their place in production, whose interests follow from that place and whose organisation turns position into force. On the representational account, subjects make it: persons formed within discourse, whose characteristic action is the negotiation, displacement, and refusal of the categories that constitute them.

The crowd is where the two agents meet and where the dispute can be tested against a single figure, because both traditions have a canonical crowd and they are not the same crowd. The founding phase's crowd is Guha's insurgent peasantry: collective, purposeful, fluent in a political grammar of signs and inversions, destroying the instruments of its subordination with a selectivity that refutes every elite description of it as a mob. That crowd is the structural agent recovered by representational means, the hybrid the early collective at its best achieved, and its existence in the literature is the permanent embarrassment of any account that separates the traditions cleanly. The later field's characteristic crowd is different: it appears in the analysis of nationalism and of communal violence as a body whose identities are the stakes of representation, formed and reformed by rumour, print, and ritual, capable of a violence that class position predicts poorly and that the politics of the fragment must be invoked to understand. The historian who has read both crowds knows that actual crowds have been each in turn, sometimes in the same week, and that the choice of which crowd a theory sees is a wager about the episode, not a discovery about crowds in general. The agent row of the grid therefore ends, as every row ends, in a specification rather than a verdict: a combined account must show a collectivity whose position disposes it and whose self understanding directs it, with the disposition and the direction both evidenced and their occasional divergence, the crowd that starves as a class and kills as a community, faced as the datum it is rather than explained away by whichever register the analyst did not bring. The traditions' dispute about the agent is the least resolvable of their disputes by evidence alone, because the evidence is generated by the instruments, and it is correspondingly the dispute in which the reader gains most from holding both instruments in view at once.

## Choosing an instrument: what each approach can and cannot explain

Comparison articles owe the reader a decision procedure, and the grid supplies one if its rows are read as instructions rather than as scores. Begin with the phenomenon and ask which register it lives in. If the question is why an empire was profitable, why a colony's labour fled or was driven in a particular direction, why a nationalist movement split where it split or a postcolonial state adopted the economic policies it did, the instruments are the Marxist ones, because the causes in play are property, coercion, and accumulation, and no reading of representations will substitute for the reconstruction of the circuits. If the question is why the colonised described themselves in the coloniser's categories long after the coloniser left, why a census category acquired the force of a fate, why a novel could reorganise a metropole's conscience or fail to, or how a liberation movement came to imagine the nation in a borrowed grammar, the instruments are the postcolonial ones, because the causes in play are representation, subjectivity, and the authority of descriptions, and no ledger will substitute for the reading of the texts. The harder and more valuable case is the compound question, why a movement that won the state reproduced the state's categories, why extraction wore the cultural forms it wore, why resistance took the idioms it took, and for those the grid's fourth column is the honest specification: both instruments, on the same episode, with the relations between the levels argued rather than assumed. The traditions' respective cannots are the negative space of the same procedure. The Marxist instrument cannot, by itself, explain the tenacity of a category after its material function has lapsed, the inner life of domination, or the forms in which a future is imagined; the postcolonial instrument cannot, by itself, explain why the categories were backed by famine and rifles at the moments they were, or why the whole representational order bent where the circuits of accumulation bent.

### Which questions should send a reader to the other approach?

Questions of profit, labour, land, and state policy send the reader to the structural instruments, and questions of category, selfhood, legitimacy, and the afterlife of descriptions send the reader to the representational ones; compound questions, in which a material arrangement and the categories that clothed it explain each other, require both instruments on the same evidence.

Stated so plainly, the procedure can sound like a peace treaty, and two warnings against that misreading belong here. The first is that the division of territory is itself contested at the borders, because each tradition's strongest practitioners deny that the border runs where the procedure draws it: the Marxist will claim the inner life of domination for a theory of ideology properly understood, and the postcolonial reader will claim that the circuits themselves were constituted by the representations that made land, labour, and race thinkable as governable quantities. The border skirmishes are the living research frontier, and a decision procedure that pretended to settle them would be a fraud. The second warning is that the choice of instrument is not innocent of consequence. Funding, appointments, syllabuses, and the self-understanding of whole disciplines hang on which questions a generation is trained to ask, and the two traditions' institutional rivalry, conducted in the universities where both now live, is a material fact of the story rather than a vulgar intrusion into it. The first wave's sociology of knowledge, whatever its defects as refutation, was pointing at something real: fields reproduce themselves, and the questions they cannot ask become the world they cannot see. A reader equipped with the grid can at least see both fields seeing, and can perform, privately and for the price of attention, the stereoscopic act that institutions find so difficult.

The procedure can be watched working on the case that generated half the literature: an anti-colonial nationalist movement, of the kind that filled the twentieth century. Why did it arise where and when it did? The structural instruments answer with the new classes the colonial economy had manufactured, the clerks, lawyers, merchants, and workers whose interests the empire created and then frustrated, and with the crises, wars, famines, and price movements that turned frustration into mobilisation. Why did it speak as it spoke, in the idiom of the nation, the golden past, the mother, the stolen essence? The representational instruments answer with the categories the colonial knowledge factories had installed, the history textbooks, the census boxes, the revived and codified traditions, out of which alone a self could be assembled that was large enough to claim a state. Why did it, victorious, reproduce so much of the order it had expelled, its bureaucracy, its categories, its developmental staircase? Neither instrument answers alone, because the answer lives in the traffic: a movement formed by the categories it inherited used them to mobilise the classes the economy had made, and the categories, victorious with the movement, set the limits of what the new state could imagine changing. The stereoscopic reading does not average the two answers. It locates each in its register and then does the one thing neither register does by itself, which is to narrate their interference. That narration, repeated across episodes, is the combined literature the grid specifies, and its scarcity, relative to the abundance of single register work on both sides, is the truest measure of the distance the comparison has still to travel.

## Where the argument is produced: metropole and postcolony

One question remains from the first wave that the verdict should not leave in the hands of the polemic, because it is a real question in a false form. The false form asks where a theory's producers sit and treats the answer as a verdict on the theory. The real form asks how the sites of production shape the questions a field learns to ask, and it is a question both traditions, at their most reflective, have asked about themselves. The collective's own historians have written with candour about the transformation that overtook a project conceived in the universities and archives of South Asia as its readership, recruits, and eventually its centre of gravity shifted to the metropolitan academy: the shift rewarded the portable and the theoretical over the local and the archival, elevated the essay over the monograph, and made the subaltern available as a figure for seminars that would never meet one. That is the insiders' version of the observation the first wave made from outside, and its existence entitles the observation to a hearing while denying it the status of a refutation. A field's questions are shaped by its sites; so are a rival field's. The materialist historiography that the collective argued with was itself produced in universities, party institutes, and metropolitan publishing houses, and Marxism as a world tradition was for its first century an overwhelmingly metropolitan production, exported to the colonies in printed form and indigenised there by readers who transformed it, exactly the itinerary the postcolonial instruments were later to travel in reverse.

### Does where a theory is produced determine what it can see?

It shapes the questions a theory learns to ask, the evidence it finds portable, and the audience it must please, and in that sense it leaves marks on every field; it does not determine the truth of the answers, which is settled, where it is settled, by the evidence the theory's claims are about.

The comparison's use for the question is diagnostic rather than judicial. When the two traditions are found talking past each other, the sites explain part of the failure: the questions that glow in a seminar room in New York or London, where the text is the available empire, differ from the questions that glow in a seminar room in Delhi or Cape Town, where the empire's successor state is outside the window and its categories are this morning's newspaper, and both differ from the questions of the party school or the trade union archive where the older tradition was formed. A reader who knows the sites can perform a correction that no single site performs for itself: to ask of a celebrated formulation what its site rewarded, and of a neglected one what its site taxed. Applied even handedly, the correction is one more instrument in the kit this article assembles, and it has the same property as the others: it locates claims without acquitting or convicting them. The location arguments of 1992 and 1994 are hereby assigned their exact standing in this article's economy. They are genuine contributions to the sociology of the dispute, indispensable for understanding why the field took the shape it did and why its blind spots lie where they lie. They are not, and in their authors' more careful pages do not claim to be, substitutes for the grading of the field's claims, which is done, where it can be done, on evidence about colonies rather than about professors. Readers who keep that assignment straight can have the sociology and the evidence both, which is more than either camp's folklore offers.

## The fourth instance: a pattern the series keeps meeting

A reader of this series will have noticed the shape of the verdict before, and the brief for this article directs that the recurrence be noted, since it has now repeated often enough to count as a finding rather than a mannerism. When Marxism is set beside marginalism, the comparison found two instruments trained on different objects, the theory of distribution at the margin and the theory of the social relation, each misconstruing the other as a failed version of itself. When Marxism is set beside the economics of Keynes, the same anatomy appeared: different objects, effective demand and the management of a crisis-prone order on one side, the constitution of that order and its classes on the other, with the loudest blows landing, on inspection, against degenerate forms of the rival rather than against its legitimate object. The mainstream economics comparison repeated the pattern a third time. The present article is the fourth instance. Postcolonial theory and Marxism take different objects, discourse and social relations respectively; the strongest criticism in each direction strikes a specific, checkable, degenerate or phase-bound form of the other, stagism in one direction and the founding phase's historical thesis in the other; and the residue, after the objects are distinguished and the phases dated, is a set of narrower, harder, more honest questions, about universalisation's versions, about categories and their travel, about the floor beneath critique. Four independent instances establish the pattern as a property of these encounters rather than a preference of this series: Marxism's rivals are typically constituted around an object its own object does not cover, so that refutation is rarely available to either side and the real work is always the location of the joints, where the objects touch and the claims genuinely compete. Readers who internalise the pattern acquire a transferrable skill: meet any announced war between frameworks by asking first for the objects, then for the dates, and only then for the verdicts. The wars that survive the questions are the ones worth having, and they are always smaller and better than their posters.

## Combined accounts: what joining the objects would require

The combination question can now be asked in its disciplined form: not whether the approaches can be combined in the abstract, a question whose affirmative answer costs nothing, but what a combined account would have to accomplish on a single episode to satisfy the grid's fourth column. The requirements, collected from the preceding sections, are severe. The account must reconstruct the material constitution of the episode, its property, labour, and circuits, with the structural instruments at full strength. It must reconstruct the representational constitution of the same episode, the categories through which its participants, rulers and ruled, understood what they were doing, with the reading instruments at full strength. And it must do the thing neither instrument does alone: specify the traffic between the levels, showing where a category altered a circuit, where a circuit selected a category, where a subject formed in one register acted in the other with consequences neither register predicts. Work that meets the specification exists and is not rare, though it is rarely produced under either camp's flag: it appears in the best social history of colonial societies, in anthropologies of the state that follow a file from the ledger to the imagination, in studies of nationalism that track a printed category into a crowd and a crowd into a ministry. Its authors tend to describe themselves by their archives rather than by the war's banners, which is itself diagnostic: the combination is achieved in practice, at the level of the episode, by scholars who decline the civilisational framing, and it dissolves in theory, at the level of the manifesto, where the framing is compulsory.

### What would a combined account have to hold together?

It would have to hold, on a single episode and in a single argument, the reconstruction of the material relations that constituted the episode and the reconstruction of the representations through which its participants understood it, together with a specified account of the traffic between the two, so that neither level serves as the other's decoration.

Two honest obstacles to combination deserve record, lest the requirement list read as a counsel of perfection whose only flaw is that nobody follows it. The first obstacle is asymmetry of standing: the two registers are not, in the present state of the disciplines, equally available to the same person, because the training that produces a reader of ledgers and the training that produces a reader of tropes diverge early and meet late, and combined work therefore tends to be collaborative or to be done by scholars formed before the divergence hardened, a diminishing stock. The second obstacle is that the traffic between the levels is exactly where the traditions' deepest commitments conflict: grant the category causal force and the structural account protests that determination has been abandoned; derive the category from the circuit and the representational account protests that its object has been dissolved into a reflex. The specification cannot be met by diplomacy between these protests; it can only be met episode by episode, by demonstrations in which the traffic is shown in documents, and the accumulation of demonstrations is slow, unglamorous, and unfinishable by declaration. The article's judgement on combination follows: it is the correct ideal, it is partially achieved in the best work of both traditions' penumbras, it will not be achieved by merger of the traditions as institutions, and any reader who encounters a synthesis announced at the level of principle should apply the grid to it and ask for the episode. The demand for the episode is this article's most portable bequest, and it works on Marxists, postcolonial theorists, and comparativists alike.

## Misreadings this comparison corrects

The dispute's folklore can be discharged in a final set of corrections, each stated in the form the evidence supports. It is said that postcolonial theory is a single position, uniformly hostile to Marxism and uniformly textualist. The record shows a field divided against itself on method, politics, and the status of its own founding slogans, whose most contested collective began as a Marxist project and whose canonical authors include several who worked, and work, inside the Marxist tradition's questions. It is said that the leading Marxist critique rejects the study of colonial discourse. The book of 2013 does no such thing; it grants the exposure of colonial representation and confines its attack to a determinate historical claim about capital and the colonial bourgeoisie, which is why attempts to answer it by defending discourse study in general miss it entirely. It is said that provincialising Europe means rejecting universal categories. Its author denies the reading explicitly and structures his central distinction around the retention of capital's universal history as one register of the real; the position under attack in that slogan is held by no one in the exchange. It is said that the dispute concerns whether colonialism was bad, a moral pageant in which one side defends the empire. No party to the literature defends the empire; the dispute concerns objects, methods, and two or three checkable historical claims, and the moral pageant is a recruiting poster for audiences who will never read the books. It is said, finally, that the two approaches cannot be combined because they contradict each other. They contradict each other at specified joints, on the versions of universalisation and the status of the critical floor, and they coexist without contradiction across the wide territory the grid maps, wherever a phenomenon lives in one register and its explanation in the other. The corrections share a form, and the form is the article's whole method in miniature: replace the slogan with the text, the camp with the phase, the essence with the claim, and then, only then, argue.

## A reader's protocol: four questions for any text in the dispute

The article's instruments can be compressed, in closing, into a protocol for reading any intervention in this literature, including the celebrated ones and including this article. Ask first for the object. Determine, from the text's evidence and its characteristic examples, whether the claim concerns the material constitution of a colonial order or its representational constitution, and refuse to grade it until the determination is made, since most misgrading in the literature is a failure of this first step. Ask second for the phase. Where a collective, a tradition, or an author with a long career is invoked, date the claim: the founding manifesto is not the pivot book, the stagist textbook is not the late notebooks, and a verdict earned against one is not transferable to the other without a fresh argument. Ask third for the version. Where a master term is doing the work, universalisation, discourse, class, difference, establish which of its senses the text requires, and whether the refutation on offer strikes that sense or an easier neighbour. Ask fourth for the episode. Where a synthesis or a condemnation is announced at the level of principle, demand the single worked case in which the traffic between the registers, or the failure of the rival claim, is shown in documents, and suspend judgement, in either direction, until the case is produced. Four questions, none of them technical, all of them fatal to folklore. A literature read under this protocol becomes smaller, as the article has several times observed, and the smallness is the sign of health: what remains after the questions have done their work is the real dispute, about the versions of universalisation, the travel of categories, and the floor beneath critique, conducted between named authors over checkable claims.

### How should a reader approach any celebrated claim in this dispute?

By asking four questions in order: which object the claim concerns, which phase of its author's or its collective's career it belongs to, which sense of its master term it requires, and in which worked episode its traffic between the registers, or its rival's failure, is shown in documents.

The protocol is offered as the article's practical residue, and it applies with equal force to the article itself, whose claims the reader is invited to process through it. The article's object has been the comparison rather than either tradition's territory; its phases have been dated as scrupulously as its sources allowed; its versions have been distinguished wherever a master term carried weight; and its episodes, the settlement report, the jute mills, the census and the crowd, have been produced wherever a synthesis was asserted. Where it has failed in any of the four, the failure is locatable by the same instruments, which is all a comparison can ask. The dispute between Marxism and postcolonial theory will continue, because the phenomena that generated it, an empire that was at once an extraction machine and a machinery of description, and a capitalism that universalises and differentiates in the same motion, have not retired from the world. It will continue better if its readers arrive armed with the grid, the trajectory, and the four questions, and worse if they arrive, as generations have, with a camp. The choice of equipment is the reader's. This article has made its recommendation in the only way comparisons can, by demonstration.

## For students and exam candidates

Your examination questions on this comparison reward one skill above all others: the refusal to grade one approach by the other's object. When a question asks what postcolonial theory contributes, answer in its register, the analysis of representation, category, and subject formation, and illustrate from the founding study of the Orient or from the subaltern voice essay, before you concede the limit that it brackets extraction. When a question asks the same of Marxist approaches, answer in theirs, property, labour, and the circuits of the world market, and concede the limit that meaning and selfhood arrive under-theorised. Then earn the higher band with the joints: name the universalisation question, date the collective's phases before invoking it, and attribute every criticism to its author and year, Ahmad in 1992, Dirlik in 1994, Chibber in 2013, the replies in their turn. The companion treatment of [marxism imperialism exam answers](/2017/06/15/marxism-imperialism-exam-answers/) drills the structure under timed conditions, and the [VaultBook study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) carry a card of the objects and methods grid for revision, with the trajectory explanation reduced to the five dates that unlock it: a collective founded in 1982, a pivot in 2000, and the critiques and replies arranged around those poles.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The objects and methods grid was built for your classroom. Set it before the students in the first session, with its fourth column blank, and let the term's reading fill the column in: every text the seminar meets can then be interrogated for what a combined account of its episode would require, which converts the usual recital of positions into a specification exercise with a checkable product. Teach the collective chronologically and refuse to let it be summarised as a doctrine: the founding manifesto against the pivot book, set side by side, teaches the trajectory explanation more efficiently than any lecture on it, and the internal dissents, Sarkar's above all, give students a participant's evidence for the migration they are being asked to map. Stage the 2013 exchange as a structured controversy, charge and replies in matched extracts, and assess the staging rather than the sides: the student who can state what Chatterjee's reply concedes and what it refuses has learned the transferrable skill, which is the localisation of claims. Keep the moral temperature low and the dating discipline absolute, and the seminar will reproduce, in miniature, the movement this article recommends for the literature as a whole, from camps to claims.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier in this territory is the traffic between the registers, and it is specified more precisely than it is occupied. Three commissions follow from the comparison. The first is evidentiary: the universalisation quarrel can be narrowed further by formation-level studies that state, for a given colony and period, which relations were subsumed, which categories were installed, and in what sequence the two processes drove each other, work for which the founding phase's archive skills and the later phase's reading skills are both indispensable and rarely combined in one scholar. The second is textual: the collective's own archive of self-argument, its internal critics, its drafts and correspondence as they become available, would repay the attention the [gramsci reception and misreadings](/2015/05/01/gramsci-reception-and-misreadings/) treatment gives to its Italian source, since the trajectory remains partly a matter of inference from published volumes. The third is disciplinary self-knowledge: the named critical exchange, Ahmad and Dirlik in the first wave, the book of 2013 and its replies in the second, constitutes an unusually complete record of how two fields police each other's objects, and journalists covering the universities will find in it a standing lesson, that the loudest academic wars are usually about phases, versions, and slogans, and that the documents, dated and read whole, are the only reliable correspondents.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between Marxism and postcolonial theory?

Primarily a difference of object and of the machinery used to work on it. One tradition reconstructs the material constitution of colonial societies: property regimes, labour systems, the extraction of surplus and its movement through the circuits of a world market. The other examines the representational constitution of those same societies: the categories through which colonised peoples were classified, the texts that authorised rule, and the self-understanding formed inside those descriptions. Because the objects differ, direct refutation is rarer than the polemics suggest, and genuine collisions occur only at named joints, above all the question of how far capitalism transforms the societies it enters.

### Q: What is postcolonial theory?

It is the body of scholarship, formed across literary studies, history, and anthropology from the late nineteen seventies, that analyses colonialism chiefly as a regime of knowledge and representation. Its founding inquiries asked how European writing manufactured the colonised world as an object fit for rule, how the colonised subject was formed within the descriptions supplied for it, and how those descriptions survived formal independence inside scholarship, literature, and national memory. Its characteristic instruments are close reading, the analysis of archives for their genres and silences, and suspicion toward any category that presents itself as innocent. Its unity is a family resemblance among those questions, not a creed its practitioners sign.

### Q: What is Orientalism?

In the usage the 1978 founding study fixed, it names the durable Western discourse about the Orient: a corporate institution of scholarship, literature, and administration that produced the East as sensual, stagnant, despotic, and irrational, the standing opposite of a rational and progressive West. The argument was not simply that the descriptions were prejudiced, though they were, but that they formed a system with its own rules of evidence and citation, generated inside the machinery of empire, and powerful enough to organise what could be known and said regardless of any individual writer's sympathy. The term now also carries the name of the book that made the argument.

### Q: What did Subaltern Studies argue?

At its founding in the early nineteen eighties, the collective argued that the historiography of colonial India, in both its imperial and its nationalist versions, was elitist, crediting the state or the national leadership with a history actually made also in an autonomous domain of subaltern politics, with its own consciousness and traditions, recoverable by reading the records of repression against their grain. Its guiding category came from Gramsci, and its guiding problem was hegemony, the relation between rule by coercion and rule by consent. Across later decades its emphases shifted toward representation and the critique of historical categories, which is why summaries of its argument must specify which period they describe.

### Q: What is the Marxist criticism of postcolonial theory?

It exists in two waves with different methods. The first, associated with a 1992 study of classes, nations, and literatures and an essay of 1994 on criticism in the age of global capitalism, charged the field with substituting textual politics for political economy, generalising the migrant intellectual's situation, and leaving the material offensive of capital unexamined and unopposed. The second, a book of 2013, proceeded differently, contesting on historical evidence a determinate claim it attributed to the field's most influential collective: that capital had failed to universalise its relations in the colonial world. Neither wave amounts to a rejection of studying colonial representation, a point the polemics on both sides persistently blur.

### Q: What do postcolonial theorists say Marxism gets wrong?

The indictment has several counts. Its narrative of history is charged with Eurocentrism, a sequence of stages generalised from one continent's path and then applied as the measure of all others. Its politics is charged with the waiting room: the consignment of colonised peoples to an antechamber of history until their societies ripen along the prescribed line. Its founder's journalism on India is charged with welcoming conquest as modernity's solvent. Its categories are charged with travelling badly, deforming societies organised by kinship, caste, and devotion when they are forced into a kit built for European class formations. The counts strike hardest at the stagist orthodoxy that long governed official Marxism, and weakest at the founder's own late, multilinear work.

### Q: What did Chibber argue?

In the 2013 book that reorganised the debate, Vivek Chibber argued that the Subaltern Studies collective's central historical claim fails in both of its halves. Capital, he maintained, did not fail to universalise its social relations in the colonies: its drive was never toward cultural sameness or liberal citizenship, only toward the subsumption of labour and the generalisation of market dependence, which it pursued through colonial difference rather than despite it. And the European bourgeoisie, the supposed standard of successful universalisation, had been neither liberal nor consent-based in its own rule, so the colonial shortfall the claim requires is an artefact of a flattering baseline. He concluded that abandoning universal categories disarms criticism itself.

### Q: Does capital universalise social relations?

On the relational definition, overwhelmingly yes: the dependence of life's reproduction on markets, wages, and states bound to accumulation was installed across the colonised world, by violence where necessary, and no serious party to the debate denies the installation. On the cultural definition, no: the sameness of institutions, identities, and beliefs did not follow, and colonial difference was deepened by the very administrations that generalised the market. The live dispute concerns the space between those answers, whether the universalising tendency is constitutionally incomplete, interrupted by the lifeworlds it subsumes, or whether talk of incompletion mistakes capital's ordinary method, rule through difference, for its failure.

### Q: Can the two approaches be combined?

Yes, at the level of the episode, and only there with any rigour. A combined account must reconstruct an episode's material constitution, who held the land, who laboured, where the surplus went, and its representational constitution, the categories through which participants understood their places, and must then specify the traffic between the two: where a classification altered a circuit of extraction, where a circuit selected and hardened a classification. Such work exists, mostly written by scholars who describe themselves by their archives rather than by either banner. What cannot be combined by declaration is the level of principle, where the traditions disagree about the status of universal categories and the weight of representation, and those disagreements must be argued, not dissolved.

### Q: Which approach dominates in universities?

The honest answer is disciplinary and historical rather than a single scoreline. The representational approach achieved its greatest institutional strength in literature and cultural studies programmes from the nineteen eighties onward, reshaping syllabuses and hiring across the Anglophone humanities. The structural approach retained older strongholds in history, sociology, and political economy, and its fortunes have risen and fallen with the wider standing of Marxist scholarship in each period and place. Any statement of a present balance would date quickly and would conceal the more useful fact, which is that the two formations now share institutions, students, and sometimes authors, while continuing to train different reflexes about where an explanation begins.

### Q: How does the trajectory explanation account for two fields sharing an object?

By denying that the sharing was ever simultaneous in the form the polemic imagines. The collective both fields claim began as a Gramscian historiographical project, South Asian in location and archival in method, devoted to recovering subaltern action. Over two decades it altered its object toward representation, its methods toward discourse analysis, its personnel toward literary scholarship, and its home toward the metropolitan humanities, with a book of 2000 as the pivot. The fields are therefore not two armies disputing one inheritance; they are the heirs of different phases of one changing enterprise, and most of their crossfire strikes positions the other heir never held. Specify the phase, and the shared object resolves into a succession of distinct ones.

### Q: What should a student separate between a difference of object and a difference of method?

A difference of object is a disagreement about what there is to explain: extraction and class formation in one case, classification and subject formation in the other. A difference of method is a disagreement about how an agreed object is best approached: by statistical series and structural reconstruction, or by close reading and the archaeology of statements. Separating them matters because the remedies differ. Object differences are settled, if at all, by asking which phenomena each object captures and which it misses. Method differences are settled by testing instruments against the same evidence. Polemics become confused, and examiners become unimpressed, when a method quarrel is fought as though it were an object quarrel or the reverse.

### Q: Why did the two fields divide over the universalising claim?

Because the claim is the point where their objects touch and their architectures lean against each other. The structural tradition's account of capitalism requires a universalising drive: without it, there is no world market, no general logic of accumulation, and no basis for expecting the colonised world to be transformed rather than merely raided. The representational tradition's founding observation was that the colonies were governed through manufactured difference, indefinitely prolonged, which looked like the drive's failure and suggested that the universal was a European self-image rather than a world process. Each side heard the other as denying the phenomenon its own object had disclosed, extraction's reach in one case, difference's durability in the other, and the quarrel hardened around the ambiguity of one word.

### Q: How does the objects and methods grid order the comparison?

It fixes the dimensions in advance so that neither side can be silently redefined between paragraphs. Across its rows it sets the object of study, the method, the treatment of capitalism, the account of the historical agent, and each approach's explanatory reach, with a final column stating what a combined treatment would require on that row. Used properly, it converts verdicts into locations: instead of declaring a winner, the reader can say on which row a claim sits, which phase of which literature it belongs to, and what evidence would grade it. That conversion, from allegiance to location, is the grid's entire purpose, and it survives the reader's eventual disagreement with this article's own placements.

### Q: What separates analysing colonialism as discourse from analysing it as production?

The choice of what about colonialism is to be explained, and consequently of what counts as evidence. Analysis as production begins from the arrangement of material life and asks how land, labour, and surplus were reorganised, treating texts chiefly as records and legitimations of that reorganisation. Analysis as discourse begins from the arrangement of knowledge and asks how the colonised were made describable and governable, treating material arrangements chiefly as the occasions on which descriptions were enforced. The separation is not between the real and the unreal, since classifications taxed, conscripted, and disarmed as surely as ledgers did. It is between two causal careers that every colonial document led at once, and the separation is maintained so that each career can be followed without being collapsed into the other.
