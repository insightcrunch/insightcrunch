The domestic labour debate was an argument among Marxist feminists, conducted mainly in journals through the nineteen seventies, over a deceptively narrow question. Did the unpaid work performed in households, overwhelmingly by women, produce value or surplus value in the sense those terms carry inside Marx's critique of political economy? The question sounds like a classification exercise, the kind of thing a glossary settles in a paragraph. It consumed roughly a decade of theoretical energy, drew in some of the sharpest writers the movement produced, filled special issues and pamphlet series, and ended with an answer that nearly every participant, including many who had argued the other side, came to accept. That answer can be stated in one sentence. Household labour does not create value in the technical sense, because value arises where labour produces commodities for exchange, yet household labour remains indispensable to the reproduction of labour power, the one commodity capitalism cannot do without. Both halves matter. Neither half cancels the other.

Why the classification mattered so much is the first thing a reader needs. Feminists entering Marxist politics at the end of the nineteen sixties confronted a theory that explained exploitation at the point of production with great power and said almost nothing systematic about the kitchen, the nursery, or the laundry basket. If housework could be shown to stand inside the value relation, then women's unpaid labour was not a private arrangement or a leftover custom but a load-bearing component of the capitalist economy, and the politics followed from the economics. If it stood outside, a different account of women's subordination was required, and the movement needed to know that too. The debate therefore carried political weight disguised as a technical dispute, which is also why its ending dissatisfied so many people. It produced a determinate answer to the question it had posed and no answer at all to the question that had motivated it: why women, rather than men, performed this work, and what could change that distribution. This article reconstructs the argument position by position, sets out the technical settlement and the political remainder as the debate's joint product, and gives the mockery the episode attracts, together with the defence against that mockery, each at full strength.

A note on method before the history begins. The value categories used throughout, value, surplus value, labour power, necessary labour, are Marx's technical instruments, and they are explained in the series treatments of the [surplus value complete guide](/2010/11/01/surplus-value-complete-guide/) and the [labour theory of value explained](/2010/07/01/labour-theory-of-value-explained/). Those articles carry the definitions. What follows uses the definitions to follow an argument, and the argument cannot be followed unless the definitions are held steady. Much of the confusion surrounding this debate, in summary accounts and in hostile caricatures alike, comes from letting the word value drift between its everyday meaning, worth or importance, and its technical meaning inside a specific theory of commodity production. Housework is plainly valuable in the first sense. Nobody in the debate denied that, and several participants built their reputations insisting on it. The dispute concerned the second sense only. Keeping the two senses apart is not pedantry. It is the difference between understanding what was settled and repeating the muddle the participants spent ten years working through.

## What the debate was actually asking

Strip the episode to its skeleton and three distinct questions appear, often tangled together inside a single article. One question was economic in the strict sense: where does unpaid household work sit in relation to capital's circuits? Does it enter the production of value, does it lower the cost of reproducing the workforce, does it subsidise employers by delivering fed, rested, raised workers whose upkeep the wage alone does not cover? A second question was categorial: granted Marx's definitions, which box does this labour belong in, the box marked productive of value, the box marked unproductive but necessary, or no box the theory supplies? A third question was political and strategic: what follows for organising, for demands, for the relation between women's liberation and socialist transformation, from whichever answer the first two questions receive? Individual contributions usually announced themselves as answering the second question while hoping to settle the third, and the friction between those levels generated much of the heat. A writer who proved a categorial point could be accused of evading the politics. A writer who moved straight to a demand could be accused of building strategy on unexamined categories.

The tangling explains a feature of the literature that puzzles newcomers, its combination of rigour and inconclusiveness at the same time. Read in sequence, the articles are models of close conceptual work: definitions are tested against passages from Capital, implications are drawn out, hidden premises are exposed in an opponent's footnote. Yet a reader who finishes the whole exchange can feel that the world outside the journals went unmentioned for long stretches. Both impressions are accurate. The participants were doing real theoretical labour on categories they believed carried political consequences, and the connection between a category and a consequence turned out to be looser than the founding contributions assumed. Tracing where the connection held and where it snapped is the most instructive way to read the episode, more instructive than either celebrating it as a golden age of feminist theory or dismissing it as a scholastic detour.

### Why did housework become a question for value theory at all?

Because the women's movement had established that unpaid household work was enormous, indispensable, and assigned to women, while Marxist economics, the theory the movement's socialist wing worked with, classified labour by its relation to value production. Locating housework inside that classification promised to convert a grievance into an analysis of capitalism itself.

That conversion was the prize. A grievance can be acknowledged and then set aside, accommodated with sympathy and minor reforms. An analysis, if it succeeds, shows a mechanism: it identifies who benefits, through what channel, and which change would interrupt the flow. The early contributors believed the value categories could supply exactly that mechanism for women's oppression. If capital drew value from the household without paying for it, the beneficiary was named. If the wage concealed a second worker's contribution, the mystification Marx had diagnosed in the wage form had a domestic chapter he had left unwritten. The ambition was not academic decoration on an existing politics. It was an attempt to give the politics foundations, to answer the sceptic who asked what housework had to do with capitalism at all. Later sections will show how the answers developed. The point to hold at the outset is that the question was forced on the participants by their own theoretical commitments. Working with Marx's categories meant either finding housework's place among them or explaining why the categories fell silent at the household door.

The silence itself had a history. Classical political economy had treated the household as a site of consumption, and Marx, for all his attention to reproduction, analysed the reproduction of labour power mostly from the side of the wage and the commodities it buys. What happened to those commodities between the shop and the worker's restored capacity to labour, the cooking, cleaning, mending, nursing, and child raising that turned purchases into a workforce, appeared in his work as background rather than as a process with its own labour. Feminist readers noticed the gap because their own lives filled it. The debate was, in that sense, an argument about whether a theory built to illuminate the factory could be extended across the threshold of the home without breaking its central concepts, or whether the extension required those concepts to change. Some participants attempted the extension and broke the concepts. Others defended the concepts and accepted that the household would be analysed as a condition of the system rather than a component of its value production. The second group prevailed, and the terms on which it prevailed define the settlement this article will state precisely.

## The scene before the opening move

To grasp why an article published in 1969 could open a debate, consider what preceded it. Socialist writing on women's position existed, and some of it was distinguished. Engels had connected the family to property relations generations earlier. Alexandra Kollontai had written about the socialisation of domestic work. Mid-century Marxist parties, however, largely treated the woman question as subordinate: women's emancipation would arrive with socialism, as a consequence of changed property relations and women's entry into social production, and detailed analysis of unpaid work in the home was rarely thought necessary. The household appeared in party literature as a unit that consumed wages, not as a workplace that produced anything. Meanwhile the postwar decades in the wealthy capitalist countries had built an ideology of domesticity around the full time housewife even as married women's employment rose, so the figure at the centre of the coming debate was simultaneously celebrated in advertising and disappearing in statistics. The women's liberation movement that emerged at the decade's end brought its own experience into this theoretical vacuum. Its members did housework, or watched their mothers do it, and they refused the assurance that the matter would resolve itself after the revolution.

The movement also brought a method, consciousness raising, that treated personal experience as evidence about structures. When women compared accounts of their days, the sheer volume of unpaid labour surfaced as a shared fact rather than a private failing, and the question of its economic character posed itself naturally to those with Marxist training. Was this work part of the economy or an adjunct to it? The journals that would host the debate were ready. Monthly Review in the United States had a tradition of opening its pages to heterodox Marxist argument. New Left Review in Britain combined theoretical ambition with movement connections. The Bulletin of the Conference of Socialist Economists provided a forum where economists and feminists argued in the same pages. Pamphlet culture did the rest, carrying arguments from journals into women's centres and study groups within weeks. A debate requires infrastructure as much as ideas, and by 1969 the infrastructure existed: journals willing to print long theoretical exchanges, a readership trained to follow them, and a movement eager for the results.

### What did the opening contribution change?

Margaret Benston, writing in Monthly Review in September 1969, relocated women's oppression from ideas and attitudes to a material assignment: women as a group bore responsibility for producing use values in the home, outside the money economy, and that structural position, rather than prejudice alone, grounded their inferior status within capitalist society.

The force of that relocation is easy to underestimate now, when materialist accounts of gender are common currency. At the time, explanations of women's subordination inside the socialist movement oscillated between ideology, false beliefs implanted by a sexist culture, and function, the claim that capitalism somehow needed women's inferiority without a specified mechanism. Benston offered a mechanism of a particular kind. She pointed to a division in the economy itself. One sector produced commodities for exchange, governed by money, recognised as real work. Another sector, the household, produced goods and services consumed directly, without passing through the market, and its output therefore failed to register as value in a society where money measures worth. Women were assigned to the second sector. Their labour was socially necessary in huge quantities, Benston insisted, while being structurally invisible and personally devalued, and the assignment, not any natural disposition, defined women as a group with a common relation to production. From that relation she drew the further suggestion that women could be understood as a class in an extended sense, and the reserve army function she assigned to women's wage labour tied the domestic analysis to the labour market. The next section takes her argument apart slowly, because the whole debate grew from its strengths and from the questions it left open.

## Benston and the household that produced use values

Benston's article, The Political Economy of Women's Liberation, was short by the standards of what followed, and its brevity contributed to its influence: it could be read in an evening and argued about for years. Its central distinction divided production into two kinds. Commodity production yields goods for exchange, and the labour involved counts socially because its products carry prices. Household production yields use values consumed within the family, meals eaten, children raised, rooms cleaned, and the labour involved escapes the money economy entirely. Because capitalist society recognises worth through money, Benston argued, the group confined to the unmonetised sector carries a stigma that no improvement in attitudes can remove while the structure persists. She described household production in its existing form as resembling a pre-capitalist mode surviving inside capitalism, a formulation that would be debated fiercely in its own right, since it raised the question whether the home constituted a separate mode of production with its own laws or a subordinated corner of one larger system.

Her proposals followed from the diagnosis. If the problem was the private, unmonetised character of the work, the remedy was to draw the work into public industry: communal kitchens, laundries, and childcare, organised socially rather than performed household by household. She was sceptical that such conversion could be achieved under capitalism, given the interests served by cheap domestic reproduction, and her scepticism kept the article inside a revolutionary frame rather than a reformist one. Yet for all its influence, the article left the debate's central question unanswered, almost unasked. Benston had established that housework produces use values and that its performers form a group defined by that production. She had not claimed that housework produces value in Marx's technical sense, and her framework, with its sharp line between commodity production and household production, implied it did not. The question she left open was whether some route could connect the household's undoubted economic necessity to the value relation after all, because on that connection depended the strongest version of the claim that capital exploited housewives directly. The next contributions tried to build that connection, in two very different styles.

Retrospect makes Benston's omissions look like invitations, and contemporaries read them that way. Peggy Morton and others responded within months, disputing the portrait of women as primarily houseworkers when so many women held jobs, and pressing the analysis toward the double burden of women who laboured in both sectors. The exchange already displayed the debate's characteristic rhythm: a strong categorial claim, a correction from social fact, a refinement, a counter-refinement. It also displayed a tension that never resolved, between the household as the theory's object and the actual variety of women's working lives, which included single women, women in employment throughout their lives, households without a male wage, and domestic arrangements in communities where kin networks spread the work widely. Critics of the whole debate would later seize on this narrowness. Defenders reply, with justice, that a theory of the housewife under capitalism addressed the dominant ideology and a real mass experience of the postwar decades, even if the figure never exhausted women's situations. Both judgements can stand together, and the reader should let them.

## The wages intervention and the pamphlet that forced the pace

The second founding contribution arrived as a pamphlet rather than a journal article, and its genre mattered. Mariarosa Dalla Costa and Selma James published The Power of Women and the Subversion of the Community in 1972, and where Benston had analysed, they agitated. Their claim cut across the caution of the use value framework. Housework, they argued, does produce surplus value, because it produces the commodity labour power itself, the one commodity whose consumption creates new value in production. The housewife is therefore not outside capitalist production but hidden inside its foundation, and her unwaged state is not a pre-capitalist survival but a capitalist strategy: capital obtains the reproduction of its workforce without paying the full cost, because the wage paid to the worker silently covers, or fails to cover, a second person's labour. From this analysis the authors drew the demand that gave a campaign its name, wages for housework, advanced not as a bookkeeping request but as a lever of refusal, a way for women to gain independence from individual men and to expose the work by making its price visible.

The pamphlet's reception split the emerging field. Admirers found in it the boldest possible assertion of housework's centrality and a politics with a demand attached, something the journal articles lacked. The wages campaign built organisations, held conferences, and carried the argument far beyond academic Marxism, a diffusion treated in the series article on the [federici wages for housework](/2016/10/15/federici-wages-for-housework/) campaign and its leading theorist. Critics found the economics loose where it most needed to be tight. If housework produced surplus value, the mechanism had to be specified: to whom was the surplus transferred, through which exchange, at what rate? The claim that labour power's production was simply like any commodity's production ignored how peculiar that commodity is, since its value, on Marx's account, is fixed by the labour embodied in the worker's means of subsistence, the purchased goods, rather than by labour performed after purchase. The pamphlet had announced a conclusion and a campaign. It had not yet supplied the technical demonstration, and the attempt to supply one fell to a Canadian contributor writing in a British journal two years later.

### What did the pamphlet claim about surplus value?

It claimed that housework produces surplus value indirectly by producing labour power, the commodity whose use creates value in capitalist production, so that the housewife's unpaid labour stands inside the circuit of capital rather than outside it, and capital gains from her work a benefit it never pays for directly.

Notice the structure of that claim, because the debate would spend years on it. It does not say the housewife sells anything. It says her product, the restored and raised worker, enters the market when the worker sells labour power, and that the value realised in that sale should therefore be credited partly to her labour. The move is ingenious and, its defenders stressed, faithful to the spirit of a theory that tracks hidden labour behind visible exchanges. Marx had shown the wage concealing the division of the working day into paid and unpaid portions. Here was a second concealment, the household's labour concealed inside the wage of another person. The analogy had rhetorical power and a certain textual warrant, since Marx acknowledged that the value of labour power includes the costs of maintaining the worker's family and replacements. What the analogy lacked, opponents would argue, was the exchange relation that makes labour count as value producing in the first place: abstract labour, socially validated through the market. The housewife's hours are never validated that way. Her ten hours and her neighbour's six, spent on identical meals, do not confront each other in any market, are not levelled into social averages, and cannot be summed into the value of anything. That objection, developed fully by the middle of the decade, is the hinge on which the settlement turns.

## Seccombe and the value said to be embodied in labour power

Wally Seccombe's article The Housewife and Her Labour under Capitalism, published in New Left Review at the start of 1974, gave the pro-value position its most rigorous form. Seccombe began where Benston had, with the split of the labour process into an industrial unit and a domestic unit, and he granted the household its distinct character: privatised, unmechanised by comparison, resistant to the productivity revolutions transforming factory work. His innovation lay in following the product. The commodities the wage buys, raw food, clothing, fuel, are not yet labour power. They must be worked on, transformed by domestic labour into meals, maintained bodies, raised children, before labour power exists in saleable form. That transformation, Seccombe argued, is production, and the labour performing it creates value that is embodied in labour power and realised when labour power is sold. Part of the wage thus represents, on his account, value created by the housewife, transferred through her husband's pay packet. Her labour is necessary labour in an extended sense, and its privatised form explains both its invisibility and the housewife's dependence, since she receives her subsistence as a share of a wage nominally paid to someone else.

The article impressed even those who rejected it, because it accepted the discipline of the categories while pushing them. Seccombe conceded that housework produces no surplus value directly and is not governed by the law of value in its own sphere, concessions that made his remaining claim, value creation without value governance, look either subtle or unstable depending on the reader. He also drew a strategic conclusion, arguing that the housewife's structural position, privatised and outside socialised production, limited her capacity to prefigure a new order, a judgement that enraged the wages campaign's supporters and drew a furious printed response from Nicole Cox and Silvia Federici, who heard in it the old hierarchy of struggles returning in theoretical dress. The controversy did the debate a service. It forced the value question and the political question into the same room and made their non-identity harder to ignore, since here was a writer granting housework value creating status while denying its performers strategic centrality, the reverse of the pamphlet's pairing of the two claims.

### Does housework produce surplus value?

On the position that prevailed in the debate, no. Value in Marx's technical sense is created by wage labour producing commodities for exchange. Household labour is necessary to reproduce labour power without itself producing value, and both claims can hold at once.

That answer, the settlement in miniature, deserves its placement here in the middle of the narrative rather than at the end, because everything after 1974 is the story of how the field arrived at it. The answer distinguishes two relations that earlier contributions had run together: contributing to the reproduction of the workforce, which housework unquestionably does, and producing value, which on the prevailing account it does not. It also refuses the inference that had motivated much of the argument, the inference from economic importance to value production. Importance is a fact about consequences. Value production, in the technical vocabulary, is a fact about social form, about labour exchanged against capital and validated in markets. A thing can be supremely important and formally external to value production, as fresh air is important to the factory without appearing in its accounts. The comparison is imperfect, since housework is labour and air is not, but it isolates the logical point the settlement rests on: the categories classify forms, not deservingness. Later sections examine how this answer was argued into dominance, what it cost, and why stating it still provokes the charge that the theory has demeaned the work it describes.

Reading down the final column gives a fair picture of the debate's net product. Nobody's opening claim survived intact, including the winning side's, since the settlement's defenders absorbed the subsidy account and the double position correction along the way. What the ledger cannot show is the labour of the exchanges themselves, the replies and rejoinders in which distinctions were hammered out: whether realisation through another person's wage could count, whether necessity to reproduction conferred any value status, whether the concept of productive labour had one sense in Marx or two. Those exchanges filled the middle years and produced the two critiques that did most to shape the outcome, the political critique of 1975 and the formal argument of 1977. Each deserves separate treatment, because they attacked the pro-value position from opposite directions, one from the standpoint of women's actual lives, the other from the internal logic of the value form.

## The critique that changed the subject

Margaret Coulson, Branka Magas, and Hilary Wainwright published their critique of Seccombe in New Left Review in the opening weeks of 1975, and its target was double. Against the economics, they pressed the contradiction already visible in his concessions: labour that creates value while standing outside the law of value, realising its product through a wage paid to another person, stretches the theory's central category past coherence, since value in Marx is not a substance labour secretes but a social relation established in exchange. Against the framing, they argued that the debate's fixation on the housewife misdescribed the population it claimed to theorise. Women's defining condition under capitalism, they insisted, is the combination of domestic labour and wage labour, the double day that was becoming the statistical norm, and a theory organised around the full time housewife mistook one historically specific arrangement, already dissolving, for the structure itself. A politics adequate to women's position would have to address both spheres and the traffic between them, the way low wages push women toward dependence on a male wage while domestic obligation restricts the jobs they can take.

Jean Gardiner's contribution in the same journal issue, and the joint paper she published with Susan Himmelweit and Maureen Mackintosh in the Bulletin that the Conference of Socialist Economists issued, worked the economic seam more finely. Gardiner asked what domestic labour does to the value of labour power rather than whether it creates value, and answered that unpaid household work holds that value down: because a worker's subsistence is partly produced at home without payment, the wage capital must advance to reproduce the workforce is lower than it would be if every element of upkeep had to be bought as a commodity or service. Housework thus functions as a subsidy to employers, not by creating value but by cheapening the commodity they purchase. The formulation proved durable because it assigned the household a definite economic effect, measurable in principle against the wage, while surrendering the contested claim about value creation. It also reframed the housewife's relation to capital: exploited in the loose sense of being used without payment, certainly oppressed, but not exploited in the technical sense that requires surplus extraction within production, a distinction the debate was learning to observe even when it stung.

### What did the critique object to in Seccombe's article?

It objected that his position was formally contradictory, granting domestic labour the power to create value while placing that labour beyond the law of value and outside exchange, and it objected politically that theorising the full time housewife ignored the growing majority of women who combined household work with employment.

The year's exchanges left the pro-value position damaged in a specific way. Its defenders could still assert the importance of housework, still describe its subsidy to capital, still insist on the transformation of wage goods into labour power as genuine production of use values. What they could no longer do comfortably was call the process value creation, because every attempt to specify the mechanism had run into the same wall: value requires the social validation of labour through exchange, the levelling of concrete labours into abstract labour, and the household has no such mechanism. Hours at one family's stove do not compete with hours at another's. No average forms. No socially necessary labour time for a meal exists in the way it exists for a coat produced for sale, where inefficient producers are disciplined by the market. Seccombe himself, in later reflections, moved toward acknowledging the force of the difficulty. The ground was prepared for a statement of the negative position with full formal rigour, and two years later it arrived from within the feminist camp itself, a fact that mattered for its acceptance.

## The formal settlement

Susan Himmelweit and Simon Mohun published Domestic Labour and Capital in the first issue of the Cambridge Journal of Economics in 1977, and the article's placement mattered: the argument moved from movement journals into a professional economics forum, stated in the idiom of the discipline. Their reasoning proceeded from the character of value itself. In Marx's theory, value is not a physical property imparted by effort. It is the social form taken by labour under generalised commodity production, where private labours are rendered commensurable through exchange and labour time becomes socially necessary labour time. Domestic labour is private in exactly the sense that blocks this process. It is performed for direct use, its products are consumed without exchange, and its duration is set by need, custom, and affection or their absence rather than by market competition. Therefore, whatever else it is, it does not produce value, and since surplus value is a portion of value, it does not produce that either. The article simultaneously refused the conclusion that housework is therefore economically irrelevant. It reproduces labour power, a real contribution to the conditions under which value production proceeds, and the subsidy argument developed by Gardiner and her co-authors could be restated inside the settlement without strain: unpaid transformation of wage goods keeps down the value of labour power and hence the wage bill.

Acceptance was not instant, but the terms of discussion shifted. Later contributions increasingly treated the negative answer as established and argued about consequences instead, which is the practical sign that a debate has settled: the burden of proof moves. A writer wishing to revive the claim that housework creates value now had to answer the form argument, not merely reassert the importance of the work, and the few who tried found the form argument waiting. The settlement also clarified neighbouring confusions. It separated the question of value from the question of exploitation in its looser political usage, letting writers say precisely that housewives are not exploited by capital in the technical sense while being subordinated, used, and benefited from in ways the theory could describe through the wage and the value of labour power. Precision of that kind disappointed those who wanted the vocabulary itself to deliver condemnation. Its defenders considered the disappointment a sign of health: a theory that classifies by form will sometimes classify a grave injustice outside its central category, and the remedy is better politics informed by the classification, not a looser classification adopted for rhetorical comfort.

### Why does the absence of exchange settle the value question?

Because value, in the theory being used, names a social relation established when labour's products are exchanged and private labours are levelled into abstract, socially necessary labour time. Household production skips that process entirely: nothing is sold, no average forms, and so no value is produced however necessary the work may be.

Hold that reasoning up against the strongest intuitive resistance and it still stands, which is why it prevailed. The resistance says: the worker who arrives fed and rested embodies the housewife's labour as surely as the coat embodies the tailor's, so denying value to one while granting it to the other is arbitrary. The settlement's reply distinguishes embodiment from form. Many things are embodied in labour power, including sunlight grown wheat and a grandmother's unpaid care a generation earlier, without thereby becoming value. What makes the tailor's labour value producing is not that its effects persist in a useful thing but that the coat is produced for exchange, so the labour counts socially through the market. Labour power is indeed a commodity, but its value is determined, on Marx's own account, by the value of the means of subsistence entering into its reproduction, the purchased basket, not by domestic labour applied to that basket afterwards. To credit the housewife's hours with value creation would require the theory to measure value by effort where no exchange performs the measurement. The debate's settlement is thus less a verdict on housework than a defence of the theory's internal consistency, purchased by accepting a boundary the theory had always implied but its feminist readers were the first to patrol with full attention.

## What the settlement settled, and the shape of its limits

The settled core can be enumerated in plain propositions, and doing so honours the participants, who wanted exactly this kind of clarity. Domestic labour is not value producing in the technical sense. It does not produce surplus value. It is nevertheless necessary to the reproduction of labour power, without which no value production occurs. Its performance without payment lowers the value of labour power below what fully commodified reproduction would cost, and in that indirect way capital benefits from its unwaged character. The two sets of claims, negative on value, positive on necessity, are compatible, and asserting both became the standard position in the literature that followed. The tradition's own summary of the outcome is carried in the [marxist feminism complete guide](/2016/09/01/marxist-feminism-complete-guide/), where the debate appears as a closed episode whose result later work presupposes.

Enumerating the limits matters just as much. The settlement says nothing about why the labour falls to women. A Martian reading only the settled propositions could imagine the work rotated by lot among adults of both sexes, and nothing in the value argument would change. The settlement says nothing about the experience of the work, its mixture of love, obligation, boredom, and coercion, because the categories classify social form, not feeling. It says nothing about how the division might be transformed, whether by socialisation of services, by wages, by men's equal participation, or by technologies that shrink the work itself. And it says nothing about households that depart from the model, the single parent, the wage earning wife whose husband does nothing, the extended kin network, cases the critics of 1975 had already catalogued. Each silence corresponds to a question the debate had hoped, in its founding moments, to answer with the same apparatus. The mature assessment of the episode starts from this double character: a small set of propositions established beyond reasonable reopening, surrounded by the large questions that had motivated the inquiry, still open. This article names that double character the technical settlement paired with its political remainder, and the name is meant as a description of the debate's product, not as praise or blame.

### How can the work be necessary and still produce nothing the theory counts?

Necessity and value production are different properties in this vocabulary: necessity describes what a process requires in order to continue, while value production describes a social form labour takes when its product is exchanged. Reproduction of the workforce is a precondition of capitalist production, and preconditions need not themselves be value producing activities.

The distinction between a precondition and a component does quiet work throughout Marxist argument, and the debate taught a generation to use it carefully. Capitalist production presupposes many things it does not produce: a legal order, a money system it did not invent, workers' bodies formed in households. To show that capital depends on something is not yet to show that capital produces it or that it participates in value. Some participants treated dependence as sufficient for inclusion in the value relation, as if the system's need annexed everything it touched. The settlement resisted that annexation. Dependence was affirmed, its mechanism specified through the wage and the value of labour power, and the boundary of value production was drawn where the theory's own definitions put it. Readers who find the boundary cold should notice what it protects analytically. If every necessary condition of production counted as value production, the concept of value would expand until it explained nothing in particular, the precision objection that critics raised against the whole debate and that the next cluster article examines in another connection. A category that covers everything a system needs describes the system as a whole and illuminates no part of it.

## Productive, unproductive, and the words that caused the trouble

No pair of terms generated more avoidable pain in this literature than productive and unproductive labour. In Marx's usage, productive labour is labour exchanged against capital and producing surplus value; the term classifies a relation, not a degree of usefulness, and Marx himself chose examples designed to offend common sense, the singer employed by an impresario counting as productive while the same singing performed for friends does not. Unproductive labour, correspondingly, can be useful, skilled, exhausting, and socially indispensable. Domestic labour, on the settled account, presents a further twist: it is not exchanged against capital, nor against revenue in the manner of a paid servant, since the housewife receives maintenance through a family relation rather than a wage for services. Several writers therefore concluded that the productive and unproductive pair does not classify it at all, that the debate's attempt to force a classification was part of the original error, and that housework belongs to a third space the theory names only indirectly, as labour reproducing labour power within relations that are not commodity relations. The broader taxonomy is treated in the article on [surplus value modern firms](/2011/01/15/surplus-value-modern-firms/), where the productive boundary is traced through contemporary employment.

The vocabulary's everyday meanings kept sabotaging the technical ones. To call a woman's working day unproductive sounded, to any normal ear, like a judgement that she had done nothing, and participants who used the term precisely spent paragraphs apologising for it in advance, while opponents sometimes exploited the ambiguity, presenting the settlement as the theory's confession that it considered housework worthless. That presentation is a misreading this article will return to among the standard errors, but its persistence taught the successor literature a stylistic lesson it largely heeded: social reproduction writing tends to avoid the productive label altogether, preferring to specify functions and relations in sentences rather than fight over an adjective. The avoidance is itself evidence of the debate's influence. A controversy can shape a field through the phrases it renders unusable as much as through the propositions it establishes.

There is a deeper stratum beneath the terminological quarrel, worth excavating once. Marx's category of productive labour serves his analysis of capital's self expansion; it answers the question where surplus value comes from. The feminist question was different in kind: where does the subordination of women come from, and what sustains it materially? Expecting the first apparatus to answer the second question unaided was, in the retrospective judgement of many participants, the founding overreach. The apparatus could determine whether housework belongs to the surplus producing core. It could not determine why a sex, rather than a rotating share of all adults, staffs the kitchen. Answers to that required other resources: histories of the family, analyses of ideology and law, accounts of male trade unionists defending a family wage, the whole terrain that later Marxist feminism cultivated once the value question stopped absorbing its energies. The debate's boundary drawing, resented at the time as a retreat, redirected a field toward the questions its tools could actually work on. Whether that redirection was worth a decade is the question the mockery and the defence dispute, and it is time to give both their full force.

## The mockery, stated as strongly as its makers state it

The case against the debate, as made by its detractors inside and outside the movement, has several layers, and softening any of them would cheat the reader. At the surface sits the sheer disproportion: by Maxine Molyneux's count in her 1979 retrospective, more than fifty articles in the British and American socialist press alone pursued the value status of housework within a few years, an outpouring on a single categorial puzzle unmatched by attention to wages, childcare provision, or the organisation of women workers. The spectacle invited ridicule, and ridicule arrived, from hostile journalists who found in it proof that academic Marxism preferred puzzles to people, and from feminists of other persuasions who watched socialist sisters debate whether cooking dinner produced value while the women's movement needed refuges, nurseries, and equal pay campaigns. The phrase scholastic, with its medieval echo of angels on pinheads, attached itself to the episode early and has never detached. On the mockery's telling, the debate's participants behaved like theologians of a small church, excommunicating one another over a distinction no worker had ever asked about.

Beneath the ridicule sits a methodological charge with real teeth. The debate proceeded almost entirely by textual and conceptual argument: passages of Capital were arrayed, definitions parsed, implications unfolded, while empirical inquiry into actual households, time use, and the changing composition of women's work lagged behind the conceptual output. A science of housework would have begun with the time budgets and the wage data; this literature began with the Grundrisse. Worse, the conceptual labour was conducted at a level of abstraction that guaranteed sterility, because the question posed, does this labour fit a category defined for another sphere, could only ever receive the answer the definitions already contained. Critics noted that the outcome was available on the first day to anyone who read Marx's definition strictly, making the intervening years an elaborate ceremony of reaching a foregone conclusion. The scholasticism charge, the mockery's core, holds that the ceremony was not an accident but a temptation built into a certain style of Marxism, which mistakes the refinement of its own vocabulary for knowledge of the world, and which a political movement pays for in the currency of neglected campaigns.

A third layer targets the debate's picture of women. The typical text theorised a housewife married to a male wage earner in an advanced capitalist country, at the very moment when married women's employment was climbing, when many households had no male wage, and when the majority of the world's women performed domestic labour inside peasant or informal economies the models ignored. Black feminists and feminists from the colonised world objected that the debate universalised a parochial figure and that the subordination it analysed was not theirs in the same form; a woman whose grandmother was enslaved, or who employed a poorer woman to clean, encountered the theory as a description of somebody else's kitchen. The retrospective assessments concede much of this. Even sympathetic historians of the episode describe its social imagination as narrow, its archive as small, its politics as deferred. The mockery, assembled at full strength, amounts to this: a decade of brilliant energy spent proving a definitional point about a stylised household, while the distribution of the work, the thing that mattered, went unexamined and unchanged. Remember that formulation. The defence must answer it whole, not in pieces.

## The defence, stated as strongly as its makers state it

Begin the defence where the mockery begins, with the definitional point, and ask what depended on it. The claim that housework produced value or surplus value was not a curiosity; it was the theoretical foundation of a live political strategy, the wages for housework campaign, which organised internationally on the strength of it, and of broader assertions that housewives constituted an exploited class in the strict sense, with strategic consequences for alliances and priorities. If the foundation was unsound, a movement deserved to know, and knowing required exactly the work performed: the testing of the claim against the theory it invoked. Movements that skip such testing build demands on errors their opponents will eventually expose in public, at worse moments. The debate, on this account, was quality control conducted before the strategy hardened, and its negative answer saved the tradition from staking its credibility on a category mistake. Nor was the answer available on the first day in the way the mockery suggests. Benston's own framing left the value question open, the pamphlet answered it affirmatively with arguments that persuaded many, and Seccombe's sophisticated version required detailed refutation. Conclusions look foregone only after someone has done the work of foreclosing the alternatives; before Himmelweit and Mohun, a well read Marxist feminist could hold the affirmative position in good conscience, and after them, could not. That change is what a settlement is.

The defence next disputes the accounting of costs. The debate did not consume the movement's energies in the way a diversion would have, because its participants were simultaneously the people writing on women's employment, abortion rights, and union organisation, and because the journals involved existed for theoretical work whose absence would not have produced a single additional refuge. The counterfactual underneath the mockery, that the same hours would have flowed into campaigns, assumes a fungibility of intellectual labour that real movements never exhibit. What the debate demonstrably produced, beyond its settlement, was a trained cadre and a conceptual infrastructure: the reproduction of labour power moved from a phrase in Marx to the centre of feminist analysis, the wage was denaturalised as a household income rather than an individual's price, and the ground was prepared for social reproduction theory, which inherited the settlement and built the analysis of capitalism's dependence on unwaged life making work that now organises conferences and curricula. A detour does not usually end by founding the successor field. This episode did, and its productiveness in that respect is the defence's strongest card: the successor theory can state its claims precisely because the debate determined which claims the categories would not support. The settlement functions as a fence, and fences, however unglamorous, are what allow building.

Finally the defence answers the charge of narrowness by conceding its fact and contesting its implication. The stylised housewife was a limitation, and the corrections began inside the debate itself, in the 1975 critique's insistence on the double day and in Molyneux's attention to the variety of households. A literature that generates its own corrective is not fairly described as sealed. As for the unchanged distribution of the work, the defence asks by what standard a theoretical debate is being graded. The settlement of the value question was never going to redistribute a single evening's washing up, and judging it by that standard repeats the founding error in reverse, expecting a categorial result to do political work. Theory's contribution is constraint and orientation: it tells a movement which of its beliefs are false and where the real mechanisms lie. The real mechanism of the work's distribution, the debate's end state implies, lies in the wage structure, the organisation of employment, law, custom, and men's refusal, a research programme rather than a value puzzle. The defence, like the mockery, can be compressed. The debate asked a narrow question because a live strategy turned on it, answered the question correctly and permanently, exposed the larger question it could not answer, and equipped the successor field to address that larger question with better tools. Stated that way, the episode looks less like scholasticism and more like a discipline working as it should. The reader now holds both cases at full strength, which is where this article's checkpoints require them to be held, in equilibrium and without a thumb on either scale.

## The French detour: a domestic mode of production

One major contribution stood partly outside the value argument and complicated every attempt to summarise the debate as a single question with a single answer. Christine Delphy, writing in France from the start of the decade and reaching English readers through the pamphlet The Main Enemy in the middle of it, proposed that married women's labour is extracted within a distinct domestic mode of production, in which the husband, as head of household, appropriates his wife's labour power directly through the marriage contract rather than through any market. On this account the housewife is exploited, but the exploiter is her husband and the relation is patriarchal before it is capitalist; capitalism and the domestic mode coexist, allied but analytically separate. The proposal shifted the debate's centre of gravity for many readers, because it answered the question the value argument could not: who benefits from the work, immediately and personally. It also supplied a theory of the assignment, since the marriage relation itself, not an employer's hiring decision, delivers the labour to its beneficiary.

Delphy's thesis drew the retrospective's sharpest fire. Maxine Molyneux devoted the first section of her 1979 assessment to the domestic mode thesis and found it wanting on grounds that have structured discussion since: the supposed mode lacks the defining features the concept requires, its own dynamic and laws of motion, and the extraction it describes varies so much by class and circumstance that a unitary patriarchal mode explains less than it promises. Defenders responded that the thesis named a reality the value framework rendered invisible, the direct appropriation of one person's labour by another inside marriage, and that demanding a full mode of production from the insight set the bar where hostile examiners set it. The episode matters for the ledger because it shows the debate overflowing its stated question almost from the beginning. Alongside the categorial argument ran a second argument about the unit of analysis: one system or two, capital alone or capital and patriarchy as articulated structures. That second argument would flower into the dual systems debates of the following decade, and readers who meet it there should know its roots in the domestic labour years, when the inadequacy of the value answer pushed serious thinkers to look for a second mechanism rather than stretch the first one further.

### What separates Delphy's answer from the value answers?

Delphy changed the question: instead of asking whether household labour creates value within capitalism, she asked who appropriates that labour and through what relation, answering that a husband appropriates his wife's labour through marriage, inside a domestic arrangement she treated as a mode of production in its own right.

The contrast clarifies why the debate could feel, to participants, like several arguments wearing one title. A writer could lose on value and win on appropriation; the pamphlet's claim about surplus value could fail while its portrait of the husband as beneficiary survived through Delphy's different route. Summaries that report a single winner therefore mislead. The technical settlement governed one track, the categorial track, and on the other tracks the debate ended not with a settlement but with a diaspora, its questions scattering into dual systems theory, into the sociology of housework, into campaigns and into what became social reproduction theory. Keeping the tracks distinct is essential to a fair verdict, and it also explains the debate's odd afterlife, in which it is cited simultaneously as a closed episode and as the source of half the research programmes that followed it. A closed episode does not usually have descendants that numerous. A diaspora does.

## How labour power gets its value

Because the settlement routes housework's economic significance through the value of labour power, that concept deserves a section of its own, stated slowly. Labour power, the capacity to work, is the commodity the worker sells. Like other commodities it has a value, and Marx determines that value by the labour embodied in the means of subsistence required to maintain the worker and to raise the next generation of workers, at a standard set historically by custom, climate, and the outcomes of class struggle. Food, housing, clothing, fuel, training: the basket is social, not biological, and it expands or contracts as expectations change. The wage, in value terms, gravitates around the price of that basket. Now insert the household. The basket arrives as commodities, but commodities do not feed anyone unprocessed. Raw ingredients become meals, rooms become habitable, children become adults, through labour that the wage does not separately purchase. Two consequences follow, and the debate's mature position asserts both. First, the same standard of life can be maintained with a smaller purchased basket than a fully commodified life would require, because household labour stretches each commodity further; the value of labour power is thereby lower than it would be if reproduction were bought entire. Second, variations in household labour do not alter that value in the manner of variations in factory labour, because the value is fixed by the basket's embodied labour, not by the hours worked upon the basket after purchase. A worker whose partner cooks elaborately does not thereby sell dearer labour power. The mechanism is asymmetrical, and the asymmetry is the settlement's economic content.

That asymmetry frustrated participants who wanted the housewife's contribution priced by the theory, and their frustration is intelligible. The theory registers her effect in aggregate, through the level of wages across an economy, and refuses her an individual account. She appears in the value of labour power the way rainfall appears in the price of wheat, as a condition affecting a magnitude without holding a share in it. Critics called this a second invisibility imposed by the theory after the first invisibility imposed by the market. Defenders answered that theories register what their categories are built to register, and that the demand for an individual account was really a demand for a different theory, one organised around contribution rather than exchange. The argument matters beyond this episode because it recurs wherever Marxist categories meet unwaged work, in discussions of subsistence farming, of voluntary labour, of the care performed inside every economy's interstices. The debate's handling of it, concession of the effect combined with refusal of the account, became the template later discussions inherit, whether they acknowledge the inheritance or not.

### Why does an elaborate dinner not raise the value of a worker's labour power?

Because that magnitude is fixed by the labour embodied in the purchased means of subsistence, not by labour applied to them inside the home. Additional household effort raises the use value obtained from a given basket, improving life on the same wage, without changing what the basket cost in value terms.

Consider the same point from the employer's side, where its political edge shows. Capital purchasing labour power buys a capacity whose reproduction costs have been partly borne off its own books, in kitchens it does not fund and by hands it does not employ. The saving is real and general: an economy where reproduction is fully commodified, where workers must buy prepared food, commercial laundry, and paid childcare out of wages, is an economy where the wage consistent with a given standard of life sits higher, and the share of the working day accruing to employers sits correspondingly lower, other things equal. Household labour therefore transfers resources toward capital in aggregate even though no individual housewife produces surplus value for an individual employer. The debate's vocabulary for this was subsidy, and the word has survived every change of fashion because it captures the direction of the flow without overstating its form. Later writers would build entire frameworks on that flow, describing reproduction as a hidden abode counterpart to Marx's hidden abode of production, and while the metaphor postdates the settlement, the settlement is what licenses it, since the flow's existence no longer depends on the contested claim that value is created in the home.

## The wage form and the second concealment

Running beneath the categorial exchanges was an argument about appearances, inherited from Marx's analysis of the wage itself. The wage form, Marx argued, makes the working day look fully paid, concealing the division between the hours replacing the wage and the hours yielding surplus. Several contributors proposed that the household exhibits an analogous concealment at one remove. The wage paid to an employed husband presents itself as the price of his labour alone, an individual income for individual effort, while in fact it must stretch across the reproduction of a household, including the subsistence of the person whose unpaid labour makes the whole arrangement function. She eats from a wage she did not earn in the market's eyes; he appears as breadwinner because the form assigns the income to the seller of labour power. The couple's internal dependence, her reliance on his pay packet, his reliance on her work, is thus dressed as a natural order, provider and provided for, rather than recognised as an interdependence structured by capital's preference for reproducing its workforce at the lowest monetary cost.

Seccombe built much of his article's rhetorical force on this appearance argument, and it survived his article's technical defeat, which is a pattern worth noticing: in this debate, analyses of form and appearance often outlasted the categorial claims they were attached to. The concealment argument does not require housework to create value. It requires only that the wage's individual form misdescribe the collective process of reproduction, and that misdescription does ideological work, naturalising the housewife's dependence and the husband's authority as facts of personality or providence. Feminist critics of the family wage demand, the trade union call for a wage sufficient to keep a wife at home, drew directly on this strand, showing how a demand framed as protection for workers' families entrenched the very dependence the analysis exposed, and how sections of the organised working class had historically preferred that entrenchment to women's independent earnings. Here the debate touched interests inside its own movement, and the discomfort it caused is part of why the argument's political remainder could not stay politely open: the mechanisms assigning the work included decisions by labour movements themselves, not only by employers and states.

## The strongest objection: Molyneux's retrospective, stated and answered

Every debate deserves to be judged partly by the best assessment written from inside it, and this debate has one. Maxine Molyneux published Beyond the Domestic Labour Debate in New Left Review in the summer of 1979, and the article is the retrospective assessment this article treats as the strongest objection to the whole enterprise. Molyneux wrote as a participant sympathetic to the project, which is what gives the assessment its authority: nobody could dismiss her as a hostile journalist amusing readers with the spectacle of feminist scholasticism. Her objection, stated at the length it deserves, runs as follows. The debate set out to explain women's subordination on a material basis and to ground a politics in that explanation. Nearly a decade and more than fifty articles later, it had produced refined positions on whether domestic labour creates value, a question internal to Marxist theory, while the phenomena the inquiry was meant to illuminate, why women are assigned the work, how the assignment is enforced, how it connects to women's position in employment and to male power inside and outside the family, remained unexamined by the value argument and largely unadvanced by the debate as a whole. The theoretical work, she argued, had not adequately addressed the problems it had identified at the start. Effort had flowed into the channel the theory made available rather than the channel the problem required, because determining a category is a tractable, publishable task, while explaining a social assignment demands history, empirical research, and confrontation with interests, including men's interests, that the categorial debate allowed its participants to postpone. The debate thus stands, in her assessment, as a demonstration of the cost of forcing a political question into an economic apparatus not designed for it: the apparatus processes what it can, refines itself, and leaves the question standing outside the door, exactly where it was.

That objection must be answered, not absorbed, and the honest answer has two parts, one a defence and one a concession, in proportions this article will not fudge. The defence first. The technical answer the debate produced constrains what successor theories may claim, and constraint is a genuine intellectual gain, not a consolation prize. After the settlement, no theory of women's oppression can responsibly rest on the assertion that housewives produce surplus value for capital; strategies premised on that assertion lost their warrant, and the energy that would have defended them was freed for better founded work. The successor field, social reproduction theory in its mature form, states its central claim, that capitalism depends constitutively on unwaged life making labour performed largely by women, in a register that does not require value creation in the home, and it can state the claim cleanly because the debate cleared the ground of the stronger, falser version. Molyneux's own article participates in that clearing even as it criticises the process, which suggests the retrospective is better read as the debate's self correction than as its obituary. A literature that produces its own most damaging assessment, in its own leading journal, is exhibiting a virtue rarer than efficiency.

Then the concession, which the brief for this article requires to be made without varnish: the criticism is substantially correct about the debate's political yield. Measured by the standard the participants themselves announced, an account of women's subordination adequate to ground a politics, the decade's output fell short, and it fell short in the direction Molyneux identified, toward the refinement of categories and away from the assignment mechanism. The literature's own trajectory confesses as much, since its most vital continuations abandoned the value question for the questions she listed: the family wage and its history, the sexual division of labour in employment, the state's role in structuring dependence, the ideology of domesticity. A reader deciding what the episode teaches should hold both parts together. The settlement is real and the constraint it imposes is valuable. The yield on the motivating question was meagre and the reasons were structural, not a failure of individual talent. Techniques have opportunity costs even when they succeed, and a movement choosing its theoretical instruments is choosing, among other things, which questions will wait. In this case the question that waited was the political one, and Molyneux's assessment endures because she said so first, from inside, with the evidence assembled.

### What exactly did the retrospective assessment charge the debate with?

Writing in New Left Review as the decade closed, Maxine Molyneux charged that the theoretical work had not adequately addressed the problems it set out to solve: years of refinement on whether housework creates value had left unexplained why women are assigned the work and how their subordination is reproduced.

Her prescription followed her diagnosis, and it is worth recording because later work obeyed it so thoroughly that its origin is forgotten. She directed attention toward the concrete mechanisms of women's subordination in specific social formations, toward the restructuring of relations within the home as a political task in its own right rather than a byproduct of other transformations, and toward measures equalising the domestic load and socialising the care of children. Read the agenda of the following decades of Marxist feminist scholarship against that prescription and the correspondence is striking: the field did roughly what its sharpest internal critic told it to do. That is not the profile of a scholastic cul de sac. It is the profile of a debate that ended by criticising itself accurately enough to redirect its successors, a rarer achievement than settling a category, and one the mockery never mentions because the mockery needs the episode to be pure waste. The defence can rest there, having conceded the political shortfall in full: what the shortfall purchased was not only a technical answer but a map of the theory's limits, drawn by the people best placed to draw it.

## The political remainder: what assigns the work

Since the technical answer does not settle who performs the labour or why, the honest article must say what kinds of explanation the remainder requires, even though providing them in full belongs to other articles in this cluster. The assignment of domestic work to women is overdetermined, to borrow a term this series has examined elsewhere, by mechanisms operating in several registers at once. In the register of the labour market, wage differentials do quiet coercive work: where a man's earnings systematically exceed a woman's, a household maximising income under time pressure will rationally specialise her time toward the home, and each such household decision reproduces the differential that prompted it, through interrupted careers and foregone experience. In the register of the state, tax structures, benefit rules, the scheduling of schools, and the absence or presence of public childcare define the practical options within which households choose, so that policy made for other purposes silently allocates the work. In the register of custom and ideology, the equation of good motherhood with personal performance of care raises the moral cost of delegation for women while leaving men's reputations untouched by identical delegation. In the register of direct power, some men refuse the work and some households enforce the refusal, and any account too genteel to mention refusal has abandoned explanation for decorum.

None of these mechanisms is a value theoretic mechanism, which is precisely the remainder's lesson. They are historical, institutional, and ideological, and they yield to historical, institutional, and ideological analysis, together with the political organisation that acts on such analysis. The debate's service, at its end, was to make that division of labour unmistakable: to show that a question about capitalism and a question about patriarchy, though entangled in every kitchen, are not one question, and that the tools answering the first leave the second standing in need of its own instruments. The cluster to which this article belongs takes up those instruments in turn, in the treatments of socialist feminism's development, of the family wage, and of the care economy. The successor theory that stays closest to home ground, [social reproduction theory explained](/2016/09/15/social-reproduction-theory-explained/), begins from the settlement and asks the systemic question the debate left: not whether reproduction creates value, but how a system organised around value depends on what it does not pay for. The contemporary form of the practical question, who performs care and on what terms, is pursued in the article on [care work and capitalism](/2016/12/01/care-work-and-capitalism/), whose terrain the present debate cleared without entering.

### If the theory cannot say why women do the work, what can?

History and institutional analysis can: the formation of the family wage, the legal and fiscal construction of dependence, the organisation of working time, the ideology of motherhood, and the distribution of bargaining power inside households together explain an assignment that no value category was ever going to explain by itself.

Notice, too, what the remainder is not. It is not a mystery, and treating it as one would flatter the debate's detractors. The mechanisms listed above are researchable and partly researched; the assignment of housework has histories that have been written, statistics that have been gathered, policies whose effects have been compared across countries. The remainder is simply the portion of the original problem that the chosen instrument could not process. Every instrument has such a remainder. The episode's distinction is that its participants, in their retrospective mood, identified theirs with unusual candour, and that candour, more than the settlement itself, is the example worth teaching. A field that can enumerate what its favourite method cannot do has acquired protection against its own scholastic temptation, the temptation Molyneux diagnosed. Later Marxist feminism's characteristic refusal to let a single mechanism explain women's subordination, its plural causal accounts, descends from that moment of candour as surely as its settled value position descends from Himmelweit and Mohun.

## Inside the household labour process

Analyses pitched at the level of categories can leave the work itself abstract, a substance called domestic labour with no texture. The debate's better contributions resisted that abstraction by examining the labour process in the home, and their findings complicate any simple picture. Household work is a bundle of unlike tasks sharing only their location and their performer: cooking governed by hunger and habit, cleaning governed by standards that expand to fill available effort, laundry transformed by machinery yet stubborn in its sorting and folding, and childcare, which resembles no factory process at all, since its object is a person whose needs arrive unscheduled and whose development is the product. Seccombe emphasised the sector's technological stagnation relative to industry: capital revolutionises the production of things it sells, while the production it does not sell attracts no comparable drive to economise labour, so the housewife of the nineteen seventies worked with appliances her grandmother would have recognised beside a factory transformed beyond recognition. The point connected the debate to a material mechanism. Because no employer profits from cheapening housework's labour time, no competitive pressure disciplines its duration, and the socially necessary labour time that governs commodity production has no domestic analogue, one more reason the value category refuses to apply.

Later scholarship complicated the stagnation thesis in a direction the debaters would have welcomed. Ruth Schwartz Cowan's study of household technology, published a few years after the settlement, argued that labour saving devices in the home had historically raised standards rather than reduced hours: the washing machine arrives, and garments are washed more often; the vacuum cleaner arrives, and spotlessness becomes the expectation. Her research, conducted outside the Marxist journals, converged with the debate's structural insight from the side of social history. The work expands because its product is consumed by people whose standards are socially set, and because its privatised organisation prevents the pooling that turns individual tasks into efficient services. The convergence matters methodologically. The debate's participants, accused of ignoring empirical inquiry, had in fact generated propositions, about the absence of productivity pressure and the privacy of the labour process, that empirical researchers working independently found themselves confirming. A scholastic exercise does not usually make predictions a neighbouring discipline verifies. This one did, quietly, and its defenders are entitled to cite the fact when the scholastic charge is pressed in its sweeping form.

Ann Oakley's sociological work on housework, appearing in the middle of the decade from outside the Marxist frame, supplied another empirical counterpoint the debaters read with interest. Interviewing London housewives, Oakley documented long hours, fragmentation, monotony, and a striking uniformity of dissatisfaction, and she treated housework as work in the sociological sense years before that treatment was conventional in her discipline. Her findings armed the debate's participants with something their own method rarely produced: testimony. When a theorist claimed the work was experienced as both obligatory and endless, Oakley's transcripts stood behind the claim. The coexistence of the two literatures, one categorial and one empirical, addressing the same kitchen from different instruments, is the fairest answer to the charge that the decade produced only definitions. It produced definitions in the journals where definitions were the assigned task, while allied work in other genres assembled the evidence, and the retrospective habit of judging the journals alone mistakes a division of intellectual labour for an absence of inquiry.

## Paid hands in the home: the boundary case that tests the settlement

No test probes a classification like its boundary cases, and this debate has a superb one: the paid domestic worker. Consider three women performing identical tasks in identical kitchens. The first is the housewife, unwaged, maintaining her own household. The second is a cleaner employed by a commercial agency that sells her services to households at a price covering her wage and the agency's profit. The third is a servant employed directly by a wealthy family, paid from the family's income. The settled theory classifies the three differently, and the differences instruct. The agency cleaner's labour is productive in the technical sense, exchanged against capital and yielding surplus value to her employer, although the service she sells is consumed unproductively by the purchasing household. The directly employed servant's labour is unproductive in the technical sense, exchanged against revenue rather than capital, however hard she works and however much her employers benefit. The housewife's labour falls outside the productive pair altogether, being neither exchanged against capital nor hired for a wage. Same mop, same stairs, three classifications. A reader who finds this absurd has located the exact point where the theory's form based logic either persuades or fails. The classifications track relations, not efforts, and the debate's settlement depends on accepting that tracking as the theory's method rather than its defect.

The boundary case did political work as well, because paid domestic service was never a marginal curiosity. In many societies it was, and remains, a major employer of working class women, frequently migrant women, whose labour frees other women for employment while reproducing within the workforce the hierarchies of class and race the housewife model flattened. The debate's stylised household, in which a wife performs all the labour, described the professional strata of the wealthy countries better than the whole of any country, and the critics who said so, including feminists organising domestic workers, forced a widening of the frame that later literature absorbed. The settlement survives the widening intact, since its claims concern forms rather than populations, but the episode teaches a standing lesson about theorisng from a stylised case: the case selects the questions. A debate that began from the agency cleaner's kitchen would have asked about wages, migration, and labour law first and value categories later, and would have produced a different, not obviously lesser, body of work. Recognising that contingency is part of reading the debate historically rather than devotionally.

There is also the generational boundary, the raising of children, which several participants singled out as the household task least assimilable to production metaphors. A child is not a product in any sense the theory can use, and the labour of care directed at a child's flourishing has its ends inside the relationship rather than in an output separable from it. Contributions that treated reproduction narrowly, as the manufacture of labour power, drew objections from writers who insisted that reducing child raising to the production of a future commodity reproduced, inside feminist theory, the very instrumental view of persons the movement existed to oppose. The objection did not overturn the settlement, which concerns value rather than meaning, but it marked a limit the successor literature respected by speaking of life making and social reproduction in vocabularies deliberately wider than production. When later theorists distinguish the reproduction of labour power from the broader reproduction of life and community, they are honouring a distinction this debate's internal critics forced into the open.

## Two senses of productive labour, and the confusion they seeded

Historians of the debate often locate its deepest knot in an ambiguity inside Marx's own texts. Across his writings, productive labour carries two distinguishable senses. In one, drawn from the analysis of the labour process in general, productive labour is labour that produces a useful result, labour as the metabolism between human beings and nature, common to every social form. In the other, specific to the capitalist mode, productive labour is labour that produces surplus value for capital, a definition that excludes much useful activity and includes some that common sense finds frivolous. Marx was aware of the duality and exploited it, sometimes within a page. His inheritors in the domestic labour argument inherited the ambiguity along with the texts, and several exchanges reduce, on inspection, to one side wielding the general sense against the other's specific sense, each able to quote chapter and verse. The housewife is productive in the first sense trivially, since she produces meals and raised children. The debate only exists in the second sense, where the answer is negative. Contributions that failed to declare their sense could argue past each other for thousands of words, and a few famous polemics did exactly that.

The settlement can be restated as the moment the field agreed, tacitly and then explicitly, to conduct the argument in the second sense and to stop claiming victories in the first. Himmelweit and Mohun's article is the clearest site of the agreement because its reasoning makes no sense except on the specific definition, and its wide acceptance ratified the choice. The episode offers a transferable warning about inherited vocabularies. A movement that takes its terms from a founder's corpus takes the corpus's tensions as well, and a portion of its theoretical labour will always be spent, as this debate spent so much of its portion, adjudicating which of the founder's usages governs a question the founder never posed. Whether that expenditure is a necessary cost of working within a tradition or a tax the tradition levies on original thought is a question the mockery and the defence answer differently, and the reader has now seen both answers in their strong forms. What cannot be claimed is that the expenditure was hidden. The participants knew they were parsing texts. Their dispute was over whether the parsing governed anything real, and on that dispute the political stakes, a campaign's foundation, were genuinely engaged.

## The Italian root and the operaist inflection

The pamphlet of 1972 did not emerge from a vacuum, and its intellectual parentage explains its distinctive tone, at once maximalist and strategic. Dalla Costa wrote from within Italian operaismo, the workerist current that read Capital through the lens of working class refusal and treated every terrain where capital commanded unpaid or underpaid effort as a front of struggle. In that milieu, extending the analysis to the housewife was not an academic extension but a recruitment: the home was a hidden factory, its worker a producer whose refusal, a strike of housewives, could interrupt reproduction itself. Selma James, working between Italy, Britain, and the United States, carried the current's instincts into the women's movement and gave the resulting campaign its internationalist structure, linking housewives to other unwaged workers, colonised peoples, and the unemployed in a single category of those whose work capital takes without the wage. The framing explains both the pamphlet's reach and its vulnerability. Read as strategy, the claim that housework produces surplus value functioned as a refusal to accept the work's invisibility, a demand for recognition in the only currency the theory honoured. Read as economics, it required a mechanism it never supplied. The debate's tragedy, if the word is not too large, is that its participants were forced to choose the second reading to test the first's foundation, and the testing, however necessary, could look and feel like a refusal of recognition to the women doing the recognising work in the streets.

That tension, between a claim's strategic life and its technical warrant, runs through the whole episode and accounts for much of its bitterness. The wages campaign heard the settlement as the movement's own theorists withdrawing a weapon mid battle. The settlement's defenders replied that a weapon built on an error is a liability the enemy will eventually seize, and that recognition could be demanded on other grounds: on the subsidy's reality, on the work's necessity, on justice. Both hearings were sincere. Later feminist theory learned to mark the distinction explicitly, separating the question of what is true in the categories from the question of what a demand does in the world, and the wages demand itself survived its foundation's removal by migrating to new justifications, as the series article on the campaign traces in detail. Ideas in movements rarely die when their proofs do; they find other proofs or live as orientations. The debate's historians do it a disservice when they write as if the settlement ended the politics it disappointed. It ended one argument for the politics. The politics continued, chastened and in places strengthened, on grounds the settlement itself had clarified.

## How a theoretical debate actually ends

Sociologists of knowledge distinguish several ways controversies close: by proof that compels assent, by exhaustion, by the migration of the participants to new problems, by generational turnover. The domestic labour debate exhibits all four, and disentangling them refines the notion of its settlement. The formal argument of 1977 did compel wide assent on the value question among those working inside the categories; that is closure by something close to proof, relative to shared premises. Exhaustion operated alongside it: by the decade's end the exchanges had begun to repeat, special issues found fewer new things to print, and editors' appetite waned. Migration carried the talent elsewhere, into the family wage's history, into state policy, into what became social reproduction theory, where the settled result could be used rather than reargued. Turnover completed the process, as a new cohort encountered the debate as a literature to cite rather than a fight to join, citing it precisely in the settled form this article has described. To say the debate settled is therefore to say several true things at once, and the ledger format honours the strongest of them, the proof shaped closure, without denying the others.

Closure by shared premises deserves one more look, because it defines the settlement's jurisdiction. The proof binds those who accept Marx's value form analysis; a reader who rejects that analysis wholesale will find the settlement as uninteresting as a ruling inside a game they do not play, and neoclassical economists of the household, building their own models of domestic production in the same years, proceeded in serene indifference to the entire controversy. The debate's world was the Marxist and socialist feminist world, and its settlement is a fact about that world's knowledge. Within the world, the fact is solid. Outside it, the episode still instructs, as a case of a community testing whether its prized concepts could bear a new weight, discovering they could not, and saying so in print. Communities that can perform that operation on themselves are rarer than communities that can win arguments, and the performance is the deepest answer to the scholasticism charge: scholasticism, properly defined, is the refinement of distinctions to protect a system from falsification, while this debate refined its distinctions in a direction that falsified one of its own movement's founding claims. The distinction between those two activities is the difference between a church and a discipline.

## The socialisation alternative and its longer history

One practical horizon haunted the debate from its first page, rarely argued at length because the value question crowded it out: the socialisation of domestic work. Benston had ended with it, communal kitchens, laundries, and childcare replacing private housewifery, and behind her stood a longer socialist tradition reaching back through Kollontai to the communal house experiments of the early Soviet years and to nineteenth century utopian designs for collective living. The idea carried an attractive logic. If the work's privatisation was the problem, its misery compounded by isolation and duplication, then collectivising the work attacked the misery directly, whatever the categories said. Yet the debate's decade saw the socialisation proposal discussed mostly in asides, for reasons that themselves instruct. Socialist feminists had watched the actually existing socialist states proclaim women's emancipation through employment while leaving the domestic load largely where it was, producing the double burden as an official outcome, and the spectacle cooled enthusiasm for state provided solutions administered without women's power over them. Meanwhile the women's movement's own communal experiments, shared houses and collective childcare, taught hard lessons about the labour of organising collectivism itself, and about how readily the conscientious end up doing for the collective what they once did for a family.

The wages demand and the socialisation demand thus stood as rival practical poles between which the value argument was expected, naively, to adjudicate. It could not, and recognising that it could not is part of the settlement's wisdom as this article presents it. Whether care should be waged in the home, socialised into public services, redistributed among household members, or some layered combination, depends on judgements about freedom, efficiency, intimacy, and the kind of childhood a society wants, judgements economic categories inform but cannot make. The debate clarified the information: it established that capital benefits from the unwaged arrangement through the wage level, which tells reformers where resistance to change will concentrate and what a transition costs whom. A reform that socialises childcare raises the social wage and is financed from the same surplus the subsidy was quietly augmenting; the accounts connect even though the categories do not dictate the choice. Theory that delivers the accounts and declines the choice is not evading politics. It is observing the boundary the debate spent ten years locating, the boundary between what an economic analysis can settle and what only politics, in the full sense, can.

## Exploitation, oppression, and the quarrel over words with charges in them

A semantic fault line ran under the whole exchange and periodically split it open: the relation between exploitation and oppression. In the technical vocabulary, exploitation names the extraction of surplus value within production, a specific mechanism with a location. Oppression names the broader condition of being subordinated, disadvantaged, and ruled, by mechanisms of many kinds. Are housewives exploited, oppressed, both, or neither in the strict senses? The question sounds like a labelling exercise until its consequences appear. If housewives are exploited by capital in the strict sense, then their interest aligns structurally with workers' and the alliance of movements rests on shared mechanism. If they are oppressed through a different mechanism, the alliance must be argued politically rather than read off the structure. Delphy's answer, exploitation by husbands within a domestic mode, and the pamphlet's answer, exploitation by capital through labour power's production, both tried to secure the structural alignment by finding an extraction. The settlement denied the second and the retrospective damaged the first, leaving the tradition to build its alliances as political constructions rather than structural deductions, a discipline later socialist feminism accepted as maturity. The vocabulary lesson generalises. Movements reach for the strongest word their theory licenses, and theories are corrupted when the reach succeeds by stretching. The debate at its best refused the stretch, accepting the weaker classification and the harder political work it imposed, and its refusal is among the behaviours that distinguish it from the scholasticism it is accused of.

The distinction also governs how the housewife's undoubted grievances should be described, and description is not a small matter. Her work is taken without a wage; her dependence is enforced by the wage structure; her labour's product is appropriated by husband, children, and, through the wage level, by employers in aggregate. Every clause of that sentence can be defended after the settlement, and together they describe a serious injustice with named beneficiaries. What the settlement removes is only the compression of those clauses into the single claim that she produces surplus value. Writers who experience the removal as a weakening mistake rhetorical compression for analytical strength. The uncompressed sentence is harder to chant and impossible to refute, and a movement armed with irrefutable sentences is better equipped than one armed with slogans its opponents can falsify in a seminar room. This is the defence's argument again, encountered now at the level of vocabulary, and it gains force from the concession paired with it: the uncompressed sentence, for all its truth, still does not say why she, and not he, stands in the kitchen, which remains the remainder no vocabulary settles.

## The debate in the classroom and the archive

Because the episode is closed, it can be taught whole, and its teachability has become one of its afterlives. A seminar can assign Benston, the pamphlet, Seccombe, the 1975 critique, Himmelweit and Mohun, and Molyneux in sequence and watch a field argue itself to a conclusion within a decade, a pedagogical luxury most controversies deny. The sequence demonstrates, better than any methodological lecture, how a literature corrects itself: a founding simplification, a maximalist overreach, a rigorous overcorrection, a political objection, a formal resolution, a retrospective that reopens the purpose while accepting the result. Students who work through it acquire the value categories in their applied form, since each abstract term is fought over in a concrete setting, and they acquire something rarer, a sense of what theoretical agreement looks like when it is earned rather than announced. The anthologies that gathered the debate's texts, Ellen Malos's collection The Politics of Housework prominent among them at the decade's turn, fixed a canon that libraries still hold, and the journals named in the ledger, Monthly Review, New Left Review, the CSE's Bulletin, the Cambridge Journal of Economics, keep the originals in their archives for the researcher who wants the texture the summaries flatten: the footnotes where concessions hide, the postscripts where authors adjust, the review essays where the field takes its own temperature.

For the researcher, the archive holds one more lesson in its margins. Reading the exchanges in order reveals how much of the debate's movement happened through distinctions introduced almost casually, in a paragraph of Gardiner here, a footnote of Himmelweit there, and only later recognised as the hinges. The history of a controversy is often written as a duel of set pieces, but the domestic labour texts reward a finer attention: the settlement was assembled from parts manufactured in different workshops, the subsidy mechanism, the double day correction, the form argument, the retrospective's redirection, no one of which its maker presented as the conclusion of the whole. Synthesis was the community's work, performed across years by readers who combined the parts. That is why the ledger in this article lists positions rather than heroes, and why its final column credits survivals to arguments instead of persons. The habit of collective assembly it documents is the same habit the successor field institutionalised, where major statements are as likely to emerge from conferences and edited volumes as from single authored interventions.

## Wartime, welfare states, and the history pressing at the debate's edges

The debaters wrote in a specific historical weather, and restoring that weather explains emphases that puzzle readers who meet the texts cold. Within living memory of the founding contributions, the advanced capitalist states had conscripted women into factories during a world war and expelled many of them afterward in the name of domestic restoration, a cycle that demonstrated how elastic the assignment of housework could be when employers and governments needed it to stretch. The postwar settlement in Britain and elsewhere then institutionalised the male breadwinner model in benefit rules and tax codes, at the very moment rising married women's employment was hollowing the model out in practice. The debate's housewife was thus a figure simultaneously embedded in law and eroding in life, which accounts for the literature's double character, its confidence that the figure named a structure and its recurring unease, voiced by the 1975 critics and others, that the structure was mid transformation. Theorising a moving object is not a mistake; every social theory does it. But the movement of this object, toward the double day as the typical female condition, steadily shifted the debate's centre from the full time housewife toward the analysis of women's combined labours, a shift the settlement's abstract propositions survive because they concern forms any combination exhibits.

The international variation pressed similarly. In social democratic Scandinavia, public childcare and individual taxation were already redrawing the household's boundaries while the debate raged in English, demonstrating that the assignment of the work was a policy variable and not a fate; the debaters knew this, cited it, and drew the correct moral that institutions mediate what value categories leave open. In the socialist states, the official position that women's emancipation followed from employment, combined with unexamined domestic arrangements, supplied the negative example Molyneux invoked when she insisted that entering employment alone had proven inadequate wherever the home's internal relations went unreformed. In the colonised and postcolonial world, the household forms under discussion barely applied, a limitation the debate registered late and partially. Each comparison performs the same service for the reader: it relocates the explanatory weight from the category to the institution, which is where the political remainder lives. A theory of housework that travels must therefore be a theory of households under specific states, labour markets, and customs, with the value settlement as its fixed point rather than its engine. That is, in outline, the research programme the successor literature executed, and the debate can be credited with specifying the programme by exhausting the alternative.

## Men's interests and the family wage: the mechanism with names on it

If the remainder needs mechanisms, one of the best documented has names, dates, and union conference resolutions attached to it: the family wage. Through the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, significant sections of organised male labour demanded a wage for the male worker sufficient to maintain a dependent wife and children, and employers and states variously conceded, resisted, or exploited the demand according to circumstance. Heidi Hartmann's analysis, written as the debate closed and drawing its terms, argued that this history shows male workers acting to preserve a material advantage, the services of a wife and a labour market sheltered from cheaper female competition, and that any account of the assignment of housework ignoring that advantage mistakes an defended arrangement for a natural one. The argument matters here because it exemplifies the kind of explanation the remainder requires and the value debate could not supply: archival, interested, unembarrassed about conflict between groups the movement hoped to unite. It also complicates every simple story in which capital alone engineers the sexual division of labour for its own convenience. Capital benefited from the subsidy, certainly. But the arrangement had multiple authors and multiple beneficiaries, and the politics of changing it accordingly faces opposition distributed through the society, including through the households and organisations of the working class itself.

Jane Humphries, writing in the same years and in one of the settlement's own journals, offered a counterweight from within Marxist feminism, reading the working class family's persistence as partly a defensive achievement: the family, on her account, protected workers' reproduction from the full ravages of the market, preserving use value production in the home against a capitalism that would otherwise have consumed the labour of every family member, children included, at subsistence wages. The juxtaposition of Hartmann and Humphries instructs because both cannot be simply flattened into a verdict on the family, and because the debate's mature readers learned to hold the two together: an institution can entrench a sex's subordination and shelter a class's survival at the same time, and most durable institutions do something of the kind. The domestic labour debate's contribution to this richer picture was mostly preparatory, clearing the value question away so the historical argument could proceed on evidence. Preparatory work is easy to undervalue in retrospect, like scaffolding in photographs of a finished building. The mockery trades on that ease. The defence's task, performed in an earlier section at full strength, is to keep the scaffolding in view long enough for its necessity to be judged fairly.

### Why does the family wage belong in an account of this debate?

Because it names a historical mechanism, demanded and defended by identifiable actors, that entrenched the assignment of unpaid work to wives, and the debate's political remainder consists exactly of such mechanisms, which its value theoretic instruments could identify as leftovers but never explain from within their own resources.

There is a methodological moral in the family wage's prominence, worth stating for students of the episode. The explanation that finally illuminated the assignment was found not by refining the contested category further but by changing the archive, from treatises to union records, wage books, and legislation. Molyneux's retrospective had pointed toward the concrete and the historical; the family wage literature walked in the direction she pointed. When a debate's retrospective and its successor practice align that neatly, the retrospective has earned the authority this article grants it as the strongest objection, and the debate has earned a more nuanced epitaph than either golden age or scholastic detour. It asked its question, answered it, discovered the question's insufficiency through its own best critic, and handed the inquiry to methods that could carry it further. The sequence is almost a model of how a research tradition is supposed to behave, which is why the episode repays study even by readers with no stake in its categories.

## The successor: what social reproduction theory inherited

A settlement's value is proven by what it lets others build, and the building on this one began quickly. Social reproduction theory, as it consolidated in the following decades, took the debate's results as premises: unpaid domestic labour does not produce value in the technical sense; it reproduces labour power and, beyond that, life and social relations themselves; capital depends on this unwaged sphere while disavowing the dependence; the wage conceals the dependence by presenting a household process as an individual's transaction. From those premises the successor theory asked the systemic question the debate had circled: how does a mode of production organised around the production of value organise, subordinate, and periodically crisis manage the life making activities outside value production that it cannot do without? The question could only be posed in that form once the temptation to dissolve the boundary, by declaring the outside activity a species of value production, had been tested and set aside. In that precise sense the debate is the successor's foundation, and the successor's flourishing is the empirical refutation of the dead end charge in its strong form. Dead ends do not have research programmes built on their conclusions.

The inheritance included cautions as well as premises. Successor writers tend to specify mechanisms rather than rely on functional assertion, having watched the debate's functionalist moments, housework exists because capital needs it, draw justified fire: a need explains nothing until the process meeting it is identified, and the reproduction of labour power occurs through households for historical reasons that might have been otherwise and in places are otherwise. Successor writers also tend to mark the distinction between reproducing labour power and reproducing people, a distinction forced in the debate's exchanges about children, and to build race and migration into the analysis from the start rather than append them, having absorbed the criticism of the stylised household. None of this makes the successor theory a refutation of the debate. It makes the debate a foundation with the unusual property that its own retrospective critic anticipated most of the corrections. Readers approaching the successor literature first, as many now do, can use the present article's ledger in reverse: each premise they meet there has a row in the table, a year, a venue, and an objection it survived.

## The standard misreadings, corrected in sequence

Because the episode is mostly encountered through summaries, a family of errors has crystallised around it, and correcting them is a service reference writing owes its readers. The first error holds that the debate concluded housework is not work. Nothing in the settlement touches the reality or the quantity of the labour, which every participant affirmed and several measured; the conclusion concerns a category of value theory, and the participants would have regarded the error as evidence that their distinctions had been reported by someone who never read them. The second error, cousin to the first, holds that the debate concluded housework is worthless, trading on the everyday sense of value the whole argument laboured to separate from the technical sense. In the technical sense the conclusion is that the work produces use values and reproduces the workforce; in the everyday sense the participants valued it so highly they spent a decade on it. The third error inverts the second pair: it reports the debate as having proven that capital exploits housewives in the strict sense, a claim the settlement rejected, usually in the service of a rhetoric the rejection was meant to discipline. The fourth error imagines unanimity where none existed, either at the start, when positions ranged from Benston's use value separation to the pamphlet's maximalism, or at the end, when Delphy's track and the wages campaign's convictions continued outside the settlement's jurisdiction. The fifth error pronounces the debate's terms obsolete, superseded by the successor vocabulary, when the successor vocabulary presupposes the debate's results at every step and cannot be understood historically without them.

A sixth error deserves separate emphasis because it appears in respectable places: the claim that the debate was a purely British affair or a purely academic one. Its journals were British and American, its pamphlet Italian and international, its campaign organisational on several continents, its French track distinct and influential, and its participants included activists for whom the journals were one arena among several. The error matters because it feeds the scholastic caricature, confining to a seminar room an argument that moved through women's centres, conferences, and campaign offices. The caricature has been answered at length in the defence section; the factual correction belongs here, among the misreadings, since a caricature is only a misreading with better jokes. Set against the corrected record, the episode's true shape is neither a golden age nor a farce: a transatlantic, movement connected literature that posed a categorial question with political stakes, answered the question, argued about the answer's meaning with exemplary honesty, and dispersed into the fields its answer made possible.

### Did the debate conclude that housework is not real work?

No participant concluded anything of the kind, and the settlement presupposes the opposite: the work is real, extensive, and indispensable, which is exactly why its categorial status mattered enough to argue about. The conclusion reached concerns value production in a technical sense, not the existence or the burden of the labour.

One more correction, subtler than the rest, concerns the debate's relation to Marx himself. It is sometimes said that the participants discovered Marx had nothing to say about domestic labour, or alternatively that they merely applied what he had said. Both flatter the record. Marx left remarks, asides, and the analytical materials, on the reproduction of labour power, on the family, on the value of the capacity to work, from which positions could be constructed in more than one direction, as the debate's opening years demonstrated by constructing several. The participants were not applying a doctrine; they were extending a method to a case its author had treated only at the margins, and the extension's failure to yield a value theoretic result is a finding about the method's reach, not about anyone's fidelity. Traditions learn their boundaries by such expeditions. The domestic labour debate was the Marxist tradition's expedition to its own household boundary, and the map it brought back, with the boundary drawn in and the territory beyond it marked as requiring other instruments, remains in use.

## Why the market did not simply absorb the work

A sceptical reader may ask why the boundary between household production and commodity production persisted at all. Capitalism is famous for commodifying whatever it touches; why did cooking, cleaning, and care remain substantially uncommodified in the very societies where the debate occurred, performed privately rather than bought from firms? The debaters and their successors assembled answers that double as a map of the political remainder's mechanisms. Part of the answer is cost arithmetic. The unwaged arrangement is cheap for everyone except the worker performing it: a commercial service must cover wages, premises, and profit, while the household version draws on labour whose price is hidden inside an existing wage, so commodified reproduction would raise the wage bill or lower living standards, and every interested party except housewives had reason to let the arrangement stand. Part is the character of the tasks, their simultaneity and their embedding in affection: a parent reading to a child is producing comfort that markets replicate clumsily, and households rationally keep such tasks in the realm of gift and duty even where purchased substitutes exist. Part is standards and trust, since the home is where a society stores its anxieties about cleanliness, nutrition, and children's safety, and purchased care is monitored in ways purchased goods are not. And part is the inertia of the family form itself, a legal and emotional institution that predates capitalism, which capitalism adapted rather than dissolved because the adaptation served it.

The same analysis explains the directions in which commodification did advance, always at the household's edges: prepared foods invading the larder, laundries and then machines, commercial entertainment displacing home production of leisure, childcare purchased by the households that could pay, frequently from women whose own households then faced the original problem one rung down. The frontier moved by class, which is why generalisations about the housewife's disappearance or persistence both find evidence. For the debate, the moving frontier posed a genuine analytical challenge the participants recognised: if portions of domestic labour migrate into commodity production over time, the boundary the settlement polices is historical, not eternal, and the classification of any given task can change as its social form changes. Nothing in the settlement forbids that recognition. The settlement classifies by form, and forms change; a meal cooked by an employed cook for a paying customer belongs to value production in a way the identical meal cooked at home does not, and may cease to if the arrangement changes back. The theory's consistency across such changes is a virtue the mockery rarely credits, since it shows the categories doing their intended work, tracking social relations rather than blessing or condemning tasks.

## The reserve army and the double day: Benston's other legacy

Benston's article contained a second thesis, less debated at the time and arguably more prescient than the use value framework that housed it: women function as a reserve army of labour, drawn into employment when labour is scarce and expelled when it is not, their domestic assignment supplying the ideology that makes the expulsion look like a homecoming. The thesis connected the household to the labour market's cycles with a single mechanism and explained a pattern every reader knew from wartime and postwar experience. Later feminist labour history filled the thesis with evidence, complicating it, since women's employment proved stickier across cycles than a pure reserve model predicts and grew secularly through the very decades of supposed domestic restoration, but the core insight, that domestic ideology serves as the labour market's shock absorber, entered the tradition's permanent stock. Its presence in the founding article is a useful corrective to the caricature of the debate as a single question fanatically pursued. The founders wrote about employment, ideology, the state, and strategy alongside the value problem; the value problem is what posterity, and the participants' own polemics, selected for emphasis. A debate's reception history narrows it. Reading the founding texts whole, as the archive invites, restores the breadth and makes the subsequent narrowing itself an object of study: why did the most technical strand become the famous one? Because it was the strand where disagreement could be made precise enough to fight over in print, month after month, which says something about journals as institutions as much as about the participants' priorities.

The double day, the 1975 critique's central figure, deserves its own paragraph of elaboration because it reorganised the field's imagination. A woman employed full time who then performs the bulk of a household's unpaid work labours in two systems of obligation with different logics: her employer buys her hours and disciplines them by the clock, while her household claims her hours through need and love, undisciplined by any clock and therefore prone to expand. The phenomenology differs, the fatigue compounds, and the politics that address only one shift will misdescribe her life. Trade union feminists built campaigns on exactly this figure in the years after the debate, demanding shorter hours for all workers, childcare as a collective provision, and men's equal share as an explicit bargaining and cultural goal. That practical lineage, running from a critique inside a value debate to bargaining agendas, complicates the mockery's claim that nothing came of the episode but distinctions. Distinctions, in this lineage, became demands, after passing through the correction the debate's own participants supplied. The path was indirect, as paths from theory to practice usually are, and the retrospective critics are right that it was slower than it should have been; but a slow path is not a dead end, and the vocabulary of the double day, now universal, is one of the debate's unacknowledged exports to ordinary language.

## Counting the hours: the measurement problem behind the theory

Behind the categorial argument lay a quantitative embarrassment the participants acknowledged intermittently: nobody could say with confidence how much domestic labour there was. Time budget studies existed, and the debaters cited them, but the work's boundaries resist measurement in ways factory labour does not. Is a woman cooking while supervising children and listening for the laundry performing one labour or three? Does care delivered while resting count? The simultaneity and standby character of much household work, its fusion with leisure and with love, defeated the stopwatch methods that time study had perfected on assembly lines, and the estimates in circulation varied widely enough to support almost any rhetoric. The measurement problem mattered theoretically, because several arguments turned on magnitudes: the size of the subsidy, the comparison with waged hours, the plausibility of the claim that the household sector rivalled the industrial sector in labour time. It mattered politically because the wages campaign's opponents demanded figures the campaign could gesture at but not produce in agreed form. And it mattered for the debate's method, since a literature strong on categories and weak on quantities was always liable to the charge that it preferred the former because they were cheap to produce.

The successor fields took the problem seriously in ways that vindicate treating it here at length. Feminist economists built satellite accounts and time use surveys into national statistical programmes over the following decades, arriving at robust findings that unwaged household and care work amounts to a very large share of all labour performed in every society measured, findings the debaters would have greeted as the empirical foundation they lacked. The findings did not reopen the value question, because magnitudes cannot change a form classification, any more than the discovery of a vast ocean changes the definition of water. What they changed was the subsidy's scale in the public imagination and, eventually, in policy argument, where the sheer size of the unwaged sector became a premise in debates over pensions for carers, credits for care years, and the design of working time. The sequence offers a small parable about theoretical and empirical work: the debate settled a classification before the quantities were known, and the quantities, when they arrived, found the classification waiting and were the more usable for it. Critics who treated the sequence as evidence of scholastic priority, categories before facts, must reckon with the fact that the facts, once gathered, were interpreted through the categories the earlier work had cleaned. Cleaning is not the opposite of inquiry. It is inquiry's less photogenic half.

## Necessary labour, in two registers

One refinement, introduced quietly and now standard, separates the senses of a phrase the early debate used loosely. Necessary labour, in Marx's analysis of the working day, names the portion in which the worker produces the equivalent of the wage, as against surplus labour producing surplus value. When contributors called housework necessary labour, they usually meant something different: labour necessary for the reproduction of the workforce and of life, necessary to the system and to the household rather than necessary in the accounting of a single working day. The two senses can coexist in an argument, housework is necessary in the reproductive sense while forming no part of necessary labour in the day's division, but only if the writer marks which register is speaking. Unmarked, the phrase slid between registers and manufactured agreements and disagreements alike: a critic denying that housework is necessary labour in the second register could sound as if she denied its necessity in the first, and the polemic would ignite over a difference of bookkeeping. The mature literature's habit of writing necessary to reproduction in full, reserving the bare term for the working day's division, is a small monument to the larger process this article has described, the slow conversion of a polemical literature into a disciplined one.

The same discipline eventually governed the word exploitation, the phrase labour of love, and the description of the husband's position, each term acquiring, through the debate's corrections, a specified meaning or an explicit warning. Reference works inherit such disciplines without noticing them, which is why this article pauses over them: a glossary entry that distinguishes senses without saying why repeats a conclusion while hiding the labour that produced it, and readers who know the labour can defend the distinction when it is challenged. The domestic labour debate, whatever else it was, was a factory for such distinctions, and its products are embedded so deeply in the successor vocabulary that speakers use them daily without provenance. Provenance matters to historians and to anyone who wants to know which distinctions were fought for and which arrived by accident. This article's ledger, its insistence on years and venues, serves that knowing.

## The pattern this debate exemplifies for the whole series

This series has met the shape before and will meet it again: a dispute that reaches a determinate answer on its own terms while leaving the question that motivated it untouched. The domestic labour debate is the shape's clearest instance because both halves are so legible. On its own terms, inside the value categories, the dispute closed; the answer has held for decades, is presupposed by the successor literature, and would require genuinely new arguments, not renewed indignation, to reopen. Off its own terms, in the territory of the question that brought the participants to their desks, the distribution of the work and the explanation of women's subordination, the dispute's closure changed little directly, and the progress that occurred came from other instruments taken up afterward. Naming the pattern matters because readers of controversies habitually grade them on a single scale, triumph or fiasco, and the pattern supplies the two dimensional grade most real controversies deserve: what was settled, and what was never going to be settled by that route. The grade this article has given throughout, a real technical settlement and an open political remainder, is the pattern's name applied to its exemplar.

There is a practical corollary for any reader involved in a living controversy, worth drawing before the personas convert the material for their constituencies. When a debate feels simultaneously productive and futile, the pattern suggests a diagnostic question: which of the questions in the room does the current instrument actually process, and which is it merely hosting? The domestic labour participants hosted the political question for a decade while processing the categorial one, and the hosting was not worthless, since the room kept the question alive and assembled the people who later processed it otherwise. But the retrospective's sting came from the confusion of the two activities, the belief that processing the hosted question was under way when only the other was. Movements and disciplines that learn to ask the diagnostic question early can allocate their decade better, running the instruments in parallel rather than in sequence. That the domestic labour debate teaches this by negative example, at the cost of its own years, is the final item in the defence's ledger and the one its detractors least often answer.

## A short guide to the debate's afterlife in print

For readers who want the primary sequence, the venues named in the ledger remain the addresses: Benston in Monthly Review for September 1969; the pamphlet by Dalla Costa and James from 1972, reprinted and translated widely; Seccombe in New Left Review at the opening of 1974, with the critique by Coulson, Magas, and Wainwright and Gardiner's companion piece in the same journal a year later; Gardiner, Himmelweit, and Mackintosh in the CSE Bulletin for 1975; Himmelweit and Mohun in the Cambridge Journal of Economics for 1977; Delphy in English through The Main Enemy and her subsequent translated work; Molyneux in New Left Review for the summer of 1979. Around that spine sits a penumbra the specialist will want: John Harrison's early contribution on the political economy of housework, Peggy Morton's responses to Benston, the wages campaign's own journals and conference papers, the anthology assembled by Ellen Malos as the decade turned, and the retrospective essays that multiplied once the successor field could look back from solid ground. The cluster's pillar article on the Marxist feminist tradition situates the sequence inside the longer arc, from the nineteenth century arguments forward, and readers who arrived here from a footnote in some other literature are advised to continue there for the frame this article deliberately narrowed in order to do its work.

## Domestic labour and the capitalist working day

One way to feel the settlement's stakes is to follow a single working day through both of its economies. A machinist rises before dawn in a household where another adult has already begun the unpaid shift: breakfasts produced, children washed and dressed, a packed lunch assembled from groceries bought with the machinist's wage, the kitchen restored. The machinist sells labour power at the factory gate, works a day the theory divides into the hours replacing the wage and the hours yielding surplus, and returns to a household where the second shift resumes: the evening meal, the laundry, the children's homework and illnesses and fears, the preparation of sleep from which tomorrow's labour power will emerge. Political economy in its classical form narrates only the middle segment of that day. The feminist intervention insisted that the segments flanking it are not leisure and not nature; they are labour, socially organised and sexually assigned, and any account of the working day that begins at the factory gate has cut short the phenomenon it claims to explain. The debate's categories were the instruments chosen to force that insistence into the theory's own language, which is why the argument about value was also, always, an argument about what counts as the working day.

The insistence survived the categories' refusal. Labour history and the sociology of work now routinely treat the total working day, paid and unpaid together, as the unit of analysis, and time use research sums the two into measures of total labour burden that show the unpaid portion rivalling the paid in most economies. When those measures are disaggregated by sex, the pattern the debaters asserted from experience appears in columns: women's total labour time typically exceeds men's, with the excess concentrated in the unwaged segment and in the years when children are small. None of this required the household's labour to create value, and none of it is embarrassed by the settlement. The working day lens does, however, explain the heat the settlement generated better than any story about scholastic temperament. To be told that the flanking segments of the day, segments one has lived as exhaustion, produce nothing the central category registers is to receive a truth of the theory and a provocation in the same sentence, and the movement's theorists spent a decade deciding which of the two the sentence principally was. Their eventual answer, both, and the provocation is the theory's problem rather than the work's, is the mature position this article has presented as the settlement's human meaning.

There is a further strand here, concerning rest and readiness, that the debaters touched and later work developed. Labour power is not reproduced by calories and shelter alone; it is reproduced as a capacity held by a person whose morale, health, and sense of a life beyond the shift are maintained or eroded inside the household. The emotional labour of the home, the management of feelings, the absorption of the factory's frustrations at the kitchen table, belongs to reproduction in this fuller sense while resisting every metaphor of production the debate tried on it. Arlie Hochschild's studies of emotional labour, written for a different conversation, gave the phenomenon its durable name soon after the settlement, and social reproduction writing absorbed it as part of what the household supplies. The absorption illustrates again how the debate's boundary drawing enabled growth beyond the boundary: once the claim that such labour creates value was surrendered, its actual character could be studied without the distortion of a Procrustean category, and the study found kinds of work, affective, relational, anticipatory, that the production metaphor had obscured even while trying to honour them.

## The discussion travels: Germany, the subsistence perspective, and housewifisation

The debate's texts travelled, and their travels changed them. In West Germany, a group of sociologists associated with Bielefeld, among them Maria Mies, Claudia von Werlhof, and Veronika Bennholdt-Thomsen, took up the question of unwaged work from the early nineteen eighties and radicalised one of its strands into a global claim. Their subsistence perspective treated housewives in the wealthy countries, peasants in the colonised world, and other unwaged or underwaged producers as a single class of the exploited whose labour subsidises accumulation worldwide, and Mies gave the process a name, housewifisation, for the ideological and material assignment of women to the status of housewife as a model exported through development policy. The German development matters for this article because it shows a road the Anglophone settlement had closed being reopened under new management: where Himmelweit and Mohun had refused to extend value categories to unwaged labour, the Bielefeld writers extended a broader notion of exploitation to cover colonised land and labour alongside the housewife, accepting the loss of technical precision as the price of a unified politics of the unwaged.

The resulting argument replayed, at world scale, the tensions the original debate had worked through at household scale. Critics charged the subsistence perspective with the same functionalism and category stretching the earlier critics had charged against Seccombe, while its defenders replied that a theory unable to connect the German housewife, the Indian peasant woman, and the domestic worker in a Gulf household was a theory of fragments. The exchange never achieved a settlement comparable to the earlier one, partly because the parties shared fewer premises, and its inconclusiveness retrospectively illuminates the conditions that made the domestic labour settlement possible: a compact field, shared texts, a precise question, and journals willing to host the sequence to its end. Settlements are institutional achievements as much as intellectual ones. Later sections of this article have leaned on that observation; the German case is its clearest evidence, since the same question, posed in a wider and more divided field, generated a literature rather than a conclusion.

### What did the German writers add to the debate's questions?

They widened the frame from the household of the wealthy countries to a world system of unwaged work, treating housewives, peasants, and colonised producers as linked subsidies to accumulation, and they coined a vocabulary, subsistence production and housewifisation among its terms, for the assignment of women to unwaged status as a global process.

The widening also carried the debate's unresolved assignment problem into development studies, where the question of why women perform subsistence labour could be posed with comparative evidence across agrarian systems, bride wealth and dowry regimes, land tenure rules, and colonial labour policies. Posed there, the question yielded to exactly the instruments Molyneux had prescribed: history, law, and political economy in the broad sense rather than value categorics. The German literature thus confirms this article's central construction from an unexpected direction. The value question, exported, refused to settle; the assignment question, exported, proved tractable to institutional inquiry. A debate's geography can teach as much as its arguments, and the geography here maps the boundary the settlement drew: inside the compact world of shared categories, closure; outside it, proliferation, with the fruitful work migrating toward the mechanisms of assignment.

## Vogel and the attempt to gather the threads into one theory

The most ambitious attempt to rebuild on the settlement's ground came from Lise Vogel, whose book Marxism and the Oppression of Women, published in 1983 after articles through the preceding years, proposed what she called a unitary theory. Vogel accepted the debate's negative result and asked what positive account of women's oppression a Marxism disciplined by that result could offer. Her answer centred on the reproduction of labour power as a process internal to capitalism yet standing in permanent tension with production: capital needs the workforce renewed, preferably at low cost, and the renewal occurs in kin based households whose organisation capitalism inherits, adapts, and repeatedly destabilises. Women's oppression, on this account, is rooted neither in a separate patriarchal system nor in ideology alone but in their assigned place within that renewal process, an assignment sustained by the interplay of wage structures, state policy, and the material interests of male workers, yet never simply functional, since capital's own demands for women's employment perpetually undermine the arrangement capital also relies upon. The tensions built into the account were the point: a unitary theory that derived oppression from a contradiction could explain both the arrangement's persistence and its instability.

Vogel's book arrived after the debate had dispersed, and its reception illustrates how settlements alter a field's reading habits. Reviewers measured it against the debate's fixed points, accepting its refusal of value creation in the home as settled ground and arguing instead about the adequacy of its positive mechanism, which is precisely the argumentative situation a settlement is supposed to create: progress consisting of new disputes on ground the old dispute cleared. The book's long eclipse and later revival, when social reproduction theory's consolidation sent readers back to its neglected precursor, completes the lesson. A field's memory is itself an institution, and the debate's afterlife includes the shelving and unshelving of the very work that best embodied its lessons. This article's treatment of Vogel is brief because a neighbouring article carries the successor theory in full; the point here is genealogical. Between the settlement of 1977 and the mature successor theory stands Vogel's attempt, drawing the consequences the retrospective had demanded, and the line from her chapters to the contemporary literature runs straighter than the contemporary literature always acknowledges.

## The household as economists outside the tradition modelled it

While Marxist feminists argued over value, another economics of the household was being built in a different idiom, and the contrast sharpens both. Gary Becker's household production models, developed from the nineteen sixties and gathered in his treatise on the family, treated the household as a small firm combining purchased goods and members' time to produce commodities like meals, health, and children, with specialisation between spouses explained by comparative advantage in market versus domestic productivity. The formal elegance was undeniable, and the model's treatment of time as a scarce input dignified household work as allocation worthy of analysis. Yet from the Marxist feminist side, the model's founding assumptions looked like the debate's targets wearing mathematics: comparative advantage naturalised the wage gap that was itself produced by discrimination and by the assignment under investigation, so the explanation circled quietly back into its premise. A specialisation adopted because her wage is lower perpetuates the lower wage, and a model that begins after the differential cannot explain the differential. The feminist economists who later rebuilt household economics on bargaining foundations, modelling spouses as negotiators with separate interests and unequal fallback positions, in effect imported the domestic debate's central insistence, that the household is a site of conflict and appropriation as well as cooperation, into the mainstream's own language.

The parallel histories carry a historiographical warning against treating the domestic labour debate as the decade's only serious thought about housework. It was the most theoretically ambitious treatment within one tradition, conducted in that tradition's demanding vocabulary, while other traditions produced models, surveys, and ethnographies of the same kitchens. The debate's participants were aware of the neighbours and occasionally raided them, Oakley's interviews being the clearest import. What they did not do, and what a full history must, is mistake their conversation for the whole of the era's knowledge. The settlement's jurisdiction, stated earlier through the metaphor of a game, applies here with force: the negative answer binds those who pose the question inside Marx's value analysis. Becker's households produce no value either, strictly speaking, since his framework lacks the category, and the absence never troubled his readers. Categories are instruments of traditions. The debate's enduring interest lies in watching one tradition discover, publicly and at cost, what its prized instrument could and could not cut.

## When reproduction is squeezed: the shock absorber thesis

A later generation of writers drew from the debate a claim about crises that its originators had implied but never assembled. If households reproduce the workforce through unpaid labour whose quantity can expand, then households constitute a buffer between capital and the consequences of its own downturns: when wages fall, hours are cut, or public services retreat, the shortfall is absorbed by intensified domestic work, more cooking from cheaper ingredients, more care supplied at home that services once provided, more mending and making do, overwhelmingly by women. Diane Elson and others in feminist economics formalised the thought as the depletion and resilience of the reproductive sphere, and the financial crisis that began in 2008 gave it a grim field test, as austerity programmes across wealthy states transferred costs from public budgets to kitchens in a pattern the thesis predicts. The episode matters here because it shows the settlement's propositions doing explanatory work far from their origin. The subsidy mechanism, specified in 1975 as an effect on the value of labour power, reappears as a dynamic account of who pays for crises, and it does so without requiring anyone to reopen the question of whether the absorbing labour creates value. It does not, and the absorption is real: that conjunction, which the nineteen seventies found paradoxical enough to fight over for a decade, had become by the crisis years a premise analysts could build on in a sentence.

Hold the conjunction up once more, since it is the debate's gift in its most usable form. A sphere can be outside an economy's value circuits and inside its causal structure, indispensable to its functioning, exploited in the plain sense that its resources are drawn upon without equivalent return, and central to its crises, all without producing value in the technical sense the theory reserves for commodity production. Every clause was contested at some point between 1969 and 1979. Their combination is the settled inheritance, and most confusions in summary accounts arise from dropping one clause and then finding the remainder incoherent. Drop the first clause and the wages campaign's difficulties return. Drop the causal clauses and the settlement looks like a demotion of the work. Keep all four and the debate's product stands as what this article has called it: a technical settlement carrying a political remainder, where the remainder now includes a research programme on crises that its original participants could not have foreseen.

### What happens to household labour when wages and services contract?

It expands and intensifies to fill the gap, as cheaper ingredients are worked longer, purchased care is replaced by family care, and maintenance is deferred into effort, so that the costs of a downturn are transferred from employers and budgets onto the unwaged hours of the household, principally onto women.

That transfer is not automatic or costless to the sphere performing it, and the literature the thesis generated is careful on the point: reproduction can be depleted. A household absorbing shocks draws down health, time, and the carer's own future earnings, and a society that treats the buffer as infinite discovers its limits in carers' exhaustion and children's unmet needs. The vocabulary of depletion, alien to the original debate's categories, grew from its soil nonetheless, since the debate had established the analytical posture the newer work needed: take the unwaged sphere seriously as a structured part of the economy's causal order, specify the flows across its boundary in both directions, and refuse both the romanticism that calls the sphere a refuge and the reduction that calls it a factory. The posture, more than any single proposition, is what the successor fields inherited, and postures are inherited through exemplars. This debate became the exemplar its tradition reaches for when it needs to show how the posture is earned.

## The wages demand judged by the settlement's own light

Suppose the settlement is granted and the campaign's founding claim withdrawn. What becomes of the demand itself, wages for housework, when its theoretical warrant changes? The debate's later literature and the campaign's historians offer a surprisingly nuanced answer, and rehearsing it exercises the distinction, now familiar, between a claim's truth and a demand's function. As a description of an existing economic relation, the demand's original rationale failed with the value claim: if housework creates no value and no surplus, the wage cannot be represented as payment for value produced, owed by capital as wages are owed. As a political instrument, the demand was always defended on other grounds as well: that demanding a wage names the work as work in the public language of entitlement, that it would give housewives an independent income and thereby alter power inside households, that it would raise the cost of the unwaged arrangement and force its reorganisation. Those grounds survive the settlement untouched, since they concern what a wage would do rather than what housework is. Critics of the demand also retained grounds independent of the value dispute: that a wage paid into the home might consolidate the sexual division it claimed to attack, pricing women into the kitchen at public expense, and that the demand's constituency and administration raised problems its advocates answered better in rhetoric than in design.

The mature assessment, which this article adopts, treats the episode as the cleanest demonstration available that strategies and theories require separate audits. The campaign chose a demand for its disruptive charge and borrowed a theory to underwrite it; the theory failed its audit while several of the demand's justifications passed theirs, and the ensuing confusion, in which defenders of the strategy felt obliged to defend the economics and critics of the economics were heard as enemies of the strategy, wasted more goodwill than the original error ever had. Movements repeat the pattern whenever a slogan's proof is mistaken for the slogan. The domestic labour debate's distinction between the audits is among its most exportable products, applicable wherever a movement's favourite claim is also its most vulnerable one. It also softens, in retrospect, the judgement on the pamphlet's authors: they wrote as strategists in a workerist idiom where the fusion of claim and weapon was a deliberate method, and the scholars who tested the claim as pure economics were, from the strategists' vantage, changing the rules mid game. Both vantages are now part of the record, and the record is richer for containing the collision rather than a sanitised victory.

## The conduct of the argument: what the exchanges show about the field

Read as a whole, the debate's exchanges display a standard of conduct worth naming, because later polemics in adjacent fields have not always matched it. Positions were attributed to named authors and answered in their strongest available form more often than caricatured; concessions were made in print, Gardiner's relocation of the question and Seccombe's acknowledgements among them; journals printed replies adjacent to the articles they answered, allowing a reader of a single issue to reconstruct the state of play; and the retrospective that judged the enterprise was commissioned, in effect, by the field's own leading journal from one of its own participants. These are the behaviours of a literature that expects to converge, and they help explain why convergence, unusually, occurred. Where they were breached, the breaches are memorable precisely for their rarity: the strategic dismissal in Seccombe that drew the wages campaign's fury, occasional imputations of bad faith across the value divide. The field's institutions, the journals and the conference circuit and the anthology makers, rewarded the convergent behaviours with space and citation, a mundane mechanism with large effects on what kind of argument a controversy produces.

There is a counterpoint, and completeness requires it. The debate's civility coexisted with exclusions that its participants only intermittently noticed: the field of speakers was narrow by class formation and by race, its venues were metropolitan, and its canonical figure, the housewife of the advanced capitalist household, silently centred one population's experience. The strongest internal critics of the following decade, many of them Black feminists and feminists from the postcolonial world, directed their fire as much at that centred figure as at any proposition, arguing that a theory of women's work built from one kitchen would misdescribe the labours of women who cleaned that kitchen for wages or whose households were organised by extended kin and informal economies. The debate's settlement, being about forms, survived their criticism in a way its framing did not, and the successor literature rebuilt the framing on broader evidence. A fair history keeps both judgements in the same paragraph: the argument was conducted well, and the room it was conducted in was too small. The second judgement is the one the successor field acted on, which is why its rooms look different.

### Why did this controversy converge when so many do not?

Because its participants shared premises precise enough to make refutation possible, its journals staged replies beside the claims they answered, its central question was narrow enough for a formal argument to close, and its own retrospective critic redirected the field instead of merely scoring it, converting closure into a programme.

Convergence had one more condition, easy to miss: the losing claims had somewhere to go. The writers who had invested in the value claim did not face professional extinction when it failed, because the field offered adjacent territories, the history of the family wage, the analysis of the state, the successor theory itself, where their skills and standing transferred. Controversies harden into permanent schisms when defeat means exile; they settle when defeat means relocation. The institutions of the nineteen seventies left, pamphlet series hungry for content, new journals founding, a movement generating questions faster than any literature could answer them, provided relocation routes in abundance. Theorists designing environments for productive disagreement in any field could do worse than study the plumbing: many venues, low barriers, rapid reply, and more honourable questions than any single answer can monopolise.

## The passages everyone fought over: the debate as a reading of Marx

Because the argument proceeded by textual warrant, a handful of passages from Marx became its battlegrounds, and knowing them explains exchanges that otherwise look like quibbles. Chief among them stands the chapter of Capital where the value of labour power is determined. Marx writes there that the value of that peculiar commodity is fixed, like any other, by the labour time necessary for its production and reproduction, and that its use value possesses the peculiar property of being a source of value. He immediately complicates the formula with what he calls a historical and moral element: the subsistence standard entering the determination is not a biological floor but a product of a civilisation's habits and of class struggle, unlike the value of other commodities. Every party to the debate mined that chapter. The pamphlet's side stressed the phrase production and reproduction of labour power itself, reading domestic labour into the process the chapter names. The settlement's side stressed the determination's mechanism, the labour time contained in the worker's means of subsistence, and the chapter's consistent treatment of the worker's consumption as individual consumption, a moment of reproduction that Marx explicitly distinguishes from productive consumption inside the labour process. The text, characteristically, gave both parties something and neither everything, since Marx had analysed reproduction from the standpoint of capital's circuit and had simply not posed the feminist question his readers brought to the page.

The second battleground lay in the writings on productive labour gathered in the notebooks published as Theories of Surplus Value, where Marx worries the distinction through examples, the schoolmaster, the clown, the singer, and remarks that the same labour can be productive or unproductive depending on the relation into which it enters. The pamphlet tradition quoted the relational emphasis hopefully; the settlement quoted the examples' moral, that the classification follows the purchaser and the purpose, capital seeking valorisation versus revenue spent on enjoyment. A third site, less quoted and more suggestive, was the chapter on the buying and selling of labour power, where Marx notes that the worker's individual consumption, though it reproduces the commodity capital needs, appears from capital's standpoint as an unproductive consumption left to the worker's drives and to the household. Readers attentive to that standpoint language could see the whole debate prefigured: Marx himself registers the household's labour as necessary to his system and external to its valorisation, in adjacent sentences, without treating the conjunction as a problem. The feminists made it a problem, which is their original contribution, and then solved it in his vocabulary, which is their discipline. The episode is therefore best described neither as exegesis nor as invention but as the completion, under pressure, of an analysis its originator had left with a door ajar, and the completion's direction, boundary rather than annexation, was chosen by the arguments the participants actually gave, not dictated by the passages. Another generation, arguing differently, might have chosen otherwise and paid the costs the settlement's defenders enumerated. The passages remain, patient as ever, for whoever tests them next.

## The missing variable: men's share of the work

For a debate about who performs domestic labour, the participants wrote remarkably little about the obvious experimental group: men who perform it. The omission was partly the stylised household at work, its husband defined by the wage he brings and little else, and partly the era's data, which showed men's contribution to housework in the wealthy countries as small and stubbornly stable across the very years when women's employment transformed. Sociologists of the family, Michael Young and Peter Willmott most influentially, had predicted a symmetrical family in which spouses would converge in both employment and domestic sharing, and their prediction circulated through the debate's years as the optimistic alternative to the feminists' structural gloom. The evidence available even then was unkind to symmetry: time studies found men's domestic minutes rising slowly from a very low base, concentrated in discretionary and outdoor tasks, while the routine, daily, non postponable core of the work, cooking, cleaning, laundry, the care of small children, remained assigned with a rigidity that looked less like lag than like design. The debate's framework could say why the core's assignment suited capital and suited husbands; it could not, on its own categories, model the husband's refusal as an act of power inside the household, which is why Delphy's attention to appropriation and Hartmann's to male workers' interests attracted readers the value argument left hungry.

Later research made the missing variable its subject, and its findings complete the remainder's map in a way worth summarising for the record. Studies of couples who share the work equally tend to find not exhortation but structure at the origin: comparable earnings, working hours that permit alternation, explicit negotiation early in the household's life, and frequently a deliberate ideology of fairness held by both partners. Where sharing fails, the mechanisms are equally concrete: the earnings gap invoked as arithmetic, the gatekeeping by which the partner who has done the work polices standards for the partner beginning it, the scheduling of men's contributions as help offered to a manager rather than ownership assumed, the sheer path dependence by which whoever first performs a task becomes its permanent assignee. None of these mechanisms is visible to value theory, and all of them are visible to the institutional and interpersonal analysis the retrospective prescribed. The debate's silence about men is thus the sharpest single illustration of its remainder, because the answer to the motivating question was sitting, unexamined, on the other side of the kitchen table, in the conduct of the person the theory cast as a wage with legs. Naming that blindness is not anachronistic scolding; the participants' own successors named it within the decade, and the naming reorganised the research agenda toward bargaining, time, and standards, where it has remained.

### What did time studies show about men's part in the work?

They showed a contribution small beside women's, rising slowly, and sorted by task, with men concentrated in occasional, discretionary, and outdoor jobs while the daily core of cooking, cleaning, laundry, and small children's care stayed assigned to women with a persistence that suggested structure rather than oversight.

The sorting by task deserves emphasis because aggregate hour counts conceal it, and concealment serves the appearance of convergence. A household in which he mows, repairs, and drives while she cooks, cleans, launders, and minds is not approaching equality along a single dimension; it is reproducing a division between the episodic and the relentless, between work that can be scheduled at convenience and work scheduled by other people's needs. The relentless tasks carry the mental burden as well, the remembering, anticipating, and monitoring that later vocabulary would call the mental load, and that burden, invisible in time diaries unless specifically probed, fell outside every party's accounting during the debate, the pamphlet's included. Its eventual naming belongs to the long list of phenomena the debate's framework could not see and its successors, working the remainder, brought into view one by one. Reading the episode through that list, rather than through its propositions alone, gives the fairest sense of its historical function: it defined a frontier, and on the frontier's far side lay most of the territory the next half century of research would settle.

## Nurseries, canteens, and communal houses: the socialised alternative in practice

The socialisation of domestic work, the remedy hovering at the edge of so many contributions, had a practical record by the time the debate began, and the record's mixed character shaped what the debaters dared propose. The early Soviet state had sponsored communal houses with shared kitchens and laundries and had theorised, through Kollontai and others, the withering of the private household as a socialist goal; within two decades the experiment had been largely abandoned, its communal facilities starved of investment and its ideology reversed toward the stabilised family. The kibbutz movement collectivised childcare and cooking more durably, in children's houses and communal dining halls, yet researchers repeatedly found women concentrated in the collective services themselves, the laundry and the kitchen and the children's houses, so that socialisation changed the work's location more readily than its sexual assignment. Wartime Britain and the United States built nurseries and communal restaurants at speed when women's factory labour was needed, then dismantled or neglected them when the emergency passed, demonstrating the state's willingness to socialise reproduction exactly as long as production required it. Each case taught the debate's generation a clause of the lesson its retrospective would formalise: the organisation of domestic work is a political outcome, revisable under pressure, and no mode of production, capitalist or avowedly socialist, had yet revised it in women's favour without women's organised power making the revision a condition.

The debaters absorbed the lesson unevenly. Benston's founding article had proposed conversion of private housework into public industry as the direction of solution, with a clear eyed doubt that capital would permit it. The wages campaign inverted the geography, proposing to import the wage into the home rather than export the work out of it. Scandinavian social democracy, unmentioned by some and envied by others, was meanwhile socialising childcare through the ordinary machinery of the welfare state, neither as socialist transformation nor as feminist victory parade, but as labour market policy, and thereby quietly proving that large portions of the domestic burden could be collectivised inside capitalism when employers needed women's labour and women voters demanded the means. That proof complicates any account in which a single system's logic dictates the household's form, and the debate's better historians have used the Scandinavian case as Molyneux used the Soviet one, as evidence that the assignment and organisation of the work answer to politics in the open sense, to parties, budgets, and movements, rather than to the value form's silent requirements. The remainder, once again, is where the action is, and the practical record is the remainder's archive.

There is a design question buried in the record that the debate touched and the successor literature made central: socialised services can be organised as employment like any other, which means the work's performance passes to paid hands, disproportionately women's and often migrants', and the problem of care's devaluation reappears inside the service as low wages and poor conditions rather than disappearing. The Nordic nurseries employed women at wages set by a labour market that priced care cheaply because the home had always supplied it free; the price of the unwaged original shadows its commodified successors everywhere. Analysts call this the care penalty, and tracing it leads back, by a long road, to the debate's founding insight that a society which obtains a necessity without paying for it builds the cheapness into every subsequent arrangement. The settlement did not predict the care penalty. The sensibility the debate created, its refusal to let unwaged necessity pass as natural background, is what allowed later writers to see the penalty as a single phenomenon across the public and private provision of care. Sensibilities, like postures, are real inheritances, and this one may outlast the propositions.

## Weighing the two cases: this article's verdict, with its reasons exposed

The checkpoints governing this article required the mockery and the defence to be carried at equal strength, and they have been. A verdict is still owed, and giving it requires the judge to show the scales. On the facts, there is no serious dispute left: the propositions of the settlement are correct inside the theory they interpret, the scholasticism charge describes a real disproportion in the literature's attention, the political question was left where it stood, and the successor field grew from the settlement's ground. The dispute is over weights. How much does a correct, durable, field founding technical result weigh against a decade's opportunity cost, measured in the campaigns not theorised and the mechanisms not investigated while the categories were refined? Reasonable readers will weight differently according to their theory of how movements learn, and this article does not pretend the scales are calibrated by anything stronger than judgement. Its judgement is that the defence carries the greater weight, for one controlling reason: the false claim the debate destroyed was not an ornament but a foundation, load bearing for a strategy and for the tradition's credibility, and foundations are audited at whatever cost in time the audit requires. A movement that had built its house on the assertion that housewives produce surplus value would have paid for the error later, in public, at compound interest. The decade's price, paid early and in the seminar room, was the cheaper bill.

The concession paired with that verdict has been made in full at the place the brief required it, in the assessment of Molyneux's retrospective, and it is repeated here without softening so that no reader can mistake the verdict for a whitewash: the criticism is substantially correct about the debate's political yield. The literature's energy flowed toward the tractable question; the assignment of the work, the enforcement of the assignment, the interests defending it, waited; and the waiting was a cost borne by the women whose evenings the theory described. Both halves of the verdict are the article's position, and they are not a compromise splitting a difference but a conjunction of two findings that happen to be compatible, as the settlement's own halves were. The technical settlement is real. The political remainder is real. The debate produced the first and exposed the second, and the exposure, performed by its own best critic in its own best journal, is part of the product being weighed. What the reader does with the weights is the reader's politics, which is where this subject has always insisted its questions finally live.

Let it be said once more, plainly and for the record, since summaries of this article may be tempted to blur it: the technical answer does not settle the political question. Knowing that household labour creates no value in Marx's sense tells a movement nothing about whether to demand wages, build nurseries, bargain for shorter hours, or organise for men's equal share, and nothing about why the work belongs to women in the first place. Those decisions require the institutional and historical analyses the remainder names, and the political will to act on them. The debate's misuse, wherever it occurs, is the citation of its settlement as if it closed those decisions, whether to bless the status quo as theoretically certified or to dismiss the work as theoretically negligible. Both citations are errors this article has corrected in its misreadings section, and both will recur, because a settled question adjacent to an open one is a standing invitation to smuggle the closure across the border. The border is the contribution. Patrol it, and the debate still works for its readers; let it lapse, and the mockery's prophecy fulfils itself, a distinction without a difference, endlessly reargued by people who have not read the ledger.

## Raising the next generation: the share of reproduction nobody could privatise in theory

Among the household's tasks, one dwarfs the rest in duration and consequence, and the debate handled it with a curious mixture of centrality and evasion: the making of new people. Children are, from the standpoint of an economy, the future workforce in its most literal form, years of feeding, teaching, nursing, and civilising away from any employer's gate. The pamphlet tradition spoke accordingly of producing the commodity of commodities. Yet even writers comfortable with production language elsewhere balked at the nursery, sensing that a child raised is not an output and that the mother's relation to the growing person overflows every category of manufacture. The evasion had a practical dimension as well. Societies had already collectivised large slices of childhood before the debate began: schooling removed children from homes for much of the day under public provision, health services socialised portions of their maintenance, and the boundary between the household's share and the state's share of raising a generation was a live political frontier throughout the century, moved by wars, by women's employment, and by demographic anxiety. A theory of reproduction that treated the household as the sole site therefore misdescribed its object from the start, and the participants who insisted on the household's embeddedness in public systems, schools, clinics, benefits, housing policy, were widening the frame in the direction the successor literature eventually took as its default.

The frontier's movement supplies some of the cleanest evidence for the article's governing distinction. When a state extends schooling, funds nurseries, or pays child benefits, it alters the division of reproductive labour between home, market, and public provision, and it does so through budgets and statutes that any citizen can inspect. Nothing in the value categories predicts or forbids any particular division; the categories register the consequences, in the wage level, in women's employment patterns, in the cost of the social wage, after politics has chosen. Fertility itself, the decision or drift by which households produce fewer children across the wealthy world during the very decades under discussion, sits in the same register: an outcome of housing costs, of women's education and earnings, of contraception's availability, of the sheer arithmetic by which the unwaged years a child requires grew harder to fit inside two earner lives. Demographers narrate that transition without needing a single value category, while the feminists of the debate supplied the question the demographers' tables silently pose: who performs the remaining work of the smaller family's children, under what support, at what cost to her own life course? The question, once again, is the remainder wearing demographic dress, and the debate deserves credit for having formulated it before the statistics made it unavoidable.

There is also the matter of quality, a word the production metaphor handles badly. Reproduction in the household is not the manufacture of interchangeable units of a capacity; it is the formation of particular persons, with languages, expectations, wounds, and ambitions that they carry into every workplace and polity they later enter. Households transmit class in this deep sense, accents, confidences, senses of entitlement or deference, knowledge of how institutions work and whether they can be trusted, and the transmission is labour, performed in conversation and example as much as in meals and laundry. Sociologists of education built an adjacent literature on this transmission, on cultural inheritance and its conversion into credentials, largely without reference to the Marxist argument proceeding in parallel. The two literatures describe one process from two sides, the formation of the worker and the formation of the person being the same years of a life, and their separation is itself an artefact of disciplinary boundaries the debate's generation was trying, in its own vocabulary, to cross. A reader synthesising them finds the household restored to its true scale: not a annex of the economy nor a refuge from it, but the site where a society decides, mostly without deciding, what kinds of people it will consist of.

## The school day, the working week, and the architecture of other people's time

Institutions reveal their assumptions about the household in their timetables. The school day ends in the middle of the afternoon, the school year opens and closes around holidays far longer than any employment's leave, the clinic and the benefits office keep hours that presume a person free at midday, and the whole lattice silently presupposes an adult whose time is unsold and available, the very figure whose existence the official statistics were busily recording as exceptional. The debaters noticed the lattice because they lived inside it, and their analyses of the housewife's function gain concreteness when read as commentary on a built environment of schedules. A society that runs its services on the assumption of unpaid availability has made a fiscal decision in the grammar of the timetable, and the decision's beneficiaries include every employer whose workers arrive unencumbered and every treasury whose services cost less than their sticker price. Making that decision visible, repricing it, and contesting it became, in the decades after the argument closed, a standard move of feminist policy analysis: the care audit, the gender budget, the impact assessment that asks whose unwaged hours a proposal spends. Each technique descends from the sensibility the earlier literature formed, the refusal to treat the household's time as a free good entering public calculations at zero.

The working week tells the complementary story from the employer's side. The standard full time job was designed, historically, for a worker with a wife: its hours presume that somebody else absorbs the errands, the illnesses, the deliveries, the school gates, and its career ladders punish the interruptions that care imposes, which is why campaigns for shorter hours for everyone, rather than flexibility for mothers alone, became the labour movement feminists' characteristic demand in the years the debate wound down. The demand's logic is remainder logic throughout, institutional, directed at the architecture of time rather than at any category, and its partial victories and defeats since belong to political history. What the earlier argument contributed was the analysis that made the demand intelligible as economics rather than as lifestyle preference: if the wage system rests on an unwaged support system, then the length of the waged day is partly a decision about how much of life the support system must absorb, and shortening the day redistributes not leisure in the abstract but the material possibility of sharing the support work itself. Few of the debaters stated the chain that completely. All of its links are present in their texts, waiting for the reader this article has tried to train: one who can follow a category to its boundary and then keep walking, with other instruments, into the territory where the question that mattered most was standing all along.

## Echoes in unlikely places: demography, management, and the price of care

A test of a literature's reach is whether its distinctions surface, unattributed, in conversations that never cite it. By that test the domestic argument travelled far. Demographers explaining the postponement of childbearing across wealthy societies reach, whatever their politics, for the opportunity cost of women's time, a formulation that treats the unwaged years of care as an economic quantity with alternatives forgone, which is the founding generation's insistence translated into another discipline's grammar. Management literature discovered the two person career and the trailing spouse, acknowledging in the language of executive retention that certain jobs are performable only by an employee whose household labour is supplied by somebody else, the concealment argument from the wage section reappearing as a human resources problem. Health economists costing informal care, the looking after of elderly and disabled people by relatives, learned to impute values to hours nobody purchased, and their imputations, billions in aggregate on any national accounting, rest on the premise the journals fought to establish in the face of the official accounts: the work is real production of welfare whether or not a market registers it. Even the national accountants, slowest of audiences, eventually built household satellite accounts alongside the official product measures, a bureaucratic monument to a proposition the movement's pamphleteers had asserted with more passion and less arithmetic two generations earlier.

Notice what travelled and what stayed home. The proposition that the work is vast, necessary, and economically consequential conquered every neighbouring field. The narrower proposition about value in a specific theory's sense remained where it belonged, inside that theory's house, of interest to its inhabitants and to historians of their arguments. The division of the inheritance confirms the construction this article has defended from its opening pages. A broad truth, established by the founding texts and carried by the campaign, was always available and has been universally received. A technical truth, established by the middle texts, governs a smaller jurisdiction with complete authority. And an open question, named by the closing retrospective, continues to organise research and politics wherever households divide their labour. Confusion enters only when the three are collapsed, when the broad truth is expected to do the technical truth's work, or the technical truth is blamed for the open question's persistence. Reference writing exists to prevent collapses of exactly that kind, and the present article, at the length its subject's traps require, has attempted nothing more exotic than keeping three true things distinct for the reader who will meet them entangled in every summary the culture supplies.

## What a reader should carry away

Carry away, first, a sequence, since the sequence is the argument: a founding article that grounded women's position in household production, a pamphlet that claimed the work yielded capitalism's central magnitude and built a campaign on the claim, a rigorous restatement that forced the issue, a pair of critiques from politics and from the theory's internal logic, a formal resolution that separated necessity from production, and a retrospective that judged the whole by its founding purpose and found the purpose unmet. Carry away, second, the resolution's content in its exact form, because its exactness is what half a century of summary has eroded: the work creates no value and no surplus in the senses the theory defines, and it sustains the workforce, cheapens its maintenance, absorbs the shocks of downturns, and forms the next generation of persons, all at once, without contradiction. Carry away, third, the discipline of the remainder: when a powerful method settles the question it can process, ask after the question it was hosting, and expect to find the politics there. The domestic argument's participants, at their best, modelled that discipline on themselves, in print, at the height of their investment in the method being limited. Modelling of that kind is rarer than brilliance and more useful.

There remains the human residue, which reference prose should not pretend away. Behind the categories stand the evenings the statistics abbreviate: the second shift begun in tiredness, the care given inside love and resentment simultaneously, the meals whose production nobody counted because counting was never the point of them. The women and men who argued over the categories in the nineteen seventies knew those evenings from inside, which is why the argument's heat was never really about definitions, and why its resolution, however correct, could satisfy nobody completely. A theory that classifies the evening as outside production has described the evening's economic form and missed its weight, and the participants felt the miss as a personal reproach long before they could articulate it as the remainder. The successor fields exist to carry the weight the categories set down. This article ends its own account where the debate ended its, with the work still being done, mostly by the same sex, under arrangements that budgets and statutes and customs keep choosing, and with the instruments for changing those arrangements lying, as they always did, in the open territory beyond the settlement's fence.

## The inheritance the founders worked from: Bebel, Zetkin, and the older socialist promise

The debaters of the nineteen seventies did not invent their tradition's concern with housework; they inherited a promise and found it unkept. August Bebel's book on women and socialism, the nineteenth century's most widely read socialist treatment of the woman question, had already identified the private household as an archaic economic unit destined for replacement by socialised provision, and Clara Zetkin's organising among working women had insisted that emancipation required more than suffrage. Lenin, in the revolution's first years, described petty housework in withering terms, as labour that crushes and stultifies, and the young Soviet state briefly legislated as if it agreed, promoting communal dining and laundries while legal codes attacked the old family's authority. The promise, in its classic form, ran: capitalism draws women into social production, socialism socialises the domestic sphere, and between the two the housewife vanishes into history. What the postwar decades taught the founding generation was that the sequence had stalled at its first step and reversed at its second in the states claiming to enact it. Women had entered employment in millions; the kitchen had not withered; the double load had become the ordinary female biography in both systems. The debate can be read, at this depth, as the tradition auditing its own oldest promise about women and finding the audit impossible to complete with the concepts the promise had used, emancipation, socialisation, entry into production, because none of them specified the mechanism by which the work at home was assigned or the economic character of that work. Benston's article is the audit's opening entry, which is why its tone differs from mere complaint: she writes as a creditor of the tradition, presenting a bill a century old.

Reading the founders against the inheritance also clarifies a choice they made collectively and rarely defended in so many words: to conduct the audit inside the master's categories rather than discard them. A different generation might have concluded that a theory silent about the kitchen should be replaced, not extended, and some feminists outside the Marxist frame did conclude exactly that, building accounts from patriarchy as a freestanding system or from the phenomenology of women's experience. The debaters stayed, partly from conviction that the categories explained the factory as nothing else could, partly from the movement logic that their audiences and organisations lived inside the tradition being audited. Staying had the consequence this article has tracked throughout: the audit could only ask the questions the categories formulate, and its most famous product is a boundary, the place where the categories stop. But staying also produced the audit's authority. A verdict on housework's place in value theory delivered by the theory's own practitioners, against their own movement's preferred claim, carries a weight no external critique could match, and the tradition's subsequent confidence in the verdict, its treatment as bedrock by successor schools that agree on little else, rests on that provenance. Debates are remembered for their conclusions; they persuade by their personnel.

## Across the colour line: the critique that rebuilt the frame

Angela Davis published Women, Race and Class in 1981, two years after the retrospective closed the decade, and its chapter on housework reads as the debate's most consequential external audit. Davis wrote from a history the canonical texts had bracketed: the enslaved woman in the American South performed domestic labour that was neither private nor unwaged in the housewife's sense, since her work in the master's house and her work in the quarters were both coerced by property in her person, and her own household's labour was an act of survival and resistance conducted in stolen hours. A theory whose subject was the wife maintained by a wage could not describe her, and Davis drew the conclusion the decade's critics had approached from several directions: the movement's housewife was a classed and raced figure, and a politics of housework that began from her would mislead the women whose foremothers were never permitted to be only housewives. Her positive argument reached back past the debate to the socialisation tradition, proposing that the work's emancipation lay in its industrial and public reorganisation under transformed social relations, and she treated the wage demand for unwaged wives with scepticism, as a reform that would leave the underlying assignment untouched while ratifying it in cash.

The decades after Davis widened the aperture she opened. Evelyn Nakano Glenn's research on the racial division of paid care and cleaning work showed the two spheres of the old debate, the unwaged wife and the waged worker, connected by a third figure the canonical texts had barely seen: the woman, disproportionately a woman of colour or a migrant, paid low wages to perform in other people's homes the labour the housewife performed in her own, so that one woman's release from the work was purchased with another's assignment to it. The finding did not disturb the settlement, whose claims concern forms, and Glenn's cleaner, when employed by an agency, is productive labour in the strict sense while the wife she relieves is not, a classification the earlier sections worked through. What the finding disturbed was the debate's social picture, and through it every politics that had imagined the household as a self contained unit with a single woman's labour inside. The frame the successor fields built in response, in which reproduction is analysed as a stratified system distributing care work among women by class, race, and citizenship as well as between women and men, is unthinkable without the external audit. A debate's boundaries, this article has argued throughout, are among its products. The colour line was the boundary the domestic argument discovered last and the successor literature has treated as foundational, an ordering of discoveries that itself belongs in any honest ledger of the episode.

### How did Davis's intervention change the debate's legacy?

She relocated its centre by showing that the figure it theorised, the unwaged wife in a wage supported home, described one historically specific arrangement rather than women's universal condition, and that enslaved and colonised women's domestic histories required the frame to be rebuilt around race and class together.

Her intervention also demonstrates a pattern in how closed debates continue to matter. The settlement stayed shut; nobody needed to reopen the category question to absorb her criticism, because her criticism governed the frame rather than the classification. What changed was the population over which the settled propositions were applied and the company they kept in argument: after Davis, a treatment of reproduction that begins and ends with the white metropolitan household reads as a period piece, while the propositions themselves, necessity without production, subsidy without surplus, dependence without value, apply to the migrant cleaner's household and the enslaved woman's quarters with undiminished precision, indeed with greater moral clarity, since the subsidy those households supplied was extracted under coercions the wage supported home only echoes. A classification that survives the widening of its frame has passed the strongest test available to it. The debate's result passed, and the passing is the deepest reason the scholastic verdict, however partly deserved on the politics, fails as an account of the episode's intellectual worth.

## The accountant's version of the quarrel: Waring and the national product

A parallel insurgency, fought with ledgers rather than categories, confirms the episode's reach from an unexpected flank. Marilyn Waring, a New Zealand parliamentarian turned scholar, published If Women Counted in 1988, an indictment of the system of national accounts by which governments measure their economies. Her charge was simple to state and devastating to document: the accounting rules, devised in the middle of the century, count a meal as economic activity when a restaurant sells it and as nothing when a spouse prepares the identical dish, count childcare when purchased and erase it when a parent supplies the hours, and thereby build the invisibility of the household into the headline number by which nations judge their success. Waring's targets differed from the debaters' targets; she wanted the accounts reformed, not a category of Marx's adjudicated, and her framework owed more to a parliamentarian's impatience with official arithmetic than to any reading of Capital. Yet the kinship is unmistakable. Both literatures begin from the scandal of a necessity that registers as zero. Both insist the zero is a convention with beneficiaries, since a state can shrink services, lean on families, and watch its measured product hold steady while unmeasured effort swells to fill the space. Both end up assigning the statistician a political role nobody elected them to.

The accounting insurgency even reproduced, in miniature, the older argument's internal split. One party wanted the household's output valued in money and folded into an expanded product measure, achieving recognition by commensuration. Another party warned that pricing care by imputation repeats the original offence, subjecting relations of love and obligation to a metric that distorts whatever it touches, and preferred separate accounts that display the hours in their own unit, time, alongside the monetary aggregates. The resemblance to the split between those who wished the work enrolled among value's producers and those who defended the boundary while honouring the work will not be lost on readers of the preceding sections. Two fields, starting from opposite ends of the problem, one from a revolutionary theory's concepts and one from a finance ministry's spreadsheets, arrived at a structurally identical dilemma: recognition seems to require the dominant measure, and the dominant measure seems to falsify the thing recognised. The settlement's way through the dilemma, specify the mechanism and refuse the enrolment, has its bookkeeping analogue in the supplementary accounts that statistical offices eventually adopted, publishing the household's hours beside the product figures rather than inside them. Adjacent, visible, and unmerged: the architecture of the compromise is the same in both houses.

## Leave, quotas, and the engineering of sharing

If the assignment question belongs to institutions, its most instructive laboratory is the design of parental leave, where states discovered that rules could move men's behaviour in ways exhortation never had. Sweden replaced maternity leave with a gender neutral parental insurance in 1974, the first scheme of its kind, expecting fathers to claim a share; for years they claimed very little, since a benefit a couple may allocate freely will be allocated by the same arithmetic and custom that allocate everything else in the household, her lower earnings and her presumed competence deciding the matter in advance. The Nordic response, adopted in the nineteen nineties, reserved portions of the leave for each parent on a use it or lose it basis, and fathers' uptake climbed steeply wherever the reservation applied. The episode is a controlled demonstration of the remainder's logic, conducted by accident on whole populations. Nothing about the work changed; nothing about its categories changed; a rule altered the default, the default altered the arithmetic of households, and the arithmetic altered, measurably, who rose in the night. Writers on the older controversy cite the leave reforms when they need a compact proof that the distribution they left unexplained was never a fact of nature, since a parliament could redistribute a portion of it by amending a schedule.

The laboratory also displays the limits of engineering, which the same writers are quick to add. Reserved months shift the care of infants more readily than the management of households; the planning, anticipating, and remembering that constitute the mental side of the enterprise migrate more slowly than the hours, and several studies find mothers retaining the organiser's role even in couples whose leave was shared with unusual equality. Occupations sort the outcome further, since workplaces differ in whether a father's absence reads as commitment to family or defection from seriousness, and public sector fathers have historically found the reading kinder than private sector ones. None of this diminishes the demonstration. It refines its teaching into the shape this article has given the whole subject: layers of mechanism, each tractable to a different instrument, with the deepest layers, custom and identity, yielding last and slowest. A reader who began this article wondering how an argument about an economic category could matter to a nursery's rota can now trace the connection entire, from a journal exchange about the form of value, through a retrospective that redirected a field toward institutions, to a schedule in a statute book deciding whose alarm rings at three in the morning. The chain is long, and every link in it was somebody's correction of the link before.

## Taylorism in the kitchen: the efficiency experts who arrived first

Long before the journals took up the household, another set of experts had claimed it, and their forgotten priority casts an ironic light on the later argument. In the nineteen tens and twenties, disciples of scientific management crossed the threshold of the home promising to subject its routines to the stopwatch and the motion study. Christine Frederick applied the gospel of efficiency to cooking and cleaning in bestselling manuals, redesigning kitchens around the sequence of tasks, while Lillian Gilbreth, an industrial engineer of genuine distinction, brought the Gilbreth motion economy methods to the layout of stoves, sinks, and work surfaces. Their project shared a premise with the feminists who came fifty years later: the labour performed in the home was real labour, susceptible of analysis, and scandalously wasted by tradition. It differed in its allegiance. The efficiency experts served the housewife as a manager to be trained, within an arrangement they never questioned; their ideal was the same work, done faster, by the same woman, in a kitchen arranged like a well run workshop. When the Marxist generation rediscovered the household as an object, it inherited rooms the Taylorites had already measured and, in the main, ignored their literature, a reminder that a question's history depends on who is remembered as having asked it. The settlement's generation asked after the work's place in an economic system. The earlier generation had asked after its motions. Between the two questions lies the whole difference between a technique and a theory, and the household, patient object of both, went on being cleaned.

The episode rewards a moment's reflection on standards, the hidden governor of the home's workload. Efficiency in a factory is disciplined by a market test; efficiency in a kitchen answers to norms of cleanliness, nutrition, and respectability that float upward with every labour saving device and every advertisement. The home economists knew the phenomenon without naming it as later scholars would: a floor that can be polished in half the time does not rest at its former shine but is polished twice as often, so the saving is consumed by the standard it made affordable. This ratchet explains a paradox that runs beneath the entire controversy, the persistence of the workload across a century of mechanisation, and it identifies a mechanism, socially enforced standards, that operates entirely within the remainder, invisible to any classification of the labour's form. Whoever sets the standards governs the hours: advertisers, neighbours, health visitors, mothers in law, and eventually the internalised inspector every homemaker carries. A politics of the household that confines itself to wages or services and never addresses the ratchet will watch its victories absorbed by rising expectations, which is one reason the successor literature learned to treat culture and ideology as material forces in their own right rather than as decoration upon an economic base. The kitchen's history thereby teaches the same lesson as the seminar room's: the decisive mechanisms are plural, and an analysis armed with one of them will be perpetually surprised by the others.

## Evidence from the diaries: what the record of ordinary days adds

One archive the theorists seldom cited, though it lay in print, deserves a section in any full account, because it preserves the phenomenon before anyone theorised it. From the late nineteen thirties, the Mass Observation project enlisted ordinary Britons as diarists of their own routines, and its most celebrated contributor, the Barrow in Furness homemaker Nella Last, recorded the texture of an unwaged working life across the war years with a concreteness no journal article attempted: the budgeting of rations, the queueing, the mending economies, the emotional weather of a household held together by one woman's vigilance. Her pages show a mind performing, unassisted, much of the analysis the later literature formalised, counting costs nobody reimbursed, apportioning scarcity, registering the difference between labour that is noticed and labour whose success consists in nothing going wrong. Advice columns and letter pages in women's magazines form a companion archive, where homemakers compared techniques, complained with precision about the workload's invisibility, and occasionally demanded, in plain language, the recognition the movement would later demand in theoretical language. A history of the subject written from these sources would not mention value categories at all, and would not need to; it would establish the burden, the ingenuity, and the grievance across decades in which the official record saw a dependent at leisure.

The diaries discipline the theory in one specific way worth stating before the personas take over. They show the work's demands fluctuating with circumstance, a sick child, a husband's unemployment, a war, in patterns that mock any fixed quantum of necessary domestic effort, and they show the worker's own judgements about her situation varying independently of her burden's size, shaped by gratitude, resentment, faith, ambition for her children, and the presence or absence of respect. No classification captures that variance, and none was meant to. What the classification settled was a question about an economic form. What the diaries preserve is everything the form leaves out, which turns out to be most of what the work meant to the people performing it. Keeping the two records side by side, the ledger and the diary, is the reading posture this article recommends in closing its evidence: the first tells you what the work is in the system's accounting, the second what the work was in a life, and a reader who consults only one will misunderstand the subject in opposite directions, as a mechanism without a face or as a face without a mechanism.

Oral history projects added voices to that archive once recording equipment grew cheap, and their transcripts hold a surprise for readers arriving from the journals. Asked about their lives, retired homemakers seldom organise a narrative around oppression or liberation; they speak of competence, of pride in a well kept house on a small wage, of neighbours and kin, of the war's camaraderie and the peace's loneliness, and their evaluations of the arrangement they lived inside range across every position the theorists occupied, sometimes within a single interview. The surprise is salutary. It warns against conscripting the dead and the elderly as extras in an argument they never joined, and it explains why the movement's own organisers learned to begin from listening rather than from categories when they knocked on doors. Theory that cannot survive an afternoon with Nella Last's diary, or with a recorded grandmother, has mistaken its jurisdiction; theory that can, that knows precisely which of its sentences the diary confirms and which it ignores as none of the diary's business, has earned the authority the settlement claims. That composure, the ability to sit beside the evidence of lived days without either capitulating to it or overruling it, is the final intellectual virtue the episode teaches, and the one its scholastic caricature most completely misses.

Preservation itself has become a quiet battleground in the years since, as local archives solicit homemakers' papers, recipe notebooks annotated with prices, household account books kept in pencil across decades, and the shoeboxes of clippings that once passed for a family's reference shelf. Each accession rescues evidence of decisions, substitutions, and small triumphs that no aggregate will ever disclose, and each reminds the researcher that the unwaged economy ran on information systems of its own, handwritten, memorised, exchanged across fences and telephone calls, long before any scholar arrived to ask whether its outputs belonged inside a theory of value. The account books in particular, with their weekly columns balancing a wage against rent, coal, insurance, and the children's boots, are the subsidy mechanism rendered in a housewife's own arithmetic, and they deserve citation beside the journals whenever the episode is taught.

## For students and exam candidates

Hold the debate as a two part answer and you hold its examinable core. Part one is the technical settlement, statable in a paragraph: the prevailing position, argued into dominance by Himmelweit and Mohun in 1977, holds that domestic labour produces no value or surplus value in Marx's technical sense, because value arises only where labour produces commodities for exchange, while the same labour remains necessary to the reproduction of labour power and lowers its value through the subsidy mechanism. Part two is the political remainder: the settlement does not explain why women perform the work, and Molyneux's 1979 retrospective in New Left Review, the assessment to cite by name and venue, charged the debate with leaving that question untouched. Answers that give both parts, attribute the objection to its author, and name the positions in the ledger in sequence will outscore answers that report a winner. Transfer the positions ledger into your notes as a study card, using the [VaultBook study notes prepared for this series](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), and continue to the successor theory in the article on social reproduction theory for the question examiners increasingly pair with this one.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The debate is the most teachable controversy in this cluster because its archive is short, sequenced, and closed: six or seven texts take a seminar from Benston's founding simplification through the pamphlet's maximalism and Seccombe's rigorous version to the 1975 political critique, the 1977 formal settlement, and Molyneux's retrospective, which lets students watch a literature correct itself within a decade. Teach the distinction between the two senses of value, everyday and technical, in the first session and police it kindly throughout, since every standard misreading flows from letting them merge. Set the mockery and the defence as paired readings rather than as a verdict to announce, and let the class discover that both are partly right, the scholasticism charge partly deserved and the settlement real. The resolution then serves your students as the case study in how a theoretical debate concludes: not by exhaustion alone, but by a formal argument that moves the burden of proof, followed by a retrospective that redirects the field toward the questions the resolved argument could not process.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Treat the debate as a closed archive with an open historiography. The primary sequence is recoverable in full from the journals named in the positions ledger, Monthly Review, New Left Review, the socialist economists' Bulletin, and the Cambridge Journal of Economics, supplemented by the pamphlet literature and Ellen Malos's anthology, and the verification standard for any claim about the episode is a named text with its year and venue. Attribute the retrospective assessment to Maxine Molyneux in New Left Review in 1979 rather than to a general mood, and attribute the settlement's canonical statement to Himmelweit and Mohun rather than to the field in general. For the tradition framing the episode, the series pillar on the [marxist feminism complete guide](/2016/09/01/marxist-feminism-complete-guide/) supplies the longer arc. Journalists summarising the episode for general readers should preserve the two part structure, technical settlement and political remainder, since collapsing it in either direction, triumph or farce, reproduces the errors this article has catalogued.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the domestic labour debate?

It was an argument conducted mainly in Marxist journals during the nineteen seventies about the economic character of unpaid household work. Participants asked whether cooking, cleaning, and raising children inside the home produce value or surplus value within Marx's categories, and what follows for understanding women's subordination. Beginning with an article in 1969 that treated women as a group defined by household production, the exchange ran through pamphlets, critiques, and a formal resolution before a retrospective assessment closed the decade by questioning the whole enterprise's political yield. Its subject was housework, but its stakes were the reach of value theory and the foundations of a feminist politics.

### Q: Does housework produce surplus value?

On the answer that came to dominate the exchange, it does not. Surplus value, in the vocabulary under dispute, is unpaid value created by wage labourers producing commodities for a capitalist. Household work is performed outside that relation: nothing it yields is sold, and no employer appropriates a product from it. The work can still benefit employers in aggregate, by keeping down the cost of maintaining the workforce, and it can still be appropriated inside the family. Neither benefit converts it into surplus production. The distinction between benefiting from labour and extracting surplus value from it is the precision the debate was fought to obtain.

### Q: Who took part in the domestic labour debate?

The central names form a sequence. Margaret Benston opened the exchange in Monthly Review in 1969. Mariarosa Dalla Costa, writing with Selma James, carried the maximal claim into their 1972 pamphlet. Wally Seccombe gave that claim its most rigorous defence in New Left Review in 1974. Margaret Coulson, Branka Magas, and Hilary Wainwright answered him there in 1975, while Jean Gardiner, Susan Himmelweit, and Maureen Mackintosh developed an alternative account in a socialist economists' bulletin. Himmelweit, with Simon Mohun, stated the prevailing resolution in 1977. Christine Delphy argued a separate French track, and Maxine Molyneux assessed the whole in 1979. Around them stood a wide penumbra of respondents, campaigners, and critics.

### Q: How was the domestic labour debate resolved?

It was resolved by an argument about the form of value rather than by a vote or a truce. Himmelweit and Mohun reasoned in 1977 that value exists only where private labours are made commensurable through exchange, and that household labour, performed for direct use and never sold, cannot meet that condition. After their article, contributions increasingly treated the negative answer as the starting point and disputed consequences instead, which is how a literature signals that the burden of proof has moved. The resolution was reinforced by a sympathetic retrospective in 1979 that accepted the technical result while indicting the debate's priorities, allowing the field to close the question and redirect its energies in a single gesture.

### Q: Why is the debate often mocked?

It is mocked for disproportion and abstraction: dozens of articles, over years, on whether a category defined for commodity production covers work outside it, while the distribution of the work itself went unexamined. The mockery fastens on real features, the textual method, the stylised housewife at the centre, the conclusion some say was implicit in the definitions from the start. This article presents the ridicule at full strength and answers it in kind: a live campaign rested on the contested claim, the answer was not obvious before the work was done, and the successor field was built from the results. The charge of scholasticism is widely made, partly deserved, and not the whole story.

### Q: What did the debate actually settle?

It settled a small set of connected propositions. Unpaid household labour does not create value or surplus value in the technical sense. It is nonetheless indispensable to reproducing the workforce. Its unwaged performance cheapens that reproduction, lowering what employers must pay in wages across an economy. And the two groups of propositions, the negative and the positive, are compatible rather than contradictory. Just as important is what the settling excluded: no conclusion was reached about why women rather than men perform the labour, or about which remedy, wages, socialised services, or equal sharing, a movement should demand. Those questions were handed on, named as the remainder.

### Q: Is domestic labour productive or unproductive labour?

The honest answer is that the pair classifies it badly, which the debate itself discovered. Productive labour, technically, is labour exchanged against capital that yields surplus value; unproductive labour is labour exchanged against revenue. A housewife's work is exchanged against neither, being performed inside a family relation for maintenance rather than a wage. Some writers therefore called it unproductive as the nearest available box, while others insisted it sits outside the classification entirely and should be described by its function, reproducing labour power, instead of by a label whose everyday meaning insults the work it names. The functional description is the one later literature standardised.

### Q: How does the debate relate to social reproduction theory?

As foundation to building. The later theory takes over the debate's conclusions as premises: unwaged household work creates no value in the technical sense, it reproduces the capacity to labour and life itself, and capitalism depends on that unwaged sphere while concealing the dependence behind the individual wage. Freed from rearguing the category question, social reproduction theory asks how a system organised around value production organises and subordinates the life making activity outside it, especially in periods of crisis. The relationship is developed in the dedicated article on [social reproduction theory explained](/2016/09/15/social-reproduction-theory-explained/), which begins where this debate ended.

### Q: What determines the value of labour power in this debate?

On Marx's account, which the settlement retained, it is set by the labour time embodied in the means of subsistence a worker's household must consume to maintain the worker and raise replacements, at a historically given standard of life. Groceries, rent, clothing, fuel: the purchased basket carries value into the home. Work performed on that basket inside the home changes how much life the basket sustains, not the value it embodies. This asymmetry explains housework's economic route to significance in the debate's final position: by stretching purchased goods further, unpaid labour permits a given standard of life at a lower wage, without the hours in the kitchen entering the magnitude itself.

### Q: Was the debate a dead end?

The verdict of this article is no, with a qualification the record supports. As a route to explaining why women perform the work, it led nowhere, and its own retrospective critic said so in 1979 with evidence. As a foundation, it proved fertile: it falsified a claim on which a major campaign rested, fixed the concepts the successor theory uses, trained a generation in distinguishing a system's dependence on unwaged work from that work's production of value, and produced, in its self assessment, a model of how a field corrects course. A dead end terminates a road. This episode terminated one argument and opened several others, which is a different shape entirely.

### Q: How does the political remainder survive the technical settlement?

By changing address rather than by disappearing. Once the category question closed, the unexplained distribution of the work stood revealed as a problem about institutions and interests: wage differentials that push households toward specialisation, states that design dependence into tax and benefit rules, customs that moralise mothers' personal care, and men who decline an equal share. Each mechanism invites its own kind of inquiry, historical, sociological, legal, and its own kind of politics. The remainder survives, then, as a research programme and a set of campaigns, no longer disguised as a puzzle about value. Naming it precisely, as something the settlement leaves behind, is what prevents the settlement from being mistaken for a solution to it.

### Q: What should a student separate between the debate's technical question and its political one?

Separate the claims each question can support. The technical question, where household labour sits among Marx's value categories, supports conclusions about classification, about what may and may not be asserted of housework inside that theory. The political question, why the labour is assigned to women and how the assignment could change, supports conclusions about mechanisms and remedies, and it is answered by history and institutional analysis, not by classification. Examination answers lose marks when a classification is offered as if it were an explanation, or when an explanation is faulted for failing as a classification. Keep the two operations distinct, state which one each paragraph performs, and the whole episode becomes legible.

### Q: Why did a question about housework divide the movement?

Because more than classification rode on the answer. One wing had built an international campaign on the assertion that housework yields surplus value, treating the wage demand as the lever that would expose the work and free its performers from dependence on individual men. Another wing feared that the assertion, if false, would discredit the movement when opponents exposed it, and that the wage demand itself would entrench women in the home it claimed to undermine. Beneath the strategy lay identity: to be told by fellow socialists that the work filling your days created no value sounded, however technical the intent, like the old dismissal in new dress. The division was therefore simultaneously theoretical, strategic, and personal, which is why it ran so hot for so long.

### Q: What separates necessary labour in the household from value production?

The boundary is exchange. Labour becomes value producing, in the framework under discussion, when its product is made for sale and the labour is thereby rendered abstract and measurable against all other labour in a market. Household labour meets none of those conditions: its products are consumed where they are made, its hours are set by need rather than competition, and no process averages one family's effort against another's. Its necessity is of a different order, belonging to the reproduction of people and of the capacity to work. The separation is not a ranking. A process can stand among the preconditions of an economy, indispensable every day, while producing none of the value whose circulation the economy's theory describes.
