The most consequential disability rights sentence in American law arrived without a hearing, without extended debate, and almost without anyone in Congress noticing it had been added. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 provides that no otherwise qualified individual with a disability shall, solely by reason of disability, be excluded from participation in, denied the benefits of, or subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving federal financial assistance. The sentence was copied nearly word for word from the race provision of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the sex provision of the education amendments of 1972, with the protected characteristic swapped out. That copying is the single most important fact for understanding how the provision works, because the enforcement machinery of the older statutes traveled with their sentence structure, and the courts have read the copied sentence as carrying the interpretive freight of its parents.

![The 1977 protests and regulations that gave Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act its operative meaning - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The enacted text was nearly nothing by itself. For four years the section sat in the statute books with no implementing regulations, which meant no definitions, no compliance obligations spelled out, no enforcement machinery engaged, and no way for a program administrator to know what obedience required. What changed that was not a congressional amendment but a sustained campaign of protest in the spring of 1977, when demonstrators occupied offices of the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare in cities across the country and held the department's San Francisco regional office for twenty-five days, from April 5 to April 30. The department's secretary signed the final regulations on April 28, 1977, and they were published in the Federal Register on May 4. This article follows the one sentence from its drafting through its four-year limbo, its protest-forged implementation, its companion sections, its remedies and immunity rules, the Supreme Court cases that gave it shape, and the reason the later comprehensive disability statute did not replace it.

## The Sentence That Had No Hearing

### Why did the operative sentence carry almost no legislative history?

Section 504 was added to the rehabilitation bill by congressional staff without extended floor debate or committee hearings devoted to it, and the staffers who worked on the measure later could not recall precisely where the provision originated, leaving the courts almost no legislative history to construe.

The CRS report RL34041 on the section states that there was very little discussion of its meaning or importance during its 1973 enactment, and the most detailed treatment was a floor observation by Senator Humphrey in 1972.

That absence of legislative history is not a gap in the record; it is the record, and it has shaped every fight over what the sentence means. Courts construing the provision have had almost nothing from Congress to guide them, so they have built its meaning from the text itself, from the regulations, from its older statutory parents, and from general principles about how federal funding conditions work. The brief record does exist, but it amounts to scattered remarks rather than the committee reports, hearing testimony, and negotiated explanations that usually accompany a major civil rights provision.

The formal identity of the law is straightforward and worth stating once in full. The Rehabilitation Act of 1973, Public Law 93-112, the 112th law enacted by the 93rd Congress, was signed by President Richard Nixon on September 26, 1973, and published at 87 Stat. 355 in the Statutes at Large. Section 504 of that statute is codified at 29 U.S.C. 794, where the current section heading reads "Nondiscrimination under Federal grants and programs; promulgation of rules and regulations." The public law number itself tells the reader how to find the enacted text: Congress number followed by the sequential number of the law within that Congress, so 93-112 is the 112th enactment of the 93rd Congress.

The operative sentence as enacted in 1973 did not use the vocabulary that later generations of readers associate with it. The original text read: "No otherwise qualified handicapped individual in the United States, as defined in section 7(6), shall, solely by reason of his handicap, be excluded from the participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance." That is the version quoted by the Supreme Court when it traced the provision's history. The modern formulation, reading "individual with a disability" and "her or his disability," arrived through later amendments. The Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1992, Public Law 102-569, changed "handicapped person" terminology to "individual with a disability," and technical amendments in 1988, Public Law 100-630, introduced the gender neutral "her or his" phrasing. The definition itself had already grown in 1974, when amendments added the "record of" and "regarded as" prongs to the definition of handicapped individual, giving the statute three ways to establish coverage: an actual impairment, a documented history of one, or being treated by others as having one. The 1978 amendments, Public Law 95-602, extended the reach of the section beyond federally assisted programs to programs conducted by executive agencies and by the United States Postal Service, and added a remedies provision at section 505.

The parentage is exact and documented. The operative sentence of section 504 was modeled on the language of section 601 of Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, codified at 42 U.S.C. 2000d, which bars discrimination on the ground of race, color, or national origin in federally assisted programs. Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972, codified at 20 U.S.C. 1681(a), was itself patterned on Title VI, barring sex discrimination in federally assisted education programs, as traced in this series' account of [Title IX](/2014/10/15/title-ix-1972-explained/). The three sentences share a skeleton: "No person in the United States shall, on the ground of [characteristic], be excluded from participation in, be denied the benefits of, or be subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance." Section 504 kept the skeleton, changed the characteristic, and added the qualifier "otherwise qualified," a two word addition that would generate decades of litigation about what qualification means for people with disabilities. Senator Humphrey's floor remarks in September 1972, at 118 Congressional Record 32310, tied the provision to his earlier bills that would have amended Titles VI and VII, supplying one of the few explicit statements of legislative purpose in the thin record.

The copying matters because the enforcement architecture was copied along with the words. Title VI had produced an administrative regime: federal agencies were to write regulations, recipients of funds were to give assurances of compliance, agencies could investigate complaints and cut off funds after a hearing, and a private right of action had been recognized by the courts. When section 504 took over the Title VI sentence, it inherited the expectation that an agency would write rules, that fund termination would be the sanction, and that private plaintiffs could sue. It also inherited the ambiguity about how far the copied sentence reached, because Title VI itself had never been precise about whether it reached only intentional discrimination or also neutral practices with unequal effects. That ambiguity traveled into the disability context and would be resolved, partially, by the Supreme Court in Alexander v. Choate in 1985, more than a decade after enactment.

The phrase "otherwise qualified" deserves attention because it is the one substantive addition to the inherited skeleton, and it is the phrase on which the earliest and most consequential judicial narrowing turned. In the race and sex versions of the sentence, the protected person is simply "no person"; the disability version protects only those who are "otherwise qualified," meaning qualified apart from the disability. The courts read that phrase to mean that the statute does not require a program to admit someone who cannot meet its essential requirements even with accommodations, and does not require substantial modifications that would change the program's fundamental nature. That reading, established in Southeastern Community College v. Davis in 1979, set the balance between access and institutional autonomy that every later case has worked within.

The jurisdictional trigger, "program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance," is equally load bearing. The statute binds recipients of federal money, not the general public and not private businesses that operate without federal support. A university that accepts federal research grants, a hospital that accepts Medicaid funds, a school district that accepts federal education dollars: all are recipients, and their programs are covered. A private employer with no federal contract and no federal assistance is not, even if it is covered by the later comprehensive disability statute for other reasons. This recipient based trigger is what makes the section a funding condition rather than a universal mandate, and the funding condition character is what makes states amenable to suit: a state that takes the money submits to the condition, and Congress made that submission explicit in 1986.

The side by side comparison of the two texts shows how little and how much changed across the decades. The 1973 version protected the "otherwise qualified handicapped individual" against exclusion "solely by reason of his handicap" in programs "receiving Federal financial assistance." The current version protects the "otherwise qualified individual with a disability, as defined in section 705(20)" against exclusion "solely by reason of her or his disability" in programs "receiving Federal financial assistance or under any program or activity conducted by any Executive agency or by the United States Postal Service." The operative verbs never moved; the protected vocabulary, the definition's location, and the jurisdictional reach did. A researcher comparing the Statutes at Large text at 87 Stat. 394 with the current United States Code text at 29 U.S.C. 794 can watch the statute modernize itself across four decades without ever restating its core command.

The thinness of the legislative record has a documented texture. The CRS report's observation that there was very little discussion of the section's meaning or importance during enactment is supplemented by the disability history scholarship associated with Richard Scotch, which records that congressional staff who worked on the bill had no clear recollection of where the provision came from. Senator Humphrey's September 1972 floor remarks, at 118 Congressional Record 32310, remain the most detailed contemporaneous statement, tying the provision to his earlier efforts to extend the older civil rights titles. That is the entire affirmative record of congressional purpose: a sentence with no hearing, a report with no recollection, and a floor remark. The courts have accordingly treated the text, the copied parentage, and the regulations as the authoritative sources of meaning, which is why the 1977 rulemaking carried more interpretive weight than the 1973 enactment.

The two words Congress added to the inherited skeleton, "otherwise qualified," deserve a final note because they are the only substantive policy judgment the 1973 text made on its own. The race and sex versions of the sentence protect "no person" without qualification; the disability version protects only the "otherwise qualified." The addition reflects an inchoate legislative sense that disability differs from race and sex as a ground of classification: a program may legitimately require abilities that a disability prevents, and the statute was not meant to abolish legitimate qualifications. The courts gave that inchoate sense doctrinal form in Davis, reading "otherwise qualified" to permit essential requirements and to forbid only exclusions that qualification cannot justify. Everything the phrase has come to mean was built after enactment, but the phrase itself was the one original contribution of the 1973 drafters to the sentence they copied.

## Two Vetoes and a Signing

Before the Rehabilitation Act became law in September 1973, two predecessor bills died at the presidential desk, both on spending grounds and neither over the civil rights sentence that would make the enacted statute famous. Understanding the vetoes matters because they explain why the final bill passed in the form it did, why it carried the language it did, and why its sponsors were willing to accept a compromise product rather than fight for a stronger one.

The first veto was a pocket veto in the autumn of 1972. The bill, H.R. 8395, had been presented to the president on October 17, 1972, after the second session of the 92nd Congress had adjourned sine die on October 18, leaving the president unable to return it with objections and therefore able to kill it by simply withholding his signature. The veto message was dated October 27, 1972. A pocket veto differs from a regular veto in that Congress cannot override it; the bill dies outright. The procedural mechanics of vetoes and overrides, and the distinction between a returned veto and a pocket veto, are explained in detail in this series' guide to [how vetoes and congressional overrides work](/2022/04/01/veto-and-override-explained/).

The second veto was a returned veto with a message, and it is the one most often misreported. The vetoed vehicle was S. 7 in the 93rd Congress, not a House bill, and the date was March 27, 1973. President Nixon's message framed the disapproval as fiscal discipline, describing the bill's authorization levels as inconsistent with his commitment to hold down taxes and prices and warning against spendthrift actions. The bill carried a two year authorization of roughly 2.6 billion dollars in nominal terms. The Senate voted on the override on April 3, 1973, and the veto was sustained, 60 yeas to 36 nays, short of the two thirds needed. Nixon's signing statement for the eventual enacted bill would echo the same vocabulary, calling the vetoed version "fiscally and programmatically unsound," confirming that the objection was to the bill's authorization levels and program design, not to any civil rights provision within it.

That the vetoes were about money rather than disability rights is worth stressing, because it corrects the impression that the statute faced principled opposition over its nondiscrimination mandate. The opposition was budgetary. The disability rights sentence traveled inside a vocational rehabilitation appropriations vehicle, and the vehicle was stopped twice for its price tag. When the third version moved, the price tag had been negotiated down and the civil rights sentence rode along again, still nearly invisible.

The third version was H.R. 8070, introduced by Representative Brademas on May 23, 1973. The House passed it on June 5 by 384 to 13; the Senate passed it on July 18; conference reports were filed on July 24; the House agreed to the conference report on September 13 by 400 to 0; the Senate agreed on September 18 by 88 to 0; the enrolled bill was presented to the president on September 19; and Nixon signed it on September 26, 1973, as Public Law 93-112. The near unanimous final votes reflect the compromise character of the product: a rehabilitation program bill whose authorizations had been brought within the administration's fiscal tolerance, carrying a nondiscrimination section that had attracted almost no floor attention. The statute was born as a funding bill with a civil rights sentence stapled to it, and the sentence would spend four years waiting for someone to give it operational content.

The legislative arithmetic of H.R. 8070 shows how thoroughly the fiscal compromise had settled the politics. The bill was introduced by Representative Brademas on May 23, 1973, cleared the House on June 5 by 384 to 13, and passed the Senate on July 18. The conference committee reconciled the chambers' versions in reports filed July 24, and both chambers adopted the conference report by lopsided margins, 400 to 0 in the House on September 13 and 88 to 0 in the Senate on September 18. The enrolled bill reached the president on September 19, and the September 26 signature made it Public Law 93-112. Votes that lopsided do not reflect enthusiasm for the nondiscrimination sentence, which no recorded debate had examined; they reflect the exhaustion of the authorization fight and the acceptability of the compromised funding levels.

Nixon's signing statement is the bookend to the veto messages. Where the March veto message had warned against spendthrift actions and invoked the commitment to hold down taxes and prices, the signing statement presented the enacted bill as fiscally and programmatically sound, the antonym of the phrase he had applied to the vetoed S. 7. The statement's vocabulary confirms what the veto messages implied: the two year struggle was about the price of vocational rehabilitation services, not about whether disabled Americans should be protected from discrimination in federally assisted programs. The civil rights sentence passed because it was never the thing being fought over.

### Did Nixon really veto the Rehabilitation Act twice before signing it?

Yes, twice, and neither veto mentioned disability civil rights. The first was the October 1972 pocket veto of H.R. 8395 after the 92nd Congress adjourned. The second was the March 27, 1973 veto of S. 7, sustained April 3 on a 60 to 36 override vote, with both vetoes turning on spending levels alone.

## The Enforcement Model That Traveled With the Sentence

Copying the Title VI sentence meant copying Title VI's enforcement expectations, and that machinery had three parts that shaped section 504's development in ways the 1973 Congress never considered. The first was the administrative route: the funding agency would write rules, investigate complaints, seek voluntary compliance, and hold the ultimate sanction of terminating assistance to a noncompliant recipient after an administrative hearing. The second was the private right of action: federal courts inferred that the persons a funding condition was meant to protect could enforce it themselves when the agency did not, and Congress ratified that inference for section 504 when the 1978 amendments added the remedies provision at section 505. The third was the constitutional theory holding the other two together: the spending power. Congress did not directly regulate the recipient's conduct; it offered money on conditions, and the recipient's acceptance of the funds constituted agreement to the conditions.

The spending power theory is what makes section 504's remedies distinctive. Because the funding relationship works like a contract, the Supreme Court has held that a recipient is on notice of, and can be held to, only those remedies traditionally available for breach of contract. That quasi-contractual framing is the direct descendant of the decision to copy the funding condition sentence rather than to write a direct regulation of conduct, and it is the reasoning that later limited damages in Barnes v. Gorman and, after this article's reference date, in Cummings v. Premier Rehab Keller. The 1973 Congress chose the device without debating the theory; the courts supplied the theory when the remedies questions arrived.

The no-exhaustion rule is the other inheritance that matters in practice. Section 504 derives its procedural requirements from Title VI, which imposes no requirement that a complainant file with the agency and exhaust administrative remedies before suing, a point the First Circuit stated in Brennan v. King and the Office for Civil Rights confirms in its guidance. A person may therefore go directly to federal court, which makes the private lawsuit faster and more predictable than the administrative process and explains why the case law, rather than agency enforcement records, is the primary source of the statute's meaning.

## Four Years Without Regulations

From September 1973 to April 1977, section 504 existed in the statute books as an unenforceable abstraction. The Department of Health, Education, and Welfare, the agency with jurisdiction over the provision, did not issue implementing regulations. Without regulations there were no definitions of who counted as a handicapped individual, no standards for what constituted discrimination, no procedures for complaints, and no clarity about what recipients of federal funds had to do to comply. A school district, a hospital, or a university asking its lawyers what the new section required would have gotten the same answer the lawyers got from the statute itself: a single sentence and silence.

### Why did enforceable rules take four years to appear?

Three causes combined: the 1974 amendments expanded the protected class without expressly ordering rulemaking; HEW wrestled with genuine scope and cost questions the copied text did not answer; and no court ordered the department to act until Cherry v. Mathews in July 1976. Even after that order, the department let the proposed rules sit for nine more months.

The delay had no single cause, and the explanations offered at the time came from both sides of the access debate. Within the department, the section's novelty and breadth created genuine drafting problems. No federal agency had ever written comprehensive disability nondiscrimination rules, and the provision touched nearly every domain the department funded: elementary and secondary education, higher education, hospitals and health programs, social services, and vocational programs themselves. Officials also worried about the compliance costs the rules would impose on recipients, and covered institutions raised those concerns directly: universities warned about the expense of retrofitting facilities, hospitals about restructuring programs, school districts about the cost of evaluations and services. Alongside the cost arguments ran access arguments from disability organizations, which pressed the department to act and argued that every month of delay left excluded people without a remedy Congress had already promised. The record of the period shows both pressures operating at once, and the department responded to neither with speed.

Congress had not made the department's job easier. The 1974 amendments to the Rehabilitation Act, Public Law 93-516, are sometimes described as having directed the department to issue regulations, but the amendments contained no express statutory command to do so. What the 1974 committee reports did contain was language contemplating swift implementation through comprehensive regulations, at Senate Report 93-1297, pages 39 to 40. That expectation, however, was legislative history, not law. It took a lawsuit to convert the expectation into a duty. On July 19, 1976, the federal district court for the District of Columbia decided Cherry v. Mathews, 419 F. Supp. 922, holding that the secretary was required to promulgate regulations effectuating section 504 and construing the 1974 legislative history as the basis for that requirement. The court retained jurisdiction to prevent further unreasonable delay, putting the department under a judicial order to finish the job. Separately, in April 1976, Executive Order 11914 had directed HEW to coordinate government wide implementation of the section, assigning the department a coordinating role across the federal establishment that went beyond its own programs.

The department had, in fact, begun drafting before the court order. Proposed rules were published on May 17, 1976, at 41 Federal Register 20296, and a revised proposal followed on July 16, 1976, at 41 Federal Register 29548. The proposals circulated for comment, and the comments revealed the breadth of what the final rules would have to decide: the definition of handicapped individual, the meaning of otherwise qualified, the obligations of schools, the obligations of health and social service programs, the standards for employment within recipient programs, and the physical accessibility of facilities. But publication of proposals is not issuance of final rules, and through 1976 and into the winter of 1977 the final regulations remained unsigned. The Carter administration took office in January 1977, and the new HEW secretary, Joseph Califano, inherited both the proposed rules and the court order.

By early 1977 the disability rights organizations that had spent years petitioning the department concluded that petitions had run their course. The American Coalition of Citizens with Disabilities set a deadline of April 4, 1977, for the secretary to sign the regulations, and announced that demonstrations would follow if he did not. The deadline passed without a signature. What followed on April 5 was a coordinated national protest, and it is the central event in the implementation history of the statute.

The content of the 1976 proposals shows what the department thought the statute required once it finally committed the analysis to paper. The May 17, 1976 proposal, at 41 Federal Register 20296, and the revised July 16, 1976 proposal, at 41 Federal Register 29548, laid out the definitional framework, the employment duties, the program accessibility standard, the education subparts, and the enforcement procedures that the final rules would adopt substantially unchanged. The comment period drew responses from the full range of affected institutions, and the comments previewed the compliance cost debate that would follow the rules for decades: universities detailed the expense of physical and programmatic access, health providers raised operational concerns, and disability organizations pressed for stronger and more specific duties. That the final rules issued unchanged from the draft is therefore a substantive fact about the rulemaking, not just a procedural footnote; the department's analysis had been complete for months, and the missing element was the decision to act.

Executive Order 11914, issued in April 1976, added a second track to the implementation story. While Cherry v. Mathews ordered HEW to write rules for its own programs, the executive order directed HEW to coordinate section 504 implementation government wide, making the department the standard setter for every federal funding agency. The coordination guidelines that followed as 45 CFR 85 on January 13, 1978, required each agency to issue conforming regulations for the programs it funded. The department was thus simultaneously the laggard on its own rules and the designated coordinator for everyone else's, a dual role that magnified the significance of the April 1977 signing beyond HEW's own programs.

### Who signed the section 504 regulations and when?

HEW Secretary Joseph Califano signed the final section 504 regulations on April 28, 1977, unchanged from the draft that had been pending, and they were published in the Federal Register on May 4, 1977, at 42 Federal Register 22676. The signing came twenty-three days into the national protests and two days before the San Francisco occupation ended.

The question matters because the date is frequently misstated. The May 4 date is the Federal Register publication date, not the signing date, and treating publication as signature compresses the causal sequence that the protesters and the department both understood at the time. The signature followed the protests; the protests did not follow the signature. Califano signed the rules as issued at 45 CFR Part 84, and the department later issued government wide coordination guidelines under the executive order as 45 CFR 85 on January 13, 1978.

## April 1977: The Occupation That Produced the Rules

On April 5, 1977, the day after the American Coalition of Citizens with Disabilities deadline expired, demonstrators moved against HEW offices in cities across the country. Protests struck the department's offices in Washington, Boston, Seattle, New York, Atlanta, Philadelphia, Chicago, Dallas, and Denver, and a separate occupation began at the department's regional headquarters in San Francisco. The action was coordinated nationally but executed locally, and the local actions differed in duration, tactics, and outcome. What united them was the demand: sign the regulations that the department had been drafting, in some form, for the better part of four years.

In Washington, the action was dramatic and brief. Roughly three hundred protesters marched on the department's headquarters on April 5, and at least seventy-five entered and occupied the secretary's sixth floor office suite, holding it overnight. Accounts of the night describe Califano standing on a table to address the occupiers, an image that captures the improvisational character of the confrontation. The Washington occupiers held for about twenty-six hours, roughly one night, not days. In most of the other cities the occupations ended within a day or two. In New York the protesters left when food and medication could not be brought in, after police refused to allow deliveries; similar logistics ended the actions elsewhere. The pattern was a burst of occupations, most of them quickly exhausted by the practical difficulty of sustaining a sit in inside a federal office building.

San Francisco was the exception, and it became the center of the story. The occupation of the HEW regional office there began on April 5 and did not end until April 30, a span of twenty-five days, with some accounts counting twenty-six days and placing the end on May 1. The occupiers sustained the action through weeks of confinement, and the occupation is widely described as the longest nonviolent occupation of a federal building in American history, a characterization that appears in multiple published accounts and commemorations. The occupiers held on even after the regulations were signed on April 28, remaining for two additional days to clean the building and leave together rather than dispersing in the moment of victory.

The signing on April 28 came twenty-three days into the national action. The regulations were issued unchanged from the draft, which is itself a measure of what the protests accomplished: the department did not rewrite the rules in response to the occupation, but it ended four years of inaction and issued them. The contrast between the two events is the point. The rules had been substantively ready, in draft form, for months; what was missing was the institutional will to sign them, and the occupation supplied the pressure that the court order, the executive order, and the comment process had not. When the final rules appeared in the Federal Register on May 4, they gave the single sentence its first operational meaning, nearly four years after enactment.

The geography of the protests matters because it shows a movement operating nationally with local discipline. The nine cities beyond San Francisco, Washington, Boston, Seattle, New York, Atlanta, Philadelphia, Chicago, Dallas, and Denver, produced actions of varying duration, but the pattern was consistent: occupiers entered HEW offices, demanded the secretary's signature, and held until logistics or negotiation ended the action. The Washington occupation's roughly twenty-six hours included the memorable scene of the secretary standing on a table to address the occupiers in his own suite, a confrontation conducted face to face rather than through statements. The New York action ended when the occupiers, denied food and medication deliveries, concluded they could not safely continue, a reminder that the tactic's costs fell on people whose medical needs made confinement genuinely dangerous.

The San Francisco occupation endured for twenty-five days, with some accounts counting twenty-six, and the occupiers' discipline through the long confinement became part of the story. When the regulations were signed on April 28, the occupiers did not disperse in celebration; they remained for two days to clean the building and leave together, an orderly exit that denied the department any narrative of disruption. The occupation is widely described as the longest nonviolent occupation of a federal building in American history, and the attribution matters as much as the claim: the characterization appears across published histories and commemorations, and this article reports it as the attributed description rather than as an independently verified superlative.

The neutrality of this account requires stating both what the protesters achieved and what the objections raised by covered institutions at the time maintained. The access argument was that Congress had enacted a promise in 1973 and the executive branch had nullified it by inaction, and that people excluded from schools, jobs, and programs had no other lever to pull. The compliance cost argument, raised by universities, hospitals, and other covered institutions during the comment period and in the years that followed, was that the regulations imposed substantial and sometimes open ended obligations, particularly around facility accessibility and program modification, without a dedicated federal funding stream to pay for them. Both arguments were real, both were voiced contemporaneously, and the regulations that emerged reflected neither fully: they were comprehensive on paper and left the hardest questions, about how much modification was enough and who paid for it, to enforcement and litigation.

The 1977 action also established a pattern that would repeat across disability rights history. The statute's meaning was not fixed by its text at enactment, nor by the regulations as drafted, but by the political struggle that forced the regulations into existence. The sentence had no hearing; the rules had a twenty-five day occupation. That is the series thesis of this article stated as fact: implementation created the statute's meaning, because without the implementation the statute had no meaning at all.

## What the 1977 Regulations Required

The final rules, codified at 45 CFR Part 84, translated the single sentence into a regulatory architecture organized by subparts. Subpart A stated the general provisions: purpose, definitions, and the scope of the part. The definitions mattered most, because they answered the questions the statute had left open. "Handicapped person" was defined to include the three prongs that the 1974 amendments had added to the statutory definition: an actual physical or mental impairment substantially limiting one or more major life activities, a record of such an impairment, and being regarded as having such an impairment. "Otherwise qualified" was defined contextually, differently for employment, education, and other programs, rather than as a single abstract test.

Subpart B addressed employment practices within programs receiving federal assistance, requiring nondiscrimination in hiring, promotion, and the terms of employment, and requiring reasonable accommodation of known limitations unless the accommodation would impose an undue hardship on the operation of the program. Subpart C covered program accessibility, the section that would generate the most sustained compliance debate. It required that programs, viewed in their entirety, be readily accessible to handicapped persons, a formulation that stopped short of requiring every existing facility to be retrofitted but required enough change that no qualified person would be excluded. New construction and alterations had to meet accessibility standards, and recipients were given a timetable for achieving program accessibility through means that could include structural changes, reassignment of services, aides, or home visits.

Subpart D addressed preschool, elementary, secondary, and adult education, and it is the subpart that governs the mechanism schools call the 504 plan. It required recipients operating public elementary and secondary education programs to provide a free appropriate public education to each qualified handicapped person, defined the appropriate education comparatively, at section 104.33(b)(1), as services designed to meet the individual educational needs of handicapped persons as adequately as the needs of nonhandicapped persons are met, and required that the education be provided without cost to the student or family, at section 104.33(c)(1). It required evaluation before placement, section 104.35, and required that placement decisions be made by a group of persons knowledgeable about the child, the evaluation data, and the placement options, section 104.35(c). The subpart did not prescribe a specific written document or a specific team composition beyond that knowledgeable group, a point that matters because schools colloquially produce "504 plans" that the regulation describes only functionally.

Subpart E covered postsecondary education, requiring nondiscrimination in admissions, academic programs, and student services, and requiring modifications of academic requirements and auxiliary aids where necessary. Subpart F addressed health, welfare, and social services, including hospitals and nursing homes. Subpart G established the procedures for enforcement, modeled on the Title VI procedures: complaint investigation, compliance reviews, and the ultimate sanction of fund termination after an administrative hearing, with recipients required to designate a compliance coordinator and adopt grievance procedures. Subpart H, added by later rulemaking, addressed the newer coverage of programs conducted by federal agencies themselves.

Beyond the department's own rules, the coordination guidelines issued under Executive Order 11914 as 45 CFR 85 on January 13, 1978, required every federal agency to issue its own section 504 regulations for the programs it funded, using the HEW rules as the model. This multiplied the 1977 framework across the federal establishment: each funding agency wrote its own part, adapted to its programs, under HEW's coordination. The result was a government wide regulatory fabric anchored in a single 1977 text, which is why the April signing mattered far beyond the department that signed it.

Subpart A's definitions repay close attention because they answered the questions the statute had left open and fixed the meanings the courts would later construe. "Handicapped person" was defined through the three prongs the 1974 amendments had added to the statutory definition: an impairment substantially limiting major life activities, a record of such an impairment, or being regarded as having one. "Otherwise qualified" was defined separately for employment, education, and services contexts rather than as a single abstract test, a choice that channeled later litigation into domain specific inquiries. "Program or activity" was defined to reach the operations of the recipient, and "federal financial assistance" was defined to include grants, loans, and other non procurement support. These definitions, drafted in 1976 and signed in 1977, supplied the vocabulary in which the entire subsequent case law was written.

Subpart G's enforcement procedures completed the borrowing from Title VI. Recipients were required to provide assurances of compliance as a condition of funding, to designate an employee to coordinate compliance efforts, and to adopt grievance procedures incorporating due process standards for the prompt and equitable resolution of complaints. Federal agencies were to investigate complaints, conduct periodic compliance reviews, and seek voluntary compliance before moving to the ultimate sanction of fund termination, which required an administrative hearing and a written finding. The procedures made the administrative track real on paper while leaving its practical center of gravity, as under Title VI, in voluntary compliance under the threat of the sanction rather than in the sanction's frequent use.

## The Definition in Three Prongs

The question of who counts as a handicapped individual, in the statute's original vocabulary, or an individual with a disability, in its later vocabulary, is where section 504 litigation most often begins, because coverage is the threshold every plaintiff must cross before reaching the merits of discrimination. The definition has three prongs, and the three prongs do different work. The first covers a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities. The second covers a record of such an impairment. The third covers being regarded as having such an impairment. The second and third prongs were added by the 1974 amendments, and their addition reflected a legislative judgment that discrimination often operates on history and perception rather than on present functional limitation.

### Who qualifies for protection under Section 504?

An otherwise qualified individual with a disability: someone whose impairment substantially limits a major life activity, who has a record of such an impairment, or who is regarded as having one, and who meets the program's essential requirements apart from the disability. The funding trigger must also be present.

The first prong does the most work and has generated the most doctrine. "Physical or mental impairment" was construed broadly from the outset: the 1977 regulations defined it to include physiological disorders and conditions, cosmetic disfigurement, anatomical loss, and mental or psychological disorders, a deliberately capacious list. "Substantially limits" was the phrase that did the narrowing, and for two decades the courts read it restrictively, particularly in the ADA context, before the 2008 amendments reversed course. "Major life activities" was given an illustrative rather than exhaustive list in the regulations: caring for oneself, performing manual tasks, walking, seeing, hearing, speaking, breathing, learning, and working. Bragdon v. Abbott added reproduction to the judicial understanding of the term, and the 2008 conforming amendments added major bodily functions to the statutory list, so that the operation of the immune system, normal cell growth, and similar functions count directly.

The record of prong protects people whose impairments no longer substantially limit them but whose histories trigger discrimination: the cancer survivor in remission, the person with a history of mental illness now recovered, the former addict. The regarded as prong protects people whom others treat as impaired whether or not they are: the burn victim whose disfigurement provokes exclusion, the worker perceived as HIV positive, the applicant rejected on the basis of a myth about a condition. Arline's tuberculosis holding drew on the regarded as logic, and the prong has been particularly important in cases where the employer's or program's perception, rather than the plaintiff's actual limitation, drove the adverse decision. Together the three prongs make the definition reach beyond the visibly and presently limited to the formerly limited and the misperceived, which is a broader protected class than the colloquial understanding of disability would suggest.

The 2008 conforming amendments changed how the first prong is read in two ways that matter for section 504 cases. First, the amendments provide that the determination whether an impairment substantially limits a major life activity is made without regard to the ameliorative effects of mitigating measures, such as medication, hearing aids, or learned behavioral modifications; ordinary eyeglasses are the narrow exception. A plaintiff whose impairment is controlled by medication is therefore still evaluated in the unmitigated state, reversing the restrictive approach the courts had taken in the years before 2008. Second, the amendments add major bodily functions to the statute's illustrative list, so that impairments of the immune system, normal cell growth, digestive, bowel, bladder, neurological, brain, respiratory, circulatory, endocrine, and reproductive functions can qualify directly, without the detour through an activity like walking or working. The Education Department's guidance confirms that both changes apply to section 504 through the conformed definition, effective January 1, 2009.

The terminology evolution deserves a clear statement because misdating the vocabulary is one of the recurring errors in writing about the statute. As enacted in 1973, the law spoke of the "handicapped individual" and "his handicap." The 1974 amendments added the record of and regarded as prongs to that definition. The 1988 technical amendments introduced gender neutral phrasing, "her or his." The 1992 amendments replaced "handicapped person" with "individual with a disability" throughout the act. The 2008 conforming amendments tied the whole definition to the ADA's. A writer quoting the statute must therefore date the quotation: the 1973 version, the post-1974 version, the post-1992 version, and the post-2008 version are all different texts of the same section, and mixing them without dating produces the anachronism of attributing modern vocabulary to the Congress that enacted none of it.

## The Funding Trigger: What Counts as Federal Financial Assistance

The phrase "program or activity receiving Federal financial assistance" is the jurisdictional gate of the entire section, and its interpretation determines the statute's outer boundary. Federal financial assistance includes grants, loans, contracts other than procurement contracts, and other arrangements by which federal money or property supports a program. The classic recipients are state and local governments, universities, hospitals, and nonprofit organizations that take federal grants. A school district receiving federal education funds, a university receiving federal research grants, a hospital receiving Medicaid reimbursement: each is a recipient, and the section binds its programs.

The 1978 amendments' extension to programs conducted by executive agencies and the Postal Service closed the most glaring gap in the original design. Before 1978, the section bound everyone who took federal money except the federal agencies that gave it out; a disabled person excluded from a program run directly by a federal agency had no section 504 claim. The extension made the government's own conduct subject to the same nondiscrimination principle it imposed on recipients, and the later subpart H regulations implemented that coverage. Section 508's technology accessibility duties, added in 1986 and rewritten in 1998, extended the principle further into the government's procurement and use of information technology.

The boundary cases define the trigger's limits. A private employer with no federal contract and no federal assistance is not covered, even if its workforce includes people with disabilities; the ADA's employment title may cover that employer on a different trigger, but section 504 does not. A federal contractor is covered by section 503's employment duties, not by section 504's program duties, unless it also receives federal financial assistance in a non procurement capacity. A state agency that receives federal funds for one program is bound with respect to its programs under the program or activity definition, which Congress clarified to reach all of the operations of the recipient entity. The definitional work matters because defendants regularly argue that the particular subunit or activity at issue received no federal money; the program or activity definition answers that argument by tying coverage to the recipient's operations rather than to the specific dollars.

The funding trigger is also what makes the immunity waiver coherent. A state cannot be sued for damages under the Eleventh Amendment as a general matter, but a state that voluntarily accepts federal funds conditioned on nondiscrimination has waived that immunity for suits enforcing the condition. The 1986 provision at 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7 makes the waiver express and statutory, and it applies to section 504, Title IX, the Age Discrimination Act, Title VI, and other funding condition statutes together. The waiver theory is why section 504 remains the vehicle of choice against state defendants: the ADA's public entity title reaches states regardless of funding, but its remedial and immunity posture is less favorable, while the funding condition supplies both jurisdiction and waiver in one instrument.

The "program or activity" definition deserves a final word because it answers the most common jurisdictional defense. Defendants regularly argue that the particular office, subunit, or activity where the alleged discrimination occurred received no federal money, and therefore the section does not reach it. The statutory definition defeats that argument by tying coverage to the recipient's operations: where an entity receives federal financial assistance, the section reaches the entity's programs and activities as defined, not merely the line items the federal dollars nominally support. A university that accepts federal research grants cannot quarantine the nondiscrimination duty to the funded laboratory; a school district that accepts federal education funds cannot confine it to the funded program. The money opens the door, and the definition determines how far inside the duty extends. This is also why the private business with no federal funds at all stands entirely outside the statute: with no money there is no door, and the definition has nothing to attach to.

## Employment, Higher Education, and Health Care Under the Rules

The 1977 regulations translated the single sentence into domain specific duties, and three domains, employment within recipient programs, postsecondary education, and health and social services, show how the translation worked in practice.

Subpart B governs employment practices of recipients of federal assistance. It bars discrimination in hiring, promotion, compensation, and the terms and conditions of employment, and it requires reasonable accommodation of the known physical or mental limitations of an otherwise qualified applicant or employee unless the accommodation would impose an undue hardship on the operation of the program. The reasonable accommodation concept, later central to the ADA's employment title, entered federal disability law here: the employer must adjust the workplace, the schedule, or the equipment where the adjustment enables the qualified person to perform, and may decline only where the cost or difficulty is undue in relation to the program's operation. The subpart applies to employment within federally assisted programs, which means it reaches the employees of universities, hospitals, and social service agencies that take federal money, a large workforce beyond the federal civil service covered by section 501 and the federal contractors covered by section 503.

Subpart E governs postsecondary education, and its provisions read as a direct response to the exclusions that had kept disabled students out of colleges. Recipients operating colleges and universities may not discriminate in admissions: they may not use admissions tests or criteria that have a disproportionate adverse effect unless the tests are validated and no alternative is available, may not make preadmission inquiries about disability, and may not exclude qualified students on the basis of handicap. Academic adjustments are required where necessary to avoid discrimination: modifications of academic requirements, such as extended time or alternative testing methods, and the provision of auxiliary aids, such as interpreters or taped texts, for students with impaired sensory, manual, or speaking skills. The subpart stops at the Davis line: academic requirements that are essential to the program or to licensing need not be waived, and the institution need not fundamentally alter its program. The balance struck here, access through adjustment short of essential alteration, is the Davis framework rendered as regulation.

Subpart F governs health, welfare, and social services, including hospitals, nursing homes, and outpatient facilities. It bars the denial of benefits or services on the basis of handicap, requires effective communication with patients and clients, and addresses the specific problem of discriminatory medical treatment decisions. The subpart's significance lies in its reach into institutions that had routinely excluded disabled patients from treatment or shunted them into segregated and inferior services. The effective communication requirement, later echoed in the ADA, obligated hospitals to ensure that deaf patients, blind patients, and patients with other communication related impairments could actually use the services offered, through interpreters, readers, or other aids. The subpart did not dictate medical judgment, but it required that medical judgment not be a pretext for handicap based exclusion, a line that Arline and Bragdon would later police through the objective medical evidence standard.

Across the three subparts the regulatory strategy is consistent: state the nondiscrimination principle, specify the accommodations and adjustments that give it content, and mark the boundary where the institution's essential functions begin.

The undue hardship concept in the employment subpart illustrates how the regulations balanced the duty against institutional burden without using a dollar threshold. Undue hardship was defined as an action requiring significant difficulty or expense in relation to the operation of the program, considering factors such as the nature and cost of the accommodation, the overall financial resources of the recipient, and the type of operation involved. The standard is deliberately relational: an accommodation that would be an undue hardship for a small social service agency might not be one for a large university hospital system. The regulations thus avoided both a fixed monetary cap, which would have been arbitrary across recipients of wildly different size, and an open ended duty, which would have ignored cost entirely. The same relational logic appears in the program accessibility standard's allowance of nonstructural compliance methods: the duty is stated as an outcome, accessible programs, and the recipient chooses the least burdensome path to it. The strategy left the boundary's precise location to enforcement and litigation, which is why the case law matters as much as the rules. Davis drew the boundary for academic programs, Choate for benefit programs, Arline and Bragdon for health related exclusions, and each decision can be read as the judiciary completing the regulatory sentence the department had left deliberately open.

## The Coordination Regime: A Government Wide Fabric

The 1977 HEW regulations would have mattered far less if they had bound only the programs HEW funded. What made them the foundation of federal disability civil rights law was the coordination regime built around them. Executive Order 11914, issued in April 1976, directed HEW to coordinate the implementation of section 504 across the federal government, and the coordination guidelines issued as 45 CFR 85 on January 13, 1978, required every federal agency to issue its own section 504 regulations for the programs it funded, using the HEW rules as the model. Each funding agency, from the Labor Department to the Transportation Department to the Agriculture Department, wrote its own part, adapted to its programs, under HEW's coordination.

The coordination regime solved a structural problem that the single sentence could not solve on its own. Federal financial assistance flows through dozens of agencies to hundreds of thousands of recipients, and a single department's rules cannot reach programs another department funds. By requiring each agency to write conforming rules, the regime multiplied the 1977 framework across the entire federal establishment while keeping it anchored in a single text. The Justice Department later assumed the coordination role, and its coordination regulations at 28 CFR part 41 became the reference point that the ADA's Title II regulation was required to match. The chain runs from the 1977 HEW rules through the agency specific parts through the coordination regulations to the ADA's consistency instruction, a single regulatory lineage spanning four decades.

The regime also explains why the April 1977 signing had consequences far beyond HEW. The occupation forced one department to sign one set of rules, but the coordination structure converted that set of rules into the template for the whole government. Implementation created the statute's meaning not once but repeatedly, as each agency adapted the template to its programs and each adaptation generated its own compliance questions, complaints, and cases. The sentence had no hearing; the rules had an occupation; and the coordination regime gave the rules a government.

## The Four Section Table

The Rehabilitation Act's civil rights architecture rests on four sections that are often confused with one another because they share a statute and a vocabulary but bind different actors on different triggers. The table below sets out, for each section, who is bound, what triggers the obligation, what the obligation is, and how it is enforced. Section 502, the Architectural and Transportation Barriers Compliance Board at 29 U.S.C. 792, is a body rather than a duty and is discussed in the text rather than the table.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 501 (29 U.S.C. 791) | Federal agencies as employers | Federal employment | Nondiscrimination and affirmative action in hiring, placement, and advancement of individuals with disabilities | Administrative complaint process within the employing agency and the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission; private action after exhaustion |
| 503 (29 U.S.C. 793) | Employers holding federal contracts or subcontracts above the threshold amount | The federal contract | Affirmative action to employ and advance qualified individuals with disabilities; nondiscrimination in employment | Complaints to the Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs in the Labor Department; contract sanctions including debarment; no private right of action |

The table makes the division of labor visible. Section 501 governs the federal government as an employer. Section 503 governs private employers that do business with the government. Section 504 governs anyone who takes federal money, plus the government's own programs. Section 508 governs the government's technology. A single disabled person might be protected by all four in different parts of a working day: as a federal employee under 501, as an applicant to a contractor under 503, as a student at a federally assisted university under 504, and as a user of a federal agency's website under 508. The confusion arises because the later comprehensive disability statute uses different triggers, and readers carry its categories back into the older law.

## Inside the Four Sections: The Statute's Architecture

The Rehabilitation Act of 1973 is a vocational rehabilitation funding statute that grew civil rights appendages over two decades, and reading it as a whole requires keeping the two functions distinct. The core of the act authorizes grants to states for vocational rehabilitation services: counseling, training, placement, and related services aimed at preparing people with disabilities for employment. That core is what the appropriations fights of 1972 and 1973 were about, and it is what the 2.6 billion dollar authorization in the vetoed S. 7 was funding. The civil rights sections, 501 through 508, sit alongside that core as a separate regime that uses the funding relationship as its jurisdictional hook.

Section 501, at 29 U.S.C. 791, addresses the federal government in its capacity as the nation's largest employer. It requires each federal department and agency to maintain an affirmative program for the hiring, placement, and advancement of individuals with disabilities, and it bars discrimination in federal employment. The affirmative action character distinguishes it from the pure nondiscrimination of section 504: agencies must take positive steps, set goals, and report on their progress, not merely refrain from discriminating. Enforcement runs through the administrative complaint process, with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission as the reviewing body, and a federal employee must exhaust that process before filing a private action, a contrast with the no exhaustion rule under section 504 that has practical consequences for how cases are filed.

Section 502, at 29 U.S.C. 792, creates the Architectural and Transportation Barriers Compliance Board, the body now known as the Access Board. The Board is not a duty imposed on recipients but an institution: it develops accessibility guidelines, enforces standards for federally funded facilities, and later became the author of the accessibility standards under both the Rehabilitation Act and the later comprehensive statute. Its presence in the act reflects the drafters' understanding that physical barriers were a distinct problem requiring a distinct institutional response, separate from the programmatic nondiscrimination of section 504.

Section 503, at 29 U.S.C. 793, extends affirmative action obligations to private employers that contract with the federal government. Any employer holding a federal contract or subcontract above the statutory threshold must take affirmative action to employ and advance qualified individuals with disabilities and must not discriminate in employment decisions. Enforcement belongs to the Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs within the Labor Department, which investigates complaints and can impose sanctions up to and including debarment from federal contracting. Critically, section 503 creates no private right of action: an individual who believes a contractor violated the section complains to the Labor Department, and only the department can pursue the matter. This is the sharpest enforcement contrast with section 504, where the private lawsuit is the central remedy.

Section 504, at 29 U.S.C. 794, is the general nondiscrimination provision, and its architecture is the funding condition in its purest form. Subsection (a) states the prohibition. Subsection (b) defines program or activity for purposes of the funding trigger. The 1978 amendments added subsections extending the prohibition to programs conducted by executive agencies and the Postal Service, closing the gap that had left the government's own programs outside the section's reach. Section 505, added by the same 1978 amendments, supplies the remedies: it makes available the remedies, procedures, and rights set forth in Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 for section 504 violations, which is the statutory basis for the private damages action that the courts had already begun to recognize. The 1978 extension matters because it ended the anomaly of a statute that bound every recipient of federal money but not the agencies that disbursed it.

Section 508, at 29 U.S.C. 794d, is the youngest of the four and the most transformed. It was added to the act by the Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1986, Public Law 99-506, the same amendments that addressed sovereign immunity, and in its original form it was concerned with the accessibility of electronic office equipment, setting nonbinding guidelines for federal agencies. Congress substantially rewrote the section as part of the Workforce Investment Act of 1998, Public Law 105-220, converting the guidelines into binding, enforceable standards for electronic and information technology that federal agencies develop, procure, maintain, or use, and incorporating those standards into the Federal Acquisition Regulation so that they bite at the procurement stage. The Access Board's guidance on the 1998 amendments confirms the transformation from hortatory guidelines to enforceable procurement standards. Section 508 now governs the accessibility of federal websites, software, telecommunications, and office technology, a domain that did not exist when the 1973 act was written.

Section 502's Compliance Board warrants a fuller note because the institution it created has outgrown its original assignment. The Architectural and Transportation Barriers Compliance Board, at 29 U.S.C. 792, was established to ensure compliance with accessibility standards for federally funded facilities and to develop the guidelines those standards required. Over the following decades the Board, operating as the Access Board, became the federal government's central author of accessibility design criteria, writing the guidelines that underlie both Rehabilitation Act and ADA accessibility standards. Its presence in the 1973 act reflects a legislative judgment that physical barriers required an institutional response distinct from the programmatic nondiscrimination duties of section 504: one section told recipients not to discriminate, while the Board told the government what accessible design meant in practice. The four-section table above omits section 502 because it creates a body rather than imposing a duty, but the body has shaped compliance as concretely as any duty.

The amendment history between 1973 and the reference date shows a statute that grew by accretion rather than by redesign. The 1974 amendments, Public Law 93-516, expanded the definition of handicapped individual with the record of and regarded as prongs and, through committee report language rather than express text, set the expectation of swift comprehensive regulations that Cherry v. Mathews would later enforce. The 1978 amendments, Public Law 95-602, extended section 504 to federally conducted programs, added the section 505 remedies, and created new programs. The 1986 amendments, Public Law 99-506, added section 508 in its original form and, in section 1003, titled the Civil Rights Remedies Equalization provision, abrogated state sovereign immunity for section 504 suits, codified at 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7. The 1992 amendments, Public Law 102-569, modernized the terminology from handicapped individual to individual with a disability throughout the act. The 1998 amendments, Public Law 105-220, rewrote section 508 and reauthorized the act's programs. The ADA Amendments Act of 2008, Public Law 110-325, reached back into the Rehabilitation Act through its section 7 conforming amendments, rewriting the definition of disability at 29 U.S.C. 705 so that it carries the meaning given in section 3 of the Americans with Disabilities Act, effective January 1, 2009. That last amendment is the statutory mechanism by which the later statute's broadened definition flowed backward into the older one, aligning the two without merging them.

## Remedies: The Spending Clause Framework

Because section 504 is a funding condition, its remedies work differently from the remedies of a statute that directly regulates private conduct, and the differences determine who sues under it and what they can win. The private right of action under section 504 is implied rather than express: the statute does not contain a sentence saying that an aggrieved person may sue, but the courts recognized such an action early, reasoning from the Title VI model and from section 505's incorporation of Title VI remedies. Once recognized, the action carried the remedial questions with it: what damages are available, against whom, and on what showing.

The Supreme Court's answer on punitive damages came in Barnes v. Gorman, 536 U.S. 181 (2002). The Court held that punitive damages are not available in private suits under section 504 or Title VI, applying the contract law analogy that governs spending clause legislation. The reasoning runs as follows. Federal funding statutes operate like contracts: Congress offers money on conditions, and the recipient accepts the money and the conditions together. A funding recipient is therefore on notice of, and can be held to, only those remedies that would traditionally be available for breach of contract. Punitive damages are generally unavailable for breach of contract, so they are unavailable under section 504. The same analogy governs compensatory damages, which are available but require a showing of intentional discrimination; most circuits apply a deliberate indifference standard to establish that intent, meaning the plaintiff must show the recipient knew of a substantial likelihood of a violation and failed to act.

Sovereign immunity is the second pillar of the remedial structure, and it is where section 504's funding condition character gives plaintiffs an advantage they lack under the later statute. The Eleventh Amendment generally shields states from private damages suits in federal court, and in Atascadero State Hospital v. Scanlon, 473 U.S. 234 (1985), the Supreme Court held that section 504 as then written did not contain the clear statement required to abrogate that immunity. Congress responded the following year with section 1003 of the 1986 amendments, the Civil Rights Remedies Equalization provision, Public Law 99-506, codified at 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7 and signed on October 21, 1986. The provision states that a state shall not be immune under the Eleventh Amendment from suit in federal court for a violation of section 504, Title IX, the Age Discrimination Act of 1975, Title VI, or any other federal statute prohibiting discrimination by recipients of federal financial assistance, and it makes available the same remedies that would be available against any other public or private entity. The provision applies to violations occurring after October 21, 1986. Functionally it operates as a waiver conditioned on the acceptance of federal funds rather than as a pure abrogation: the state that takes the money consents to the suit. The practical consequence is that claims against state universities, state hospitals, and state agencies often proceed under section 504 when parallel claims under the later comprehensive statute face immunity barriers, which is one of the two reasons the older statute remains heavily litigated.

### Can a state be sued for damages under section 504?

Yes, where the state accepts federal financial assistance, because Congress conditioned the money on a waiver of Eleventh Amendment immunity in 1986. The Civil Rights Remedies Equalization provision, 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7, makes states suable in federal court on the same terms as any other defendant.

Punitive damages remain unavailable under Barnes v. Gorman, and compensatory damages require intentional discrimination, but the immunity door that Atascadero closed in 1985 was reopened by statute the next year.

The third remedial question, emotional distress damages, was the subject of a circuit split through the reference date of this article and was resolved after it. In Cummings v. Premier Rehab Keller, 596 U.S. 212, decided April 28, 2022, a six to three majority held that emotional distress damages are not recoverable in a private action to enforce the Rehabilitation Act. Chief Justice Roberts wrote for the majority, Justice Kavanaugh concurred in an opinion joined by Justice Gorsuch, and Justice Breyer dissented in an opinion joined by Justices Kagan and Sotomayor. The majority extended the Barnes contract analogy: emotional distress damages are not generally available for breach of contract, so a funding recipient lacks notice of them and cannot be held liable for them under a spending clause statute. The practical effect, as summarized in the CRS legal sidebar on the decision, is that compensatory relief under section 504 is effectively limited to economic harm. This article's reference date is July 1, 2015, seven years before Cummings was decided; the decision is therefore presented here as a later development, not as the law as it stood at the reference date. In 2015 the circuits were divided on the question, and the 2022 decision resolved the division against emotional distress recovery. A reader researching the law as of the reference date should treat the pre-2022 circuit law as the contemporary picture and Cummings as the subsequent answer.

Attorney's fees round out the remedial picture. Prevailing plaintiffs under section 504 may recover reasonable attorney's fees, which matters because the damages limitations would otherwise make many meritorious cases economically unviable to bring. The fee provision, combined with the private right of action and the immunity waiver, is what makes the funding condition enforceable in practice rather than merely declaratory.

The deliberate indifference standard that most circuits apply to the intent requirement deserves a precise statement, because it is the showing on which compensatory damages most often turn. The plaintiff must demonstrate that the recipient had knowledge of a substantial likelihood that a violation was occurring and failed to act on that knowledge; negligence alone does not suffice, and neither does a showing that the recipient could have done more in the abstract. The standard sits between negligence and animus: it captures the institution that sees the exclusion happening and does nothing, without requiring proof of hostile motive. In practice the standard makes damages available in cases of sustained institutional inattention while screening out claims based on isolated errors or good faith disagreements about what accommodation required.

The interaction of the remedies with the immunity waiver is what gives section 504 its distinctive litigation profile. A plaintiff suing a state university for damages faces the Eleventh Amendment as a threshold defense; the 1986 waiver answers it where the university accepts federal funds, which state universities invariably do. The plaintiff must still prove intentional discrimination under the deliberate indifference standard and is limited to economic compensatory damages plus fees, but the courthouse door is open. Under the ADA's public entity title the immunity analysis runs through different doctrine with less favorable results for plaintiffs in several circuits, which is why complaints against state defendants routinely plead section 504 alongside or instead of the ADA title. The funding condition thus does double duty: it supplies the substantive prohibition and the jurisdictional consent in a single instrument.

## The Supreme Court's Section 504 Cases

Four Supreme Court decisions, spanning 1979 to 1998, gave the single sentence the doctrinal structure that still governs it. Each answered a question the text left open, and together they trace the arc from a narrow reading of who is protected to a broad reading of what counts as a disability.

Southeastern Community College v. Davis, 442 U.S. 397 (1979), decided June 11, 1979, was the Court's first encounter with the section and its most consequential narrowing. The plaintiff was a hearing impaired applicant to a nursing program who had been denied admission. Justice Powell wrote for a unanimous Court. The holding: the applicant was not an "otherwise qualified" handicapped individual, because section 504 does not require a college to make substantial modifications to its program that would compromise the program's essential nature. The Court distinguished between reasonable modifications, which the statute may require, and fundamental alterations, which it does not. A nursing program that requires the ability to understand spoken communication in clinical settings, the Court reasoned, is not discriminating when it declines to admit someone who cannot meet that requirement even with accommodation; it is defining its program. The decision established the otherwise qualified inquiry as the central gatekeeper of the statute: the plaintiff must be able to meet the program's essential requirements, with or without reasonable accommodation, and the program need not lower or fundamentally change those requirements. The dissent free unanimity of Davis gave the holding unusual authority, and every later case has worked within the reasonable modification versus fundamental alteration framework it created.

Alexander v. Choate, 469 U.S. 287 (1985), answered the question the copied sentence had inherited from Title VI: does the prohibition reach only intentional discrimination, or also facially neutral practices with disproportionate effects. Tennessee had reduced its Medicaid program's coverage of inpatient hospital days from twenty to fourteen per year, and disabled plaintiffs argued the reduction disproportionately harmed them. The Court held that section 504 guarantees "meaningful access" to federally funded programs, not equal results. The fourteen day limit did not violate the section because it did not deny meaningful access to the Medicaid benefit; it applied equally to all recipients and left the benefit meaningfully available. At the same time, the Court held that disparate impact claims are cognizable under section 504 in some circumstances, rejecting the argument that only intentional discrimination is actionable. The meaningful access standard became the section's central substantive test: the question is whether the program, as structured, affords handicapped persons genuine access to its benefits, not whether outcomes are equal. The decision also confirmed that the section does not require a state to restructure its programs to achieve substantive equality of results, a limit that has bounded the statute's reach in benefits cases ever since.

School Board of Nassau County v. Arline, 480 U.S. 273 (1987), addressed whether a contagious disease could be a handicap under the section. The plaintiff was an elementary school teacher with tuberculosis who had been discharged after relapses. The Court held that a person with a contagious disease can be a handicapped individual within the meaning of the statute: tuberculosis substantially limited major life activities, and the record of and regarded as prongs of the definition also supported coverage. The Court then remanded for findings on whether the teacher was otherwise qualified, directing the lower courts to evaluate the risk of contagion on the basis of objective medical evidence rather than on fear or stereotype. The case is sometimes misremembered as involving asymptomatic HIV infection; it did not. It involved tuberculosis, and its holding about contagious disease was later applied in the HIV context. Arline established two principles that outlived its facts: that the definition of handicap reaches conditions whose primary disabling effect is the reaction of others, and that the otherwise qualified inquiry for contagious conditions must rest on medical evidence about actual risk, not on speculation.

Bragdon v. Abbott, 524 U.S. 624 (1998), was technically a case under the later comprehensive statute's public accommodations title, but its definition analysis governs section 504 because the two statutes share the definition of disability, a sharing made explicit when the 2008 amendments conformed the Rehabilitation Act definition to the ADA's. The plaintiff was an HIV positive woman, asymptomatic at the time, whose dentist refused to fill her cavity in his office. The Court held that asymptomatic HIV infection is a physical impairment from the moment of infection; that reproduction is a major life activity; and that HIV infection substantially limits reproduction, so the plaintiff was an individual with a disability. The Court further held that a health care provider's "direct threat" defense, the claim that treating the patient would pose a significant risk to others, must be based on objective medical evidence and is subject to judicial review rather than to the provider's subjective judgment. The majority's reasoning treated the definition as reaching conditions in their asymptomatic stages, a reading that expanded the protected class well beyond the visibly disabled. The dissenting justices would have required a tighter connection between the impairment and the claimed limitation, disputing the majority's treatment of reproduction as a major life activity and its conclusion that asymptomatic infection substantially limits it. The majority view prevailed, and after the 2008 conforming amendments its definition analysis applies to section 504 directly.

The dissent pattern across these cases is worth noting as a whole. Davis was unanimous. Choate produced no dissent on the core holdings. Arline and Bragdon divided the Court, with the divisions turning on how broadly to read the statutory definitions and how much deference to give to institutional judgments about risk and qualification. The through line of the majority holdings is a statute read neither as a guarantee of equal outcomes nor as a nullity: meaningful access, objective evidence, reasonable modification short of fundamental alteration, and a definition of disability broad enough to reach asymptomatic conditions.

Each of the four decisions can be read as answering a question the 1977 regulations had left to the courts. Davis answered the question the "otherwise qualified" definition had deferred: how much must a program change for the applicant. The Court's answer, that substantial modifications compromising essential nature are not required, gave the regulatory definition its operative boundary and has been cited in nearly every subsequent dispute about academic and programmatic adjustments. The unanimity of the decision amplified its authority; with no dissent to cabin the holding, the reasonable modification versus fundamental alteration framework became the default grammar of section 504 litigation across domains.

Choate answered the question the copied sentence had carried over from Title VI: whether the prohibition reaches disparate effects. The Court's two part answer, that meaningful access is the standard and that disparate impact claims are cognizable in some circumstances, threaded a needle between two untenable positions. A pure intent only reading would have left structural exclusions untouched; a pure effects reading would have required states to equalize outcomes across populations with radically different needs. Meaningful access asks instead whether the benefit as structured is genuinely available to handicapped persons, a test that invalidates exclusions without demanding restructuring. The fourteen day hospital limit survived because it applied across the board and left the Medicaid benefit meaningfully accessible; a limit designed around or foreseeably excluding disabled beneficiaries would present the harder case the opinion left open.

Arline and Bragdon together map the definition's expansion across two decades. Arline held that contagious disease can be a handicap and that the otherwise qualified inquiry for such conditions must rest on objective medical evidence, a holding that moved the definition beyond visible functional limitation into the realm of condition based exclusion. Bragdon extended the logic to asymptomatic infection, holding that HIV is a physical impairment from the moment of infection and that reproduction counts as a major life activity substantially limited by the infection. The dissent in Bragdon would have demanded a tighter nexus between impairment and limitation, but the majority's broader reading prevailed and, through the 2008 conforming amendments, now governs section 504 directly. The arc from Davis's narrowing to Bragdon's broadening traces the statute's maturation: the protected class contracted at the qualification gate in 1979 and expanded at the definitional gate through the 1980s and 1990s.

A fifth Supreme Court encounter with the section's history deserves mention for what it preserved. In Lane v. Pena, 518 U.S. 187 (1996), the Court addressed the immunity question that Congress had resolved by statute a decade earlier, and Justice Stevens in dissent quoted the 1973 enacted text of the section verbatim, preserving in the United States Reports the original "handicapped individual" and "his handicap" formulation. The dissent's quotation is one of the cleanest sources for the as enacted language, because the current code no longer carries it.

### What damages can a section 504 plaintiff recover?

Compensatory damages for economic harm on a showing of intentional discrimination, generally deliberate indifference, plus attorney's fees for a prevailing plaintiff. Punitive damages are unavailable under Barnes v. Gorman. Emotional distress damages were contested among the circuits at this article's 2015 reference date.

The Supreme Court held them unavailable in Cummings v. Premier Rehab Keller in 2022, a later development presented here with its date rather than as contemporary law.

## The Doctrine After Bragdon: How the Frameworks Work in Practice

The four Supreme Court decisions supply the vocabulary, but the daily work of section 504 happens in the lower courts, agencies, and compliance offices that apply that vocabulary to new facts. The "otherwise qualified" inquiry from Davis has become a structured analysis: identify the essential requirements of the program, ask whether the person can meet them with reasonable accommodation, and ask whether the requested accommodation would fundamentally alter the program's nature. In education, the essential-requirements step protects academic standards, so a university need not waive a clinical competency that patient safety requires, but it must provide the auxiliary aids that let a student demonstrate the competency. In health care, the inquiry protects medical judgment exercised on objective evidence while forbidding judgment based on stereotype, the Arline and Bragdon line.

The "solely by reason of" causation standard sets a strict test. The plaintiff must show that the disability was the reason for the adverse action, which in practice means defeating the defendant's legitimate explanations. A university that denies admission to a student with a disability and points to academic qualifications forces the court to examine whether the qualifications were applied evenhandedly or were pretextual. A hospital that transfers a patient with HIV and cites medical necessity forces the court to examine the objective medical basis for the decision. Litigators on both sides treat the causation inquiry as the factual center of most cases, with the definition of disability and the qualification question serving as threshold gates.

The meaningful access framework from Choate operates as the limit on disparate impact theory under the section. Courts applying Choate ask whether a facially neutral policy denies people with disabilities meaningful access to the benefit, distinguishing between policies that incidentally burden and policies that effectively exclude. The framework's flexibility is its strength and its weakness: it reaches genuinely exclusionary policies without converting every funding decision with unequal effects into a violation, but it leaves the line between burden and exclusion to be drawn case by case.

The objective medical evidence requirement from Arline and Bragdon has proven durable beyond contagious disease, extending to any context where a recipient justifies exclusion on safety or health grounds. Schools, employers, and health care providers invoking safety must ground the assessment in current medical knowledge and individualized evaluation rather than in generalizations about the condition. The requirement does not eliminate safety-based exclusions; it disciplines them, forcing the decisionmaker to show the work. That discipline reflects the 1974 amendments' "regarded as" prong at work: much of what passes for safety judgment is fear of a condition rather than assessment of a risk, and the statute treats the two differently.

## Why the Later Statute Did Not Supersede It

The most common error about section 504 is the assumption that the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 replaced it. It did not, and the non replacement was deliberate, structural, and consequential. The two statutes coexist, cover overlapping but distinct ground, and in a significant class of cases the older funding condition provision is the better vehicle for a plaintiff. Three mechanisms keep the older provision alive and independent.

First, the later statute was written to align with the older one rather than to displace it. Section 204(b) of the ADA, at 42 U.S.C. 12134(b), required the Attorney General's Title II regulations to be consistent with the coordination regulations issued under section 504. The Justice Department's Title II regulation, at 28 CFR 35.103(a), provides expressly that the part "shall not be construed to apply a lesser standard than the standards applied under title V of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (29 U.S.C. 791) or the regulations issued by Federal agencies pursuant to that title." The 1991 preamble to the Title II regulations stated that the Title II standards are generally the same as those required under section 504 for federally assisted programs. The instruction runs one way: the newer public entity standards may not fall below the older funding condition standards. A reader who wants the full map of the newer statute should consult this series' guide to the [Americans with Disabilities Act](/2015/06/01/americans-with-disabilities-act-guide/), and a reader who wants the side by side comparison of the two regimes should consult the dedicated comparison of [ADA and section 504 coverage](/2015/10/01/ada-vs-section-504-coverage/).

Second, Congress conformed the definitions rather than duplicating them. The ADA Amendments Act of 2008, Public Law 110-325, in its section 7 conforming amendments, amended section 7 of the Rehabilitation Act at 29 U.S.C. 705 so that "disability" under the Rehabilitation Act carries the meaning given in section 3 of the ADA, effective January 1, 2009. The Education Department's guidance on the amendments confirms that the broadened construction rules, including the expanded treatment of major life activities and the disregard of mitigating measures, apply to section 504 as well as to the ADA. The two statutes thus share a definition by express statutory instruction, which means Bragdon's definition analysis and the 2008 expansions flow into section 504 cases directly.

Third, the older statute's distinct procedural and immunity rules make it the preferable vehicle in a significant class of cases, and this is the complication the brief requires this article to carry. Section 504 suits generally require no exhaustion of administrative remedies before filing, while employment claims under the ADA's employment title require a charge filed with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. And the 1986 immunity waiver, 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7, makes states amenable to section 504 damages suits on a funding waiver theory that has no parallel of equal strength under the ADA's public entity title. A plaintiff suing a state university, a state hospital, or a state agency will often plead section 504 precisely because the immunity and exhaustion posture is better there. The later comprehensive statute expanded the universe of covered actors to include private businesses and state and local governments regardless of funding; it did not repeal the funding condition, and the funding condition's advantages survived the expansion intact.

### Why did the ADA not supersede section 504?

Because Congress aligned the standards instead of replacing them. The ADA's Title II regulation at 28 CFR 35.103(a) forbids applying a lesser standard than the Rehabilitation Act standards, and the 2008 amendments conformed the section 504 definition of disability to the ADA's.

The older statute also kept two advantages the newer one lacks: no administrative exhaustion before suit and an express statutory waiver of state sovereign immunity tied to federal funds.

## The Borrowing That Built the ADA

The relationship between section 504 and the Americans with Disabilities Act runs deeper than coexistence; the later statute was drafted against the background of the older one, and its drafters borrowed freely. The ADA's definition of disability was modeled on the Rehabilitation Act's three prong definition. The reasonable accommodation concept in the ADA's employment title descends from subpart B of the 1977 regulations. The program accessibility standard of the ADA's public entity title echoes subpart C. The effective communication duties echo subpart F. The direct threat defense, construed in Bragdon, appears in both statutes. The borrowing was deliberate: by 1990 there were nearly two decades of regulatory text and case law under section 504, and the ADA's drafters used that accumulated meaning as their starting vocabulary rather than inventing a new one.

The borrowing explains why the non supersession point is structural rather than sentimental. When Congress writes a new statute using the old statute's words, it incorporates the old statute's construed meaning, and the courts read the two together. The 2008 conforming amendments made the incorporation express for the definition. The Title II regulation made it express for the standards. The result is a paired regime in which the older funding condition provision and the newer comprehensive statute interpret each other, and in which the older provision's procedural advantages, the private action without exhaustion and the immunity waiver, give it independent life in the cases where those advantages decide the outcome.

## Schools: The 504 Plan

In American public education, section 504 is known less as a statute than as a document: the 504 plan. The plan is the school level mechanism for delivering the free appropriate public education that the 1977 regulations require, and understanding it requires distinguishing the regulation's functional requirements from the paperwork schools have built around them.

The regulatory source is Subpart D of 34 CFR Part 104, specifically section 104.33. It requires recipients operating public elementary or secondary education programs to provide a free appropriate public education to each qualified handicapped person within their jurisdiction. Section 104.33(b)(1) defines the "appropriate education" comparatively: the provision of regular or special education and related aids and services designed to meet the individual educational needs of handicapped persons as adequately as the needs of nonhandicapped persons are met. Section 104.33(c)(1) requires that the education be provided without cost to the student or the family, apart from fees imposed on nonhandicapped students. Before any placement, the recipient must evaluate the student, section 104.35, and placement decisions must be made by a group of persons knowledgeable about the student, the evaluation data, and the placement options, section 104.35(c). What schools colloquially call a 504 plan is a written document recording that evaluation, the accommodations and related services determined necessary, and the placement decision. The regulation does not mandate a specific document with specific contents; it mandates the evaluation, the knowledgeable group, and the services, and the plan is how schools operationalize those mandates.

The 504 plan must be distinguished from the individualized education program under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, and the confusion between the two is one of the most persistent errors in the field. A reader who wants the full treatment of the education statute should consult this series' guide to [special education law](/2014/10/01/idea-special-education-law-guide/). The distinctions are structural. Section 504 is a civil rights nondiscrimination law; the IDEA is an educational benefit law that provides federal funding for special education. The section 504 definition of disability is broader: it reaches any physical or mental impairment substantially limiting a major life activity, while the IDEA covers only students falling within its thirteen enumerated categories who need special education by reason of those disabilities. Every student covered by the IDEA is also covered by section 504, but the converse is not true; a student with a health condition or impairment that does not fit an IDEA category may still be entitled to accommodations under section 504. The section 504 regulations require no specific written document equivalent to the IEP, no mandated team composition beyond the knowledgeable group, and fewer procedural safeguards. Section 504 carries no federal funding stream comparable to the IDEA's; the Office for Civil Rights enforces it as a civil rights obligation, not as a grant condition with dollars attached.

### How does a 504 plan differ from an IEP?

A 504 plan documents accommodations under a civil rights law; an IEP is a detailed written program under the IDEA, an educational-benefit law with its own funding and procedures. Section 504 covers a broader group, requires no specific document format, and is enforced by the Education Department's Office for Civil Rights rather than through IDEA due process hearings.

The exhaustion caveat belongs here because it is where the caveat bites most often. The general rule is that a section 504 plaintiff may file a private lawsuit at any time without first filing an administrative complaint or exhausting administrative remedies: the regulations contain no exhaustion requirement, the Justice Department's Title VI manual cites Brennan v. King, 139 F.3d 258, 268 n.12 (1st Cir. 1998), for the proposition that section 504 derives its procedural requirements from Title VI, which has no exhaustion requirement, and the Office for Civil Rights guidance confirms that a person may file a private lawsuit without first going through the agency. But where the relief sought is also available under the IDEA, the IDEA's own exhaustion provision at 20 U.S.C. section 1415(l) requires exhaustion of the IDEA's administrative procedures before a section 504 or ADA suit may proceed. The Supreme Court established the test in Fry v. Napoleon Community Schools, 581 U.S. 154 (2017): if the gravamen of the complaint seeks relief for the denial of a free appropriate public education, the IDEA procedures must be exhausted first, even when the complaint is pleaded under section 504. The rule is therefore no exhaustion as a general matter, with a school specific exception where the claim is in substance an IDEA claim wearing section 504 clothing.

The evaluation and placement mechanics repay attention because they are where the regulation's functional approach differs most visibly from the IDEA's procedural formalism. Section 104.35 requires evaluation before any initial placement and before any significant change in placement, drawing on a variety of sources and administered by trained personnel. Section 104.35(c) requires the placement decision to be made by a group including persons knowledgeable about the student, the meaning of the evaluation data, and the placement options, a deliberately flexible composition that contrasts with the IDEA's mandated team membership. The regulation requires periodic reevaluation but sets no specific timeline comparable to the IDEA's triennial cycle, leaving the interval to the recipient's judgment. Schools that build elaborate 504 plan documents with mandated participants and timelines are adding procedural structure the regulation does not require; the regulation requires only the evaluation, the knowledgeable group, and the resulting services.

The procedural safeguards comparison completes the picture. The IDEA provides parents with prior written notice, consent requirements, independent educational evaluations at public expense in defined circumstances, mediation, due process hearings with specific timelines, and stay put protections during disputes. Section 504's regulation requires notice, an opportunity for parents to examine records, an impartial hearing with parental participation, and a review procedure, but leaves the details to the recipient. The Office for Civil Rights enforces these obligations as civil rights requirements rather than as conditions on a funding stream, which means a violation can draw federal enforcement without any grant dollars being at stake for the particular service. Parents navigating both systems need to understand that the 504 plan's informality is a feature of the regulation, not a sign that the protections are lesser; the substantive right to a free appropriate public education is stated in mandatory terms even where the procedures are spare.

### Must a student exhaust administrative remedies before suing under section 504?

Generally no. Section 504 plaintiffs may file a private lawsuit without first complaining to the agency or exhausting administrative remedies, a rule confirmed by OCR guidance and the courts, because the regulations contain no exhaustion requirement. The IDEA school context is the exception.

Where the suit seeks relief that is also available under the IDEA, the IDEA's procedures at 20 U.S.C. section 1415(l) must be exhausted first, under Fry v. Napoleon Community Schools.

## Enforcement Without Exhaustion

Outside the school context, enforcement of section 504 runs on two tracks that operate independently. The administrative track follows the Title VI model: each federal funding agency investigates complaints against its recipients, conducts compliance reviews, seeks voluntary compliance, and may ultimately terminate federal funds after an administrative hearing. Recipients are required to designate a compliance coordinator, adopt grievance procedures for the prompt resolution of complaints, and provide assurances of compliance as a condition of receiving funds. Fund termination is the ultimate sanction and, as under Title VI, it is rarely used; its power lies in the threat, which drives the voluntary compliance process that resolves most matters.

The private track is the implied right of action in federal court, and it is the track that has produced the doctrine. Because no exhaustion is required, a plaintiff may go directly to court, which makes the private action faster and more predictable than the administrative process and explains why the case law, rather than agency enforcement records, is the primary source of the statute's meaning. The Office for Civil Rights in the Education Department handles education complaints administratively, the Justice Department coordinates government wide enforcement, and each funding agency enforces within its domain, but the courthouse door stands open without a prior agency stop. That openness is itself a legacy of the copied sentence: Title VI practice supplied the model, and the model included direct private enforcement.

The administrative complaint mechanics are worth describing because they are the route most non lawyers encounter first. A person who believes a recipient violated the section may file a complaint with the federal agency that funds the recipient: the Education Department's Office for Civil Rights for schools and colleges, the Health and Human Services Department for hospitals and health programs, and the corresponding offices elsewhere. The agency evaluates the complaint, may investigate, and seeks voluntary compliance where it finds a violation. Recipients are separately required to maintain their own grievance procedures with due process protections, so a complainant may also pursue the internal process. Neither the agency complaint nor the internal grievance is a prerequisite to filing suit; the private action stands open from the start, and the administrative and judicial tracks may proceed on parallel courses. The practical advice embedded in that structure is that the agency route costs nothing and can produce compliance without litigation, while the court route offers damages and enforceable orders at the price of proving intentional discrimination.

## What Compliance Cost and What It Changed

The honest assessment of section 504's effects must present the compliance cost concerns alongside the access gains, because both were argued contemporaneously and both shaped the statute's development. The cost argument, raised by covered institutions during the 1976 comment period and in the decades since, holds that the regulations imposed substantial obligations, particularly around physical accessibility and program modification, without a dedicated federal funding stream to pay for them. Universities faced the expense of making programs accessible across sprawling campuses; hospitals faced restructuring of intake, treatment, and communication practices; school districts faced the cost of evaluations, accommodations, and related services for a broader population than the special education statutes covered. The program accessibility standard, requiring programs viewed in their entirety to be readily accessible, was designed as a compromise between full retrofitting and inaction, but its application to specific facilities and programs generated sustained dispute about how much was enough.

The access argument, pressed by disability organizations from the 1970s forward, holds that the statute ended categorical exclusions that had operated without justification: schools that refused to enroll children with disabilities, colleges that denied admission on the basis of impairment alone, hospitals that turned away patients, employers in federally assisted programs that would not consider qualified applicants. The regulations' reasonable accommodation framework, the FAPE requirement in schools, and the program accessibility standard gave those exclusions a legal answer for the first time. The 1977 occupation itself is evidence of the access argument's force: hundreds of people sustained a twenty-five day occupation of a federal building because the promise of 1973 had gone unfulfilled for four years.

Measured effects are harder to state with the precision the series demands, and the verification record for this article does not supply enrollment numbers, compliance rates, or dollar figures that meet the no invented numbers rule. What can be stated from the durable record is structural. The statute created the first federal regulatory definition of disability discrimination and the first government wide enforcement framework for it. It supplied the sentence structure, the regulatory template, and the case law that the ADA's drafters borrowed in 1990, which is why the later statute could move as fast as it did. It made the federal funding relationship a civil rights lever across education, health care, and social services simultaneously, reaching sectors that no single program statute could have reached. And it established the principle, through the immunity waiver, that states accepting federal money accept the nondiscrimination conditions attached to it, a principle that extends well beyond disability law.

The compliance cost debate was never fully resolved because it is not fully resolvable in the abstract. The costs were real and fell on institutions that had not budgeted for them; the exclusions were real and fell on people who had no other remedy. The regulations split the difference with standards like program accessibility and reasonable accommodation that require judgment in application, and the courts have spent four decades supplying that judgment case by case. That is the implementation creating the statute's meaning, extended across decades: the text states a principle, and the principle's price and scope are worked out in enforcement.

The program accessibility standard illustrates the compromise character of the regulatory settlement. Rather than requiring every existing facility to meet new construction standards, section 104.22 required recipients to operate each program so that, viewed in its entirety, it was readily accessible to handicapped persons. Compliance could be achieved through structural changes, reassignment of services to accessible locations, assignment of aides, home visits, or other methods, and recipients were given a timetable with outside dates for achieving accessibility. Covered institutions argued during the comment period that even this flexible standard imposed uncertain and potentially large costs, particularly for older campuses and facilities, while disability organizations argued that flexibility without deadlines would reproduce the very inaction the regulations were meant to end. The standard's endurance suggests the compromise held: it remains the operative framework for existing facilities, and the disputes it generates concern its application to specific programs rather than its legitimacy.

The statute's structural contribution to American civil rights law extends beyond its direct beneficiaries. By establishing that federal money carries nondiscrimination conditions enforceable through private lawsuits, section 504 supplied the template Congress reused for subsequent funding condition statutes and the doctrinal foundation the courts applied to them. The spending clause contract analogy developed in Barnes and extended in Cummings now governs the remedies of an entire family of statutes, and the immunity waiver technique of the 1986 amendments has been invoked across the civil rights funding conditions. The sentence that had no hearing thus became, through its implementation and litigation, one of the load bearing walls of the federal civil rights architecture, supporting structures its drafters never imagined.

## What the Statute Did Not Do

A profile of this statute must state its limits as plainly as its reach, because the limits are where readers most often go wrong. Section 504 does not bind private actors that receive no federal financial assistance; a private business with no federal funds and no federal contract is outside the section even if it is covered by the ADA's public accommodations title. It does not guarantee equal results; Alexander v. Choate's meaningful access standard is explicit that the statute promises genuine access to the benefit, not equality of outcome. It does not require fundamental alterations of programs; Davis drew that line in 1979 and it has held. It does not provide punitive damages, and after Cummings it does not provide emotional distress damages. It does not, by itself, fund the accommodations it requires; the obligations are conditions on money appropriated for other purposes, which is the structural source of the compliance cost debate. And it does not operate through a single enforcement agency with a single complaint process; enforcement is scattered across funding agencies under coordination guidelines, with the private lawsuit as the unifying thread.

Two further limits clarify the section's place among its companions. Unlike sections 501 and 503, section 504 imposes no affirmative action duty: it bars discrimination and requires reasonable accommodation, but it does not require recipients to adopt hiring goals, timetables, or preference programs for people with disabilities. The distinction matters because readers sometimes import the affirmative action vocabulary of the employment sections into the general nondiscrimination section, where it does not belong. And the section's damages remedy runs against recipients of federal funds, not against the United States itself as a general matter; Lane v. Pena's treatment of sovereign immunity preserved the distinction between the recipient who takes the money and the sovereign who disburses it, with the 1986 waiver addressing state recipients rather than the federal government.

The fund termination sanction deserves a final clarifying note because its rarity is sometimes mistaken for its absence. Termination of federal funds after an administrative hearing is the ultimate administrative sanction under the Title VI modeled procedures, and it is genuinely available; agencies have the authority and have used the threat of it to secure voluntary compliance in countless matters. What agencies almost never do is actually terminate the funds of a major recipient, because termination punishes the beneficiaries of the program along with the discriminating administrator, cutting off the services the statute exists to make accessible. The sanction's power is therefore almost entirely deterrent, and the enforcement system is designed around that deterrence: investigate, negotiate, secure a compliance agreement, and reserve termination for the recipient that will not come into compliance any other way.

## Reading Section 504: A Researcher's Checklist

A reader who wants to work with section 504 rather than merely admire it needs a short checklist, because the statute's layered history creates traps for the unwary. First, date the text being quoted. The 1973 version speaks of the "handicapped individual" and "his handicap"; the current version speaks of the "individual with a disability" and "her or his disability," reflects the 1978 extension to federally conducted programs, and incorporates the terminology changes of 1988 and 1992. Citing the wrong version's vocabulary in a brief or a paper signals unfamiliarity with the statute's evolution. Second, identify the right regulation. Section 504 lives not in a single rule but in a family of agency regulations descended from 45 CFR Part 84, coordinated under 45 CFR 85; the Education Department's rules govern schools, the health agencies' rules govern hospitals, and the coordination guidelines keep them consistent. Third, apply the conformed definition: since January 1, 2009, the Rehabilitation Act's definition of disability carries the ADA Amendments Act's broadened construction, so pre-2009 case law narrowing the definition must be read with the conforming amendments in view.

Fourth, map the enforcement route before choosing it. The administrative complaint is free, requires no lawyer, and can produce systemic relief, but it moves slowly and depends on agency priorities. The private lawsuit is faster and offers damages, but damages require intentional discrimination under the deliberate-indifference standard, exclude punitive damages after Barnes, and, for cases decided after April 2022, exclude emotional distress damages after Cummings. Fifth, check exhaustion only where the IDEA is implicated: the general rule is no exhaustion, per Brennan v. King and OCR guidance, with the Fry exception for relief also available under the IDEA. Sixth, remember the funding trigger: the defendant must be a recipient of federal financial assistance or a federal agency conducting a program, and the complaint should identify the funds. A checklist this short cannot substitute for the analysis the article has provided, but it converts the article's history into a working method, which is what the series promises its research-grade readers.

## Closing: The Statute as Implementation Made It

The series thesis for this article is that a statute's meaning can be created by its implementation, and section 504 is the clearest case in the series. The enacted text was a single sentence copied from older statutes, added without debate to a funding bill, carrying almost no legislative history. For four years it had no regulations and therefore no operative content. The regulations that finally gave it content were produced by a court order, an executive order, and a twenty-five day occupation of a federal building. The Supreme Court then spent two decades construing the sentence's borrowed phrases: otherwise qualified, meaningful access, handicapped individual. Congress returned repeatedly to adjust the definitions, the remedies, and the immunity rules, and in 2008 it conformed the old definition to the new statute's. The later comprehensive statute aligned its standards to the older one's by express instruction rather than superseding it, and the older one's funding condition advantages, no exhaustion and the immunity waiver, keep it independently alive in the cases where those advantages matter.

A reader who can explain that sequence, the copied sentence, the four silent years, the occupation, the regulations, the cases, and the non supersession, understands the statute better than any summary of its text alone could convey.

The namable claim of this article restates the point as a proposition about lawmaking itself. The sentence that had no hearing became the most litigated disability rights provision in American law not because Congress deliberated over it but because an agency, a court, and a protest movement gave it content Congress never supplied. That is implementation creating a statute's meaning in its purest form: the enacted text was trivial in length, the regulatory struggle was the whole story, and the struggle's product, the 1977 rules and the coordination regime they anchored, has governed for nearly five decades. Students of legislation sometimes treat enactment as the moment a law is made; section 504 is the counterexample that proves the rule's limits, a law made in the Federal Register, in the courtroom, and in an occupied federal building in San Francisco. The sentence had no hearing. The rules had an occupation. And the law that resulted has outlasted every prediction that the newer statute would absorb it, because a funding condition with a private right of action, an immunity waiver, and no exhaustion requirement is a durable instrument, and durability was built into it from the day the older sentence was copied. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does Section 504 require?

Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 provides that no otherwise qualified individual with a disability shall, solely by reason of disability, be excluded from participation in, denied the benefits of, or subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving federal financial assistance. In practice this means that schools, colleges, hospitals, and social service agencies that accept federal money must operate their programs without discriminating on the basis of disability. They must provide reasonable accommodations, make their programs accessible when viewed as a whole, and give qualified students a free appropriate public education. The statute does not require equal outcomes, only meaningful access, and it does not require changes that would fundamentally alter the nature of a program. Enforcement runs through federal funding agencies and through private lawsuits in federal court.

### Q: Who is covered by Section 504?

Section 504 protects otherwise qualified individuals with disabilities, a term that covers people with a physical or mental impairment substantially limiting a major life activity, people with a record of such an impairment, and people regarded as having one. The 2008 conforming amendments aligned this definition with the Americans with Disabilities Act, effective January 1, 2009, so the broadened construction rules apply here too. On the other side of the relationship, the statute binds programs or activities receiving federal financial assistance, plus programs conducted by federal executive agencies and the Postal Service under the 1978 amendments. It does not bind private businesses or individuals that receive no federal funds. The funding trigger is the jurisdictional key: take the money and the nondiscrimination condition attaches.

### Q: Why did it take four years to write Section 504 regulations?

The Department of Health, Education, and Welfare did not issue final section 504 regulations until April 1977, nearly four years after the statute's September 1973 enactment. Several causes combined. The provision was unprecedented in scope, touching education, health care, and social services at once, and no agency had ever drafted comprehensive disability nondiscrimination rules. Covered institutions raised compliance cost concerns during the process. The 1974 amendments contained no express order to issue rules, only committee report language expecting swift comprehensive regulations. It took the July 1976 decision in Cherry v. Mathews, which held the secretary was required to promulgate the regulations, plus Executive Order 11914 coordinating government wide implementation, plus the April 1977 national protests, to convert drafts into signed final rules.

### Q: What was the 1977 sit-in over Section 504?

In the spring of 1977, disability rights demonstrators occupied offices of the Department of Health, Education, and Welfare to demand that the secretary sign the long delayed section 504 regulations. After the American Coalition of Citizens with Disabilities set an April 4 deadline that passed without action, protests hit department offices in ten cities on April 5. Most occupations ended within a day or two, including a roughly twenty-six hour overnight occupation of the secretary's office suite in Washington. The San Francisco occupation of the department's regional office lasted from April 5 to April 30, twenty-five days, and is widely described as the longest nonviolent occupation of a federal building in American history. Secretary Califano signed the regulations on April 28, 1977, and they were published May 4.

### Q: Did Nixon veto the Rehabilitation Act before signing it?

President Nixon vetoed two predecessor bills before signing the Rehabilitation Act of 1973. The first was a pocket veto of H.R. 8395 in October 1972, after the 92nd Congress had adjourned. The second was a returned veto of S. 7 on March 27, 1973; the Senate sustained that veto on April 3, 1973, by 60 to 36, short of the two thirds needed to override. Both vetoes were on spending grounds: Nixon objected to the bills' authorization levels as inconsistent with holding down taxes and prices, calling the vetoed bill fiscally and programmatically unsound in his later signing statement. Neither veto message objected to the disability nondiscrimination provision. The compromise bill, H.R. 8070, passed both chambers overwhelmingly and was signed September 26, 1973, as Public Law 93-112.

### Q: Is Section 504 modeled on Title VI and Title IX?

Yes. The operative sentence of section 504 was modeled on the language of section 601 of Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, codified at 42 U.S.C. 2000d, which bars race, color, and national origin discrimination in federally assisted programs. Title IX of the Education Amendments of 1972, at 20 U.S.C. 1681(a), was itself patterned on Title VI. All three provisions share one sentence skeleton: no person shall, on the ground of the protected characteristic, be excluded from participation in, denied the benefits of, or subjected to discrimination under any program or activity receiving federal financial assistance. Section 504 changed the characteristic to disability and added the qualifier otherwise qualified. The lineage matters because the enforcement machinery and interpretive assumptions of the older statutes traveled with the copied sentence.

### Q: Can you get damages under Section 504?

Compensatory damages are available to prevailing plaintiffs who prove intentional discrimination, which most circuits measure by a deliberate indifference standard, and prevailing plaintiffs may also recover reasonable attorney's fees. Punitive damages are not available: the Supreme Court held in Barnes v. Gorman, 536 U.S. 181 (2002), that the contract law analogy governing spending clause statutes bars them. Emotional distress damages were the subject of a circuit split at this article's 2015 reference date; the Supreme Court later resolved the split in Cummings v. Premier Rehab Keller, 596 U.S. 212, decided April 28, 2022, holding that emotional distress damages are not recoverable under spending clause statutes. States that accept federal funds face the same remedies as other defendants under the 1986 immunity waiver at 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7.

### Q: What is a Section 504 plan in school?

A 504 plan is the written document schools use to record the evaluation, accommodations, and related services provided to a student protected by section 504. The regulatory source is 34 CFR 104.33, which requires public schools to provide a free appropriate public education to each qualified handicapped student, defined as services meeting the student's individual needs as adequately as the needs of nonhandicapped students are met, at no cost to the family. The regulation requires evaluation before placement and placement decisions by a knowledgeable group, but it does not prescribe a specific document or team format; the plan is how schools operationalize those duties. A 504 plan differs from an IDEA individualized education program: section 504 is a civil rights law with a broader definition and no federal funding stream, while the IDEA is a funded educational benefit law with its own categories and procedures.

### Q: What did the 1978 amendments add to Section 504?

The Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1978, Public Law 95-602, made two structural additions to the section 504 regime. First, they extended the nondiscrimination prohibition beyond federally assisted programs to programs conducted by federal executive agencies themselves and by the United States Postal Service, closing the gap that had left the government's own operations outside the section's reach. Second, they added section 505 of the act, which makes available the remedies, procedures, and rights set forth in Title VI of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 for section 504 violations. That remedies provision supplied the statutory footing for the private damages action the courts had already begun to recognize. The coverage extension and the remedies section are the changes that reshaped section 504 litigation.

### Q: What is Section 508 of the Rehabilitation Act?

Section 508, codified at 29 U.S.C. 794d, requires that electronic and information technology developed, procured, maintained, or used by federal agencies be accessible to individuals with disabilities. It was added to the Rehabilitation Act by the 1986 amendments, Public Law 99-506, in an original form concerned with electronic office equipment and framed as nonbinding guidelines. Congress substantially rewrote it as part of the Workforce Investment Act of 1998, Public Law 105-220, converting the guidelines into binding, enforceable accessibility standards incorporated into the Federal Acquisition Regulation so they apply at the procurement stage. The section now governs the accessibility of federal websites, software, telecommunications, and office technology. Enforcement runs through administrative complaints to the procuring agency and private actions for injunctive relief and attorney's fees.

### Q: What did Cherry v. Mathews decide?

Cherry v. Mathews, 419 F. Supp. 922, decided July 19, 1976, by the federal district court for the District of Columbia, held that the Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare was required to promulgate regulations effectuating section 504. The court relied on the 1974 amendments' legislative history, particularly Senate Report 93-1297, which contemplated swift implementation through comprehensive regulations, to establish the duty even though the amendments contained no express statutory command to issue rules. The court retained jurisdiction to prevent further unreasonable delay, placing the department under a judicial order to complete the rulemaking. The decision is the legal bridge between congressional expectation and executive action: without it, the department's four year inaction had no judicial check. The final regulations followed in April 1977, during the national protests.

### Q: What did Southeastern Community College v. Davis hold?

Southeastern Community College v. Davis, 442 U.S. 397, decided June 11, 1979, was the Supreme Court's first section 504 decision. The plaintiff, a hearing impaired applicant to a nursing program, had been denied admission. Justice Powell wrote for a unanimous Court, holding that she was not an otherwise qualified handicapped individual because section 504 does not require substantial modifications that would compromise a program's essential nature. The Court distinguished reasonable modifications, which the statute may require, from fundamental alterations, which it does not. A nursing program requiring the ability to understand spoken communication in clinical settings was defining its program, not discriminating, when it declined to admit someone unable to meet that requirement even with accommodation. Davis made the otherwise qualified inquiry the statute's central gatekeeper and set the balance between access and institutional autonomy.

### Q: What did Alexander v. Choate establish?

Alexander v. Choate, 469 U.S. 287 (1985), established that section 504 guarantees meaningful access to federally funded programs, not equal results. Tennessee had reduced Medicaid coverage of inpatient hospital days from twenty to fourteen per year, and disabled plaintiffs challenged the cut. The Court upheld the limit, holding it did not deny meaningful access because it applied equally and left the benefit genuinely available, while also holding that disparate impact claims are cognizable under section 504 in some circumstances. The decision resolved the ambiguity the section had inherited from Title VI about whether the copied sentence reached only intentional discrimination. The meaningful access standard became the section's central substantive test in benefits cases: the question is whether the program affords handicapped persons genuine access, not whether outcomes are equal across groups.

### Q: What did School Board of Nassau County v. Arline decide?

School Board of Nassau County v. Arline, 480 U.S. 273 (1987), held that a person with a contagious disease can be a handicapped individual under section 504. The plaintiff was an elementary school teacher with tuberculosis who had been discharged after relapses. The Court held that tuberculosis substantially limited major life activities and that the record of and regarded as prongs of the definition also supported coverage. It then remanded for findings on whether she was otherwise qualified, directing the lower courts to assess contagion risk on objective medical evidence rather than fear or stereotype. The case is sometimes misremembered as involving asymptomatic HIV infection; it involved tuberculosis, though its reasoning was later applied in the HIV context. Arline established that the definition reaches conditions whose disabling effect runs through others' reactions and that risk assessments must rest on medicine, not speculation.

### Q: What did Bragdon v. Abbott hold?

Bragdon v. Abbott, 524 U.S. 624 (1998), held that asymptomatic HIV infection is a physical impairment, that reproduction is a major life activity, and that HIV infection substantially limits reproduction, making the plaintiff an individual with a disability. The case arose under the public accommodations title of the Americans with Disabilities Act, but its definition analysis governs section 504 because the statutes share the definition, a sharing Congress made explicit in the 2008 conforming amendments. The Court further held that a health care provider's direct threat defense must rest on objective medical evidence and is subject to judicial review, not on the provider's subjective judgment. The dissenting justices would have read the definition more narrowly, disputing the treatment of reproduction as a major life activity. The majority's broad reading of the definition applies directly to section 504 after the 2008 amendments.

### Q: What does Section 501 of the Rehabilitation Act require?

Section 501, codified at 29 U.S.C. 791, governs the federal government as an employer. It requires each federal department and agency to maintain an affirmative program for the hiring, placement, and advancement of individuals with disabilities and bars discrimination in federal employment decisions. Unlike section 504's pure nondiscrimination rule, section 501 imposes affirmative action duties: agencies must take positive steps, set goals, and report progress rather than merely refrain from discriminating. Enforcement runs through the administrative complaint process with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission as the reviewing body, and a federal employee must exhaust that administrative process before filing a private action in court. That exhaustion requirement contrasts with section 504, where no administrative exhaustion is generally required before suit.

### Q: What does Section 503 require of federal contractors?

Section 503, codified at 29 U.S.C. 793, requires employers holding federal contracts or subcontracts above the statutory threshold to take affirmative action to employ and advance qualified individuals with disabilities and bars disability discrimination in their employment decisions. Enforcement belongs exclusively to the Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs in the Labor Department, which investigates complaints and can impose sanctions up to debarment from federal contracting. Unlike section 504, section 503 creates no private right of action: an individual who believes a contractor violated the section must complain to the Labor Department, and only the department can pursue the matter. The section thus protects workers at federal contractors through agency enforcement rather than private lawsuits, the sharpest procedural contrast with the private action centered regime of section 504.

### Q: How did the ADA Amendments Act of 2008 change Section 504?

The ADA Amendments Act of 2008, Public Law 110-325, reached back into the Rehabilitation Act through its section 7 conforming amendments, which rewrote the definition of disability at 29 U.S.C. 705 so that it carries the meaning given in section 3 of the Americans with Disabilities Act. Effective January 1, 2009, the broadened construction rules of the 2008 act, including the expanded treatment of major life activities and major bodily functions and the rule that mitigating measures are disregarded, apply to section 504 as well as to the ADA. The Education Department's guidance confirms the conformed reading. Congress thus aligned the two statutes' definitions by express instruction rather than letting them drift apart, which is why Supreme Court definition cases decided under the ADA, such as Bragdon v. Abbott, inform section 504 analysis directly.

### Q: What did the 1986 amendments change about suing state governments?

Section 1003 of the Rehabilitation Act Amendments of 1986, Public Law 99-506, titled the Civil Rights Remedies Equalization provision and codified at 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7, provides that a state is not immune under the Eleventh Amendment from suit in federal court for violating Section 504 or the other listed funding-condition statutes. The same remedies are available against a state as against any other defendant. Congress enacted it to supply the clear statement of abrogation the Supreme Court had required in Atascadero State Hospital v. Scanlon, 473 U.S. 234 (1985). Doctrinally it operates as a waiver accepted as a condition of taking federal funds, effective for violations occurring after October 21, 1986.

### Q: Why did the ADA not replace Section 504?

Congress designed the two statutes to coexist, with the Rehabilitation Act as the floor. The Justice Department's Title II regulation at 28 CFR 35.103(a) provides that Title II shall not be construed to apply a lesser standard than the standards under Title V of the Rehabilitation Act, and ADA section 204(b) required consistency with the Section 504 coordination regulations. The ADA Amendments Act of 2008 then conformed the Rehabilitation Act's disability definition to the ADA's by amending 29 U.S.C. 705, effective January 1, 2009. Section 504 retains independent force because its funding trigger reaches private recipients the ADA covers differently, its administrative complaint route carries the fund-termination backstop, and its remedies developed under a distinct spending-power jurisprudence.
