## Introduction

The Truth in Lending Act answers a question that sounds simple and proved, in practice, to be anything but: what does a given extension of consumer credit actually cost? Before Congress acted, that question had no single answer, because American lenders did not describe the price of credit in a shared language. A borrower shopping for a loan in the middle of the twentieth century encountered a market in which the same economic transaction, borrowing a sum of money and repaying it over time with a charge for the privilege, could be priced in several incompatible vocabularies, each of them lawful, each of them capable of making an expensive loan sound cheap, and none of them readily convertible into the others by a person without training in the mathematics of interest. The statute that resulted from that condition does not set the price of credit. It standardizes the description of the price, and that distinction, between regulating what credit costs and regulating what a borrower is told about what credit costs, is the central fact about the law and the source of nearly everything that followed from it.

The incompatibility was structural rather than incidental. One lender might quote an add-on rate, a percentage applied to the original principal for the full term of the loan even though the balance declined with every payment, so that the stated figure understated the effective cost by a wide margin. Another might quote a discount rate, under which interest was calculated on the face amount and deducted in advance, leaving the borrower with less money in hand than the amount on which the charge had been computed. A third might quote a simple monthly rate, a small sounding fraction of one percent each month that concealed a much larger annual burden. A fourth might state a rate on the unpaid balance, the method closest to an honest annual measure. Presented with four offers, a borrower had four numbers that looked comparable and were not. Arithmetic that a finance company performed as routine office work was, for a household deciding between a refrigerator on installments and a bank loan, effectively inaccessible. Comparison, the mechanism on which competitive markets depend, could not operate where the units of comparison differed from seller to seller.

Fees deepened the opacity. A lender could advertise a low nominal rate while a substantial part of the real return arrived through charges that the rate did not capture: origination fees, investigation fees, credit insurance premiums folded into the contract, and other amounts exacted as a condition of the extension of credit. A quoted rate therefore described only part of the price, and the part it omitted was the part a borrower had the least ability to estimate. Two loans with identical stated rates could carry materially different total costs, and two loans with different stated rates could rank in the opposite order once every charge was counted. State law offered a patchwork of partial responses, and usury ceilings in many states limited how high a rate could go without answering the prior question of how a borrower was to know what the rate was. The problem Congress legislated against in 1968 was thus not, in the first instance, that credit was too expensive, although members of Congress said plenty about expense during the long debates that preceded enactment. The problem was that the price of credit was unknowable in the form in which it was sold, and a market in which price is unknowable cannot discipline sellers who charge too much or reward sellers who charge less.

The formal identity of the statute reflects its place inside a larger legislative settlement. The Truth in Lending Act is Title I of the Consumer Credit Protection Act, Public Law 90-321, 82 Stat. 146, signed by President Lyndon B. Johnson on May 29, 1968. The title may be cited on its own as the Truth in Lending Act, and its operative provisions are codified at 15 U.S.C. sections 1601 and following. The parent act also restricted wage garnishment and created a national commission to study consumer finance, and later Congresses added titles covering credit reporting, equal credit opportunity, debt collection, and electronic fund transfers, so much of federal consumer credit law came to live inside one statutory house. Regulation Z carried the implementing rules, first from the Federal Reserve Board and later, after the transfer treated in the enforcement section, from the consumer bureau. The statute took effect on July 1, 1969, giving creditors a defined interval in which to rebuild forms, train staff, and convert pricing statements into the new federal vocabulary.

Congress stated the object of the enterprise in the declaration of purpose codified at 15 U.S.C. section 1601(a). The findings in that subsection connect the informed use of credit to economic stabilization and to stronger competition among financial institutions and other firms engaged in extending consumer credit, and the operative sentence declares the purpose to be assurance of a meaningful disclosure of credit terms so that a consumer can compare more readily the various credit terms available and avoid the uninformed use of credit. The wording repays attention. The declared evil is uninformed use, not expensive use. The declared remedy is disclosure that is meaningful, a standard that reaches beyond dumping raw figures on a page and toward communication a borrower can act on. And the declared beneficiary mechanism is comparison: disclosure matters, in the statutory theory, because a borrower holding two offers stated in identical terms can choose the cheaper one, and lenders competing for that borrower's business must respond to the choice. A later clause in the same subsection, added by amendment in the following decade, extended the stated purpose to protection against inaccurate and unfair credit billing and credit card practices, an early sign that the statute would grow beyond its original disclosure core in the directions later sections of a full profile examine in detail.

Read as a whole, the introduction to the statute makes a restrained claim and a bold one at the same time: government would not set prices, while a single federally defined measure of credit cost, imposed uniformly on a national market, would do the work that price regulation would otherwise be asked to do. Everything in Title I flows from holding those two claims together.

## Disclosure Instead of Price Control

Congress chose in 1968 to make credit comparable rather than to make it cheap. That sentence, which names the statute's philosophy in its shortest form, describes a deliberate allocation of regulatory effort. The federal government had available to it, at least as a matter of legislative imagination, several more intrusive instruments: a national ceiling on consumer credit rates, federal licensing of lenders, substantive review of contract terms for fairness, or prohibitions on disfavored charges. Title I adopted none of them. The statute contains no rate ceiling, no maximum finance charge, no federal judgment about what a fair price for a loan would be, and no power in any agency to declare a disclosed rate too high. A creditor that complied with the disclosure rules could charge a very expensive rate, provided the expense was stated in the prescribed form. What the statute regulated, with considerable precision and with serious penalties for error, was information: what had to be said, in what units, in what form, and at what point in the transaction.

The reasoning behind that choice drew on a market discipline theory that section 1601(a) states in compressed form. Competition strengthens when buyers can compare, and comparison requires a common measure. If every creditor must express the cost of credit through the same two anchors, the finance charge stated in dollars and the annual percentage rate stated as a standardized yearly figure, then price competition becomes possible in a market where it had been largely theoretical. A lender charging more than competitors can no longer hide the difference inside a favorable sounding method of quotation, because the method of quotation is no longer the lender's to choose. A lender charging less gains a visible advantage, because the lower cost appears in a number the borrower can set beside every rival offer. Over many transactions, borrowers migrating toward cheaper credit reward efficient lenders and pressure expensive ones, and the market, supplied with legible prices, performs the disciplining function that a rate ceiling would perform by command. Disclosure was therefore not a retreat from regulation in the minds of its architects. It was a theory of regulation, one that treated opacity as the market failure and standardization as the cure.

The theory also fit the federal structure of American credit law as it stood in 1968. States had long regulated credit pricing through usury statutes and small loan laws, and a federal rate ceiling would have collided with that state role across thousands of categories of lender and loan. A federal disclosure standard, by contrast, could overlay state law without displacing it: states remained free to cap rates, while the federal government supplied the uniform language in which prices, capped or not, had to be announced. Consumer advocates who wanted stronger medicine could accept disclosure as a foundation, and representatives of the lending industry, many of which had resisted rate regulation fiercely, could accept a rule governing the description of prices rather than the prices themselves. The philosophy was thus also a coalition strategy, and its durability owed something to how many different positions could shelter under it.

### What does the Truth in Lending Act actually require?

The Truth in Lending Act requires creditors to disclose the cost of consumer credit in standardized terms before consummation, including the finance charge in dollars and the annual percentage rate, together with the amount financed, the payment schedule, and other material terms specified by Regulation Z for each transaction type.

Stated in operation, the requirement is a duty placed on the creditor rather than a right a borrower must demand. A creditor covered by the statute must deliver the prescribed disclosures for a transaction without waiting to be asked, in writing, in a form the borrower can keep, and early enough for the information to inform the decision to proceed. The content of the disclosure varies by transaction type, because an open end account such as a credit card presents a different informational problem from a closed end installment loan or a mortgage, and Regulation Z organizes its rules around that division. Across the categories, the borrower learns the total the credit will cost in dollars, the rate at which that cost accrues expressed on the statutory annual basis, the amount being financed, and the schedule on which repayment must occur. Advertising came inside the scheme as well, and the statute therefore regulated the terms a creditor could trumpet in advertising and the further terms that had to accompany them.

The philosophy carried consequences for how the statute would be administered and litigated for decades afterward. A disclosure statute lives or dies on definitions. If the finance charge is defined narrowly, charges escape the annual percentage rate and the standardized number understates cost; if the definition is drawn broadly, the number approaches the full economic price of the transaction. Because Title I chose information as its instrument, the fiercest contests under the statute concerned the contents of that definition and the precision with which disclosures had to match the transaction as it actually performed. Accuracy tolerances, model forms, and the treatment of charges imposed by third parties became questions of real legal consequence, and private litigation turned on whether a number on a form fell inside or outside a permitted margin. None of that litigation would have taken the same shape under a rate ceiling, where the contest would have concerned the level of the price rather than the truthfulness of its description. The choice of philosophy in 1968 determined the agenda of consumer credit law for a generation: argument after argument that might have been about fairness of price became an argument about completeness and accuracy of disclosure.

That template, disclosure first and substantive control only at the margins, governed American consumer finance regulation for roughly four decades after 1968. Statute after statute in the consumer field followed the pattern Title I had set, requiring standardized statements of terms for transactions ranging from consumer leases to real estate settlements to electronic fund transfers, on the shared premise that a properly informed consumer was the primary engine of market fairness. Regulators built examination systems around disclosure compliance, and courts read Title I as a remedial disclosure law whose requirements were to be interpreted in favor of the borrower trying to understand an offer. Even statutes with substantive elements tended to express them through disclosure triggers, imposing extra statements and waiting periods for disfavored categories of loan rather than forbidding the loans outright.

The departures, when they came, mark the limits legislators eventually placed on the original theory. The credit card legislation of 2009 amended Title I itself and crossed the line the 1968 Congress had declined to cross, regulating card pricing directly through requirements such as reasonableness and proportionality for penalty fees and restrictions on interest rate increases for existing balances. The following year, mortgage legislation required creditors to determine that a borrower had the ability to repay. In both instances Congress regulated the term itself, not only the telling of the term, and the disclosure framework became the floor beneath substantive rules laid over it. The amendments section below traces that departure in full.

An honest account of the philosophy has to name the assumption on which the entire structure rests. Disclosure disciplines a market only if the people receiving the disclosures read them, understand them, and act on what they understand in time to affect the choice being made. The 1968 design presupposes a consumer who behaves in roughly that way, or who can be brought to behave in that way by forms standardized enough to reward attention. Whether actual borrowers, deciding under time pressure and facing documents of growing length and complexity, matched that presupposition is an empirical question that researchers pressed with increasing force in the decades after enactment, and the answers they produced form the counter-reading that any complete assessment of the statute must weigh.

The sections that follow take the statute in the order its own logic suggests: origins and passage, the disclosure machine built from the finance charge and the annual percentage rate, coverage, the rescission right, enforcement through private damages and agency supervision, amendments from the nineteen seventies through 2010, and litigation and measured effect.

## The Credit Market That Produced the Statute

The Truth in Lending Act emerged from a credit market that had changed in scale and in everyday importance during the two decades after the Second World War. Installment contracts, store charge accounts, and small loans from licensed finance companies had existed long before the war. Rising postwar incomes, suburban housing, automobile ownership, and durable goods, from refrigerators to television sets, turned those devices into a mass system, supplied by banks, sales finance companies, credit unions, and national retailers, with sellers competing as much on monthly terms as on price.

Federal Reserve figures convey the scale of the change. Consumer credit outstanding stood near 5.6 billion dollars in January 1945, a wartime level held down by production limits on durable goods and by federal credit controls. The total reported by the Federal Reserve and reproduced in the Economic Report of the President reached 62.2 billion dollars in December 1961, 96.0 billion dollars in December 1965, and 117.4 billion dollars in December 1968. A market of that size touched rent, food budgets, medical care, and education, because the goods bought on credit were household necessities as often as luxuries, and a family that misunderstood the price of credit misunderstood a recurring charge layered across several contracts at once.

Installment buying supplied the largest part of the expansion. An automobile might be financed by a bank directly, or by a dealer who assigned the contract to a sales finance company, and furniture dealers offered a stated number of monthly payments with the credit charge folded into the contract. Each channel had a distinct vocabulary and method of quoting price, and the cost depended on the amount financed, the number of payments, fees for insurance or investigation, and the method used to compute a rebate after prepayment. Two contracts with the same monthly payment could carry different total charges, and two contracts with the same stated rate could produce different charges because the stated rate rested on different balances.

Revolving credit added a second transformation. The postwar department store account became a revolving account, under which a customer could carry a balance from month to month, pay a fraction of the balance, and borrow again up to a limit without signing a new contract for each purchase. The service charge was commonly expressed as a monthly percentage of the unpaid balance. A charge of one and one half percent a month sounded modest, and the arithmetic that converted a monthly charge into an annual rate of 18 percent was rarely performed at the sales counter.

Bank credit cards converted revolving credit from a store relationship into a general purchasing instrument. Bank of America launched BankAmericard in Fresno, California, on September 18, 1958, mailing cards to about 60,000 residents after lining up several hundred local merchants to accept the card. Licensing carried BankAmericard to banks in other states, and rival groups organized competing plans during the following decade, including the plan marketed as Master Charge from 1966.

Pricing practices defeated comparison shopping even for a careful borrower, because a rate quotation described a method of computation as much as a price. Under the add-on method, the creditor computed the finance charge on the original amount for the full term, added that charge to the principal, and divided the sum into equal installments. The borrower repaid principal from the first payment onward, so the average balance outstanding was far below the original amount, yet the charge had been figured on the original amount for the entire term. An add-on rate therefore understated the cost relative to a rate charged on the declining balance, and a borrower comparing an add-on quotation with a simple interest quotation compared unlike quantities. Under the discount method, interest for the term was deducted from the face amount at the outset, and the borrower received the reduced proceeds while owing repayment of the full face amount. The stated rate applied to money the borrower never held, so the effective cost exceeded the quoted figure, and the gap widened as fees joined the amount deducted in advance.

Precomputed contracts raised a further problem at prepayment, governed in many contracts by the rule of 78s. The method took its name from the sum of the digits from 1 through 12 for a loan of twelve months, which totals 78. The creditor treated the finance charge as earned in fractions of that total, with 12 seventy-eighths earned in the first month, 11 seventy-eighths in the second month, and declining fractions thereafter. A borrower who paid early, refinanced, or had the goods repossessed received a rebate of the unearned charge computed under the rule, and the rebate was smaller than an actuarial computation would have produced. The difference was concrete for borrowers whose finances were under strain, the very borrowers least able to absorb a short rebate. Balloon terms completed the pattern. A contract could set modest periodic payments for most of its life and leave a large final payment at maturity, dependent on refinancing, on sale of the goods, or on savings that the borrower might never accumulate. Advertising reinforced the difficulties, leading with a small down payment and a small monthly figure and omitting the number of months, the total of the payments, and the finance charge that would have allowed a household to place the offer beside a competing offer.

### The State Law Patchwork

State law had regulated consumer credit for decades before Congress acted, and the state record furnished both the techniques and the limits that shaped the federal statute. Small loan laws, many of them descended from the Uniform Small Loan Law promoted by the Russell Sage Foundation, licensed lenders, capped charges for small loans, and required some statement of the rate. Retail installment sales acts regulated contracts for goods and set rules for repossession and deficiency claims, and usury laws capped interest for loans in general terms. The result varied by state, by lender type, and by transaction type, so that a single purchase could fall under different rules depending on whether a bank lent the money, a finance company bought the contract, or a retailer carried the account.

A doctrinal gap ran through the state structure. Under the time price doctrine, adopted in many jurisdictions, a seller could charge one price for a cash sale and a higher price for a sale on time, and the difference escaped treatment as interest under usury law because the transaction was a sale rather than a loan. Installment sales often sat outside the rate ceilings that governed direct loans, and disclosure requirements attached unevenly across that divide. Rate ceilings in some states produced a second pressure that disclosure law in any single state could not answer. Where a ceiling pressed against the cost of serving small or risky borrowers, lenders had incentives to recharacterize price as fees, to require credit insurance, or to route business through forms that the ceiling reached weakly. Each fee moved part of the true price outside the figure that a rate ceiling or a rate disclosure described.

Massachusetts supplied the demonstration that a modern disclosure statute could operate. Massachusetts adopted a truth in lending law in 1966 with requirements close to the federal proposal, following congressional hearings held in Boston in 1963 as part of a series of field hearings in New York City, Pittsburgh, Louisville, and Boston. The Senate Banking Committee report on the 1967 bill stated that experience with truth in lending legislation in Massachusetts had confirmed the belief that full disclosure of credit charges benefited business as well as consumers. The Department of Defense furnished a parallel example through a rule requiring lenders extending credit to service members to state charges in dollar cost and as a simple annual rate. Each example weakened the claim that annual rate disclosure was unworkable, and each also showed the limit of piecemeal action, because a state statute governed only its own market and a department rule governed only its own borrowers.

### The Champions Who Carried the Proposal

Senator Paul H. Douglas of Illinois supplied the proposal, the persistence, and much of the economic reasoning behind the statute. Douglas, an economist who had served on a committee drafting credit code revisions for the National Recovery Administration in 1934, recounted that a suggestion in that setting that sellers quote rates on an annual basis and charge interest only on the unpaid balance met a cold silence followed by a letter suggesting resignation. In 1960, after a decade in the Senate, Douglas introduced the first federal credit price disclosure bill, requiring disclosure of the total finance charge in dollars and of the charge expressed as a simple annual rate on the unpaid balance. No jurisdiction in the United States, including the District of Columbia, then required price disclosure in that form, a novelty that helps explain the intensity of the reaction.

Historian Christopher L. Peterson, in a history of the statute published in the Florida Law Review, recorded the array that formed against the 1960 bill. Opponents included automobile dealers, finance companies, mail order houses, the National Association of Manufacturers, the United States Chamber of Commerce, the American Bankers Association, and the American Bar Association, joined by virtually all congressional Republicans and most Southern Democrats. Industry witnesses argued that a single annual rate could not fairly represent transactions as different as a discounted bank loan, a revolving store account, and an installment sale, and that an inaccurate rate would mislead borrowers more than no rate at all. Creditor groups argued that annual rate figures would confuse consumers accustomed to monthly payments and dollar charges. Retail organizations argued that compliance would burden small retailers who extended credit as an accommodation, forcing such retailers to buy calculation services, retrain clerks, or abandon credit selling to larger competitors. Lenders also argued that the computation itself was unworkable at acceptable accuracy for irregular transactions, an argument that carried weight until Treasury actuaries produced rate tables and schedules that reduced the computation to a clerical exercise.

The committee structure of the Senate gave the opposition a durable veto. Hearings in 1961 on the Douglas bill, designated S. 1740 in the Eighty-seventh Congress, aired the proposal without producing a law. Banking Committee Chairman A. Willis Robertson of Virginia kept the measure from floor consideration for years, and Senator Wallace Bennett of Utah led the Republican voice on the creditor side. Douglas reintroduced the proposal across successive Congresses and used hearings, including the field hearings of 1963, to build a public record of borrowers who had signed contracts without grasping the dollar charge. Progress took the form of documentation rather than votes.

The consumer movement of the middle 1960s changed the setting in which the proposal was heard. Congress had already accepted mandated price information in a neighboring field through the Automobile Information Disclosure Act of 1958, which required a window sticker stating price information on new cars, a label that took the name Monroney sticker from its Senate sponsor. Ralph Nader published Unsafe at Any Speed in November 1965, and the book helped produce the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act of 1966. General Motors acknowledged that investigators had examined Nader and his background, and the resulting publicity widened public suspicion of corporate conduct toward consumers. Truth in lending fit the emerging federal consumer program as the credit counterpart of the window sticker, a requirement that the seller state the price in a form that allowed comparison.

Douglas lost his Senate seat before that program reached credit. In the election of November 8, 1966, Charles H. Percy defeated Douglas, who was seeking a fourth term, by about 55 percent to 44 percent of the Illinois vote. President Johnson, at the signing ceremony in 1968, greeted Douglas as a guest and described the measure in paraphrase as the bill Douglas had championed for eight years. The defeat removed the sponsor and, paradoxically, freed the proposal.

Senator William Proxmire of Wisconsin took the leading Senate role in the Ninetieth Congress, introducing the Consumer Credit Protection Act as S. 5 on January 11, 1967. Proxmire brought a reputation for frugality and for skepticism toward both government waste and corporate privilege, credentials that made a disclosure bill harder to portray as hostility toward business. He negotiated where Douglas had insisted: legal historian Anne Fleming identified Proxmire willingness to compromise on revolving credit as a decisive change, and the concession helped win support after a stalemate that Fleming measured at seven years. Representative Leonor K. Sullivan of Missouri supplied the central House leadership, holding the House to a bill that could meet the Senate measure in conference without surrendering annual rate disclosure.

### Why did earlier truth in lending bills fail?

Earlier bills failed because banking, retail, and finance groups opposed a uniform annual rate, committee leaders blocked floor action, and lenders disputed whether varied credit forms could share one calculation method without error or disproportionate compliance cost for small creditors.

The failure had a structural character. A disclosure bill threatened no creditor right to charge a chosen price, so the effective lines of resistance were delay, jurisdiction, and workability. Delay kept the bill in committee, where hearings could be held and adjourned without a vote. Jurisdiction allowed opponents to argue that consumer credit belonged to the states, an argument with genuine roots in small loan law and usury law, even as national retailers and bank card plans operated across state lines. Workability turned every hard calculation into a reason to postpone the entire requirement, until Treasury rate tables reduced the contested mathematics to lookup and clerical exercise. The proposal advanced only when three further conditions coincided: a Massachusetts demonstration that disclosure operated without disrupting a state credit market, a consumer movement that supplied public attention, and new sponsorship that supplied a Senate manager willing to trade and a House leader prepared to defend a strong text.

### Passage as Part of a Larger Consumer Act

The measure that became law traveled as S. 5, the Consumer Credit Protection Act, with truth in lending as Title I. The same act restricted garnishment of wages in a separate title and created the National Commission on Consumer Finance to study the consumer finance industry. Disclosure stood at the head of the act as the general remedy, applicable before default and before dispute, and the surrounding titles marked the boundaries of congressional willingness in 1968: Congress would inform the borrower, soften one collection remedy, and study the rest.

The Senate passed S. 5 on July 11, 1967, by a vote of 92 to 0. The House passed the bill on February 1, 1968, by a vote of 383 to 4. Margins of that size describe a measure whose final form had absorbed the negotiable objections of the creditor industries while retaining the annual percentage rate at the center of the design. The chambers then reconciled differing texts into the spring of 1968, and on May 22, 1968, the House agreed to the reconciled arrangement with a further amendment and the Senate agreed to the House amendment, clearing the bill for the president. President Johnson signed the Consumer Credit Protection Act on May 29, 1968, as Public Law 90-321.

Enactment left the hardest administrative work ahead, and Congress allowed time for that work. The disclosure chapters of Title I took effect on July 1, 1969, more than a year after signing. The interval gave the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System time to write Regulation Z, which translated the statutory commands into definitions, calculation rules, tolerances, and model forms. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation manual on the statute records the same pairing of dates, enactment on May 29, 1968, and effectiveness on July 1, 1969, under Regulation Z. Congress had standardized the statement of price and had declined to standardize price itself, leaving usury and rate regulation largely to state law and to the state patchwork that federal disclosure overlaid without replacing.


## The Architecture of the Disclosure Machine

The Truth in Lending Act operates through definitions before it operates through duties. Congress did not write a general command that creditors be fair, nor a general command that credit be cheap. Congress instead built a machine with interlocking parts, each part defined in the statutory text, each definition feeding the next calculation, and the final output presented to the consumer in a prescribed vocabulary. The finance charge supplies the raw material. The annual percentage rate converts that material into a yearly figure. The amount financed fixes the base against which the rate is measured. Separate disclosure sections then dictate which figures appear for a closed end transaction with a fixed sum and a payment schedule, and which figures appear for an open end plan under which credit may be drawn, repaid, and drawn again. Advertising rules extend the same vocabulary outward, so that a term used to attract a consumer carries defined content rather than promotional content. Regulation Z, the implementing regulation, supplies the working detail underneath each statutory term, including the model forms that translate abstract duties into documents a creditor can complete and a consumer can read.

That architecture explains why disputes under Title I so often look technical. A disagreement about a single fee classification can move the finance charge, and a moved finance charge can move the annual percentage rate, the total of payments, and the accuracy analysis that follows from both.

### What counts as a finance charge?

The finance charge is the dollar cost of consumer credit expressed as a single sum. Section 1605 counts charges payable by the consumer and imposed by the creditor as an incident of credit, including interest, points, service charges, loan fees, and certain broker fees, while excluding specified real estate settlement charges and comparable cash transaction charges.

Section 1605(a) states the definition in deliberately wide language. The finance charge in connection with any consumer credit transaction is determined as the sum of all charges payable directly or indirectly by the person to whom credit is extended, and imposed directly or indirectly by the creditor as an incident to the extension of credit. The language about payment reaches charges paid from loan proceeds, charges added to the balance, and charges routed through intermediaries, so a creditor cannot remove a cost from the calculation merely by arranging for a third party to collect it. The qualifying phrase, as an incident to the extension of credit, draws the outer boundary, separating the price of borrowing from the price of the goods, services, or property that the borrowing makes possible. The same subsection qualifies the breadth with a sentence of equal consequence: the finance charge does not include charges of a type payable in a comparable cash transaction.

The statutory examples in section 1605(a) show the range Congress intended. Interest heads the list, joined by a time price differential and any amount payable under a point, discount, or other system of additional charges, then a service or carrying charge, a loan fee, a finder fee, or a similar charge, a fee for an investigation or credit report, and premiums or other charges for guarantee or insurance protecting the creditor against default. Borrower paid mortgage broker fees complete the list, including fees paid directly to the broker and fees paid to the lender for delivery to the broker, whether paid in cash or financed. A charge counts because of the role it plays in the transaction, not because of the label printed on a closing sheet.

The exclusions for transactions secured by real property, set out in section 1605(e), remove a defined set of settlement charges from the computation: fees or premiums for title examination or title insurance, fees for preparation of loan related documents, escrows for future payments of taxes and insurance, fees for notarizing deeds and other documents, appraisal fees, and credit report fees. Regulation Z layered a discipline over the corresponding regulatory exclusions, treating real estate related fees as excluded only when the fees are bona fide and reasonable in amount, so an inflated appraisal fee could not shelter part of the price of credit inside an excluded category. Taxes imposed by government, of a type payable in a comparable cash transaction, sit outside the finance charge under the opening definition.

The central analytical point follows directly from that structure. Because the finance charge definition decides what enters the number, litigation over disclosure accuracy is largely litigation over the finance charge boundary. A plaintiff alleging an understated annual percentage rate ordinarily alleges, at bottom, that a charge belonging inside the finance charge was left outside the calculation, or that an excluded category was manipulated so that part of a credit cost hid within an exclusion. A defendant creditor ordinarily responds that the contested charge falls within a statutory or regulatory exclusion, or that the charge is not imposed as an incident to the extension of credit, or that any error fell within a tolerance. The court then construes section 1605 and the corresponding provisions of Regulation Z, in particular the finance charge provisions recodified at section 1026.4, and the classification decision resolves most of the case. Charges with soft labels, such as administrative fees or processing fees, generate the contested cases precisely because the label settles nothing and the statutory test of incidence to credit settles everything.

Section 1606 converts the finance charge from a dollar sum into a rate. The annual percentage rate for credit other than credit under an open end plan is the nominal annual percentage rate that yields a sum equal to the amount of the finance charge when applied to the unpaid balances of the amount financed, calculated according to the actuarial method of allocating payments between the amount financed and the finance charge. Under that method, each payment is applied first to accumulated finance charge and the balance of the payment is applied to the unpaid amount financed. For open end credit, section 1606(a) states a different mechanical formula suited to revolving balances: the quotient of the total finance charge for the period divided by the amount on which the finance charge for that period is based, multiplied by the number of such periods in a year. The statute also permits a method prescribed by the rulemaking agency that materially simplifies computation while retaining reasonable accuracy compared with the actuarial rate.

Tolerance rules keep the uniformity from becoming a trap built on rounding. Section 1606(c) treats a disclosed annual percentage rate as accurate when the rate disclosed falls within a tolerance not greater than one eighth of one percent more or less than the actual rate, or when the rate is rounded to the nearest one fourth of one percent. Regulation Z, at section 1026.22, expresses the working rule for closed end credit in terms of regular and irregular transactions, with the one eighth tolerance associated with regular transactions and a wider one fourth tolerance for irregular transactions involving features such as multiple advances or irregular payment schedules. Section 1605(f) adds a finance charge tolerance for transactions secured by real property or a dwelling, treating the disclosed finance charge as accurate when the amount disclosed does not vary from the actual finance charge by more than one hundred dollars or is greater than the amount required to be disclosed, with a separate standard governing accuracy for rescission purposes. The tolerances acknowledge that creditors compute rates from payment streams that include odd first periods and calendar irregularities.

The annual percentage rate is not the interest rate stated in a note, and the difference between the two figures is the most instructive comparison in the entire disclosure system. The note rate governs the accrual of interest on the unpaid principal balance under the contract. The annual percentage rate takes the finance charge, which includes interest plus other charges that satisfy section 1605, and spreads that broader cost across the amount financed and the payment schedule. A loan can therefore carry a note rate of eight percent and an annual percentage rate above eight percent whenever points, loan fees, broker fees, or other included charges add to the finance charge. A consumer comparing two offers by note rate alone compares only one component of cost, and a creditor competing on note rate alone can shift compensation into fees without changing the advertised interest figure. The annual percentage rate blocks that maneuver within the limits of the finance charge definition, which is a further reason the boundary of that definition carries such weight in enforcement and litigation.

An illustration with invented round numbers makes the mechanism concrete, and the figures in the illustration describe no actual loan and carry no recommendation for any transaction. Suppose two loans each rest on a note promising repayment of ten thousand dollars of principal with interest at eight percent over one year, with a single payment due at the end of the year, a simplified structure chosen so the arithmetic stays transparent. Under the first loan, the creditor imposes no charge beyond interest, so the amount financed is ten thousand dollars, the interest for the year is eight hundred dollars, the finance charge is eight hundred dollars, and the annual percentage rate is eight percent. Under the second loan, the creditor imposes the same interest and also a four hundred dollar loan fee that counts as part of the finance charge and that the consumer pays before consummation. Because a prepaid finance charge is subtracted in computing the amount financed under section 1638, the amount financed is ninety six hundred dollars, the finance charge is twelve hundred dollars, and the payment due at the end of the year remains ten thousand eight hundred dollars. The actuarial rate that yields twelve hundred dollars on an amount financed of ninety six hundred dollars for one year is twelve and one half percent. The two notes state the same interest rate, yet the disclosed annual percentage rates differ by four and one half percentage points, and the entire difference traces to a single fee classification under section 1605.

Sections 1637 and 1638 turn the computed figures into delivered disclosures, dividing the field by transaction structure. Section 1638 governs consumer credit transactions other than transactions under an open end credit plan, the closed end category that includes installment loans, automobile financing through a creditor, and mortgage loans outside the later integrated mortgage disclosure regime. Before consummation, and subject to timing rules the regulation elaborates, the creditor must disclose the identity of the creditor required to make disclosure, the amount financed using that term, the finance charge using that term and not itemized in the segregated disclosure, and the finance charge expressed as an annual percentage rate using that term. The creditor must also disclose the total of payments, defined as the sum of the amount financed and the finance charge, and the number, amount, and due dates or period of payments scheduled to repay the total of payments. In a sale of property or services in which the seller is the creditor required to disclose, the creditor must disclose the total sale price, the total of the cash price, additional charges, and the finance charge. Descriptive explanations of the core terms accompany the figures in the form the rulemaking agency specifies.

The remaining closed end items in section 1638(a) address the terms that determine how a transaction behaves after signing. Where the credit is secured, the creditor must state that a security interest has been taken in the property purchased as part of the transaction, or in property not purchased as part of the transaction identified by item or type. The creditor must disclose any dollar charge or percentage amount that may be imposed solely on account of a late payment, other than a deferral or extension charge. On prepayment, the creditor must state whether the consumer is entitled to a rebate of any finance charge upon refinancing or prepayment in full where the obligation involves a precomputed finance charge, and whether a penalty will be imposed in the same circumstances where the finance charge is computed by applying a rate to the unpaid principal balance. The creditor must also direct the consumer to the appropriate contract document for information about nonpayment, default, acceleration, and prepayment rebates and penalties, and residential mortgage transactions carry an additional statement about whether a subsequent purchaser or assignee may assume the debt on the original terms.

Section 1637 governs open end consumer credit plans, the category that includes general purpose credit cards, charge cards, and home equity lines, and the disclosure problem differs because no fixed amount financed or payment schedule exists at account opening. Before an account is opened, the creditor must disclose the conditions under which a finance charge may be imposed, including the time period, if any, within which credit extended may be repaid without incurring a finance charge, and the creditor must disclose the absence of such a grace period when no period is provided. The creditor must disclose the method of determining the balance on which a finance charge will be imposed, a matter of real consequence because average daily balance, adjusted balance, and previous balance methods produce different charges on identical spending patterns. The creditor must also disclose the method of determining the amount of the finance charge, including any minimum or fixed amount. Where one or more periodic rates may be used, the creditor must disclose each rate, the range of balances to which the rate applies, and the corresponding nominal annual percentage rate determined by multiplying the periodic rate by the number of periods in a year. Other charges must be identified with the method of computation, secured plans require a statement identifying the security interest, and the creditor must furnish a statement of the billing rights protections that the later sections of Title I supply.

The Fair Credit and Charge Card Disclosure Act of 1988 added a further layer for card applications and solicitations, requiring the key rates, fees, grace period, and balance computation method to appear in tabular format in the applications and solicitations described in section 1637(c) and the companion charge card provisions. The amendments section below treats the chronology in full, so the architectural point alone belongs here: Congress extended the disclosure machine to the moment of marketing for cards, standardizing the solicitation itself because comparison at account opening arrives late for a consumer who has already responded to an offer framed around a single attractive term. Advertising rules perform the same function across credit generally, and the regulation states the organizing principle directly: an advertisement may not state a specific credit term unless the term actually is or will be available.

Regulation Z is where the statutory architecture becomes a working system. Congress directed the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System to prescribe regulations carrying out the purposes of Title I, and the Board issued Regulation Z, codified at Part 226 of Title 12 of the Code of Federal Regulations. The Dodd Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, enacted on July 21, 2010, transferred rulemaking authority under Title I to the Bureau of Consumer Financial Protection, with a designated transfer date of July 21, 2011. The Bureau then recodified Regulation Z at Part 1026 of Title 12, preserving the substance and, in large measure, the numbering logic of the Board regulation. A citation that once read section 226.18 for closed end content of disclosures therefore reads section 1026.18 in the recodified regulation.

Model forms and model clauses give the regulation a distinctive compliance mechanism. Section 1604(b) authorizes the rulemaking agency to publish model disclosure forms and clauses for use by creditors, and a creditor that uses a model form or clause properly is deemed to be in compliance with the disclosure provisions addressed by that form or clause. The Board published model forms in the appendices to Part 226, and the Bureau carried the model forms forward into Part 1026. A creditor adopting the model form transfers the drafting risk to the agency that wrote the form, retaining responsibility for completing the form accurately with figures that match the transaction.

The dependence of the disclosure system on the regulation runs deeper than forms. The statute states the finance charge definition in a paragraph and a set of examples, but the regulation decides recurring classification questions that the paragraph cannot answer unaided: which application fees count, how charges imposed by third parties are treated when a creditor requires the service or retains a portion of the charge, which security interest charges may be excluded when itemized and bona fide, and how tolerances apply to transactions with irregular features. In each instance the pattern repeats: Congress fixed the right, the figure, or the duty, and the agency supplied the operational content that determines whether the duty has been satisfied in a particular file. A disclosure statute lives or dies in the regulation because disclosure is a practice, repeated millions of times on paper and on screens, and only a regulation can govern a practice at that granularity.

The architecture as a whole therefore rests on a chain of dependencies. Section 1605 draws the boundary of cost, section 1606 converts bounded cost into a comparable rate surrounded by tolerances, sections 1637 and 1638 order the disclosures for revolving and nonrevolving credit, and section 1604 together with Regulation Z supplies the forms and interpretations that let the chain operate uniformly across creditors. A weakness at any link degrades every later link, since an excluded fee understates the finance charge, an understated finance charge understates the annual percentage rate, and an understated rate defeats the comparison that the disclosure exists to support.

## Who the Statute Covers and Who It Leaves Out

Coverage determines everything else in the Truth in Lending Act. A disclosure duty, a rescission right, a billing error procedure, and a claim for statutory damages all depend on a prior answer to a boundary question, namely whether a particular extension of credit falls inside the statute at all. Congress did not attach the disclosure regime to credit in general. The regime attaches to consumer credit, extended by a creditor, to a natural person, primarily for personal, family, or household purposes. The boundary therefore expresses the same philosophy as the disclosures themselves. Protection follows the consumer transaction, defined by purpose and by party, rather than the financial product in the abstract, and identical loan forms receive different legal treatment when purpose or borrower changes.

The statutory definition of creditor supplies the first filter. Under Title I, a creditor is a person who regularly extends consumer credit that is payable by written agreement in more than four installments or that is subject to a finance charge. The definition contains a disjunction that matters in practice. A transaction payable in more than four installments falls within the formulation even when the price of credit is embedded in the sale price or spread across payments without a separately labeled finance charge, and a transaction subject to a finance charge falls within the formulation even when repayment occurs in fewer installments. Either payment structure or charge structure suffices, because Congress understood that the cost of credit could be concealed by restructuring repayment, by relabeling charges, or by combining both techniques.

Regulation Z gives numerical content to the phrase regularly extends, and the numerical thresholds separate an enterprise engaged in consumer lending as a business from a person who extends credit only occasionally. The regulation at 12 CFR 1026.2(a)(17)(v) treats a person as regularly extending consumer credit when the person extended consumer credit more than 25 times in the preceding calendar year. For credit secured by a dwelling, the threshold falls sharply, and a person regularly extends such credit after extending dwelling secured consumer credit more than five times in the preceding calendar year. The lower dwelling threshold reflects the weight assigned to transactions that place a home at risk. A lender needed only a modest volume of mortgage lending to enter the regulated class, whereas a lender making general consumer loans entered that class at a higher volume.

The thresholds also explain why isolated private lending usually stands outside the federal disclosure regime. A relative who lends money on two occasions, or a seller who finances a single sale as an accommodation, lacks the regularity that the definition demands, and the absence of regularity removes the transaction from the core creditor definition even when repayment involves installments or a charge. Congress addressed the resulting gap through other bodies of law, including state usury law, state retail installment sales law, contract doctrine, and fraud law, rather than through the federal disclosure forms.

The consumer purpose test supplies the second and most consequential filter. Covered credit must be extended primarily for personal, family, or household purposes. The adverb primarily carries the analytical burden in mixed cases, because borrowed funds frequently serve more than one end and because a borrower may direct part of the proceeds toward a residence, part toward a vehicle, and part toward a small enterprise conducted from the home. The inquiry asks for the primary purpose of the extension, assessed from the circumstances of the transaction rather than from labels chosen by the parties. A loan agreement that recites a business purpose does not control when the proceeds in fact finance household consumption, and a borrower statement of purpose, while relevant evidence, does not end the inquiry when surrounding facts point in another direction. Considerations that recur in Regulation Z commentary and in case law include the amount of the loan relative to income, the relationship between the borrowing and an occupation or enterprise, the degree to which personal assets secure the obligation, and the use actually made of the proceeds after disbursement.

Purpose also explains why the identity of collateral does not decide coverage by itself. Credit secured by a dwelling may be business purpose credit when the proceeds primarily finance a commercial enterprise, and credit secured by business equipment may be consumer credit when the equipment serves a household function and the proceeds meet a family need. The statute follows economic use rather than collateral category, a choice that prevents both underinclusion and overinclusion. A contrary rule keyed to collateral would have subjected commercial mortgage lending to consumer rescission and disclosure rules designed for homeowners, while denying protection to consumers who borrowed against nontraditional collateral for plainly personal ends.

Dwelling secured coverage occupies a special position inside the larger category of consumer credit, because the statute attaches its strongest protections to transactions secured by a consumer principal dwelling. The right of rescission for non purchase money credit, the enhanced disclosures for high cost mortgages, and the appraisal and repayment rules added by later amendments all presuppose a security interest in a home. Default on unsecured consumer credit produces collection pressure and credit reporting harm, but default on dwelling secured credit adds loss of shelter and loss of accumulated equity to that sequence. The graduated structure of the statute, general disclosure for consumer credit and heightened protection for dwelling secured credit, matches remedy to exposure.

### Does the statute cover a loan taken out for a business?

No. Credit extended primarily for a business purpose falls outside the statute, because coverage requires credit primarily for personal, family, or household purposes. Regulation Z treats business purpose credit as an exempt transaction, and the exemption applies even when a natural person borrows and even when a dwelling secures the loan.

The business purpose exemption, stated in 15 U.S.C. section 1603(1) and carried into Regulation Z at 12 CFR 1026.3(a)(1), reaches credit extended primarily for a business, commercial, or agricultural purpose. A borrower obtaining funds for an enterprise is expected, rightly or wrongly in particular cases, to evaluate cost as one business input among many and to absorb credit information through commercial experience rather than standardized federal forms.

Commercial purpose credit follows the same logic, and the exemption covers loans for inventory, equipment, commercial real estate, and working capital, provided the primary purpose standard is met. The exemption does not depend on corporate form: a sole proprietor borrowing primarily to stock a shop is engaged in business borrowing for purposes of Title I, even though personal liability may follow default. Courts and examiners instead police the boundary through purpose evidence, and mischaracterization of consumer borrowing as business borrowing removes an exemption that the facts do not support.

Credit extended to organizations rather than natural persons forms a distinct exemption that overlaps with, but does not duplicate, the purpose exemptions. Section 1603(1) also excludes extensions of credit to government or governmental agencies or instrumentalities, or to organizations, and Regulation Z states the related coverage principle by defining a consumer as a natural person. Regulation Z commentary softens the entity line in a narrow setting, treating credit extended to certain trusts established for tax or estate planning purposes, and to land trusts in defined circumstances, as credit extended to a natural person.

Securities and commodities transactions present a different rationale for exclusion, grounded in regulatory allocation rather than borrower sophistication alone. Section 1603(2) exempts transactions in securities or commodities accounts by a broker dealer registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission, and Regulation Z at 12 CFR 1026.3(d) carries a corresponding exemption for securities and commodities accounts. Layering Title I cost disclosures, designed for installment and revolving consumer credit, onto a brokerage account would have produced overlapping forms addressed to different risks. The exemption is bounded by registration and by account character. Commentary to Regulation Z makes clear that the exemption does not extend to a transaction with a broker registered solely with a state, and does not protect a separate extension of credit merely because loan proceeds are later used to purchase securities.

Public utility credit rests on a conditional exemption that depends on the presence of a substitute regulator. Section 1603(4) exempts transactions under public utility tariffs when the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau determines that a state regulatory body regulates the charges for the public utility services involved, the charges for delayed payment, and any discount allowed for early payment. Commentary illustrates covered services with examples that include gas, water, and electrical service and cable television service. The exemption does not apply to credit extended to purchase appliances or to finance home improvements such as heating or air conditioning systems.

Student loan programs under Title IV of the Higher Education Act of 1965 supply the clearest example of an exemption based on a separate and comprehensive disclosure regime. Section 1603(7) exempts loans made, insured, or guaranteed pursuant to a program authorized by Title IV, codified at 20 U.S.C. sections 1070 and following. Commentary to Regulation Z states the limit with equal clarity: the exemption does not apply to private education loans as defined in the regulation, which receive Title I treatment under provisions added for that market.

The exemptions share a structural lesson that clarifies the whole statute. Each exemption identifies either a borrower expected to proceed without standardized federal cost disclosure, a transaction already governed by a specialized federal or state regime, or a setting in which the consumer purpose at the center of Title I is absent. The exemptions allocate regulatory responsibility; the exemptions do not deny economic significance.

### One Parent Act Holding Many Consumer Statutes

The Consumer Credit Protection Act gives the Truth in Lending Act a second identity that a reader of Title I alone may miss. Public Law 90-321, enacted on May 29, 1968, at 82 Stat. 146, is both the vehicle that enacted Truth in Lending as Title I and a parent act whose structure Congress used repeatedly during the following decade to house additional consumer credit statutes. The parent act arrangement is more than a shelving convenience. Cross references among the titles give operative content to one another, and conduct protected under one title may supply a prohibited basis or a defense under another. The cluster coheres legally because the titles share a single statutory home.

The original enactment already displayed that multi subject character. Title I contained the consumer credit cost disclosure provisions that became the Truth in Lending Act. Title II addressed extortionate credit transactions, principally through amendments to Title 18 of the United States Code directed at loansharking and the use of extortionate means to collect extensions of credit, a subject far removed from disclosure forms and rooted in criminal law enforcement against organized lending abuses. Title III restricted garnishment, and Title IV provided for a National Commission on Consumer Finance charged with studying the consumer credit field that the new act had begun to regulate at the federal level. A single public law thus combined a disclosure regime, a criminal law intervention, a limit on a collection remedy, and a study commission.

Title III deserves separate attention because the garnishment limits operate as substantive protection rather than disclosure. Codified at 15 U.S.C. sections 1671 to 1677, Title III restricts the maximum part of an individual aggregate disposable earnings that may be subjected to garnishment in any workweek. Section 1673 sets the general ceiling at the lesser of two measures: 25 percent of disposable earnings for the week, or the amount by which disposable earnings for the week exceed thirty times the federal minimum hourly wage in effect when the earnings are payable. Title III also restricts discharge from employment by reason of garnishment for any one indebtedness, and the Wage and Hour Division of the Department of Labor administers the provisions. Exceptions qualify the ceiling for support orders, for certain bankruptcy orders, and for debts due for state or federal tax. The presence of a hard percentage cap in Title III, in an act whose first title deliberately imposed no cap on the price of credit, captures the selective character of the congressional program.

Later Congresses returned to the same parent act when new consumer credit problems required federal answers, and the pattern of addition turned the original enactment into the central shelf of federal consumer credit law. The Fair Credit Reporting Act arrived in 1970 as Title VI of the Consumer Credit Protection Act, and the credit reporting title governs permissible purposes for consumer reports, accuracy duties, dispute procedures, and disclosures to consumers by consumer reporting agencies, a subject examined in the [guide to the Fair Credit Reporting Act](/2018/11/01/fair-credit-reporting-act-1970-guide/). The Equal Credit Opportunity Act followed in 1974 as Title VII, prohibiting discrimination in credit transactions on bases that include race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, and age, and protecting an applicant who exercised a right under the Consumer Credit Protection Act in good faith. The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act became Title VIII in 1977, regulating third party debt collectors through prohibitions on abusive, deceptive, and unfair collection practices. The Electronic Fund Transfer Act became Title IX in 1978, establishing rights, liabilities, and responsibilities for participants in electronic fund transfer systems at a moment when payroll deposit and terminal based banking were displacing paper instruments for many households.

The title numbers themselves record the accretion, and codification preserves the sequence. The Fair Credit Reporting Act occupies 15 U.S.C. sections 1681 and following, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act occupies sections 1691 and following, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act occupies sections 1692 and following, and the Electronic Fund Transfer Act occupies sections 1693 and following, in sequence after the disclosure and garnishment provisions in chapter 41 of Title 15. The Equal Credit Opportunity Act treats the good faith exercise of a right under the Consumer Credit Protection Act as a prohibited basis for discrimination, so retaliation against a consumer for rescinding a transaction, disputing a billing error, or invoking a reporting right falls inside a discrimination prohibition located in a different title.

The umbrella structure supplies the structural fact that ties the consumer credit cluster together, and the fact reframes the profile of the Truth in Lending Act itself. Title I did not merely precede the reporting, discrimination, collection, and transfer statutes in time. Title I supplied the statutory home, the codification neighborhood, and part of the conceptual vocabulary into which each later statute was placed. A consumer dispute that begins with an inaccurate cost disclosure may proceed to a credit report furnished after default, a collection suit governed by the debt collection title, a garnishment limited by Title III, and an adverse action notice governed by the reporting and equal credit titles, all without leaving the parent act. Congress in 1968 built the frame. Congresses in 1970, 1974, 1977, and 1978 filled the frame with the statutes that govern most stages of a consumer credit relationship, from application and pricing through repayment, default, collection, and reporting.


## The Rescission Right

The rescission right is the point at which the Truth in Lending Act departs most visibly from a pure disclosure philosophy. Elsewhere the statute generally leaves the bargain intact and attacks the information problem, requiring a creditor to state the cost of credit in standardized form so that competing offers can be compared. Section 1635 of Title 15 does something stronger. For a defined class of transactions secured by a home, the statute gives the consumer a short period in which the transaction can be undone altogether, without proof of reliance, without proof that a better offer existed elsewhere, and without any showing that the terms were unfair. The right operates as a cooling off mechanism attached to the pledge of a principal dwelling.

### Which loans carry a right of rescission?

Rescission covers consumer credit secured by a principal dwelling, including home equity loans, home improvement loans, and refinancings by a new creditor. Purchase money residential mortgage transactions, same creditor refinancings without new money, state agency transactions, and business purpose credit carry no rescission right under the provision.

The statutory trigger is the combination of consumer credit and a security interest in a principal dwelling. Section 1635(a) reaches a consumer credit transaction in which a security interest, including a security interest arising by operation of law, is or will be retained or acquired in property used as the principal dwelling of the person to whom credit is extended. Regulation Z frames the closed end rule in parallel terms and assigns the right to each consumer whose ownership interest is or will be subject to the security interest. Ownership, rather than signature on the note, is therefore the decisive connection.

Consummation starts the inquiry but does not by itself start the clock in every case. The consumer may rescind until midnight of the third business day following consummation, delivery of the notice of the right to rescind required by the statute, or delivery of all material disclosures, whichever of those events occurs last. The three events are cumulative conditions of a completed closing package, and the period runs from the last of them. Regulation Z requires the notice to disclose the retention or acquisition of a security interest in the dwelling, the right to rescind, the method for exercising the right, the effects of rescission, and the date on which the rescission period expires.

The definition of business day does much of the practical work, and Regulation Z gives the term a special meaning for rescission. The general definition in 12 C.F.R. section 1026.2(a)(6) turns on a day on which the creditor's offices are open to the public for carrying on substantially all business functions. For rescission purposes, by contrast, business day means all calendar days except Sundays and the legal public holidays specified in 5 U.S.C. section 6103(a). Saturdays count. Days on which a particular creditor happens to be closed count, apart from Sundays and the listed federal holidays. State holidays count, because the cross reference reaches only the federal list. The consequence is a calendar applicable uniformly from the closing documents alone.

Exercise of the right is deliberately undemanding in form. The consumer notifies the creditor of the intention to rescind in accordance with the governing regulations, and Regulation Z accepts notice by mail, telegram, or other means of written communication. Notice sent by mail is considered given when mailed, a rule that removes disputes about the speed of delivery from the timeliness question and places the evidentiary weight on proof of mailing. Oral objection and continued negotiation do not substitute for the written notice the scheme requires, because the mechanism depends on a dated act that a closing file, a court, or a successor creditor can verify. A consumer inside the three day window, or inside the extended period described below, needs no lawyer, no filing fee, and no court appearance to exercise the right.

The consequences of a timely rescission follow a fixed sequence set out in section 1635(b) and section 1026.23(d) of Regulation Z. When a consumer rescinds, the security interest that gave rise to the right becomes void, and the consumer is not liable for any amount, including any finance charge. Within twenty calendar days after receipt of a notice of rescission, the creditor must return any money or property given by anyone in connection with the transaction and must take any action necessary to reflect the termination of the security interest, which in practice means releasing the lien of record. Only after the creditor has performed those obligations does the consumer corresponding duty arise, and that duty is tender of the money or property the creditor delivered. If the creditor does not take possession of tendered money or property within twenty calendar days after tender, the consumer may keep it without further obligation.

A narrow waiver doctrine completes the ordinary three day scheme. The consumer may modify or waive the right upon determining that the extension of credit is needed to meet a bona fide personal financial emergency. Waiver requires a dated written statement that describes the emergency, specifically modifies or waives the right, and bears the signatures of all consumers entitled to rescind, and printed forms for that purpose are prohibited. The prohibition has a clear function. A waiver generated as part of a standard closing package would convert an emergency exception into a routine term of the loan, and the transaction specific statement makes routine waiver harder to industrialize.

The exclusions in section 1635(e) and section 1026.23(f) decide which transactions never enter the scheme, and the first exclusion is the one most often misunderstood. The right does not apply to a residential mortgage transaction. That term is defined by the statute and by Regulation Z as a transaction in which a mortgage, deed of trust, purchase money security interest arising under an installment sales contract, or equivalent consensual security interest is created or retained in the consumer's principal dwelling to finance the acquisition or initial construction of that dwelling. A loan that finances the purchase of the home, secured by the home being purchased, carries no federal rescission right under section 1635, however large the loan and however hurried the closing. The common assumption that every mortgage on a home can be rescinded for three days after closing is wrong.

The placement of the purchase money line reflects the structure of the two transactions. In a purchase transaction, credit and conveyance are interlocking parts of a single exchange involving a seller and a transfer of title, and an unwinding right exercisable after consummation would unsettle the seller position and the chain of title as well as the loan. The purchase borrower acquires the dwelling through the transaction and pledges no preexisting equity in a home already owned. In a home equity loan, a home improvement loan, or a refinancing with a new creditor, the sequence is reversed: the dwelling and its equity exist before the credit decision, the transaction pledges that accumulated asset, and no seller waits on the outcome.

Refinancing receives a divided treatment that follows the same logic of new exposure. A refinancing or consolidation by the same creditor of credit already secured by the consumer's principal dwelling is exempt, but only in part. Regulation Z provides that the right of rescission applies to the extent the new amount financed exceeds the unpaid principal balance, any earned unpaid finance charge on the existing debt, and amounts attributed solely to the costs of the refinancing or consolidation. A same creditor refinancing advancing no new money beyond the existing balance and transaction costs therefore carries no rescission right, on the reasoning embedded in the rule that the consumer's exposure has not grown and the home was already pledged to the same creditor for the same debt. Cash advanced beyond that measure is new money, and the right attaches to the extent of that excess. A refinancing with a different creditor receives no comparable exemption.

Construction credit and liens arising by operation of law require finer distinctions than summary accounts usually give them. Financing the initial construction of a principal dwelling falls within the definition of a residential mortgage transaction and is exempt for the same reason as purchase money financing, since the loan finances the creation of the dwelling that will be the consumer home. A loan for later construction on a dwelling already owned and occupied, such as an addition, stands on the other side of the line, because the credit is secured by an existing principal dwelling and does not finance its acquisition or initial construction. Section 1635(a) expressly includes liens arising by operation of law among the interests that can trigger rescission, so a mechanic or materialman lien retained by a contractor that is itself the creditor can support a rescission right, while a lien held by a contractor outside the credit transaction cannot.

The remaining exemptions in section 1026.23(f) are narrower and more mechanical. A transaction in which a state agency is a creditor is exempt. An advance other than an initial advance, within a series of advances or a series of single payment obligations treated as a single transaction, is exempt where the required notice and all material disclosures were given for the transaction as a whole. A renewal of optional insurance premiums that is not treated as a refinancing is likewise exempt. Separate from the exemption list, the consumer credit boundary of Regulation Z excludes credit extended primarily for a business, commercial, or agricultural purpose, so a business purpose loan secured by a principal dwelling carries no rescission right even though the collateral is a home.

Where the closing package fails, the three day period gives way to a far longer exposure. Section 1635(f) provides that the right of rescission expires three years after consummation even when the required disclosures were never delivered, and section 1026.23(a)(3)(i) states the extended rule in operational form. If the required notice or the material disclosures are not delivered, the right to rescind expires three years after consummation, upon transfer of all of the consumer interest in the property, or upon sale of the property, whichever occurs first.

The concept that carries the extended period is the material disclosure, and Regulation Z defines the term for rescission purposes at section 1026.23(a)(3)(ii). Material disclosures mean the required disclosures of the annual percentage rate, the finance charge, the amount financed, the total of payments, and the payment schedule, together with the disclosures and limitations required for high cost mortgages under sections 1026.32(c) and (d) and the prepayment penalty disclosure associated with section 1026.43(g). The list is short by design. Not every disclosure error extends the rescission period, because the extension is tied to the core cost and repayment terms on which the comparison philosophy of the statute rests. An error in a peripheral disclosure may support a damages claim without disturbing the rescission calendar, while an error in the annual percentage rate or the finance charge places the extended right in issue.

Accuracy, however, is not judged against a standard of perfection, and the tolerance rules in section 1026.23(g) and (h) absorb a large share of potential disputes. Under the general rule, the finance charge and the disclosures affected by it are considered accurate for rescission purposes if the disclosed finance charge is understated by no more than one half of one percent of the face amount of the note or one hundred dollars, whichever is greater, or if the disclosed finance charge is greater than the amount required to be disclosed. Overstatement does not extend the right under the general tolerance, because a consumer told that credit costs more than the law calculates has not been misled toward a bargain worse than the one described. A wider one percent tolerance applies in a defined class of refinancings with a new creditor involving no new advance and no consolidation.

In Beach v. Ocwen Federal Bank, 523 U.S. 410 (1998), the borrowers had refinanced a Florida home in 1986, had stopped paying in 1991, and had raised a rescission claim as a defense after foreclosure proceedings began, well beyond three years after consummation. The Court held that a borrower could not assert the section 1635 right to rescind as an affirmative defense in a collection action after the three year period of section 1635(f) had run. The reasoning treated the period as a provision that extinguishes the right itself rather than a statute of limitations governing only the time for bringing suit. The opinion, delivered by Justice Souter for a unanimous Court, drew support from the contrast with section 1640(e), which expressly preserves a damages claim raised by way of recoupment after the one year period for damages actions and contains no parallel preservation for rescission. After Beach, the three year date operated as a statute of repose in substance, ending the right for offensive and defensive use alike.

Jesinoski v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc., 574 U.S. 259 (2015), addressed the different question of how the right is exercised inside the period that Beach had made absolute. The borrowers had refinanced a home loan, had mailed a letter purporting to rescind exactly three years after consummation, had received a refusal from the creditor, and had filed suit roughly a year after the letter. The district court and the Eighth Circuit treated the suit as untimely on the view that rescission required the filing of an action within three years. A unanimous Supreme Court reversed, in an opinion by Justice Scalia. Section 1635(a) states that the borrower has the right to rescind by notifying the creditor, in accordance with the governing regulations, of the intention to do so, and the Court read that language as a complete statement of the method of exercise. Notice within three years makes the rescission timely, and the statute imposes no additional requirement of suit within the same period. Jesinoski did not decide whether the borrowers held a valid ground for rescission, since the creditor maintained that the disclosures had been proper.

The consequence question most often litigated is tender. Section 1635(b) places the creditor obligations first in sequence, and Regulation Z states that the sequence may be modified by court order. Under Yamamoto v. Bank of New York, 329 F.3d 1167 (9th Cir. 2003), a district court may require a borrower to demonstrate an ability to tender as a condition of rescission. A later Ninth Circuit decision qualified the pleading side of that doctrine by holding that borrowers need not plead tender, or an ability to tender, in order to state a rescission claim at all. The Seventh Circuit in 2014 treated tender as inherent in rescission rather than incidental to it, affirming the power of a district court to require tender of the amounts advanced before the creditor must release the security interest. Creditor arguments in this line emphasize that rescission restores both parties and cannot function as a forfeiture of principal never repaid. Borrower arguments emphasize the statutory sequence and the practical reality that a borrower may be unable to produce the full proceeds on a compressed schedule.

Taken as a whole, the rescission scheme illustrates both the strength and the boundary of the 1968 settlement in consumer credit law. Inside the three day window, the right is nearly absolute, and the consumer need give no reason at all. Beyond that window, the right survives only as a remedy for the creditor own failure to deliver the notice or the material disclosures on which the window depends, and it survives under tolerances that forgive small errors and overstatements. At three years, sale, or transfer, the right ends completely, and Beach forecloses its revival as a defensive theory.

## Enforcement: How a Disclosure Statute Acquires Teeth

A statute that commands disclosure and nothing more faces an obvious enforcement problem. The Truth in Lending Act set no ceiling on the price of credit and prescribed no administrative penalty schedule for the ordinary disclosure failure. What Congress built instead, in sections 1607, 1611, 1640, and 1641 of Title 15, was a layered structure in which the injured borrower served as the principal inspector, federal agencies supplied a supervisory backstop, and criminal punishment stood in reserve for deliberate falsification. The design reflected the character of the harm the statute addressed. A misstated annual percentage rate or an understated finance charge injures a borrower in a way that is real but diffuse, measured, in most individual transactions, in amounts far smaller than the cost of proving the error in court. An enforcement scheme that depended entirely on agency examination would have left most violations unexamined, because no examination force could review every disclosure statement the consumer credit market generated. A scheme that depended entirely on private suits for provable loss would have left most violations unchallenged, because provable loss was the element least likely to justify the effort. The statute answered both difficulties by attaching a formula based award to the disclosure failure itself, shifting the cost of successful litigation to the creditor, and preserving agency authority to order account adjustments where errors appeared across a portfolio.

Section 1640 of Title 15 supplies the private right of action, and its structure explains why litigation under the statute developed at a scale unusual for a disclosure law. A creditor that fails to comply with a requirement imposed under the subchapter is liable for any actual damage sustained as a result of the failure, and alongside actual damages, independent of proof of loss, the section awards statutory damages computed by formula. In an individual action over a closed end credit transaction, the general measure is twice the amount of the finance charge in connection with the transaction, a formula that ties the award to the size and cost of the credit itself rather than to the borrower ability to document consequential harm. For an individual action relating to a closed end transaction secured by real property or a dwelling, the amendments enacted in 1995 produced a separate banded award, and the Housing and Economic Recovery Act of 2008 raised that band to not less than 400 dollars and not more than 4,000 dollars, where the amount has remained under the text amended in 2010. For an individual action relating to an open end consumer credit plan not secured by real property or a dwelling, the Credit Card Accountability Responsibility and Disclosure Act of 2009 supplied a measure of twice the finance charge with a minimum of 500 dollars and a maximum of 5,000 dollars, with a higher amount authorized where an established pattern or practice of failures is shown. Consumer leases received a parallel measure of 25 percent of the total amount of monthly payments under the lease, bounded at 200 dollars and 2,000 dollars under the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010.

The class action provision extends the same logic to violations that no single borrower could economically pursue, while capping the aggregate exposure that a single course of conduct could generate. In a class action the court allows such amount as it determines, no minimum recovery applies to any individual class member, and the total recovery may not exceed the lesser of a fixed ceiling or 1 percent of the net worth of the creditor. The fixed ceiling stood at 100,000 dollars in the original enactment, rose to 500,000 dollars under the 1976 amendments, and rose again to 1,000,000 dollars under section 1416 of the Dodd-Frank Act. Between the individual formula and the class ceiling, the statute thus calibrates deterrence at both ends, making a single violation worth suing over and a mass violation survivable for an institution that corrects it.

### Why do small disclosure errors produce lawsuits at all?

Statutory damages and mandatory attorney fees make small claims worth filing. A borrower who lost little or nothing in provable terms can recover a sum fixed by formula, and a prevailing borrower recovers fees, so counsel can accept cases that actual damages alone would never support economically in practice.

The fee provision completes the mechanism that the direct answer describes. Section 1640 awards, in any successful action to enforce the liability the section creates, or in any action in which a person is determined to have a right of rescission, the costs of the action together with a reasonable attorney fee as determined by the court. Fee shifting reverses the ordinary economics of small claims litigation, under which the cost of counsel consumes any plausible recovery. The prospect of a fee award paid by the creditor makes representation rational for the borrower and settlement rational for the creditor even where the disclosure error is conceded to be minor, because creditor exposure grows with the length of the contest rather than with the size of the original mistake. The combined effect of formula damages and fee shifting is a deputization of the affected population. Every borrower who receives a disclosure statement becomes a potential examiner of that statement, and every plaintiffs firm that develops expertise in the finance charge definition becomes, in functional terms, part of the compliance apparatus, auditing creditor forms at a granularity no agency examination cycle could match. The volume of litigation the statute generated, much of it turning on whether a particular charge belonged inside the finance charge, is the predictable output of that design rather than an accident of drafting.

Congress paired the incentive structure with defenses that distinguish the honest computational failure from the evasive one. Under section 1640, a creditor or assignee has no liability if, within sixty days after discovering an error, and before the institution of an action or the receipt of written notice of the error from the borrower, the creditor or assignee notifies the person concerned of the error and makes whatever adjustment is necessary to assure that the person will not be required to pay an amount in excess of the charge actually disclosed. Prompt self correction thus extinguishes the claim, and the provision rewards the internal audit that finds an error first. A separate defense, the bona fide error defense, protects a creditor or assignee that shows by a preponderance of the evidence that the violation was not intentional and resulted from a bona fide error notwithstanding the maintenance of procedures reasonably adapted to avoid such an error. The statutory examples are clerical, calculation, computer malfunction and programming, and printing errors, and the text expressly excludes an error of legal judgment about obligations under the subchapter from the category of bona fide error.

The limitations period reflects the same balance between a remedy meant to be used and a liability meant to be bounded. An action under section 1640 may be brought in a federal district court or another court of competent jurisdiction within one year from the date of the occurrence of the violation, a short period that forces disclosure claims to be raised while the transaction documents remain recoverable. The Dodd-Frank Act lengthened the period to three years for actions concerning the mortgage provisions codified at sections 1639, 1639b, and 1639c. Expiration of the period does not, however, erase the violation for every purpose. The statute provides that the limitations bar does not prevent a person from asserting a violation in an action to collect the debt, brought more than one year after the violation occurred, as a matter of defense by way of recoupment or setoff, except as otherwise provided by state law. The borrower as plaintiff must act within the year; the borrower as defendant may answer a collection claim with the disclosure claim arising from the same transaction.

A private remedy against the originating creditor alone would have contained a structural escape, because consumer loans, and mortgage loans above all, are routinely sold. Section 1641 of Title 15 governs the liability of assignees, and its general rule is deliberately narrower than the rule against the originator. Except as otherwise specifically provided, a civil action or an administrative proceeding that could be brought against a creditor may be maintained against an assignee of the creditor only if the violation is apparent on the face of the disclosure statement, and, under the provision added in 1995 for transactions secured by real property, only if the assignment to the assignee was voluntary. A violation is apparent on the face of the disclosure statement if the disclosure can be determined to be incomplete or inaccurate by a comparison among the disclosure statement, any itemization of the amount financed, the note, or any other disclosure of disbursement, or if the disclosure statement does not use the terms or format the subchapter requires. The rescission right stands apart from the limitation, because a consumer entitled to rescind under section 1635 may rescind the transaction against any assignee of the obligation.

The high cost mortgage provisions enacted by the Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act of 1994 displace the facial review limitation for the category of loans Congress judged most dangerous. Under section 1641, a purchaser or assignee of a mortgage covered by the high cost mortgage definition is subject to all claims and defenses with respect to that mortgage that the consumer could assert against the creditor, unless the purchaser or assignee demonstrates, by a preponderance of the evidence, that a reasonable person exercising ordinary due diligence could not determine, from the required documentation, that the mortgage was a covered high cost mortgage. For an ordinary loan, the borrower must show that the violation was visible on the face of the papers, and for a high cost loan, the assignee must show that the character of the loan was not reasonably discoverable. Regulation Z reinforces the allocation by forbidding a creditor to sell or assign a high cost mortgage without furnishing the purchaser a notice warning that assignees could be liable for all claims and defenses the consumer could assert against the creditor.

Private litigation was the engine of the scheme, but Congress did not leave enforcement to borrowers alone. Section 1607 of Title 15 committed administrative enforcement, for the first four decades of the statute, to the agencies that already supervised the institutions involved. Compliance was enforced under the Federal Deposit Insurance Act framework for depository institutions, with the Comptroller of the Currency responsible for national banks, the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System responsible for member banks, and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation responsible for insured banks outside those categories, while parallel provisions assigned savings institutions, federal credit unions, air carriers, and agricultural creditors to their respective regulators. The Federal Trade Commission held the residual commission, enforcing the requirements against creditors not specifically committed to another agency. Rulemaking, by contrast, was concentrated rather than divided: the Federal Reserve Board held the authority to prescribe Regulation Z, so that a single rulebook governed creditors whose day to day supervision was scattered across the enforcement agencies.

Administrative enforcement acquired a monetary dimension through the restitution authority added in 1980, under which the enforcement agencies could require a creditor to make adjustments to consumer accounts where an annual percentage rate or finance charge had been inaccurately disclosed, with restitution generally required for errors resulting from a clear and consistent pattern or practice of violations, gross negligence, or a willful violation intended to mislead. Behind the civil and administrative structure stood a criminal provision of deliberately narrow scope. Section 1611 of Title 15 imposes liability on whoever willfully and knowingly gives false or inaccurate information, fails to provide information required to be disclosed, uses a chart or table authorized under the statute in a manner that consistently understates the annual percentage rate, or otherwise fails to comply with a requirement of the subchapter, punishable by a fine of not more than 5,000 dollars or imprisonment for not more than one year, or both. The willful and knowing standard reserves prosecution for falsification rather than miscalculation.

The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 dismantled the divided structure and reassembled its parts inside a single agency. The act created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and, on the designated transfer date of July 21, 2011, transferred to the bureau the rulemaking authority for the statute that the Federal Reserve Board had exercised since 1968, together with supervision and enforcement authority distributed among the banking regulators and the Federal Trade Commission. The bureau republished Regulation Z in December of 2011, recodifying the regulation from Part 226 of Title 12 of the Code of Federal Regulations to Part 1026 of the same title, so that the citation change on the face of the regulation records the institutional change the statute had ordered. For the first time in the history of the statute, the agency that wrote the disclosure rules also supervised the largest creditors for compliance with them and could bring enforcement proceedings in its own name, a reassembly traced in the account of [the bureau creation and its powers](/2013/12/01/cfpb-creation-and-powers/). The Dodd-Frank Act adjusted the private remedy in the same legislation, raising the class action ceiling from 500,000 dollars to 1,000,000 dollars, raising the consumer lease band, and extending the limitations period for the principal mortgage provisions. Borrowers retained the formula damages, the fee award, and the recoupment protection that had made the statute enforceable from 1968 forward.

## Amendments and the Long Departure From Disclosure Alone

The Truth in Lending Act that entered the statute books in 1968 was a narrow instrument by design, and nearly every major amendment during the following four decades either widened the class of transactions governed by its disclosure method or tested the limits of that method. The earliest amendments stayed inside the original philosophy, adding cardholder protections, a billing error procedure, and lease disclosure, all without setting the price of credit. A second phase, beginning in 1980, worked on volume while preserving the philosophy itself, as lawmakers concluded that disclosure had grown so dense that simplification had become a consumer protection in its own right. A third phase, visible in the high cost mortgage rules of 1994, began to blur the line between informing a choice and restricting it. The final phase broke the line openly. The credit card legislation of 2009 regulated rates, fees, and payment allocation directly, and the mortgage legislation of 2010 imposed a substantive underwriting duty that made a creditor responsible for judging whether a borrower could repay. Between May 2009 and July 2010, Congress kept the disclosure apparatus intact and layered direct regulation on top of it, so that the amended statute came to do much of what the 1968 Congress had deliberately declined to do.

The first additions arrived only two years after enactment, and the date matters because the two best known cardholder protections are often misassigned to a later law. Title V of Public Law 91-508, enacted on October 26, 1970, amended the statute by adding three consecutive provisions on credit cards, codified at sections 1642 to 1644 of Title 15. Section 1642 provided that no credit card could be issued except in response to a request or application, with an exception for renewal of, or substitution for, an accepted card. The provision answered a mailing practice of the late nineteen sixties, in which issuers had sent live cards to consumers who had never asked for an account. Section 1643 set the limit on liability for unauthorized use. Under that section a cardholder could be liable only if the card was an accepted credit card, only if the liability did not exceed fifty dollars, only if the issuer had given adequate notice of the potential liability, only if the issuer had provided a means of notification in case of loss or theft, and only if the unauthorized use occurred before the issuer had been notified. Section 1643 also placed the burden of proof on the issuer in an action to enforce liability. Section 1644 addressed fraudulent use of a credit card as a federal offense. The fifty dollar ceiling and the prohibition on unsolicited issuance therefore belong to 1970, not to 1974, and the distinction is more than a dating correction: a ceiling on liability allocates loss, assigning the residual risk of theft and fraud largely to issuers, which controlled card distribution and authorization systems.

The legislation of 1974 addressed a different mechanical problem, the error embedded in a billing statement for an open end account. The Fair Credit Billing Act was enacted on October 28, 1974, as Title III of Public Law 93-495, the same public law that enacted the Equal Credit Opportunity Act in another title, and its operative provisions were codified principally at sections 1666 to 1666j of Title 15. Under the procedure, an obligor who believed that a statement contained a billing error could give written notice within sixty days after the creditor transmitted the statement, identifying the account, stating the belief that an error existed, giving the amount in dispute, and setting out the reasons for the belief. The definition of billing error covered a charge not made to the obligor or not in the amount reflected, a charge for goods or services not received or not delivered as agreed, a failure to reflect a payment or credit properly, a computational error, and a charge for which the obligor requested clarification. The creditor then owed two timed duties: acknowledgment in writing within thirty days of receipt, unless the dispute was resolved within that period, and resolution within two complete billing cycles and in no event later than ninety days after receipt. During the investigation the creditor could not collect the disputed amount, could not impose a finance charge on that amount, and could not report the amount as delinquent. A creditor that missed the time limits forfeited the right to collect the disputed amount, up to a statutory ceiling stated in the act, even if the underlying charge proved valid.

Consumer leasing supplied the next extension, following the logic of functional equivalence. By the middle nineteen seventies, leasing an automobile or another durable good for personal, family, or household purposes had become an alternative to buying the same good on installment credit, and a consumer comparing a lease against a credit sale faced terms stated in incompatible forms. The Consumer Leasing Act of 1976, Public Law 94-240, approved on March 23, 1976, amended the Truth in Lending Act by adding a new chapter on consumer leases, codified at sections 1667 to 1667f of Title 15. The operative provisions required a lessor to give specified disclosures before consummation, covering the amount due at inception, the payment schedule and total of payments, other charges, and end of term liability for the difference between the estimated residual value and the realized value. The act also regulated lease advertising and imposed civil liability for failures. One element reached beyond disclosure: the act limited liability at expiration or termination by treating an estimated residual value that exceeded the actual value by more than a stated margin as presumptively unreasonable, subject to rebuttal, and by restricting the size of a balloon payment in a consumer lease, because a lessor set an estimate that a lessee could not verify against a market that would exist only years later.

The amendment of 1980 reversed the direction of travel on volume. Experience under the original statute and Regulation Z had produced disclosures so extensive that compliance disputes had come to turn on technical omissions rather than on whether the cost of credit had been communicated. The Truth in Lending Simplification and Reform Act was enacted as Title VI of the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980, Public Law 96-221, approved on March 31, 1980. Title VI pursued simplification through several coordinated changes: the number of disclosures was reduced and the remaining disclosures were to be segregated from other contract material so that the cost terms stood apart; the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System was directed to publish model forms and clauses for common transactions; civil liability was narrowed so that statutory damages attached to failures involving the disclosures Congress designated as material; and a creditor received an opportunity to correct an error after discovery within a defined period. The act also authorized enforcement agencies to order restitution where an annual percentage rate or finance charge had been understated beyond the applicable tolerance through a pattern or practice, gross negligence, or a willful violation. Title VI did not authorize any rate, fee, or term that had previously been forbidden, because the parent statute had forbidden almost none. Title VI instead recalibrated the disclosure instrument itself, resting on a judgment that a shorter and more uniform statement communicates cost more effectively than an exhaustive statement.

Application and solicitation disclosures, added in 1988, applied the lesson of 1980 to the moment before an account opened. The Fair Credit and Charge Card Disclosure Act of 1988, Public Law 100-583, enacted on November 3, 1988, amended the statute to require specified disclosures in applications and solicitations for credit cards and charge cards, codified principally in section 1637 of Title 15. A written application or solicitation had to disclose, clearly and conspicuously and in tabular form, the annual percentage rate or rates applicable to the account, the annual fee and other issuance or availability fees, the minimum finance charge, transaction charges, the grace period, and the balance computation method. The tabular summary produced by the act became known as the Schumer box, named for Charles Schumer, then a member of the House of Representatives from New York, who sponsored the disclosure legislation. Nothing in the 1988 act limited the size of any disclosed fee or the level of any disclosed rate.

### When did Congress begin regulating credit terms directly instead of only requiring disclosure?

Congress began regulating terms directly in stages, with narrow prohibitions for high cost mortgages in the nineteen nineties and a broad break for credit cards in the Credit CARD Act, followed by a general ability to repay duty for residential mortgages in the Dodd-Frank Act.

The narrow beginning came in the Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act of 1994, enacted as Subtitle B of Title I of the Riegle Community Development and Regulatory Improvement Act of 1994, Public Law 103-325, approved on September 23, 1994. The act, known by the acronym HOEPA, amended the Truth in Lending Act by adding protections for a defined category of high cost mortgages, principally in section 1639 of Title 15, with the category defined in section 1602. As enacted, a mortgage fell within the category when the annual percentage rate exceeded the yield on Treasury securities of comparable maturity by more than eight percentage points for a first lien loan or ten percentage points for a subordinate lien loan, or when the total points and fees payable at or before consummation exceeded the greater of four hundred dollars or eight percent of the total loan amount. Congress did not regulate mortgage terms generally and did not set a maximum lawful rate. Congress instead defined a band at the expensive end of the market and attached special rules to loans inside the band. Within the band, the act combined enhanced disclosure, delivered earlier than the ordinary timetable required, with outright prohibitions: a high cost mortgage could not contain a balloon payment when the loan term was less than five years, could not provide for negative amortization, could not impose a default interest rate higher than the rate otherwise applicable, could not require advance collection of more than two periodic payments at consummation, and could not finance a single premium credit insurance payment as part of the loan. Prepayment penalties were restricted, late fees were capped as a percentage of the past due payment and pyramiding of late fees was barred, and a creditor could not recommend or encourage default on an existing loan in order to refinance that loan into a high cost mortgage.

HOEPA remained an exception bounded by its triggers for fifteen years, and a lender could lawfully design a product just outside the high cost definition. The legislation of 2009 discarded the bounded structure for credit cards. The Credit Card Accountability Responsibility and Disclosure Act of 2009, known as the Credit CARD Act, Public Law 111-24, was signed on May 22, 2009, and its principal provisions took effect in stages, with the central final rules effective on February 22, 2010, and the penalty fee and rate reevaluation provisions effective on August 22, 2010. The act regulated the substance of card accounts in several principal ways. An issuer generally could not increase an annual percentage rate, fee, or finance charge during the first year after account opening, could not apply an increased rate to an existing balance except under defined exceptions such as expiration of a previously disclosed promotional rate or movement in a variable rate index outside the issuer control, and had to give forty five days advance notice of a covered increase. Promotional rates had to remain in effect for at least six months. Double cycle billing, under which a finance charge reached balances from an earlier cycle that had been paid, was prohibited outright. A penalty fee or charge had to be reasonable and proportional to the omission or violation involved, and the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System was directed to establish standards for assessing reasonableness and proportionality. An over the limit fee could be imposed only when the cardholder had affirmatively opted in. Fees charged during the first year after account opening, other than specified penalty fees, were capped at twenty five percent of the initial credit limit. Payment allocation became a statutory command, requiring an issuer to apply any amount paid above the minimum payment first to the balance bearing the highest rate of interest. A fuller account of [the substantive turn in card regulation](/2018/12/15/credit-card-act-2009-explained/) traces how each of those commands displaced a disclosure that had previously governed the same practice. The break lay not in abandonment of disclosure, since the act retained and added disclosure requirements, but in the addition of a second regulatory mode, under which a penalty fee standard and a payment allocation command governed the term itself.

The mortgage amendments of 2010 carried the same movement further, from regulating terms to regulating the decision to lend. Title XIV of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, Public Law 111-203, enacted on July 21, 2010, and titled the Mortgage Reform and Anti-Predatory Lending Act, amended the Truth in Lending Act by adding section 1639c of Title 15, captioned as minimum standards for residential mortgage loans. Section 1639c(a) states the ability to repay rule in mandatory terms: a creditor may not make a residential mortgage loan unless the creditor makes a reasonable and good faith determination, based on verified and documented information, that at the time of consummation the consumer has a reasonable ability to repay the loan according to its terms, together with all applicable taxes, insurance, and assessments. The determination must rest on consideration of credit history, income and expected income, current obligations, debt to income ratio or residual income, employment status, and other financial resources apart from the consumer equity in the dwelling securing the loan, and income and assets relied upon must be verified and documented through reasonably reliable third party records. The rule answered a failure mode documented in the collapse of mortgage lending during the years before 2010, in which loans had been originated on the strength of collateral value rather than documented capacity to make the scheduled payments. Section 1639c also supplied the definition of a qualified mortgage, and a creditor that originated a qualified mortgage gained a presumption that the ability to repay requirement had been satisfied. As defined in section 1639c(b)(2), a qualified mortgage is a residential mortgage loan whose regular periodic payments may not increase the principal balance or allow deferral of principal, whose terms do not result in a balloon payment apart from allowed exceptions, for which income and financial resources are verified and documented, whose underwriting rests on a fully amortizing payment schedule, which complies with agency guidelines on debt to income ratios or alternative measures of ability to pay, for which total points and fees do not exceed three percent of the total loan amount, and whose term does not exceed thirty years. Title XIV also restricted prepayment penalties, restricted loan originator compensation tied to loan terms, prohibited steering, and expanded the high cost mortgage protections first created in 1994. A perfectly accurate estimate does not satisfy a creditor that never verified income, and a borrower signature acknowledging every term does not waive the creditor obligation to determine ability to repay.

Title XIV and the related Dodd-Frank disclosure provisions nevertheless produced one major reform that belonged wholly to the original tradition. Dodd-Frank directed the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau to publish a single integrated disclosure form combining the mortgage disclosures required under the Truth in Lending Act with the disclosures required under sections 4 and 5 of the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act. The Bureau issued the integrated disclosure final rule in November 2013, and the rule took effect on October 3, 2015, after a postponement from an August 2015 date. The rule, known as the TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure rule, replaced the earlier forms for most closed end consumer mortgage loans with two documents: the Loan Estimate, delivered within three business days after application, and the Closing Disclosure, delivered three business days before consummation, using the same categories and layout so that estimated and final figures could be compared line by line. The integrated forms were a direct descendant of the 1980 simplification program and the 1988 tabular experiment.

## Litigation, Evidence, and the Limits of Disclosure

Litigation over the Truth in Lending Act after the rescission cases centered less on whether disclosure mattered than on who possessed authority to define the disclosure obligation and on how far the statutory words reached. The Supreme Court returned repeatedly to the same institutional question between 1973 and 2011. Congress had written broad terms, had delegated rulemaking power to the Federal Reserve Board, and had left the Board to translate those terms into Regulation Z forms, definitions, and staff interpretations. Creditors, consumers, and lower courts then disputed whether a Board reading controlled when the statutory text pointed in another direction, whether a fee belonged inside the finance charge, and whether a damages provision amended for one class of loans had changed the rule for another class. Because the annual percentage rate depended on the finance charge, and because the finance charge depended on contested classification decisions, litigation about a line on a disclosure form was often litigation about the boundary of the central definition.

The earliest of the interpretive cases tested the Board against an evasion problem. In Mourning v. Family Publications Service, 411 U.S. 356 (1973), the Supreme Court upheld the four installment rule in Regulation Z. The rule required disclosure when credit was payable in more than four installments, even in a transaction in which the creditor asserted that no finance charge had been imposed. The seller had sold magazine subscriptions payable over thirty months, and the Supreme Court held that Congress had given the Board broad authority to issue regulations necessary or proper to carry out the purposes of Title I, to prevent circumvention or evasion, and to facilitate compliance. A rule addressed to sellers who could conceal a credit cost inside the cash price fell within that authority, even though the rule also reached some transactions in which no separate finance charge existed.

Deference became the explicit subject seven years later. In Ford Motor Credit Company v. Milhollin, 444 U.S. 555 (1980), the question concerned whether the statute always required disclosure of an acceleration clause on the face of a credit agreement. Federal Reserve Board staff had consistently read the statute and Regulation Z to impose no such uniform requirement. The Supreme Court held that a high degree of deference to the administrative interpretation was warranted and concluded that Title I did not mandate a general rule of disclosure for acceleration clauses. Meaningful disclosure, in the reasoning associated with the decision, described a balance between complete information and information overload, and the agency charged with striking that balance also possessed primary authority to say which charges counted toward the standardized cost measure.

The same deference framework decided a finance charge dispute in the following century. In Household Credit Services, Inc. v. Pfennig, 541 U.S. 232 (2004), a cardholder argued that fees for exceeding a credit limit belonged in the finance charge because the fees were imposed as an incident to an extension of credit within the language of 15 U.S.C. section 1605(a). Regulation Z expressly excluded charges for exceeding a credit limit from the finance charge. The Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit had accepted the cardholder reading. The Supreme Court reversed and held that Regulation Z was not an unreasonable interpretation of section 1605, given the express authority of the Board to prescribe classifications and differentiations necessary or proper to effectuate the purposes of the statute. Pfennig mattered for the content of the annual percentage rate as well as for administrative law. An over limit fee could be disclosed on a billing statement without entering the finance charge figure, and the distinction rested on an agency classification that the Court sustained against a plausible textual challenge. A consumer comparing offers therefore compared a constructed figure whose inclusions and exclusions had been settled through rulemaking and deferential review, rather than a total of every amount a creditor might collect during the life of an account.

A companion decision from the same year showed the limits of deference when the dispute turned on statutory structure rather than on an agency classification. In Koons Buick Pontiac GMC, Inc. v. Nigh, 543 U.S. 50 (2004), a buyer sought uncapped statutory damages of twice the finance charge after a retail installment contract had included an improperly documented charge for a car alarm that the buyer had not requested or received. Before a 1995 amendment, section 1640(a)(2)(A) clauses (i) and (ii) bracketed statutory damages between one hundred dollars and one thousand dollars. The 1995 amendment added clause (iii), which raised the floor and ceiling to two hundred dollars and two thousand dollars for closed end loans secured by real property or a dwelling. The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and held that the amendment left the one hundred dollar and one thousand dollar limits unaltered for personal property loans, so the one thousand dollar cap applied to recoveries under clause (i).

The deference line reached a regulation about timing in Chase Bank USA, N.A. v. McCoy, 562 U.S. 195 (2011). The cardholder agreement permitted Chase to raise an interest rate, up to a stated maximum, after delinquency or default, and the cardholder contended that Regulation Z required a change in terms notice before the increase took effect. The version of Regulation Z applicable to the transactions predated the rate increase notice amendments associated with the Credit Card Accountability Responsibility and Disclosure Act of 2009. The Supreme Court, in a unanimous opinion by Justice Sotomayor decided on January 24, 2011, concluded that the applicable regulation did not require such notice before Chase implemented a rate increase authorized by a previously disclosed agreement term. The Board explained its interpretation in an amicus brief submitted at the invitation of the Court, and the Court deferred to that interpretation because the Board reading was not plainly erroneous or inconsistent with the regulation.

### Does disclosure actually change what borrowers pay?

The evidence supports a narrower claim than the original market discipline theory. Standardized disclosure improved recognition of loan costs in controlled testing by Lacko and Pappalardo and by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, but testing results alone do not prove that borrowers paid less in actual markets over time.

The strongest measured findings in the record assembled for the statute concern comprehension under controlled conditions rather than price movement in live markets. James M. Lacko and Janis K. Pappalardo, economists in the Bureau of Economics of the Federal Trade Commission, published Improving Consumer Mortgage Disclosures: An Empirical Assessment of Current and Prototype Disclosure Forms in 2007. The study drew on thirty six in depth interviews and on quantitative testing with more than eight hundred mortgage customers. About a fifth of respondents could not correctly identify the annual percentage rate, the amount of cash due at closing, or the monthly payment. About a third could not identify which of two loans was less expensive. About half could not correctly identify the loan amount, and almost nine tenths could not identify the total amount of upfront charges. Prototype forms designed for the study produced significantly improved recognition of mortgage costs, showing that form design affected understanding in a measurable way while the forms in use had left large shares of tested borrowers unable to extract the price information that the statutory theory required a borrower to use.

Federal testing after the transfer of rulemaking authority produced a comparable design result. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau conducted qualitative rounds and a quantitative validation study for the integrated mortgage disclosures issued under the Know Before You Owe program, with final rules announced in 2013. The Bureau reported that the quantitative study involved 858 consumers in twenty locations and that participants who used the new Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure forms answered questions about a sample mortgage more accurately than participants who used the predecessor forms, a statistically significant improvement of twenty nine percent in the Bureau summary. Participants using the new forms also performed better on questions about affordability, risk, long term cost, and comparison of closing costs. That evidence supports a claim about tested comprehension and tested comparison. The Bureau testing did not follow borrowers into completed transactions and did not measure whether improved answers produced lower prices across a market. A form can raise scores on a controlled task while leaving untouched the conditions that govern an actual purchase, including time pressure at closing, steering by an intermediary, and limited offers available to a borrower with a weak credit record. Readers seeking [the evidence on whether disclosure changes consumer credit outcomes](/2019/01/01/consumer-credit-law-impact/) encounter that distinction throughout the research record: recognition gains are documented with named studies and samples, whereas price effects require a different kind of evidence that the testing programs were not designed to supply.

The counter reading required by the research literature begins from the gap between recognition under testing and conduct under market conditions. Lauren E. Willis, in Against Financial Literacy Education, published in the Iowa Law Review in 2008, argued that the dominant regulatory model for consumer credit, insurance, and investment products rested on disclosure and unfettered choice, and that financial literacy education embraced as a corollary to that model lacked empirical support. Willis pointed to the velocity of change in financial products, to the distance between consumer skills and the skills required to evaluate complex and nonstandardized products, to persistent biases in decision making, and to the disparity in resources between educators and financial services firms. In Performance Based Consumer Law, published in the University of Chicago Law Review in 2015, Willis extended the critique and wrote that mandated disclosure routinely had little or no positive impact on consumer marketplace decisions.

Omri Ben-Shahar and Carl E. Schneider reached a broader conclusion in More Than You Wanted to Know: The Failure of Mandated Disclosure, published by Princeton University Press in 2014. Ben-Shahar and Schneider surveyed mandated disclosure across consumer, medical, and other settings and concluded that mandated disclosure rarely worked as intended. Summaries of the book emphasized that people found disclosures complex and obscure, that decision makers tended to strip information away rather than accumulate information, and that the volume of mandates exceeded the attention any person could give to the full set. Ben-Shahar and Schneider also argued that disclosure operated as an attractive substitute for lawmakers, because a new mandate allowed a legislature to appear to address a problem without undertaking the harder work of writing a substantive rule. Applied to consumer credit, that critique identified a cost beyond wasted paper: a disclosure response to high price lending could occupy the legislative field for years while leaving the price term, the fee structure, and the product design legally available, subject only to a form that a borrower might sign without absorbing.

The defense of the disclosure model, stated at the strength of a named source, begins in the statutory text that Congress enacted in 1968. Section 102 of Title I, codified at 15 U.S.C. section 1601(a), declared that competition among firms engaged in extending consumer credit would be strengthened by the informed use of credit, that informed use resulted from awareness of cost, and that meaningful disclosure would allow a consumer to compare credit terms and avoid uninformed use of credit. Senator Paul H. Douglas of Illinois, the principal Senate sponsor identified in historical accounts, treated uniform statement of the cost of credit as a means of exposing deception and encouraging price competition, according to Anne Fleming in The Long History of Truth in Lending, published in the Journal of Policy History in 2018. Fleming also documented a qualification that complicates any simple attribution of a pure disclosure philosophy to Douglas: during the long congressional debate, Douglas viewed disclosure as a complement to price ceilings and other consumer protection laws rather than as a substitute for substantive law.

Measured against those competing accounts, the evidence supports limited propositions with confidence and leaves larger propositions contested. Lacko and Pappalardo documented failure on forms in use in 2007 and improvement on prototype forms under test conditions. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau documented improvement on integrated forms in a quantitative validation study completed before the 2013 final rule. Willis documented reasons, grounded in product complexity and decision bias, to doubt that delivery of information translated into better marketplace decisions at scale. Ben-Shahar and Schneider raised an institutional objection about disclosure displacing substantive lawmaking. None of those sources, standing alone, established that standardized annual percentage rate disclosure caused borrowers as a class to pay less for credit between 1968 and any later dated endpoint, and none established that disclosure had no effect on any transaction. The honest inference is narrower. Standardization created a common vocabulary without which comparison would have depended on incompatible creditor presentations, and comprehension of that vocabulary varied sharply with form design, product complexity, and the conditions under which a borrower decided.

### What the Statute Did Not Do

The limits of Title I follow from the choice recorded in the enacted text. The 1968 act set no general ceiling on the price of consumer credit. A Congressional Research Service analysis of interest rate caps stated that no provision in the Truth in Lending Act capped annual percentage rates for most consumer lending and that a national usury cap would require an act of Congress. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation examination manual stated the same boundary: the act and Regulation Z did not tell financial institutions how much interest an institution could charge or whether an institution had to grant a consumer a loan. A creditor could therefore comply fully with Title I while charging a price that a state usury law, a later federal statute, or no applicable law at all would govern. Disclosure regulated the statement of the price rather than the permissibility of the price.

Usury regulation remained primarily a matter of state law after 1968, and the later federal fights over exportation and preemption arose under other statutes rather than under Title I. The National Bank Act and the Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980 supplied the legal ground on which national banks and insured state banks asserted authority to charge rates permitted by a home state across state lines, a development that weakened the practical reach of a borrower state usury ceiling for many card and bank loans. Title I did not create those preemption rules. The 1968 act occupied the disclosure field for covered consumer credit, while price authority stayed divided among state usury statutes, state retail installment laws, federal banking statutes, and later targeted federal enactments.

Coverage supplied a second limit. Title I addressed consumer credit, defined around credit extended to a natural person for personal, family, or household purposes. Regulation Z exempted credit extended primarily for a business, commercial, or agricultural purpose. A loan taken to finance a business therefore stood outside the standardized disclosure system even when the borrower was an individual with limited bargaining power. The line turned on purpose rather than on sophistication or need. That boundary mattered for sole proprietors and for borrowers whose household and business finances overlapped, because classification of purpose determined whether the annual percentage rate and finance charge disclosures of Title I applied at all.

A third limit concerned the distance between disclosure and prevention. Title I as enacted in 1968 did not by itself prevent high cost lending, because a high price disclosed accurately remained a lawful price under the disclosure title. Congress addressed parts of that gap through substantive amendments housed inside the same statute in later decades. The Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act of 1994, enacted as part of the Riegle Community Development and Regulatory Improvement Act of 1994, added additional disclosure duties and substantive limitations for certain closed end home mortgage loans with rates or fees above specified thresholds. The Credit Card Accountability Responsibility and Disclosure Act of 2009 moved further from a disclosure only model for cards by restricting rate increases, fee practices, and billing conduct. The Dodd Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 added ability to repay and qualified mortgage requirements for residential mortgage credit, again through substantive standards rather than through a more detailed cost form alone. Those later enactments demonstrate the boundary of the 1968 architecture, because Congress returned to the same title to impose conduct and price related restrictions after decades in which disclosure had operated as the principal federal tool. Placed within [the longer sequence of American consumer protection law](/2019/02/15/us-consumer-protection-law-history/), Title I reads as a foundational information statute whose omissions defined the agenda for later substantive law: no rate ceiling in the original title, no reach into business purpose credit, no general federal usury rule, and no prohibition on a costly loan whose terms a creditor had stated in the required form.

## Assessment and Where to Read Next

Assessment begins where the profile began, with the choice to make credit comparable rather than cheap. On its own terms, the disclosure philosophy was a coherent theory of regulation: opacity was the market failure, standardization was the cure, and competition would discipline price once every creditor stated cost in the same two figures. The statute supplied those figures through the finance charge and the annual percentage rate, attached private enforcement with formula damages and attorney fees so that small violations were worth pursuing, and protected the pledge of a home with a rescission right that few disclosure statutes of the era matched. For roughly four decades, that architecture organized American consumer finance regulation, and statute after statute borrowed the disclosure first pattern Title I had set.

An evenhanded verdict also records what the theory could not establish. The 1968 design presupposed a borrower who would read standardized disclosures, understand them, and act on them in time to choose the cheaper offer. Controlled testing by Lacko and Pappalardo and by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau showed that form design moved comprehension in measurable ways, while baseline forms left large shares of tested borrowers unable to identify the price of the loan before them. Willis and Ben-Shahar and Schneider pressed the harder question: whether delivery of information changed marketplace decisions at scale, and whether mandated disclosure displaced the substantive rules that would have addressed price and product design directly. The evidence assembled for this profile supports recognition gains under controlled conditions and leaves price effects in live markets contested, because the testing programs were not designed to measure them. Neither the market discipline claim nor the disclosure failure claim has been established beyond the narrower findings the studies support.

Congress itself registered the limits of the theory through amendments that operated inside the statute. The cardholder protections of 1970, the billing error procedure of 1974, and the lease disclosures of 1976 stayed within the disclosure philosophy while regulating conduct at the margins. The high cost mortgage rules of 1994 defined a band of expensive lending and attached prohibitions inside it, a first admission that some terms were dangerous in operation rather than merely obscure in statement. The card legislation of 2009 and the mortgage underwriting duties of 2010 crossed the line openly, regulating fees, rate increases, payment allocation, and the decision to lend. The amended statute therefore carries two philosophies at once: information as the foundation, and substantive control as the overlay for the products and abuses where disclosure was judged insufficient.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Does Truth in Lending regulate interest rates?

No. The Truth in Lending Act sets no ceiling on interest rates, no maximum finance charge, and no federal judgment about a fair price for consumer credit. Congress chose in 1968 to regulate information rather than price, requiring creditors to state the cost of credit in standardized terms, including the finance charge in dollars and the annual percentage rate, so that borrowers could compare offers on an equal basis. A creditor that complied with the disclosure rules could lawfully charge a high rate, provided the cost was stated in the prescribed form. Rate limits remained primarily a matter of state usury law after 1968, and later federal statutes addressed pricing through other legal authority. The distinction between regulating what credit costs and regulating what a borrower is told about the cost is the central fact about the statute, and most misunderstandings of the law trace to the assumption that it caps what lenders may charge.

### Q: What is an APR under Truth in Lending?

The annual percentage rate is a statutory construct that converts the dollar cost of credit into a standardized yearly figure so that different offers can be compared. For credit other than open end plans, section 1606 defines the annual percentage rate as the nominal annual rate that yields a sum equal to the finance charge when applied to the unpaid balances of the amount financed, calculated by the actuarial method. For open end credit, the statute states a formula based on the finance charge for the period divided by the balance on which it is based, multiplied by the number of periods in a year. The annual percentage rate differs from the interest rate stated in a note: the note rate governs interest accrual on the unpaid balance, while the annual percentage rate spreads the broader finance charge, including specified fees, across the amount financed and the payment schedule. Tolerance rules treat a disclosed rate as accurate within a narrow margin.

### Q: What is the right of rescission in Truth in Lending?

Section 1635 gives a consumer a short period to undo certain transactions secured by a principal dwelling, without proof of reliance, without proof that a better offer existed elsewhere, and without any showing that the terms were unfair. The right attaches to consumer credit transactions in which a security interest is or will be retained or acquired in property used as the consumer principal dwelling, such as home equity loans, home improvement loans, and refinancings by a new creditor. It does not apply to a residential mortgage transaction, meaning a loan financing the acquisition or initial construction of the dwelling. When a consumer rescinds, the security interest becomes void and the consumer is not liable for any amount, including any finance charge. The creditor must return money or property given in connection with the transaction within twenty calendar days, and the consumer then tenders the money or property the creditor delivered.

### Q: How long do you have to rescind under Truth in Lending?

The ordinary period runs until midnight of the third business day following the last of three events: consummation, delivery of the required notice of the right to rescind, or delivery of all material disclosures. For rescission purposes, business day means all calendar days except Sundays and federal legal public holidays, so Saturdays count. The right is exercised by written notice to the creditor; notice sent by mail is considered given when mailed, and no lawsuit is required. Where the required notice or the material disclosures were not delivered, the period extends: the right expires three years after consummation, or upon transfer of all of the consumer interest in the property, or upon sale, whichever occurs first. In Beach v. Ocwen Federal Bank, the Supreme Court treated the three year period as extinguishing the right itself, ending it for offensive and defensive use alike after the period runs.

### Q: What did Jesinoski decide about Truth in Lending?

In Jesinoski v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc., 574 U.S. 259 (2015), a unanimous Supreme Court held that a borrower exercises the right of rescission by giving written notice to the creditor within the three year period, and need not also file a lawsuit within that period. The borrowers had refinanced a home loan, mailed a letter purporting to rescind exactly three years after consummation, received a refusal, and filed suit roughly a year after the letter. The lower courts treated the suit as untimely on the view that rescission required filing an action within three years. Justice Scalia, writing for the Court, read section 1635(a) as a complete statement of the method of exercise: the borrower rescinds by notifying the creditor in accordance with the governing regulations. Timely notice makes the rescission timely. The decision resolved a division among the courts of appeals but did not decide whether the borrowers held a valid ground for rescission.

### Q: What is Regulation Z under Truth in Lending?

Regulation Z is the implementing regulation that turns the statutory commands of Title I into working rules. Congress directed the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System to prescribe regulations carrying out the purposes of the act, and the Board issued Regulation Z at Part 226 of Title 12 of the Code of Federal Regulations, supplying definitions, calculation rules, tolerances, and model forms. The Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act transferred rulemaking authority to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, effective July 21, 2011, and the bureau recodified the regulation at Part 1026 of Title 12 in December 2011, preserving the substance and numbering logic of the earlier version. Regulation Z decides recurring classification questions the statute cannot answer alone, such as which application fees count toward the finance charge and how tolerances apply to irregular transactions, and it publishes model disclosure forms whose proper use gives a creditor a safe harbor on the forms covered.

### Q: Is your liability capped for unauthorized card charges under Truth in Lending?

Yes, through provisions added by Title V of Public Law 91-508 in 1970, codified at sections 1642 to 1644 of Title 15, not through the Fair Credit Billing Act of 1974, to which the protections are often misassigned. Section 1643 provides that a cardholder can be liable for unauthorized use only if the card was an accepted credit card, only if the liability does not exceed fifty dollars, only if the issuer gave adequate notice of the potential liability, only if the issuer provided a means of notification in case of loss or theft, and only if the unauthorized use occurred before the issuer was notified, with the burden of proof on the issuer. Section 1642 separately provided that no credit card could be issued except in response to a request or application, answering the practice of mailing live cards to consumers who had never asked for one. Both rules allocate conduct and loss rather than informing comparison, and neither sets an interest rate.

### Q: What other laws are in the same act as Truth in Lending?

The Truth in Lending Act is Title I of the Consumer Credit Protection Act, Public Law 90-321, enacted May 29, 1968. The same public law restricted wage garnishment in Title III, which caps the share of disposable earnings subject to garnishment, and created a National Commission on Consumer Finance in Title IV. Later Congresses added further titles to the same parent act: the Fair Credit Reporting Act in 1970 as Title VI, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act in 1974 as Title VII, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act in 1977 as Title VIII, and the Electronic Fund Transfer Act in 1978 as Title IX. The titles are linked by cross references, and a consumer dispute can move through several of them, from an inaccurate disclosure to a credit report to collection and garnishment, without leaving the parent act. The umbrella structure is the structural fact that ties much of federal consumer credit law together.

### Q: What counts as a finance charge under the Truth in Lending Act?

Section 1605 defines the finance charge as the sum of all charges payable directly or indirectly by the person to whom credit is extended, imposed directly or indirectly by the creditor as an incident to the extension of credit. The list includes interest, time price differentials, points, service and carrying charges, loan fees, finder fees, investigation and credit report fees, premiums for guarantee or insurance against default, and borrower paid mortgage broker fees. Charges of a type payable in a comparable cash transaction are excluded, as are specified real estate settlement charges such as title examination fees, preparation of loan documents, escrows for taxes and insurance, notarization fees, and appraisal fees, when bona fide and reasonable. Because the finance charge determines the annual percentage rate, most litigation about disclosure accuracy is really litigation about the finance charge boundary, turning on whether a contested fee was imposed as an incident to credit or falls within an exclusion.

### Q: When must a lender provide the Loan Estimate and Closing Disclosure for a mortgage?

Under the integrated mortgage disclosure rule issued by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, which took effect October 3, 2015, the Loan Estimate must be delivered within three business days after a consumer applies, and the Closing Disclosure must be delivered three business days before consummation. The rule combined the mortgage disclosures required under the Truth in Lending Act with those required under the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act. The three page Loan Estimate presents the early disclosures, and the five page Closing Disclosure presents the final figures in the same categories and layout, so estimated and final amounts can be compared line by line. The integrated forms descend from the 1980 simplification program and the 1988 tabular experiment with card solicitations, and they represent the disclosure method in its most developed form for closed end consumer mortgage loans.

### Q: What are the statutory damages for a Truth in Lending violation?

Section 1640 awards a prevailing borrower any actual damage sustained, plus statutory damages computed by formula without proof of loss. In an individual action over a closed end transaction, the general measure is twice the amount of the finance charge. For a closed end transaction secured by real property or a dwelling, a banded award runs from 400 dollars to 4,000 dollars. For an open end consumer credit plan not secured by real property or a dwelling, the measure is twice the finance charge with a minimum of 500 dollars and a maximum of 5,000 dollars, higher where an established pattern or practice is shown. In class actions, total recovery may not exceed the lesser of 1,000,000 dollars or 1 percent of the net worth of the creditor. A successful plaintiff also recovers costs and a reasonable attorney fee. Defenses include correction of a discovered error within sixty days and the bona fide error defense for unintentional clerical or calculation mistakes made despite reasonable procedures.

### Q: What makes a mortgage a high cost mortgage under the Truth in Lending Act?

A mortgage falls into the high cost category added by the Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act of 1994 when either the annual percentage rate or the points and fees cross statutory thresholds. As enacted, the annual percentage rate trigger was reached when the rate exceeded the yield on Treasury securities of comparable maturity by more than eight percentage points for a first lien loan or ten percentage points for a subordinate lien loan. The points and fees trigger was reached when total points and fees payable at or before consummation exceeded the greater of four hundred dollars or eight percent of the total loan amount. Loans inside the band attracted enhanced disclosure delivered earlier than the ordinary timetable, plus outright prohibitions, including restrictions on balloon payments, negative amortization, default interest rates, advance collection of periodic payments, financing of single premium credit insurance, and certain prepayment penalties. The trigger structure was the central design choice: Congress regulated only the expensive end of the market rather than mortgage terms generally.

### Q: What are the deadlines in the billing error procedure under the Truth in Lending Act?

The Fair Credit Billing Act, enacted October 28, 1974, created a procedure for disputing errors in open end account statements. A consumer who believes a statement contains a billing error may give written notice within sixty days after the creditor transmitted the statement, identifying the account, stating the belief, giving the amount in dispute, and setting out the reasons. Covered errors include a charge not made to the consumer or not in the amount reflected, a charge for goods or services not received or not delivered as agreed, a failure to reflect a payment or credit, a computational error, and a charge for which clarification was requested. The creditor must acknowledge the notice in writing within thirty days, unless the dispute is resolved sooner, and must resolve it within two complete billing cycles and no later than ninety days after receipt. During the investigation the creditor may not collect the disputed amount, may not impose a finance charge on it, and may not report it as delinquent. A creditor that misses the time limits forfeits the right to collect the disputed amount up to a statutory ceiling, even if the charge proves valid.

### Q: Does the Truth in Lending Act cover credit cards?

Yes. Open end consumer credit plans, including general purpose credit cards and charge cards, fall within Title I, and section 1637 prescribes the disclosures for such plans. Because no fixed amount financed or payment schedule exists at account opening, the disclosures differ from closed end rules: the creditor must disclose the conditions for imposing a finance charge, the method of determining the balance on which the charge is imposed, the method of computing the finance charge, each periodic rate with the range of balances and the corresponding nominal annual percentage rate, other charges, security interests for secured plans, and billing rights protections. Card applications and solicitations carry an additional tabular disclosure of key rates, fees, the grace period, and the balance computation method, added in 1988. Credit card accounts are also subject to the billing error procedure, the fifty dollar limit on liability for unauthorized use, and the substantive card rules added in 2009 governing rate increases, penalty fees, and payment allocation.

### Q: What is the ability to repay rule under the Truth in Lending Act?

Title XIV of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act added section 1639c, which states that a creditor may not make a residential mortgage loan unless the creditor makes a reasonable and good faith determination, based on verified and documented information, that at consummation the consumer has a reasonable ability to repay according to the loan terms, together with taxes, insurance, and assessments. The determination must consider credit history, income and expected income, current obligations, debt to income ratio or residual income, employment status, and other financial resources apart from the consumer equity in the dwelling, and income and assets relied upon must be verified through reasonably reliable third party records. The rule answered the collapse of mortgage lending in the years before 2010, when loans had been originated on collateral value rather than documented capacity to pay. Unlike disclosure duties, the rule cannot be cured by giving a better disclosure, and a borrower signature acknowledging the terms does not waive the creditor obligation.

### Q: Which agencies enforce the Truth in Lending Act?

Administrative enforcement was originally divided among the agencies that supervised the institutions involved. Section 1607 assigned national banks to the Comptroller of the Currency, member banks to the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, other insured banks to the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, with parallel assignments for savings institutions, federal credit unions, air carriers, and agricultural creditors, while the Federal Trade Commission held the residual commission against creditors not assigned to another agency. Rulemaking, however, was concentrated in the Federal Reserve Board, which prescribed Regulation Z. The Dodd-Frank Act dismantled the divided structure: on the designated transfer date of July 21, 2011, the newly created Consumer Financial Protection Bureau received rulemaking authority together with supervision and enforcement authority from the banking regulators and the Federal Trade Commission. The bureau therefore became the first agency to write the disclosure rules, supervise the largest creditors for compliance, and bring enforcement proceedings in its own name, while borrowers retained the private right of action with formula damages and attorney fees.

### Q: Are Title IV student loans exempt from the Truth in Lending Act?

Yes. Section 1603(7) exempts loans made, insured, or guaranteed pursuant to a program authorized by Title IV of the Higher Education Act of 1965, codified at 20 U.S.C. sections 1070 and following, because those loans operate under a separate and comprehensive federal disclosure regime. Regulation Z commentary states the limit with equal clarity: the exemption does not apply to private education loans, which receive Title I treatment under disclosure provisions added for that market. The student loan exemption is one of several exemptions that allocate regulatory responsibility rather than deny economic significance: business purpose credit, credit to organizations rather than natural persons, securities and commodities accounts with registered broker dealers, and public utility credit under tariff regulation stand outside Title I while remaining subject to other legal regimes. Conduct beyond the edges of the statute may be lawful under Title I and still heavily regulated elsewhere.

### Q: What is a qualified mortgage under the Truth in Lending Act?

A qualified mortgage is a residential mortgage loan defined in section 1639c(b)(2), added by Title XIV of the Dodd-Frank Act, whose features give the originating creditor a presumption that the ability to repay requirement has been satisfied. The definition requires that regular periodic payments may not increase the principal balance or allow deferral of principal, that the terms do not result in a balloon payment apart from allowed exceptions, that income and financial resources are verified and documented, that underwriting rests on a fully amortizing payment schedule, that the loan complies with agency guidelines on debt to income ratios or alternative measures of ability to pay, that total points and fees do not exceed three percent of the total loan amount, and that the term does not exceed thirty years. The qualified mortgage is the compliance safe harbor for the ability to repay rule: instead of proving a bespoke underwriting judgment for each loan, a creditor originating a loan within the defined features gains the presumption of compliance. Title XIV also restricted prepayment penalties and loan originator compensation tied to terms, and expanded high cost mortgage protections.

### Q: Does the Truth in Lending Act cover consumer leases?

Yes. The Consumer Leasing Act of 1976 amended the Truth in Lending Act by adding a chapter on consumer leases, codified at sections 1667 to 1667f of Title 15. Congress acted because leasing an automobile or another durable good for personal, family, or household purposes had become a functional alternative to buying on installment credit, and consumers comparing a lease against a credit sale faced incompatible terms. A lessor must give specified disclosures before consummation, covering the amount due at inception, the payment schedule and total of payments, other charges, and end of term liability for the difference between the estimated residual value and the realized value. The act also regulated lease advertising and imposed civil liability, with statutory damages of 25 percent of the total monthly payments, bounded between 200 dollars and 2,000 dollars. One provision reached beyond disclosure by treating an estimated residual value exceeding the actual value by more than a stated margin as presumptively unreasonable and by restricting balloon payments in consumer leases.

### Q: What rules govern credit advertising under the Truth in Lending Act?

Part C of the subchapter, sections 1661 to 1665, governs credit advertising, with section 1663 addressing open end plans and section 1664 addressing credit other than open end plans. The organizing principle, stated in the regulation, is that an advertisement may not state a specific credit term unless the term actually is or will be available, and an advertisement that states certain triggering terms must also state the additional terms the statute and regulation specify. For closed end credit, triggering terms include the amount or percentage of any down payment, the number of payments or period of repayment, the amount of any payment, and the amount of any finance charge; the advertisement must then state the amount or percentage of the down payment, the terms of repayment, and the annual percentage rate. Congress brought advertising inside the scheme because a disclosure delivered at closing could not undo a decision already made on the strength of a misleading advertisement, and the same logic later produced the tabular disclosures required in card applications and solicitations in 1988.
