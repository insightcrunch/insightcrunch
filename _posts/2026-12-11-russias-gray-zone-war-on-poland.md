Ask whether Russia's gray-zone war on Poland is real and you will get two confident answers, both wrong. The first says yes, obviously, and points to a list: a warehouse burns, a rail line is interfered with, satellite navigation signals fail across a region, a drone crosses a border, a coordinated falsehood spreads about Ukrainian refugees, people are pushed toward a frontier by a neighboring state. The second says no, calm down, and points to the same list with a shrug: warehouses burn for many reasons, navigation signals degrade for many reasons, drones stray, lies spread, borders are messy places. Both readings share a hidden assumption, which is that the question is settled by the incidents. It is not. The incidents are the evidence. The question is what sits behind them.

This is the analytical problem this piece exists to solve. A coercion campaign conducted below the threshold of open war does not announce itself, and it is not supposed to. Its whole design is to present the defender with a stream of events each of which, taken alone, is arguable. Was that arson or an electrical fault. Was that a probe or a navigation error. Was that an information operation or ordinary online noise. Each question is answerable only slowly, expensively, and often inconclusively, and while the defender litigates each one, the stream continues. The pressure is not in any single event. The pressure is in the aggregate, and the aggregate is invisible to anyone who insists on resolving the events one at a time before forming a judgment.

This article is the top-line assessment of that problem for Poland, and the piece the rest of this cluster defers to. It does not catalog episodes. Cataloging is what the coverage already does, and it is precisely the habit that keeps readers from seeing the shape of the thing. Instead it maps the tools into a single coercion architecture, states what that architecture appears to be for, explains the logic that keeps it below the line, and separates honestly what is established in the open record from what is inference. The judgment it reaches is neither of the two confident answers above. It is that Poland faces a real, sustained, deniable campaign of sub-threshold pressure, that this campaign is meaningfully coordinated at the level of strategy even where individual acts are not centrally scripted, that it falls well short of open war and is designed to, and that the defender's hardest task is not proving any one case but reading the campaign in the pattern. For the top-line judgment on whether the confrontation goes further than this, see [the series assessment of whether Russia would attack Poland at all](/2025/01/01/will-russia-attack-poland-risk-assessment/), which is the parent this piece sits beneath.

## Naming the Problem: Is There a Gray-Zone War on Poland?

The phrase gray zone is doing a lot of work, and it is worth being precise about what it names before it is used to carry an argument. It names the band of coercive activity that sits above ordinary diplomatic friction and below the use of armed force that a state would recognize, and be recognized as having a right to answer, as an attack. The band exists because the international order draws a bright legal and political line around armed attack and attaches consequences to crossing it, and because a state that wants to impose costs on an adversary without paying those consequences will naturally work up against the line without stepping over it. That is not a Russian invention. It is a structural feature of any order that punishes overt aggression sharply and covert pressure weakly, and states of many kinds have operated in that band for as long as the band has existed.

What makes the band strategically interesting rather than merely annoying is that it is not a waiting room. It is not where a state sits while it decides whether to attack. It is a theater of operations in its own right, with its own logic, its own measures of success, and its own kinds of victory. A campaign in this band can achieve things a war cannot: it can make a society doubt its own institutions, make an alliance doubt its own members, make a government spend attention and money on internal problems, and make a public tire of a policy it once supported, all without giving the target a moment it can point to and call the attack. That last clause is the whole point. The gray zone is not a lesser form of war. It is a different instrument, chosen for a different job, and judging it by whether it looks like war is a category error.

### Is Russia already waging a gray-zone war on Poland?

On the durable open record, Poland has experienced sabotage prosecutions, airspace incursions, satellite navigation interference, engineered migratory pressure at its eastern frontier, cyber intrusion, and sustained disinformation. That those categories are present is established fact. That they form one campaign with one strategic aim is an assessment, and this piece argues it is the better reading.

The distinction in that answer matters more than any rhetorical flourish, so it is worth dwelling on. Facts and assessments are different objects, and the great failure mode of writing about this subject is laundering the second into the first. It is a fact that Poland has investigated and, in a number of instances, prosecuted individuals for acts of sabotage connected to the war in Ukraine. It is a fact that satellite navigation interference has been a persistent, documented feature of the Baltic region's electromagnetic environment. It is a fact that unmanned aircraft and stray munitions have entered or approached Polish airspace on documented occasions. It is a fact that Belarus engineered migratory pressure against the Polish frontier, and that Poland responded with physical and legal border measures. It is a fact that Polish institutions have been the target of information operations that pushed identifiable falsehoods.

It is an assessment, not a fact, that these separate categories are directed by one authority toward one end. It is an assessment that the aim is to degrade Polish support for Ukraine rather than, say, to prepare the ground for something worse. It is an assessment that the cumulative pressure is significant rather than marginal. These assessments can be argued for well or badly, and this piece argues for them explicitly and shows the reasoning, but they do not become facts by being argued for confidently. Any reader who leaves this article unable to say which of its claims are which has been failed, and any writer on this subject who blurs the boundary is doing the adversary's work, because the boundary is exactly what a disinformation effort exists to erode.

So the answer to the naming question is yes with a qualification that carries most of the analytical content. Yes, the pressure Poland faces is best described as a gray-zone campaign, because the alternative descriptions fit the evidence worse. Calling it a run of unrelated incidents requires believing that six categories of hostile activity, all of which serve a common interest, all of which intensified in a common period, and several of which have been traced to a common source, arrived independently. Calling it war requires ignoring that nothing in it has crossed the line that would trigger the response a war triggers, and that this restraint is consistent, deliberate, and central to how the whole thing works. The qualification is that coordination at the level of strategy does not mean centralized scripting at the level of the individual act, and confusing those two is where most of the arguments on this subject go wrong.

## Hybrid, Gray Zone, and the Words That Get in the Way

The vocabulary in this field is a mess, and the mess is not cosmetic. Terms that name different things get used interchangeably, arguments proceed at cross-purposes, and readers come away believing that a disagreement about definitions is a disagreement about the world. Since this article's central claim depends on a distinction between a campaign and its incidents, the words have to be tidied before the claim can be evaluated.

Hybrid warfare, as the term is most usefully employed, names the combination of military and non-military instruments in a single coordinated effort. The emphasis is on the mixing. A hybrid effort is one where a conventional formation and an information operation and an economic lever are being used together toward one objective, and the analytical point of the term is that treating the military component in isolation misreads what is happening. Note what this definition does not require: it does not require the effort to stay below any threshold. A campaign that mixes armed force with subversion is hybrid, and it is also war. The term describes composition, not intensity.

### What is hybrid warfare, exactly?

It names the coordinated use of military and non-military instruments together toward one objective, where reading any component in isolation misreads the whole. It describes composition, not intensity, so a hybrid effort can include open force. Gray-zone activity is different: it names the band below armed attack, defined by intensity rather than by mixture.

Gray zone names something else entirely. It names a band of intensity, bounded above by the use of force a state would recognize as an attack and below by ordinary friction. The definition is about where the activity sits relative to a line, not about what instruments it combines. A purely non-military effort is gray-zone activity. A single instrument used alone is gray-zone activity if it stays under the line. The two terms answer different questions, and the sentence that uses them as synonyms has said nothing.

The practical consequence for this assessment is that what Poland faces is squarely gray-zone activity and only partially hybrid. It is gray-zone because every element of it is calibrated against the response line, which is the defining property. It is hybrid in a weak sense, because it combines multiple non-military instruments, and not in the strong sense, because it does not combine them with military force. That distinction matters, and it matters in a direction that cuts against the alarmist reading: the absence of the military component is not a detail waiting to be filled in. It is the constraint the whole design is built around, and a campaign that added it would not be an intensified version of this campaign. It would be a different object.

Two further terms deserve a note because they carry freight the reader should be aware of. Sub-threshold is the most honest of the available labels, because it says exactly what it means and smuggles in nothing, and this piece uses it where precision matters more than familiarity. Its only drawback is that it names the thing by what it is not. Active measures is an older term from a specific tradition, and it is often reached for because it sounds knowing, but it carries the implication of a continuous historical program, which is a substantive claim being imported through a word choice rather than argued. Readers should be suspicious of any argument whose weight is carried by that implication, including arguments they agree with.

There is a deeper reason to insist on the terminology, and it goes to the campaign's own interests. A field with sloppy vocabulary is a field where nothing can be pinned down, and a coercer that benefits from nothing being pinned down benefits from the sloppiness. When a commentator calls a fire hybrid warfare and another calls it a police matter, they may not disagree about a single fact. They may simply be using words that do not connect. The argument that follows is unresolvable, consumes attention, generates heat, and produces exactly the condition the information tool is trying to produce, and no one had to inject anything. The vocabulary problem is a free gift the field keeps handing over, and tightening it is one of the cheapest defensive measures available to anyone who writes about this.

One more distinction is worth making because it is the one most often collapsed. There is a difference between an act being deniable and an act being ambiguous. A deniable act is one whose author can plausibly disclaim it; the act itself may be perfectly clear. A warehouse that was deliberately set alight is unambiguously arson, and the arson is deniable only as to who ordered it. An ambiguous act is one whose nature is unclear regardless of authorship: an object in the sky might be a probe or a navigation failure, and no attribution finding resolves that. The tools in the map exploit both properties, but they exploit them separately, and a defender who conflates them will build the wrong countermeasure. Better forensics answer deniability. They do nothing for ambiguity, which is answered by better classification. The specialist pieces in this cluster divide roughly along that seam, and the division is not arbitrary.

## What Is Actually Known and What Is Assessed

The evidentiary picture in this domain has a peculiar shape, and understanding that shape is a prerequisite for judging anything else. In a conventional force assessment, the analyst's problem is that the adversary hides capabilities: units are concealed, readiness is overstated or understated, exercises are shaped for foreign consumption. The uncertainty is about the thing itself. In a sub-threshold campaign, the analyst's problem is inverted. The acts are mostly visible. A burned warehouse is visible. A jammed signal is measurable by anyone with a receiver. A migratory surge at a frontier is photographable. Disinformation is, by design, published. What is hidden is not the act but the hand behind it, and the intent behind the hand.

This inversion has a consequence that is easy to miss. It means the defender is rarely short of evidence and almost always short of proof. There is no shortage of things to look at. There is a chronic shortage of the specific kind of finding that would let a government stand up and say, with the confidence a serious accusation requires, that this act was directed by that state for this purpose. Attribution in this domain is slow, resource-intensive, frequently classified when it succeeds, and often simply unavailable. And the adversary knows this, which is why the acts are structured to keep it that way. The gap between what a defender believes and what a defender can demonstrate is not an accident of the campaign. It is a product of the campaign.

### What is known versus what is only assessed?

Known: the categories of hostile activity present in and around Poland, the documented cases prosecuted or investigated, the general logic by which sub-threshold tools buy deniability. Assessed: whether the categories form one directed campaign, what its strategic objective is, how much cumulative effect it achieves, and how central it is to Russian planning.

Work through the categories and the pattern becomes clearer. On physical sabotage, the open record contains investigations and prosecutions in Poland and across several European states involving low-level individuals recruited for specific tasks, frequently through intermediaries, frequently for money, frequently with limited knowledge of who was ultimately paying. This recruitment pattern is well documented across the region and is itself informative: it is not how a state runs its intelligence officers, and it is exactly how a state buys deniability. What is established is the pattern of proxy recruitment. What is assessed is the chain above the recruiter. The specialist treatment of this tool, including the aims, the target categories, and the attribution problem it creates, belongs to [the dedicated assessment of sabotage on Polish soil](/2026/12/21/sabotage-on-polish-soil/), which owns those questions; the point here is only how the tool fits the architecture.

On airspace, the record contains incursions of several kinds, ranging from stray munitions associated with the war next door, through unmanned aircraft of uncertain provenance, to objects whose flight profiles are hard to explain as accidents. The important analytical fact is that these categories are genuinely mixed, and that this mixture is useful to whoever benefits from it. A frontier state that has actually experienced accidental spillover cannot treat every airspace event as hostile, and a frontier state that has actually experienced deliberate probing cannot treat every airspace event as an accident. The ambiguity is not a failure of the defender's sensors. It is a resource the adversary spends.

On electromagnetic interference, the record is unusually solid because the effects are measurable by civil aviation and maritime users who have no stake in the political argument. Persistent degradation of satellite navigation across the Baltic region is documented by the people who fly and sail through it. What is assessed is intent: whether a given period of interference reflects protection of a military asset, a deliberate imposition of cost on civil users, or a byproduct of activity aimed at something else entirely. The measurement is a fact. The purpose is an inference.

On engineered migration, the record is the clearest of all, because the instrumentalization was conducted openly enough that its architects made little effort to conceal the mechanics. People were brought to a frontier and pushed toward it. This category deserves a note on framing that the rest of this piece will honor: the people involved are people, not a weapon, and the fact that a state instrumentalized their movement is a fact about the state, not about them. Analysts who slip into describing human beings as a munition have adopted the coercer's own frame, and have thereby lost the ability to see what is actually being done and by whom.

On cyber and on information operations, the record contains sustained activity of varying sophistication against Polish institutions, infrastructure operators, and public discourse. The specialist treatment of the information dimension, including the narratives, the vectors, and the resilience question, belongs to [the assessment of Russian disinformation aimed at Poland](/2027/02/01/russian-disinformation-aimed-at-poland/). What matters at the campaign level is that this tool is the only one of the six that operates continuously rather than episodically, which gives it a different function in the architecture, as the map below sets out.

## The Campaign Map: How Separate Tools Form One Architecture

Here is the artifact this article exists to provide, and the reason it is a pillar rather than another roundup. The tools are usually described one at a time, in the order the news mentioned them, which is precisely the order that hides the design. Arrange them instead by function and the architecture becomes visible: each tool buys a different kind of deniability, imposes a different kind of cost, and serves a different part of one strategic aim, and it is the combination, not any component, that constitutes the campaign.

| Tool family | What it does to Poland | Why it stays below the line | Function in the architecture | Cumulative effect if unanswered |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Physical sabotage | Damages logistics, industrial, and support infrastructure; injects fear into the support chain to Ukraine | Executed by recruited proxies with no formal link to the state; damage is local and reversible; no state signature to point at | Raises the friction and cost of Poland's role as a support corridor | Support becomes slower and dearer; a domestic argument opens about whether the role is worth it |
| Airspace and drone incursion | Forces costly alerts, scrambles, and decisions under time pressure; tests reaction | Plausibly accidental in a region with genuine spillover; damage usually nil; no casualties to answer for | Probes the response and trains the defender to hesitate | Reactions dull, or reactions overshoot; either outcome is useful to the coercer |
| Electromagnetic interference | Degrades civil navigation for aviation, shipping, and ordinary users | No damage, no injury, no clear author; effects blend with a noisy spectrum | Imposes diffuse background cost and demonstrates reach into daily life | Normalization of degraded conditions; a public habituated to disruption |
| Engineered migratory pressure | Loads the frontier with a humanitarian and political crisis the state must manage | Conducted by a neighboring state against its own visa rules; the acts are administrative, not military | Diverts governmental attention and resources; splits domestic and allied opinion | Border management consumes political capital; allies disagree about the right response |
| Cyber intrusion | Probes and occasionally disrupts institutions and infrastructure operators | Attribution is technically hard and legally slow; most activity is reconnaissance, not damage | Prepares options and gathers what the other tools need | Access accumulates quietly; the defender learns of it late |
| Information operations | Pushes narratives that corrode confidence in institutions, allies, and the Ukraine policy | Speech, not force; amplified through authentic domestic voices | Converts every other tool's effect into political argument | Each incident is refracted into division rather than resolve |

Read the rightmost column downward and the design states itself. No entry in it is a military defeat. Every entry is a political condition. That is the tell. A campaign whose success criteria are all political conditions is a political campaign that happens to use some physical means, and it should be assessed by political measures, not by counting damage. The reason so much coverage concludes that the pressure is unserious is that it counts damage, finds the damage small, and stops. The damage is small. The damage was never the product.

Read the third column downward and a second design feature appears. Each tool buys deniability by a different mechanism, and the mechanisms do not overlap. Sabotage is deniable because the hand is a proxy. Airspace incursion is deniable because accidents genuinely happen. Interference is deniable because the spectrum is genuinely noisy. Migration is deniable because the acts are administrative. Cyber is deniable because attribution is technically slow. Information operations are deniable because speech is speech. A defender who develops a good answer to one of these has developed an answer to one of these. The architecture is diversified precisely so that no single countermeasure, and no single act of successful attribution, collapses it.

### Which tools make up the pressure Poland faces?

Six families: physical sabotage through recruited proxies, drone and airspace incursion, electromagnetic interference with satellite navigation, engineered migratory pressure at the eastern frontier, cyber intrusion against institutions and operators, and sustained information operations. Each buys deniability by a different mechanism, which is why no single countermeasure answers the set.

The map also clarifies something that pure enumeration obscures, which is that the tools are not interchangeable. They are specialized. Information operations are the only continuous tool; the others are episodic. Cyber is the only tool that is primarily preparatory rather than effective in itself. Sabotage is the only tool that imposes direct physical cost on the Ukraine support chain, which is why it is the tool most tightly coupled to that particular aim. Airspace incursion is the only tool that forces a decision inside minutes, which makes it the tool best suited to studying how the defender decides. Migratory pressure is the only tool that generates a genuine humanitarian obligation the defender cannot discharge quickly, which makes it the tool best suited to consuming attention. Interference is the only tool that touches ordinary civilians continuously enough to shape their sense of normal.

Specialization implies design. Six tools that happened to be aimed at the same country by unrelated actors would not divide the functional space this neatly, and would not leave this few gaps. That inference is not proof, and it should not be dressed as proof. But it is the kind of structural observation that carries real evidentiary weight, and it is exactly what gets lost when the tools are examined one at a time.

## Why Poland Is the Object

A campaign is aimed, and asking why it is aimed here rather than somewhere else is one of the few questions in this domain where the answer is unusually clear and unusually informative. Poland is not being pressured because it is disliked, or because of history, or because of any of the atmospheric explanations that fill the space where analysis should be. Poland is being pressured because of what Poland does, and what Poland does is function as the physical and political spine of Western support to Ukraine. Remove that function and the campaign loses its explanation. Keep it and every element of the tool selection follows.

Consider what the function actually consists of, because the specifics are what make it attackable. Material moving eastward moves overland, and overland from the west means through Poland, across Polish rail gauges and Polish road networks, through Polish handling facilities, staged in Polish storage, escorted by Polish arrangements. This is not an incidental geographic fact. It is a structural one produced by the map: a landlocked recipient with a closed northern sea and a hostile southern one receives what it receives across a land frontier, and the land frontier that carries the traffic is the one that connects to the alliance's logistics. Poland occupies that position not by choice but by position. The logistics dimension of that role, the routes, the capacities, and the constraints, belongs to the pieces that own it, and this article does not restate them; what belongs here is only the consequence, which is that Poland's territory is functionally part of the support system and therefore a place where the support system can be attacked without attacking Ukraine.

Now apply the below-the-line constraint to that observation and the tool set generates itself. The support system cannot be attacked by force, because Polish territory is alliance territory and force against it is the thing the campaign exists to avoid. But the support system is not only force-vulnerable. It is fire-vulnerable, and fires are police matters. It is delay-vulnerable, and delay is what a customs argument or a labor dispute or a security incident produces. It is confidence-vulnerable, and confidence is what a narrative about the burden of the policy attacks. It is attention-vulnerable, and attention is what a crisis on a different frontier consumes. Every one of those vulnerabilities is reachable from below the line, and each of them corresponds to a tool in the map. The correspondence is not a coincidence. It is what it looks like when a campaign is designed against a specific function rather than against a country in general.

This reading has a consequence that cuts against the way the subject is usually discussed. It means the campaign is a compliment of a kind, in the specific sense that pressure is applied where pressure would pay, and pressure is being applied to Poland because Poland's role is load-bearing. A state that mattered less to the outcome next door would attract less of this. That is not a comforting thought and it is not meant as one, but it is analytically important, because it locates the campaign's cause in a Polish choice rather than in an immutable feature of the relationship. It also means the campaign is contingent in a way that the deeper structural tensions between the two states are not. The tensions predate the war and would survive its end. The campaign, on this reading, is a response to a specific policy and would have to be re-aimed if the policy changed.

The second reason Poland is the object is political rather than logistical, and it compounds the first. Poland is not merely a conduit. Poland is one of the most consistent advocates within the alliance for the policy the campaign opposes, which means Polish opinion is not only a thing to be affected but a thing whose movement would move others. An alliance argument in which the state closest to the problem and most exposed to its consequences starts hedging is an alliance argument that shifts, and a coercer who understood nothing else about European politics would understand that. This is why the information dimension is disproportionately weighted toward the sustainability of the Ukraine policy rather than toward the more obvious targets a crude campaign would pick. The aim is not to make Poles think badly of anyone. The aim is to make a specific policy feel expensive to the specific public most invested in it.

The third reason is that Poland is a demanding target in the ways that make the campaign's tools attractive and a poor target in the ways that would make cruder tools attractive. Polish institutions are robust, Polish public opinion on the core question is unusually settled by history and geography, and Polish willingness to spend on defense has been sustained rather than episodic. A coercer facing that profile has poor prospects with anything that requires a receptive audience or a fragile institution, and better prospects with tools that impose cost regardless of receptivity. Fires cost money whether or not anyone believes anything about them. Border crises consume ministerial hours whether or not anyone is persuaded. Degraded navigation annoys pilots who have no political opinion about it. The tool selection is, in part, an adaptation to a target that is hard to persuade and easy to tax, and reading it that way explains why the campaign leans as heavily as it does on friction rather than on argument.

Which produces a final observation worth carrying into the rest of the assessment. If the campaign is aimed at a function rather than at a country, then the question of what happens to it when the function changes is not idle speculation but the single most informative thing an observer could learn. A campaign that wound down would confirm the coercion reading. A campaign that persisted at strength, re-aimed at some other Polish attribute, would falsify it and would mean the assessment in this article was wrong about the most important thing in it. That is a real, checkable prediction, and stating it is the price of claiming the reading is analysis rather than assertion.

## The Below-the-Line Rule

This is the namable claim of this article and the governing rule of the cluster, and every specialist piece that follows should be read against it. State it plainly: a coercion campaign in this band works by staying below the line that would trigger a hard response, which means its power is necessarily cumulative and necessarily deniable, and which means the defender's task is to see the campaign in the incidents rather than to litigate each incident alone. Call it the below-the-line rule. Three claims are packed into it, and each does independent work.

The first claim is that the line is the organizing constraint, not a boundary the campaign is drifting toward. Everything about the tool selection follows from the requirement to stay under it. Ask why a state that possesses precision munitions in quantity uses recruited arsonists to damage a warehouse, and the answer is not that arson is more effective. Arson is dramatically less effective. Arson is chosen because a missile strike on a Polish warehouse is an armed attack on a NATO member and an arson is a police matter, and the difference in consequence between those two categories is the entire product being purchased. The tool is worse at damage and better at deniability, and the campaign is willing to trade almost unlimited amounts of the first for the second. Once that trade is understood, the whole tool set stops looking improvised and starts looking optimized.

The second claim is that power in this band is cumulative because it cannot be decisive. A single act constrained to stay below the response line cannot, by construction, achieve anything a state would go to war to prevent. That is what below the line means. So the campaign cannot win in one move, and it does not try to. It accretes. Each act contributes a small increment of cost, doubt, distraction, or division, and the theory of victory is that the increments sum over a long period to a political result the adversary would never concede to a demand and would fight to prevent from being taken by force. This is why measuring the campaign by its largest single act always understates it, in the same way that measuring erosion by its largest single rainfall always understates it.

The third claim is that deniability is not a courtesy the coercer extends to itself but the load-bearing element of the whole structure. Remove deniability and the campaign collapses immediately, because a proven state-directed program of arson against a NATO member's infrastructure is a different object in law, in politics, and in alliance procedure than a series of unsolved fires. Nothing about the physical acts would change. Everything about the response would. This asymmetry is why attribution, and not damage limitation, is the real battleground, and why the adversary invests so heavily in the recruitment structures, the mixed signal environments, and the plausible alternative explanations that keep proof out of reach.

### Why does the pressure stay below the response line?

Because crossing it would forfeit the campaign's only real advantage. Below the line, acts are police matters, insurance claims, and political arguments. Above it, they are armed attacks with alliance consequences attached. The coercer trades effectiveness for deniability deliberately, accepting weak individual acts in exchange for a response the defender cannot organize.

There is an important corollary to the rule that deserves its own statement, because it is where the strategic danger actually lives. If the campaign's power is cumulative, then the campaign has an inherent incentive to escalate slowly, and slow escalation against a fixed response line produces a specific pathology: the line moves. Not formally, not by anyone's decision, but practically, through the accumulated precedent of unanswered acts. Each incident that passes without a response establishes that acts of that kind pass without a response, which makes an act slightly beyond that kind the new marginal case. Nothing is ever decided. The band simply widens from the bottom. This is the mechanism by which a defender can end up tolerating, incident by reasonable incident, a level of hostile activity it would have refused outright if it had been proposed as a package at the start. The specialist treatment of where and how this band ends, and what the actual transition to open conflict would look like, belongs to [the assessment of when gray-zone pressure becomes open war](/2027/02/11/when-gray-zone-becomes-open-war/), which owns the threshold question; the point here is that the threshold is not a fixed feature of the terrain but a thing the campaign is actively working on.

The below-the-line rule also explains the strange emotional register of this subject, which oscillates between panic and boredom in a way that conventional threats do not. A conventional threat produces a stable emotional response because it produces a stable analytical picture: the forces are there or they are not, the buildup is happening or it is not. A sub-threshold campaign produces oscillation because each individual act genuinely does not justify alarm, and the aggregate genuinely does. A reader who focuses on the act feels foolish for worrying. A reader who focuses on the aggregate feels foolish for not having worried sooner. Both are responding correctly to what they are looking at, and the oscillation between them is not a failure of temperament. It is a diagnostic sign that the observer is switching between two levels of analysis without noticing.

## What the Coercion Architecture Aims At

Every assessment of a campaign has to answer what it is for, and this is where the honest analyst has to slow down, because intent is the least observable thing in the picture and the most confidently asserted. The acts are visible. The aims are inferred from the acts, from the context, and from the coercer's own stated positions, and inference from acts to aims is exactly where analysis goes wrong most often, because the same act is consistent with several purposes.

The most defensible reading, and the one this piece advances as its assessment, runs as follows. The campaign's primary aim is to raise the cost and lower the political sustainability of Poland's role in supporting Ukraine, which is a role that runs through Polish territory, Polish rail, Polish ports, Polish logistics, and Polish politics, and which is therefore attackable at each of those points by means that stay below the line. This aim explains the tool selection better than the alternatives explain it. It explains why sabotage clusters around logistics rather than around, say, symbolic national targets. It explains why the information operations push narratives about the burden of the Ukraine policy rather than narratives aimed at some unrelated fissure. It explains why the campaign intensified in step with the war rather than in step with any other variable. And it explains the restraint: a campaign aimed at making a policy expensive has every reason to avoid the one thing that would make the policy politically untouchable, which is an unambiguous attack.

The second aim, subordinate but real, is to test and shape the response. A campaign that expects to continue indefinitely needs to know where the line actually is, as opposed to where the treaties say it is, and there is no way to learn that except by approaching it and observing. Every airspace event that produces a measured response teaches something. Every act that produces no response teaches something. Every act that produces a disproportionate response teaches the most of all, because a defender that overreacts once can be induced to overreact again on a schedule of the coercer's choosing. Reading the response is not a side effect of the campaign. For some of the tools, particularly the airspace ones, it is the main product, which is why the specialist piece on [how to read a drone incursion over Poland as accident or probe](/2027/01/01/drones-over-poland-accident-or-probe/) treats classification rather than countermeasures as the central problem.

The third aim, the most speculative and the one that most deserves an explicit uncertainty flag, is preparation. Some fraction of the cyber activity, and possibly some fraction of the reconnaissance embedded in other tools, may be laying groundwork for options that would only be exercised in a conflict that has not happened and may never happen. This is a genuinely open question. Preparatory access looks identical to espionage until it is used, and the analyst who assumes preparation is looking at the same evidence as the analyst who assumes espionage. Anyone who tells you confidently which it is has not been honest with you about what the evidence can bear. It is stated here as a possibility with low confidence, and it is deliberately not woven into the primary assessment, because a primary assessment that rests on the least observable claim in the picture is a bad assessment.

What the campaign is not aiming at is worth stating with equal clarity, because the alarmist reading fills this space with things the evidence does not support. There is no basis in the open record for reading the campaign as the shaping phase of an imminent invasion. That reading is available, it is not absurd, and serious people hold versions of it, but it requires the campaign to be a means to an end it does not currently look like a means to. Every tool in the map is optimized for a political effect rather than a military one, and a shaping campaign for an invasion would look different: it would prioritize the preparatory tools over the political ones, it would be more concealed rather than more visible, and it would not spend its most valuable asset, deniability, on producing arguments in someone else's parliament. The absence of that signature is not proof of absent intent. Intent can change faster than tooling. But an assessment has to reason from what is there, and what is there is a coercion architecture, not a preparation architecture.

## The Aggregation Problem and the Litigation Trap

If the below-the-line rule is the campaign's logic, the aggregation problem is the defender's, and it deserves its own treatment because it is where the practical difficulty concentrates and where most well-intentioned analysis fails.

State the problem cleanly. The campaign is composed of acts that are individually arguable and collectively unarguable. This is not a paradox and it is not sloppy thinking. It is a straightforward consequence of how evidence and probability work. Suppose, generously to the skeptic, that any given fire at a logistics facility has a modest chance of being hostile rather than accidental, and suppose the same for any given navigation outage, any given airspace event, any given intrusion. Each event, taken alone, should not persuade anyone. Now observe that the events cluster in a country, in a period, and around a function, all of which are exactly what one would expect if hostility were present and none of which are what one would expect if it were not. The clustering is evidence that the individual events are not. A defender who insists on resolving each event before considering the pattern has committed to a method that cannot, even in principle, detect the thing being looked for, because the method throws away the only signal that carries the information.

Call the failure mode the litigation trap. It is a trap because it is composed entirely of virtues. Demanding evidence before accusation is a virtue. Refusing to leap from correlation to conclusion is a virtue. Insisting that an unexplained fire is unexplained rather than hostile is a virtue. A defender exercising all these virtues, one incident at a time, will reliably conclude that nothing has been proven, will be entirely correct in each conclusion, and will have missed the campaign completely. The trap is not set by the defender's failings. It is set by the defender's standards, which is why exhortations to be more skeptical or more rigorous make it worse rather than better.

Escaping the trap does not mean abandoning the standards, which would be the opposite error and would hand the coercer an even cheaper victory, since a defender who sees hostile direction in every misfortune is a defender who can be made to jump at nothing forever. It means applying the standards at the right level. The correct object of assessment is the campaign, not the incident. An individual fire may remain permanently unattributed and this should have almost no bearing on the campaign-level judgment, in the same way that an individual coin flip has almost no bearing on whether a coin is fair while a thousand of them settle the matter. The analyst's job is to hold two findings simultaneously without embarrassment: this particular event is unproven, and the pattern of events is not.

This is the reframing the article exists to install, and it is worth being blunt that it is a reframing rather than a discovery. Nothing in it is unavailable to the reader who thinks about it carefully. What the coverage does, and what the coercer relies on the coverage doing, is present the material in a form that makes the careful thought unlikely: a chronological sequence of discrete events, each with its own headline, each with its own unresolved attribution question, each arriving weeks after the last. That form is the litigation trap in publishing shape. It is not anyone's fault. It is what news is. But a reader who consumes only that form will hold, at the end of years of attentive reading, a list rather than an assessment, and the difference between a list and an assessment is the difference between knowing that things happened and knowing what is being done.

There is a practical corollary for anyone who has to brief this subject. The persuasive move is never to make the strongest single case, because the strongest single case is still weak, and an audience that has been offered a single case will evaluate the single case and, correctly, find it wanting. The persuasive move is to present the functional map first, establish that the tools divide the space by design, and only then introduce the individual events as instances of a structure already established. This is not rhetorical trickery. It is presenting the evidence in the order that matches its actual logical structure rather than the order in which it happened to arrive.

## The Attribution Battleground

If deniability is the load-bearing element, then attribution is the place where the campaign is actually fought, and it is worth understanding why attribution is hard in a way that goes beyond the usual observation that it is hard.

Attribution has three distinct layers, and conflating them is the source of most confusion in public argument. The first layer is technical: who physically did this. For a fire, that means the person who set it. For an intrusion, the infrastructure and the tooling. This layer is often achievable. Poland and its neighbors have investigated and prosecuted, which means the first layer was reached. The second layer is the chain: who tasked and paid the person who did it. This layer is sometimes achievable and often not, because it runs through intermediaries chosen for their unattributability, through payment channels chosen for their opacity, and through recruitment structures deliberately shallow enough that the executant cannot betray what he does not know. The third layer is the principal: which state authority directed the chain, and with what standing. This layer is the one that would matter politically and it is the one almost never reached to a standard that survives public scrutiny, and that is not an accident of any particular case. It is the design specification.

The design produces a specific asymmetry that the defender cannot escape by trying harder. The coercer needs the third layer to remain unreached. The defender needs it reached. But the cost of keeping it unreached is paid once, at the design stage, by structuring the chain properly, and the cost of reaching it is paid over and over, case by case, by investigators working after the fact against structures built to defeat them. Even a defender who wins most cases at the first layer and some at the second wins almost none at the third, and the two-layer wins do not aggregate into a third-layer conclusion no matter how many of them accumulate. That is the whole point of a proxy: a hundred proven proxies prove a hundred proxies.

There is a second asymmetry that is less discussed and arguably more consequential. Attribution has a burden-of-proof problem that runs in a direction the defender cannot argue with. When a state accuses another state of directing acts against it, the accusation is itself an act with consequences, and the standard the accusation must meet is set by those consequences rather than by anything epistemic. A government that accuses on the balance of probabilities and is later embarrassed has damaged something more valuable than the case. So the standard rises to something like the criminal standard, applied to a question the criminal standard was not designed for, adjudicated in a forum with no rules of evidence, before an audience that includes people who will disbelieve the finding regardless. The defender is therefore held to a standard the coercer specifically engineered to be unmeetable, in a venue where meeting it would not settle the matter anyway. Understanding this explains a pattern that otherwise looks like weakness: governments that clearly believe a thing decline to say it, and the decline is not cowardice but a calculation about what a failed accusation costs.

The consequence for the assessment is that the analyst and the government are in genuinely different positions, and readers should hold both without collapsing them. The government's threshold for saying is properly higher than the analyst's threshold for believing, and there is nothing improper about a gap between what a state's officials assess and what a state's officials assert. The public confusion arises when the gap is read as evidence about the underlying question. It is not. Official silence about attribution is evidence about the standard of proof for official statements. It is close to worthless as evidence about who did what, and a reader who treats each unattributed case as a point against the campaign thesis is treating the coercer's design as a finding.

This is also where the most promising counter-move lives, and it is worth stating even though the policy detail belongs elsewhere. If the defender's problem is that the third layer is unreachable case by case, one answer is to stop trying to reach it case by case. A pattern does not need a chain. A finding that a structure exists, that it recruits in a characteristic way, that it tasks toward a characteristic set of objects, and that it correlates with a characteristic interest is a finding that can be made from the aggregate without ever proving any single principal-to-proxy link. It is a weaker claim about any individual case and a much stronger claim about the world, and it happens to be the claim that matters. Whether a state can build the political and legal machinery to act on pattern-level attribution is an open question that the deterrence and policy pieces carry. That it is the right target is, on the logic above, hard to argue with.

## How the Coercion Lands in Polish Politics

The campaign's theory of effect runs entirely through a domestic political process, which means an assessment that says nothing about that process has skipped the step where the campaign either works or does not.

Start with what the campaign is trying to produce, stated without euphemism. It is not trying to make Poles pro-Russian, which is not available and which no serious effort would target. It is trying to make a specific policy feel like a burden rather than an obligation, and to do that it does not need to change a single mind about Russia. It needs the ordinary machinery of democratic politics to do the work: costs get noticed, costs get argued about, arguments get partisan, partisan arguments get exhausting, and an exhausting argument about an expensive policy eventually produces politicians who would rather talk about something else. None of that requires anyone to be deceived. It requires the costs to be real, which the campaign arranges, and salient, which the information tool arranges. That is a much more achievable objective than persuasion and it is why the campaign is structured to produce friction rather than argument.

Now consider why this is harder in Poland than the coercer might wish, because the honest assessment has to include the ways the effort is failing. Polish opinion on the core question is anchored by things the campaign cannot reach. It is anchored by geography, which is not an opinion. It is anchored by a historical record that is taught, remembered, and not seriously contested across the political spectrum. It is anchored by the visible fact of a war next door, which supplies a stream of evidence far more vivid than anything an information operation can inject. A campaign trying to move opinion in that environment is pushing against a foundation rather than against a preference, and foundations do not move at the rate preferences do. This is a substantial reason to think the campaign's persuasive effect is small, and it should temper any reading that treats Polish politics as a soft target.

But persuasion is not the only channel, and the second one is where the campaign is more plausibly succeeding. Democratic politics allocates a scarce resource that has nothing to do with opinion, which is governmental attention. Every hour a minister spends on a border crisis is an hour not spent on something else. Every investigation into an unattributed fire consumes police and prosecutorial capacity that is not infinite. Every navigation disruption generates a regulatory problem someone has to own. None of this requires anyone to be persuaded of anything, and none of it shows up in opinion polling, which is why assessments that measure the campaign by polling find nothing and conclude the campaign is failing. The campaign may well be failing at persuasion and succeeding at taxation, and those are different verdicts with different implications.

The third channel is the one worth watching most carefully because it is the one the campaign is least in control of and stands to benefit from most. Every incident enters Polish politics as a fact that has to be interpreted, and interpretation in a competitive political system is contested by design. A government that attributes an incident is accused of alarmism or of deflection. A government that declines to attribute is accused of weakness or of concealment. Both accusations are ordinary politics and both would be made whether or not any campaign existed. What the campaign does is supply an unending stream of occasions for that argument, and the argument itself is the product. This is the sense in which the information tool converts every other tool's effect into political division, and it is why the map places it in that functional slot. It is not that the information tool tells Poles what to think about the fire. It is that the fire gives Polish politics something to fight about, and the fight is the deliverable.

Which points at the countermeasure that no procurement can buy and that this article has already named as the defender's core task. A political system that has agreed in advance on how to interpret this class of event is a political system the third channel cannot exploit, and a political system that relitigates the interpretation every time is one that supplies the coercer a free product with every incident. Whether such an agreement is achievable in any democracy is a fair question and the honest answer is that it is hard, partial, and perishable. It is also the highest-leverage thing available, which is an uncomfortable finding for anyone hoping the answer to this campaign is a capability.

## The Capability Picture in Brief

A pillar owes the reader a capability sketch even though the specialists carry the detail, because a judgment about a campaign that says nothing about the means behind it is floating. The sketch here is short by design and hands off to the pieces that own each tool.

The capability required to run a sub-threshold campaign is strikingly modest, and this is the first thing worth understanding about it, because it inverts the usual relationship between cost and effect that governs conventional force assessment. A conventional threat to Poland requires divisions, munitions stockpiles, logistics, and a sustainment base, all of which are expensive, all of which are visible, and all of which are degraded by a war next door. A coercion campaign requires intermediaries, money in small amounts, a modest technical establishment, a broadcasting apparatus that already exists, and patience. Almost none of that competes with the requirements of a conventional force. This matters enormously for the assessment, because it means the campaign is not constrained by the thing most likely to constrain the conventional threat. A Russian military substantially consumed by Ukraine is a Russian military with less capacity to threaten Poland conventionally, and it is not, in any meaningful degree, a state with less capacity to run a coercion campaign. The two capabilities live in different budgets, draw on different people, and rise and fall independently.

The recruitment structure deserves a specific note because it is where the modesty of the requirement is most visible and most misunderstood. The documented pattern across European sabotage cases involves individuals recruited at low cost, through intermediaries, often online, often with minimal training, often with no knowledge of the ultimate principal, and often performing tasks that require no skill. The pattern is sometimes read as evidence of weakness, on the grounds that a serious intelligence service would use serious officers. The reading is backwards. Using officers would be a serious error, because officers are attributable and expendable recruits are not, and the whole purchase is deniability. The low quality of the operative is a feature, and the fact that many such operations fail or are rolled up is priced in, because the cost of a failed recruitment is a recruit, and the cost of a burned officer is a diplomatic incident. A structure that treats its executants as consumable is a structure optimized for exactly the trade the below-the-line rule describes.

The one capability that does not follow this pattern, and that is therefore worth watching differently, is the electromagnetic one, because it requires equipment and siting that a state has and an amateur does not. Interference at regional scale is not something a recruited proxy can produce. This makes it, somewhat counterintuitively, the least deniable of the tools in a technical sense, and it is worth asking why a campaign so committed to deniability includes a tool that points more clearly at a state. The available answers are that the effect is judged worth the exposure, that the interference is a byproduct of protecting something rather than an offensive act, or that the tolerance for exposure is higher than the theory suggests. This is a genuine puzzle in the assessment and it is flagged rather than resolved. For the detail on the interference picture as such, the specialist treatment carries it; the map treats it as a background-cost tool and does not pretend to settle its purpose.

What the campaign cannot do, given its means, is also worth naming. It cannot take or hold territory. It cannot destroy a formation. It cannot compel a government to reverse a policy directly, because none of its acts creates the kind of leverage that produces direct compliance. Its entire theory of effect runs through a domestic political process it does not control and can only influence at the margin. That is a real limitation, and readers who find the campaign frightening should sit with it, because it means the campaign's success is contingent on Polish choices in a way that a conventional threat's success would not be. For the conventional side of the ledger, and the question of what Russian forces could actually do against Poland, the capability cluster carries the assessment and this piece does not duplicate it.

## What Would Deter or Defend in Brief

Deterrence theory was built for a problem this campaign is designed to sidestep, and the mismatch is the central difficulty in every proposed answer.

Classical deterrence works by making an act more costly than it is worth, and it requires three things: the act must be identifiable, the actor must be identifiable, and the threatened cost must be credible. A sub-threshold campaign attacks the first two directly. If the act is arguably an accident, there is no act to deter. If the actor is arguably a criminal with a grudge, there is no actor to threaten. The campaign does not defeat deterrence by being stronger than it. It defeats deterrence by failing to meet the definition of the thing deterrence deters. Every serious proposal for answering this campaign is, at bottom, a proposal for restoring one of those three conditions.

### Can a gray-zone campaign be deterred at all?

Not by classical means, because deterrence needs an identifiable act by an identifiable actor, and the campaign is built to supply neither. What can work is changing the arithmetic underneath: attribute faster, respond to the pattern rather than the incident, and harden targets so the acts stop producing effects worth their cost.

The first family of answers works on attribution, and it is the most direct assault on the campaign's load-bearing element. If deniability is what makes the trade worthwhile, then making deniability harder to buy raises the price of every tool at once. This is why investigative capacity, forensic capability, and the willingness to publish findings even when they are uncomfortable are not administrative details but the core of the counter-campaign. It is also why the payoff is slow and unsatisfying: attribution improves incrementally, it improves after the fact, and it never produces the clean moment of vindication that would make the politics easy. A defender who invests here is buying a better position five years out, which is a hard case to make and the right one.

The second family works on the response rule rather than on the evidence. If the campaign's power comes from aggregation, then a defender who responds only to incidents is playing on the coercer's chosen ground, and a defender who responds to the pattern is not. This means declaring, in advance, that a sustained pattern of a given kind will draw a response even absent proof of any individual case, and then doing it. The advantage is that it attacks the aggregation logic directly and cannot be defeated by making each individual case arguable. The disadvantage is severe and should not be minimized: a response rule that does not require proof of the individual case is a response rule that can be triggered by a coincidence or by a third party who wants the response triggered. That is not a hypothetical vulnerability. It is the obvious countermove, and any state adopting a pattern-based rule is accepting a real risk of being played. Serious people disagree about whether the trade is worth it, and the disagreement is not resolvable from the open record.

The third family gives up on deterring the acts and works on making them not matter, which is less defeatist than it sounds. Every tool in the map is priced against an expected effect. Sabotage against logistics is worth its cost because logistics are disruptable. Interference is worth its cost because users depend on the signal. Information operations are worth their cost because the narratives find purchase. Reduce the effect and the tool becomes a bad investment on its own terms, and no attribution is required to achieve that. Redundant logistics, alternative navigation, hardened infrastructure, and a public that has been told plainly what is being attempted are all measures that work without ever proving anything about anyone. This family is unglamorous, it is expensive, and it is probably the highest-yield line available. The policy treatment of it belongs to [the assessment of how Poland builds resilience to sabotage](/2027/03/11/building-resilience-to-sabotage/), which owns the resilience question and which this piece defers to.

The fourth family is the alliance question, and it is the one where the campaign has arguably done its most effective work without touching Polish soil at all. Collective defense is organized around armed attack, and the campaign is organized around not being one. There is no mechanism that converts a pattern of unattributed arson into an alliance obligation, and there is not supposed to be, since a mechanism that did so would be a mechanism for dragging an alliance into a war on evidence no one had verified. So the campaign operates in a space the alliance is structurally poor at occupying, and every ally that says, reasonably, that this is a police matter rather than a defense matter is saying something true and something the coercer counted on. The Article 5 cluster owns the collective-defense questions and this piece will not restate them; what belongs here is only the observation that the gap is structural rather than a failure of will, and that closing it would require the alliance to become something it has deliberately chosen not to be.

## The Scenarios in Brief

A pillar sketches the paths without walking them, and hands the walking to the scenario pieces. Three paths are worth naming, and the interesting thing about them is that the most likely is also the least dramatic.

The first path is continuation. The campaign persists at roughly its established intensity, calibrated to the war next door and to Poland's role in it, producing no decisive moment and no resolution. Incidents occur, are investigated, are mostly unresolved, and are absorbed. The band does not widen sharply and does not close. This is the boring path and it is, on the current evidence, the likeliest, and it is precisely the path that the incident-by-incident reading is least able to recognize as a path at all, because a path with no dramatic moments does not look like anything happening. The strategic content of this path is that the pressure accumulates without ever presenting a decision point, and that the defender's condition at the end of a decade of it depends entirely on choices made in the absence of any forcing event.

The second path is intensification without crossing, in which the band widens from the bottom through the mechanism described earlier. Acts become slightly more consequential because the previous slightly-less-consequential acts drew no response, and the process repeats. The end state of this path is not war. It is a normal that would have been unacceptable at the start, arrived at without anyone ever having agreed to it. This path is the one most worth watching for, because it is nearly invisible in real time and obvious in retrospect, which is the signature of the failure modes that actually catch states out.

The third path is transition, in which something in the campaign produces an effect that cannot be absorbed, whether by design, by accident, or by a proxy exceeding what was intended. This is the path everyone writes about and it deserves less attention than it gets in proportion to its likelihood, though not zero attention, because its consequences are severe enough to earn a place in any planning picture regardless of probability. The transition question is genuinely hard and it is owned elsewhere; the pillar's contribution is only to insist that transition is a distinct path rather than the natural endpoint of the other two. Nothing about a coercion campaign makes it a war in waiting. The campaign is doing what it is designed to do, and what it is designed to do is not become a war.

What the scenario picture teaches, taken together, is that the framing of this subject as a countdown is wrong. There is no clock. A campaign whose theory of victory is accumulation has no deadline, no culminating point, and no reason to force a decision, and it is perfectly content to run for a decade. Readers conditioned to expect escalation to a climax are waiting for something the structure does not produce, and the waiting is itself a vulnerability, because a defender watching for the moment of transition is a defender not attending to the accumulation that is actually happening.

## A Method for Reading the Campaign

Everything above is a judgment, and a judgment is only useful to a reader who can rework it when the picture changes. So here is the method underneath it, stated so it can be applied by someone who disagrees with the conclusion.

The first move is to sort before you weigh. When an event arrives, the instinct is to ask whether it was hostile, and that instinct is the litigation trap in its purest form, because the question cannot be answered and the attempt consumes the attention the assessment needs. Ask instead which tool family the event belongs to, which deniability mechanism it relied on, and which function in the architecture it would serve if it were hostile. Those three questions are answerable from what is visible. They do not require attribution and they do not presume it. And they place the event in a structure where it can contribute to a pattern rather than sitting in a queue of unresolved cases. Sorting is not concluding. An event sorted into the sabotage family with the proxy-deniability mechanism and the corridor-friction function has not been declared hostile. It has been made comparable to the other events, which is the precondition for learning anything from it.

The second move is to weigh the pattern, not the case, and to be explicit about what the pattern is being weighed against. The campaign thesis and the noise thesis both predict events. They differ in what they predict about the distribution of events, and the differences are the only place evidence lives. The campaign thesis predicts functional partitioning, and the noise thesis predicts clustering wherever opportunity is greatest, which would produce redundancy rather than coverage. The campaign thesis predicts intensity correlated with Poland's role in supporting Ukraine, and the noise thesis predicts intensity correlated with whatever drives the underlying accident and crime rates, which are different variables. The campaign thesis predicts consistent respect for the response line, and the noise thesis predicts occasional random crossings, because noise does not know where the line is. Each of those is a live comparison and each could come out either way. Running them is what makes the judgment an assessment rather than a preference.

The third move is to keep a standing confidence level on each claim and to write it down, because the failure mode of long assessments is not error but drift. A judgment held for years without being restated migrates. The claim that began as a moderate-confidence assessment about aims becomes, through nothing but repetition, a background fact that later reasoning is built on, and the later reasoning inherits a certainty the original claim never had. This is the mechanism by which careful analysts end up asserting things they would not have asserted if asked directly, and it is invisible from the inside. The only reliable defense is a written record with confidence markers, revisited on a schedule rather than when something happens, because the drift does not announce itself and revisiting only after events selects exactly the moments when the drift is least visible.

The fourth move is to hold the disconfirmers explicitly and in advance. An assessment that has not named what would change it will not change, because the mind that made it will meet each disconfirming observation with an available explanation, and available explanations are always available. So name them now. The campaign reading in this article would be substantially weakened if the tools began to cluster redundantly rather than partition, because that is what opportunistic activity looks like. It would be weakened if intensity decoupled from Poland's role supporting Ukraine and tracked something unrelated. It would be weakened if the recruitment structures stopped showing tasking and started showing initiative. It would be badly damaged if the acts began crossing the response line at random, because a campaign that does not respect the line is not the object this article described. And it would be falsified, in the most informative way available, if the pressure persisted at full strength after the support corridor ceased to matter, because that would mean the campaign was never about the corridor and the entire aims analysis was wrong.

The fifth move is to resist the two symmetrical corruptions this domain induces, and to notice that they are symmetrical. The alarmist corruption reads direction into everything, which is not a failure of analysis so much as a failure to hold a null hypothesis, and it is genuinely destructive because a defender who sees a campaign in every misfortune can be made to spend forever on nothing. The dismissive corruption demands proof of each case before considering the pattern, which is a failure to notice that the method cannot detect the thing being looked for. Both corruptions feel like rigor from the inside. The alarmist feels vigilant. The dismissive feels skeptical. Neither is wrong about their own virtue and both have adopted a procedure that produces a predetermined answer, which is the actual definition of the failure they each accuse the other of.

The sixth move, and the one that separates an assessment from a commentary, is to state the judgment anyway. The evidence in this domain will never be sufficient in the way a reader would like, and waiting for sufficiency is a decision dressed as a deferral. The analyst's obligation is to reach the best-supported conclusion available, mark its confidence honestly, name what would overturn it, and then act as though it is true until it is overturned, which is what it means to hold a judgment rather than an opinion. A pillar that laid out the considerations and declined to conclude would have produced the sensation of rigor and none of its substance, and would have left the reader exactly where they started, which is with a list.

## What the Tool Selection Reveals About the Coercer

A campaign is a statement about the state that runs it, and reading the statement is one of the more useful things available in a domain where direct evidence about intent is denied. Tool selection is a revealed preference, and revealed preferences are harder to fake than declared ones.

The first thing the selection reveals is a serious respect for the response line, which is not what the alarmist reading expects and is worth sitting with. A state that believed the alliance would not answer an armed attack on Poland would not be spending this much on deniability. Deniability is expensive: it costs effectiveness, it costs control, it costs the reliability that comes from using trained people, and it costs the ability to signal, since a deniable act cannot be used to make a demand. A coercer pays all of that to avoid an outcome, and the size of the payment is a measure of how much the outcome is feared. Read that way, the campaign is evidence for the credibility of collective defense rather than against it, which is the opposite of how it is usually deployed in argument. The state closest to the question, with the best information and the strongest incentive to be right, is behaving as though the line is real.

The second thing the selection reveals is patience, and patience is informative because it is inconsistent with urgency. A campaign built on accumulation is a campaign that expects to be running in five years and does not need a result in one. Nothing in the tool set is optimized for speed, nothing in it forces a decision, and nothing in it has a culminating point. States in a hurry do not build instruments like this, because instruments like this cannot deliver in a hurry. This is the strongest single argument against reading the campaign as the shaping phase of an imminent move, and it is structural rather than evidentiary, which in this domain makes it more reliable rather than less.

The third thing it reveals is a calibration to what the target actually is, which implies attention. The tools are not a generic kit applied to a generic adversary. They are weighted toward friction and away from persuasion, which is the correct weighting against a public that is hard to persuade and a state that can be taxed. They are weighted toward the corridor function rather than toward symbolic national targets, which is the correct weighting if the object is a policy rather than a country. A coercer that had not studied the target would have picked differently and would have picked worse, and the fact that the selection fits the target this well is itself evidence about the seriousness of the effort behind it.

The fourth thing, and the one that most cuts against the campaign's operators, is a tolerance for failure that borders on indifference to it. Operations are rolled up. Recruits are caught. Acts achieve nothing. Prosecutions follow. A conventional military organization would treat that record as a crisis and would change something. This one does not, and the reason is that the economics are different: the cost of a failed act is a consumable recruit and some money, and the campaign is not attempting to succeed at any particular act. It is attempting to maintain a rate. Once that is understood, the frequent failures stop being evidence of incompetence and start being evidence of a design that priced failure in from the beginning, which is a more sobering conclusion than the one the failures usually invite.

Put those four together and the coercer they describe is neither the ten-foot-tall adversary of the alarmist reading nor the flailing improviser of the dismissive one. It is a state that respects the line it is working under, expects to be working under it indefinitely, has studied what it is working against, and has built a machine that treats its own losses as an operating expense. That is a more difficult adversary than either caricature, because it cannot be deterred by the thing that deters attacks, cannot be outlasted by a defender who is also waiting, and cannot be discouraged by defeating any given operation.

## The Cost of Getting This Wrong in Either Direction

Assessments have consequences, and the two available errors here have different costs, different constituencies, and different failure signatures. Naming them is part of the assessment rather than an appendix to it, because a reader who does not know what the errors would cost cannot calibrate how much confidence to require.

The cost of the alarmist error is the one this article's own thesis makes most tempting, and it should be stated first and without softening, because the piece has spent thousands of words arguing that the pattern is real and that failing to see it is the characteristic mistake. The characteristic mistake in the other direction is at least as damaging. A state that concludes it is under campaign will read every misfortune as an act, and every misfortune will supply confirmation, because misfortunes are common and the thesis explains all of them. That state will spend investigative capacity on accidents, will attribute publicly and be wrong, will damage the credibility of the attributions that are correct, and will hand the coercer a genuinely free and genuinely devastating product: a defender who can be made to jump at nothing, on a schedule, at no cost. There is no easier operation available to an adversary than doing nothing while a jumpy target manufactures crises out of its own electrical faults. And the political costs compound, because a public told repeatedly that a fire was an attack, and repeatedly shown that it was a fire, will eventually stop believing the true reports, which is a state of affairs the coercer could not have engineered directly at any price.

The cost of the dismissive error is slower, quieter, and larger. A state that concludes the pattern is noise will make a series of individually defensible decisions, each of which is correct on its own terms, and will arrive after some years at a condition it never chose. It will not have decided to tolerate a level of hostile activity. It will have declined, a hundred times, to respond to an unproven case, which is a different thing that produces the same result. It will discover the accumulated position only when something forces the reckoning, and the reckoning will be harder because the band will have widened and because the precedent of non-response will be long. The failure signature of this error is that nothing looks wrong at any point, which is precisely what makes it dangerous, since errors that announce themselves get corrected.

The two errors are not symmetrical in a way that lets a defender split the difference, and this is where most attempts to be reasonable go wrong. Splitting the difference produces a defender that is somewhat jumpy and somewhat complacent, which is the worst available combination: it overreacts to the visible and salient, which is what the coercer can supply at will, and underreacts to the cumulative and quiet, which is where the actual campaign lives. Being half-alarmed does not average out to being correct. It produces a response profile the coercer can play from both sides, and it is arguably the modal condition of states facing this problem.

What does resolve the tension is the thing the method section named, and it is worth restating because it is the practical payoff of the whole argument. The errors are only symmetrical if the analyst insists on assessing at the level of the incident, where the choice really is between believing and not believing an unprovable case. Move the assessment to the level of the pattern and the tension dissolves, because a pattern-level judgment does not require any incident-level belief at all. The correct posture is agnostic about every individual case and confident about the structure, which sounds like a dodge and is in fact the only position consistent with the evidence. A defender occupying it cannot be made to jump, because no single event moves the judgment. And it cannot be lulled, because the judgment does not depend on any single event being proven. That posture is unnatural, it is politically hard to explain, and it is the whole answer.

## The Honest Counter-Case: Coordinated Campaign or Scattered Noise

Two schools genuinely contest this subject, and both contain serious people making serious arguments. An assessment that caricatures either has not earned its conclusion. Here is each at its strongest.

The coordinated-campaign school holds that the pattern is directed, that the direction is Russian, and that the strategic aim is coherent even where the tactical execution is not. Its strongest arguments are structural rather than evidentiary, which is a strength and not a weakness in a domain where evidence is engineered to be scarce. The functional division of the tools, set out in the map above, is the best of them: six instruments that partition the space of available pressure this cleanly, with this little redundancy and this few gaps, are unlikely to be six independent phenomena. The second is the timing correlation with the war and with Poland's role in it, which is a variable that predicts the pattern well and that no innocent explanation predicts at all. The third is the recruitment evidence, which is not merely suggestive: prosecutions across multiple European states have established that individuals were recruited, paid, and tasked, and while the chain above the recruiter is often unproven, the existence of a tasking structure is not an inference. The fourth is the coercer's own doctrine and stated positions, which describe this band of activity as a legitimate and normal instrument of state competition and which make the campaign's existence unsurprising rather than extraordinary.

The scattered-incidents school holds that the pattern is substantially an artifact of attention, that a frontier state next to a large war will experience elevated rates of accident, crime, opportunism, and misfortune, and that the campaign is being constructed out of that noise by observers who went looking for it. Its strongest arguments are epistemological, and they are better than they are usually given credit for. The first is that the pattern is unfalsifiable as usually stated: if resolved incidents prove the campaign and unresolved incidents also prove the campaign, and if quiet periods prove patience and busy periods prove intensity, then no observation could disconfirm the thesis, and a thesis nothing could disconfirm is not doing analytical work. The second is that base rates are genuinely unknown, because nobody was carefully counting warehouse fires in the years before anyone was looking, so the claimed elevation is elevation against a baseline that was never measured. The third is that attention itself generates the pattern: once a category exists, events get sorted into it, and the sorting produces a cluster that the sorting created. The fourth is the incentive critique, and it should not be waved away: institutions that assess threats are funded by the threats they assess, and a finding of campaign is more useful to more people than a finding of noise.

Now the adjudication, because presenting both fairly does not mean declining to judge, and a pillar that refuses to conclude has failed its purpose.

The scattered-incidents school is right about the epistemology and wrong about the conclusion, and the gap between those two is where the honest assessment sits. Its criticism of unfalsifiability lands against the sloppy version of the campaign thesis and not against a properly stated one. A properly stated campaign thesis is falsifiable, and it is worth saying exactly how: it predicts that the tools will remain functionally partitioned rather than redundantly clustered, that intensity will track Poland's role in supporting Ukraine rather than tracking unrelated variables, that the recruitment structures will continue to show tasking rather than initiative, and that the acts will continue to respect the response line rather than wandering across it randomly as genuine noise would. Each of those is checkable. Several of them could have come out otherwise. They have not. That is what evidence for a thesis looks like in a domain where direct proof is engineered away, and demanding a confession before believing anything is not rigor but a decision to be unable to see the class of thing that does not confess.

The base-rate criticism is the school's best point and it should be conceded rather than deflected. Nobody knows what the accidental rate of logistics fires in Poland is. The honest response is not to invent a baseline but to note that the campaign thesis does not rest on rate elevation in any single category. It rests on the joint pattern across six categories, on the functional partitioning, on the timing correlation, and on the recruitment findings. A base-rate objection defeats an argument from anomaly in one category. It does not touch an argument from structure across six.

The incentive critique is also partly conceded, and it should make every reader, including the reader of this article, appropriately suspicious of anyone whose institutional position improves when the threat is judged large. It cuts both ways, though, and the school rarely notices this: governments have powerful incentives to find noise rather than campaign, because a finding of campaign obliges a response that is expensive, escalatory, and domestically contested, and a finding of noise obliges nothing. The pressure on official assessment in this domain does not run in one direction, and it is not obvious which direction it runs harder in.

Where the coordinated-campaign school overreaches, and it does overreach, is on centralization. The strong version of the thesis imagines a room where the tools are sequenced, the acts are approved, and the effects are measured against a plan. The evidence does not support that and the structure argues against it, because centralizing this campaign would defeat its purpose. The recruitment patterns suggest considerable local initiative, intermediaries with latitude, and executants who fail in ways a controlled operation would not tolerate. The better reading is a campaign coordinated at the level of intent, resourcing, and permission rather than at the level of the individual act: a standing objective, a budget, a tolerance for failure, and a set of actors who understand what is wanted without being told each time. That reading is entirely compatible with the evidence of chaos at the executing end and it is what the phrase coordinated campaign should be taken to mean here. Readers who reject the strong centralized version are right to reject it and should not conclude that they have thereby rejected the campaign.

So the verdict on the counter-case is that the truth is closer to the campaign school, arrived at by taking the noise school's methodological objections seriously rather than by dismissing them. It is a real campaign, deniable by design, coordinated at the strategic level, loosely executed, and considerably less centralized than its loudest describers imagine. That is a more modest claim than the alarmist reading and a much stronger one than the dismissive reading, and it is the claim the evidence actually supports.

## The Biggest Uncertainties

An assessment that does not name what would change it is advocacy wearing an assessment's clothes. Four uncertainties matter most, and they are ranked by how much they would move the judgment.

The first and largest is the aim, and specifically whether the campaign is an end in itself or a means to something else. This piece assesses it as an end in itself: pressure on the Ukraine policy, sought for its own sake, with no further step contemplated. The alternative, that it is a shaping effort, cannot be excluded, and the reason it cannot be excluded is that the evidence that would distinguish them is exactly the evidence a competent adversary would conceal. Preparatory access and espionage look alike. Reconnaissance and probing look alike. This is the uncertainty that most deserves an analyst's humility, and the honest position is that the coercion reading fits the observable tooling better and that this is a judgment about tooling rather than about minds.

The second is the effect, and it is genuinely unmeasured. Nobody knows how much the campaign has actually accomplished. The theory of effect runs through public opinion, political will, and the sustainability of a policy, and the counterfactual is unavailable: there is no Poland that did not experience the campaign to compare against. It is possible that the campaign has moved Polish politics measurably. It is equally possible that it has accomplished essentially nothing, that Polish opinion is driven by history, geography, and the visible war rather than by anything the campaign has injected, and that the whole enterprise is an expensive failure that persists because it is cheap and nobody has to justify it. That second possibility is underrated in most writing on the subject, and it is not a comfortable thought for anyone whose work is describing the campaign, which is a reason to take it more seriously rather than less. This article's own assessment, held with moderate confidence, is that the effect is real but modest, and that the campaign's largest achievement is not persuasion but the consumption of governmental attention, which is a resource no state has in surplus.

The third is the response line itself, and whether it has moved. The mechanism by which it moves is clear and the measurement is not. There is no instrument that reads out where a state's practical tolerance currently sits, and states themselves usually do not know until they are tested. It is possible that the accumulated precedent of unanswered incidents has widened the band substantially. It is also possible that the tolerance is bounded by things the precedent does not touch, and that a defender who has absorbed a hundred small acts would respond sharply to the hundred and first if it crossed some line that was never about magnitude. Both readings are consistent with what can be seen.

The fourth uncertainty is one almost nobody raises, which is whether the coercer knows how the campaign is doing. The theory of effect runs through Polish politics, and measuring effect in a foreign political system is hard even for the people who live in it. Polling reads persuasion, which is probably not where the effect is. Attention taxation does not show up in any instrument. A coercer running an accumulation strategy against an opaque feedback loop may be systematically wrong about its own results in either direction, and the direction matters enormously: a coercer that overestimates its progress has a reason to be patient, while a coercer that concludes the effort has failed has a reason to consider something else. This is speculative and it is flagged as such, but it belongs in the picture, because assessments of adversary campaigns habitually assume the adversary can see what it is achieving, and there is no particular reason to grant that assumption here.

The fifth is durability, and it is the one most often ignored. A campaign contingent on a war is contingent on that war, and the assumption that the pressure continues indefinitely at its established shape is an assumption rather than a finding. If Poland's role as the support corridor changes, the tool selection has no reason to stay the same, and a campaign whose primary aim was that role would either wind down or re-aim at something else. Which of those it did would be extraordinarily informative, because it would settle the first uncertainty. A campaign that persisted at strength after its stated object disappeared was never about its stated object. The bellwether cluster carries the analysis of what the war next door reveals about Poland's position, and the connection worth holding onto here is that the coercion picture and the Ukraine picture are not independent variables.

## The Verdict

Poland faces a real coercion campaign, conducted below the threshold of armed attack, composed of six functionally specialized tool families, coordinated at the level of strategy and permission rather than at the level of the individual act, aimed primarily at raising the cost and reducing the political sustainability of Poland's support for Ukraine, and deliberately calibrated to remain unanswerable by the mechanisms that answer attacks. It is not open war and it is not drifting toward open war as a natural endpoint. It is not noise, and the argument that it is noise, while methodologically the sharpest criticism in the field, defeats a version of the campaign thesis that a careful analyst does not hold.

The below-the-line rule is the thing to carry away. The campaign's power is cumulative because it must be, deniable because it must be, and invisible at the level of the incident because that is what buys the first two. Every feature of it that looks like weakness, the trivial damage, the incompetent operatives, the acts that achieve nothing, is a feature of the trade it made. It gave up effectiveness to buy a response the defender cannot organize, and the reason that looks like a bad trade to the incident-focused reader is that the incident-focused reader is measuring the wrong quantity.

The defender's task follows directly and is harder than any of the countermeasures usually proposed. It is to hold a campaign-level judgment steadily across years in which no single event ever justifies it, without sliding into the alternative failure of seeing direction in every misfortune, and to do this while the evidence that would settle the matter is being systematically denied. That is a discipline, not a policy. It cannot be procured. And it is the actual contested ground, because a defender who maintains it is a defender against whom the whole architecture stops working, and a defender who loses it has already conceded the thing the campaign was built to take.

For the tools in detail, the cluster carries them: [the sabotage threat on Polish soil](/2026/12/21/sabotage-on-polish-soil/) for the physical instrument and its attribution problem, [Russian disinformation aimed at Poland](/2027/02/01/russian-disinformation-aimed-at-poland/) for the continuous one, [the drone incursion classification problem](/2027/01/01/drones-over-poland-accident-or-probe/) for the airspace one, and [the threshold between coercion and open war](/2027/02/11/when-gray-zone-becomes-open-war/) for where the band ends. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Is Russia already waging a gray-zone war on Poland?

On the durable open record, Poland has experienced sabotage cases, airspace incursions, satellite navigation interference, engineered migratory pressure, cyber intrusion, and sustained disinformation. That those categories are present is established fact. That they form one directed campaign is an assessment, and it is the better reading, because six tools this cleanly partitioned by function, this well correlated with Poland's role supporting Ukraine, and this consistent in respecting the response line are unlikely to have arrived independently. The qualification that carries most of the weight is that coordination at the level of strategy and permission is not the same as centralized scripting of each act, and the evidence supports the first rather than the second.

### Q: What does the gray-zone campaign against Poland aim to achieve?

The most defensible assessment is that its primary aim is to raise the cost and lower the political sustainability of Poland's role in supporting Ukraine, a role that runs through Polish territory, rail, ports, and politics and is therefore attackable at each point by means that stay below the line. This aim explains the tool selection better than alternatives do: sabotage clusters around logistics, the narratives target the burden of the policy, and the intensity tracks the war. A secondary aim is testing and shaping the response, learning where the practical line sits rather than where treaties say it sits. Preparation for something larger cannot be excluded but the observable tooling does not point there.

### Q: Why does the gray-zone campaign stay below the response line?

Because crossing the line would forfeit its only real advantage. Below it, acts are police matters, insurance claims, and political arguments. Above it, they are armed attacks with alliance consequences attached. Ask why a state with precision munitions uses recruited arsonists and the answer is not that arson works better, since arson works dramatically worse. Arson is chosen because a missile strike on a NATO member is an armed attack and a fire is a criminal case, and the difference in consequence is the entire product being bought. The campaign trades effectiveness for deniability deliberately, and accepts almost unlimited amounts of the first loss to secure the second.

### Q: Is gray-zone pressure on Poland already open war?

No, and the claim that it is does the defender real harm. Nothing in the campaign has crossed the line that triggers the response an armed attack triggers, and this restraint is consistent, deliberate, and central to how the whole architecture functions. Calling it war collapses a distinction the coercer has spent enormous effort maintaining and that the defender depends on to think clearly. It also inflates expectations of drama, which is a vulnerability: a reader waiting for a climax is a reader not attending to the accumulation that is actually happening. The pressure is real and serious. It is not war, and treating it as war makes it harder to see, not easier.

### Q: How do you see the gray-zone campaign behind isolated incidents?

Stop assessing incidents and start assessing structure. Arrange the tools by function rather than chronology and the design becomes visible: each buys deniability by a different mechanism, imposes a different kind of cost, and covers a different part of the space, with little redundancy and few gaps. That partitioning is the signal. An individual fire may stay permanently unattributed with almost no bearing on the campaign-level judgment, in the same way an individual coin flip barely informs whether a coin is fair while a thousand settle it. The method is to hold two findings at once: this event is unproven, and the pattern is not.

### Q: Which tools make up Russia's hybrid campaign against Poland?

Six families. Physical sabotage through recruited proxies against logistics and infrastructure. Drone and airspace incursion that forces decisions under time pressure. Electromagnetic interference degrading satellite navigation for civil users. Engineered migratory pressure at the eastern frontier. Cyber intrusion against institutions and operators, mostly preparatory. And sustained information operations, the only continuously running tool. They are specialized rather than interchangeable: only sabotage imposes direct physical cost on the support corridor, only airspace events force a decision inside minutes, only information operations convert every other tool's effect into political argument. Each buys deniability differently, which is why no single countermeasure answers the set.

### Q: Why is gray-zone pressure cumulative and deniable?

Both properties follow from the same constraint. An act limited to staying below the response line cannot, by construction, achieve anything a state would fight to prevent, so the campaign cannot win in one move and does not try. It accretes, each act adding a small increment of cost, doubt, distraction, or division, on the theory that increments sum over years into a political result. Deniability is what makes that patience affordable, because a proven state program of arson against a NATO member is a different object in law and politics than a run of unsolved fires. Nothing about the physical acts would change. Everything about the response would.

### Q: Are the gray-zone incidents against Poland coordinated or just noise?

Coordinated, but less centrally than the loudest describers imagine. The noise school's methodological objections are strong: base rates were never measured, attention itself generates patterns, and institutions that assess threats are funded by them. Those objections defeat an argument from anomaly in one category. They do not touch an argument from structure across six. The campaign thesis is falsifiable and has survived: the tools remain functionally partitioned, intensity tracks Poland's role supporting Ukraine, recruitment shows tasking, and the acts respect the line rather than wandering randomly. The right reading is coordination at the level of intent, resourcing, and permission, with considerable local initiative below that.

### Q: How much cumulative pressure does the gray-zone campaign exert?

Honestly, nobody knows, and anyone who gives you a figure has invented it. The theory of effect runs through opinion, political will, and policy sustainability, and no counterfactual Poland exists to measure against. The possibility that the campaign has accomplished essentially nothing is underrated: Polish opinion may be driven by history, geography, and the visible war rather than by anything injected, in which case the enterprise persists because it is cheap and nobody must justify it. This assessment holds with moderate confidence that the effect is real but modest, and that the largest achievement is not persuasion but the consumption of governmental attention, a resource no state holds in surplus.

### Q: What is the defender's core task against a gray-zone campaign?

To hold a campaign-level judgment steadily across years in which no single event ever justifies it, without sliding into the opposite failure of seeing direction in every misfortune, while the evidence that would settle the matter is systematically denied. That is a discipline rather than a policy, and it cannot be procured. It is also the actual contested ground: a defender who maintains it is a defender against whom the architecture stops working, because the whole design assumes the defender will litigate each case and forget the last one. Everything else, attribution capacity, pattern-based response rules, hardening, follows from whether that judgment holds.

### Q: Why is deniability so valuable to a gray-zone campaign?

Because it is the load-bearing element, not a courtesy the coercer extends to itself. Remove it and the structure collapses immediately, with no change whatsoever to the physical acts. This asymmetry explains where the adversary invests: in recruitment through intermediaries so the hand is a proxy, in mixed signal environments where accidents genuinely happen, in acts that are administrative rather than military, and in plausible alternative explanations that keep proof permanently out of reach. It also explains why attribution rather than damage limitation is the real battleground, and why a defender who makes deniability harder to buy raises the price of every tool in the set at once.

### Q: What makes a gray-zone campaign so hard to answer?

The mismatch between the campaign and every mechanism built to answer campaigns. Classical deterrence needs an identifiable act by an identifiable actor plus a credible threatened cost, and the design attacks the first two directly: if the act is arguably an accident there is nothing to deter, and if the actor is arguably a criminal there is nobody to threaten. Collective defense is organized around armed attack and the campaign is organized around not being one, so allies who call it a police matter are saying something true and something the coercer counted on. The gap is structural, not a failure of will, and closing it would require the alliance to become something it deliberately chose not to be.

### Q: Does calling it a gray-zone war overstate the pressure on Poland?

The word war overstates it and the word campaign does not. Nothing here achieves what a war achieves: no territory taken, no formation destroyed, no government compelled directly. The whole theory of effect runs through a domestic political process the coercer does not control and can only nudge, which is a real limitation readers who find this frightening should sit with, because it means success is contingent on Polish choices in a way a conventional threat's success would not be. The honest formulation is a real, sustained, deniable coercion campaign that falls well short of war and is designed to. That claim is more modest than the alarmist reading and far stronger than the dismissive one.

### Q: Who directs the gray-zone campaign aimed at Poland?

The honest answer distinguishes what is established from what is inferred. Established: prosecutions across several European states show individuals recruited, paid, and tasked through intermediaries, which means a tasking structure exists rather than being inferred. Assessed: the chain above the recruiter. The strong centralized picture, a room where acts are approved and effects measured against a plan, is not supported and the structure argues against it, since centralizing would defeat the purpose and the executants fail in ways a controlled operation would not tolerate. The better reading is a standing objective, a budget, a tolerance for failure, and actors who understand what is wanted without being told each time.

### Q: Can a gray-zone campaign succeed without ever crossing into war?

Yes, and that is the point most often missed. The band is not a waiting room where a state sits while deciding whether to attack; it is a theater with its own logic and its own kinds of victory. A campaign here can make a society doubt its institutions, an alliance doubt its members, a government spend attention on internal problems, and a public tire of a policy it once supported, all without giving the target a moment it can call the attack. Framing the subject as a countdown is therefore wrong. A campaign whose theory of victory is accumulation has no deadline, no culminating point, and no reason to force a decision.

### Q: How should a reader judge the seriousness of gray-zone pressure?

Not by counting damage, which is the mistake that produces the dismissive verdict. Every success criterion in this campaign is a political condition rather than a military one, so it must be judged by political measures. Ask instead whether the tools remain functionally partitioned, whether intensity tracks Poland's role supporting Ukraine, whether recruitment continues to show tasking, and whether the acts continue to respect the response line. Those are checkable and they could have come out otherwise. Then hold the judgment at the level of the pattern while conceding freely that any individual case is unproven, which is the discipline the whole architecture is built to break.
