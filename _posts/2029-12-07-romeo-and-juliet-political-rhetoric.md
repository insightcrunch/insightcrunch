No tragedy in the language is borrowed by political speech as often, or as confidently, as Romeo and Juliet. A member of Parliament rises to declare himself neither Montague nor Capulet and to lay a plague on both houses of a divided legislature. A war correspondent files a dispatch from a besieged city and names two dead strangers for the lovers, and the name outlives the war. A state government in India brands its street patrols with the hero's name, turned inside out, so that Romeo becomes the word for the man the police are hunting. A Belfast stage closes a peace performance with the lovers' lines. In each case the borrowing is instant, unargued, and understood by every listener in the room, which is precisely why it deserves scrutiny. Political rhetoric does not quote this play the way scholarship quotes it, line by line and answerable to the text. Political rhetoric strips the play for three portable assets: a feud with no stated cause, a curse that condemns both sides in one breath, and a pair of lovers whose death can be pointed at as the price of division. The argument of this article is that the borrowing works because of what the play withholds. The feud has no origin the text will certify, so any conflict can be poured into it. The curse belongs to a dying man attached to neither house, so any speaker can borrow his exemption. The lovers die beautifully and young, so any peace plea can borrow their pathos without borrowing their story. What the rhetoric cuts, systematically and across four centuries of record, is the play's own political machinery: a prince who fails, a letter that miscarries by plague quarantine, a reconciliation purchased with two corpses and sealed with gold statues. The survey assembled here, the InsightCrunch rhetoric survey, grades each documented invocation by the strength of its record and by the size of the cut it makes in the text. The verdict reached is double. As an instrument of blame, the play serves public speech better than any other literary property in English, because it alone lets a speaker condemn a conflict root and branch while blaming fate and both houses rather than either. As an instrument of analysis, it serves badly, and the record shows speaker after speaker paying for the figure's convenience with the figure's blindness. The treatment throughout describes the rhetoric and grades the borrowing; it takes no side in any of the disputes the rhetoric was used to describe. The grades are therefore judgments about fit and about evidence, never about causes: a borrowing can score high for documentary strength and low for fidelity, or the reverse, and neither score implies anything about the justice of the borrower's underlying position, which the survey neither adjudicates nor needs to know.

The evidence base is deliberately narrow and deep rather than broad. Political Shakespeare quotation is a flood: leaders quote Henry V for resolve, Julius Caesar for tyranny and assassination, Hamlet for hesitation, and the histories for national myth. Romeo and Juliet is the one play in the canon whose political use is almost never partisan in that way. Its lines are not, in the main, borrowed to glorify the speaker's own cause; they are borrowed to characterize a relationship between two camps, and the borrower typically stands, or pretends to stand, outside both. That structural fact is what makes the play's political career a single subject rather than a miscellany. The feud, the curse, and the lovers are three views of one diagram, the diagram the Prologue draws, and the borrowers across four centuries are all, knowingly or not, redrawing it. A survey can therefore be graded like a set of copies of one original, and the departures from the original can be measured rather than merely deplored. The departures turn out to be systematic, and their system is the article's real subject: political speech does not misquote the play at random. It amputates the same joints every time, the sovereign, the accident, and the price, because those are the joints where the play's politics would bite the borrower.

## What does the play hand a political speaker?

The play is unusually generous to the borrower because its politics are built into its frame rather than argued in its scenes. The Chorus opens with a complete political diagram in fourteen lines: "Two households, both alike in dignity, / In fair Verona, where we lay our scene, / From ancient grudge break to new mutiny, / Where civil blood makes civil hands unclean" (Prologue, lines 1 to 4). Every element a speechwriter needs is present in that quatrain. The parties are equal in standing, alike in dignity, so neither can be cast as the aggressor by rank. The grudge is ancient, so its origin is placed safely beyond inquiry. The violence is civil, neighbour against neighbour, so the figure fits a divided polity and not a foreign war. The blood makes hands unclean, so guilt is distributed to everyone who touches the quarrel. The Chorus then completes the diagram with the ending announced in advance: the lovers' deaths will "bury their parents' strife" (Prologue, line 8). A speaker who invokes the play is therefore not borrowing a story but a structure, a shape with two equal houses, an unexplained quarrel, distributed guilt, and a reconciliation whose price is known before the first scene begins. The shape does the arguing. The speaker merely pours a local conflict into it and lets the audience's memory of the tragedy supply the emotion.

### What does the Prologue promise a political speaker before the play even starts?

It promises a conflict with the verdict already attached. Because the Chorus announces the deaths and the buried strife in advance, a speaker who frames a dispute through the play inherits an ending: the quarrel is presented not as a problem to be solved but as a tragedy to be interrupted, its price fixed before the argument starts.

One further asset in the frame deserves naming, because union rhetoric spends it constantly: the lovers' first exchange at the Capulet feast is itself a piece of joint oratory, a sonnet the two speakers build between them, line answering line, until the shared poem licenses the first kiss. The scene gives every later borrower a picture of concord manufactured by speech, two enemies' children finishing each other's verse, and it is the textual root of the hope, examined in the peace sections of this article, that saying the lines together might do the work the lines describe. The play surrounds that hope with its refutation: the sonnet is spoken in disguise, at a feast the guest has infiltrated, within earshot of Tybalt's recognition and vow of revenge, so that the concord exists for fourteen lines inside a room already filling with the feud. Rhetoric that quotes the feast's harmony without its infiltration repeats the play's own cruelest juxtaposition as if it were a promise.

The play even supplies its own commentary on the practice this article surveys, and it is worth pausing on, because no borrower has ever quoted it. The Chorus returns at the head of the second act, the only other appearance of the frame, and its subject is not the feud but persuasion: the old desire for Rosaline is dead, the new affection lies in its place, and the Chorus observes that "now Romeo is beloved and loves again, alike bewitched by the charm of looks", while Juliet, knowing her lover is her foe, must steal love's sweet bait from fearful hooks. The sonnet's lesson for the survey is that the play's own internal spokesman treats the love story as a transaction conducted under enmity's conditions, bait, hooks, and theft, the vocabulary of strategy rather than of transcendence. The Chorus, in other words, is the first political reader of the lovers: it prices their bond by what it must be smuggled past. Later rhetoric reverses the pricing. Where the Chorus sees a private feeling forced into stratagems by the feud, the peace ceremonies see a stratagem, union, dignified by a private feeling, and the difference between the two readings is the difference between the play's politics and its afterlife's. The play never claims that love reconciles. It claims that the feud makes love behave like politics, in secret, at night, by letter and by proxy, and that the behaviour is fatal. A rhetoric that had absorbed the second Chorus would borrow the lovers more cautiously, and the caution is nowhere in the record.

The frame's generosity extends to the play's one figure of government. Prince Escalus enters in the first scene as the feud's ineffective sovereign, and his first speech is a confession disguised as a threat: "Three civil brawls, bred of an airy word, / By thee, old Capulet, and Montague, / Have thrice disturbed the quiet of our streets" (1.1). The phrase "airy word" is the whole rhetoric of the feud in two syllables. The Prince himself certifies that the brawls were bred of something weightless, a word of air, and in the same breath he admits that three brawls have already escaped his authority. His remedy is an edict he will not be able to enforce at the moment it matters: "If ever you disturb our streets again, / Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1). Political rhetoric quotes the Prince's city constantly and the Prince's failure almost never. Yet the failure is the play's political thesis. Verona has a lawful ruler, clear law, and capital penalties, and none of it avails, because the feud is not a crime problem but a loyalty system, two households whose servants open the play biting thumbs and whose heads are bound by a code older than any edict. A speaker who borrows the feud borrows the loyalty system too, whether the speaker knows it or not, and the borrowing is never neutral about how conflicts end. In the play, law does not end the feud. Death ends it, twice over, in the tomb.

The loyalty system has a material base that the rhetoric also borrows without noticing. The two households are not gangs but great establishments, with liveried servants who open the play fighting as a form of employment, marriageable children deployed as assets, and credit enough to fill streets with retainers at a word. Their standing, wealth, and clientage, the economics underneath the honour code, are mapped in [the study of two households, power and class in Verona](/2022/04/22/two-households-class-power-verona/), and the mapping matters to the rhetoric because a feud between establishments is a different figure from a feud between opinions. Parties and factions in a legislature command whips, funds, and careers; the Montague and Capulet figure quietly credits them with the households' deeper machinery, loyalty as livelihood, which is one reason the figure stings more than a comparison to a mere disagreement would. The Prince's compromised position completes the political diagram. He is not an outsider to the feud's cost. Mercutio is his kinsman, and Paris, who dies at the tomb suing for admission to the Capulet vault as Juliet's affianced husband, is his kinsman too; the brace of kinsmen he confesses to losing in the final audit are the state's own blood. Verona's sovereign is thus related to the casualties on both sides of his failure, a referee drawn from the same small world as the feuding houses, and his edicts fail in part because his authority is entangled in the kinship web he polices. Political rhetoric has never borrowed this either. A figure in which the referee is implicated, by blood, in the feud's losses would be an uncomfortable gift to hand a government, and the survey's borrowers, governments among them, leave it in the text.

The play also contains, in the Friar's consent to the secret marriage, the prototype of every peace plea later made in its name: "For this alliance may so happy prove / To turn your households' rancor to pure love" (2.3). The Friar is the first rhetorician of the play's afterlife. He treats the lovers not as persons but as an instrument, an alliance whose public meaning he hopes to spend on reconciliation, and he is wrong about the instrument in every particular that matters. The alliance is secret, so it cannot do public work. It is solemnized in haste, against the Friar's own stated doctrine that violent delights have violent ends. Its announcement depends on a letter, and the letter is stopped by a plague quarantine, the one event in the play where the machinery of public health overrides the machinery of reconciliation. How the hatred itself is built and sustained, insult by insult and servant by servant, is examined in [the feud explainer's account of why the Capulets and Montagues hate](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/); the present article is concerned with the Friar's rhetorical move, which later speakers repeat with better luck and the same structure. Every invocation of the lovers as a symbol of union across a divide is the Friar's gamble restated: a private bond drafted into public service, on the assumption that love's meaning will survive being used. The play is the record of that assumption failing. The rhetoric that borrows the lovers almost always borrows the gamble and declines the record.

What the play conspicuously refuses to hand any speaker is a villain. Tybalt is proud and violent, but the play gives him a code and a corpse. Old Capulet is a tyrant in one scene and a broken mourner in the last. The Prince closes the play by distributing the sentence in every direction at once: "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate, / That heaven finds means to kill your joys with love. / And I, for winking at your discords too, / Have lost a brace of kinsmen. All are punished" (5.3). The grammatical centre of that speech is the confession in the middle. The sovereign includes himself among the punished, because he winked at the discords when winking was cheaper than governing. This is the play's final political act, an audit in which blame lands on the houses, on heaven's scourge, and on the state that tolerated the feud. It is a magnificent text for a speaker who wants to close a conflict. It is a useless text for a speaker who wants to win one, since it indicts the speaker's own office first. The rhetoric surveyed in this article can be graded by a single question: how much of that audit survives the borrowing? In most of the record, none of it does. The houses keep the blame, fate keeps the cause, and the speaker, unlike the Prince, walks away unpunished and unexamined. That exemption is the true commodity being traded whenever the play enters a speech.

## Why does the feud figure flatter every speaker who uses it?

The feud's rhetorical supremacy rests on a negative: the play never states what the feud is about. Critics have searched the text for an origin and found only the Chorus's "ancient grudge" and the Prince's "airy word". The quarrel has no territory, no doctrine, no inheritance, no insult that any character can narrate. This vacancy is usually treated as a puzzle of the play. For rhetoric it is the play's chief asset. A stated cause would anchor the figure to one kind of conflict; a vacant cause lets the figure float free and settle on any division that has lasted long enough to embarrass its participants. Party against party in a legislature, sect against sect in a city, faction against faction inside a single movement: all can be renamed Montague and Capulet without the renaming making a factual claim anyone can dispute. The figure accuses the conflict of being causeless, and it does so without the speaker having to argue that it is causeless. The accusation arrives pre-loaded in the allusion, carried by four centuries of audiences who have watched young people die for a grudge nobody on stage can explain.

Notice, too, the work done by the Prologue's courtesy. The households are "both alike in dignity", a phrase political borrowers repeat in substance whenever they insist on treating two camps as equivalent establishments, entitled to the same courtesies and charged with the same offences. The courtesy is an insult in ceremonial dress. To be alike in dignity in Verona is to be alike in the dock: the phrase grants both houses their rank the better to deny either of them a grievance. Modern commentary inherits the move entire. The editorial that laments two parties as mirror images of ambition and obstruction, the broadcast that balances blame by formula before examining any act, the summit communiqué that regrets violence on all sides in a war with an aggressor, all are spending the Prologue's courtesy, and all derive the same benefit: equality of blame is reached by courtesy rather than by inquiry, so the conclusion arrives with the allusion and cannot be cross-examined. The play earns its symmetry by staging the two households' offences in alternation, brawl for brawl, corpse for corpse, and by denying the audience any scene in which one house is simply right. The rhetoric takes the symmetry as a premise available free of charge. That is the feud figure's deepest flattery of its user: it supplies the most contestable judgement in any conflict, that the sides are equal, in the form of a compliment to both.

The figure also performs a subtler service. To call two parties Montagues and Capulets is to declare them alike in dignity and alike in guilt in a single gesture, and to place the speaker outside both houses. The position is Mercutio's. Dying of a wound taken in a fight that was not his family's quarrel, he spends his last strength on the sentence that political speech has never stopped borrowing: "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1). The migration of that sentence into everyday English at large is traced in [the census of the play's phrases in everyday English](/2029/11/02/phrases-in-everyday-english/); the concern here is narrower, the sentence's career as an instrument of public speech, where it functions as a licence. A partisan who condemns both sides risks being read as a traitor to one of them. A speaker who condemns both sides in Mercutio's words borrows the standing of a dying man whom both sides wronged, and the condemnation reads as testimony rather than treachery. The curse is also perfectly symmetrical, and symmetry is a political technology. It allows the speaker to distribute blame without measuring it, to treat the division itself as the offence, and to occupy the one piece of ground that a divided audience can still share: exhaustion with the feud.

### Why is a curse from a dying outsider more useful to a politician than a speech by a partisan?

Because the curse arrives with its credentials attached. Mercutio belongs to neither house and is killed by the quarrel he mocks, so a speaker who borrows his line borrows impartiality and injury at once, standing that no living partisan condemning both sides could claim in person.

There is, however, a theft inside the borrowing, and the parliamentary record exposes it with unusual clarity. Mercutio's curse in the play is not a programme. It is a refusal of programmes, spoken by a man who has just discovered that his wit cannot stop a sword. He does not propose the ballot, or a treaty, or a reform; he wishes a plague on the houses and dies. When legislators borrow the sentence, they borrow the refusal and attach it to a programme, Mercutio's despair conscripted as the peroration of an argument he never made. The theft is not a scandal; it is how literary quotation works in oratory, and the play's other borrowers commit versions of it. But it fixes the first law of the survey that follows. An invocation is strongest as rhetoric exactly where it is least faithful as reading, and the grading in the InsightCrunch rhetoric survey therefore tracks two things separately: the documentary strength of the record, and the size of the distance between the words' work in the play and their work in the speech.

A last service of the figure deserves notice before the record begins, because it operates inside single parties as readily as between them. Every large movement contains its own two households, an establishment wing and an insurgent wing, alike in professed dignity and bound by a grudge neither can narrate without embarrassment, and the feud figure is the standing instrument by which leaders deplore their own factions without indicting either: the party is Montague and Capulet to itself, and the leader speaks as its Prince, above the brawl, although, unlike Escalus, the leader typically omits the confession that the winking was the leader's own. The survey's parliamentary rows include no pure case of this internal use, which is itself instructive: the internal feud is the figure's most frequent modern employment in commentary and conference speech, and it is the least often preserved in verbatim records, because it lives in the reported speech of journalists and the memoirs of participants rather than in Hansard's text. The survey grades what it can document and names the gap, since a census of the play's political life that confined itself to the printed record would understate the figure's commonest habitat, the corridor, the studio, and the party meeting, where no clerk is taking the words down.

## What does the parliamentary record show?

Hansard, the verbatim record of the British Parliament, preserves the feud figure's parliamentary career in unusual completeness, and its earliest great instance is also its most self-aware. On 13 June 1854 the House of Commons debated the ballot, the demand that votes be cast in secret to protect electors from intimidation by landlords, employers, and customers. The motion was moved, as it was year after year, by Henry Berkeley, the member for Bristol, who closed a very long speech on electoral corruption with a declaration of independence from both front benches. He told the House that he had been a partisan of Liberal ministers for seventeen years but had never treated the ballot as a party question, and he ended with the play: he was "neither Montague nor Capulet", and could say "heartily and emphatically, a 'plague on both your houses'", since between the two factions the electoral rights and liberty of conscience of his countrymen were stabbed nearly to death. Every feature of the mature political use is already present in 1854. The speaker claims Mercutio's exemption for himself, neither of the factions, although he has just confessed to seventeen years of partisanship for one of them. The curse is aimed at both houses of a quarrel the speaker declines to adjudicate. And the play's dying outsider is made the patron of a concrete legislative remedy, secret voting, which has no more to do with Mercutio than a treaty would. Berkeley's peroration worked because the House knew the sentence and knew its speaker's situation in the play. The allusion did in a breath what an argument about his own impartiality could not have done at any length.

The record then shows the sentence settling into the chamber's furniture. In the debate on the Address in July 1864, with the House arguing over Denmark and the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein, a speaker declined to choose between two foreign dynasties and predicted that the taxpayers of England would say of both kings and princes, "a plague on both your houses". The curse had by then become a way of pronouncing public exhaustion without taking the responsibility of pronouncing it oneself; the speaker ventriloquizes the taxpayers, who ventriloquize Mercutio. In March 1896, during the debate on the Irish fee grant, a member protesting that Ireland had been underpaid under a bargain between the parties declared that he did not care whether the fault lay with the Liberal Party or the Tory Party, since the injustice had been committed, and said, "a plague on both your houses". The sentence by 1896 needed no naming of its source and no Montague attached; it had become the chamber's standard idiom for a wrong chargeable to the party system as such. The pattern completes itself in August 1965, in an adjournment debate on Gibraltar, when a member reported the political attitude of the Gibraltarians themselves, caught inside British party warfare over the Rock, as a plague on both houses. By then the curse describes not a speaker's stance but a people's mood, reported upward into the chamber by its representative. Across a hundred and eleven years of Hansard, the sentence travels from a reformer's peroration, to a diplomatist's shrug, to an Irish protest, to a population's reported verdict on politicians in general. The distance from the play grows at every step, and the usefulness grows with the distance. What began as a quotation ends as a category of political feeling for which English keeps no other phrase of equal force.

The ventriloquism deserves a moment's attention, because it is the curse's characteristic parliamentary technique and it is pure Escalus in reverse. The Prince speaks his audit in his own person and includes himself in its sentence; the members of 1864 and 1965 speak their curse through absent mouths, the taxpayers of England, the people of Gibraltar, whose exhaustion they report rather than confess. The device solves the curse's one inconvenience, which is that a curse on both houses, spoken by a member of one of them, invites the question why the speaker remains in his seat. Reported speech dissolves the question. The speaker is merely the chamber's ear, relaying a plague wished by others, and the wish arrives with the authority of the multitude and the deniability of a rumour. It also, not incidentally, flatters the relayed population as Mercutios, plain speakers sick of the feud, when the population in question may have held considered positions the relay conveniently dissolves into disgust. The survey marks the technique wherever it occurs, in 1864 and 1965 alike, as the curse's third cut, the cut of the speaker, who arranges to be absent from his own sentence.

The chamber's handling of the sentence also marks a distinction that the survey's grades depend on. Berkeley in 1854 quotes the play, naming its houses and expecting the allusion to be caught; the member in 1896 quotes the language, expecting only the idiom to be caught; the member in 1965 reports the idiom as the speech of others. Quotation, idiom, and ethnography are three different relations between a legislature and a text, and they carry different obligations. A quotation can be checked against its source and found, as Berkeley's is found, to have reversed its original meaning, since Mercutio's curse proposes nothing and Berkeley's proposes the ballot. An idiom can be checked only against usage, and usage acquits it. Reported speech can be checked against neither. Political rhetoric prefers the later stages of this sequence for the same reason it prefers the feud to the Prince: each stage sheds an obligation. The survey's insistence on dating and sourcing every invocation is an attempt to return the borrowings to the first stage, where they can be audited, which is the stage at which their cuts become visible and their users answerable.

The detail of Berkeley's case shows what the curse was asked to carry. His speech rested on evidence of intimidation assembled from election petitions, and its most notorious exhibit was a chain of coercion he laid before the House link by link: a creditor's sister blackmailed with her own letters, the sister pressing her brother, the brother pressing his debtor, the debtor pressing his tenants and workmen, until five electors were screwed, in Berkeley's word, into voting against conscience. The anecdote's horror is administrative rather than theatrical, a machine of screws rather than a brawl of swords, and it is the machine that the Mercutio peroration was summoned to crown. The remedy Berkeley attached to the curse entered the statute book eighteen years later, when the Ballot Act of 1872 made secret voting general, so the borrowed despair of 1854 stands in the record as the overture to a successful reform. That outcome flatters the borrowing in retrospect. It should not be allowed to. The sentence's work in the chamber was not to argue the remedy, which Berkeley's evidence did, but to certify the speaker's exemption from the party system he was asking the chamber to defy, and the certification was the part no evidence could supply. Only the play could supply it, because only the play contained a man both houses had wronged who was past being suspected of ambition.

Three lessons fall out of the table before the cases are examined in detail. First, the curse is the most borrowed single sentence and the lovers the most borrowed single image, while the Prince, the play's only political thinker, is borrowed by nobody. Second, the documentary tier of the invocations is highest exactly where the play's presence is thinnest: Hansard quotes one sentence verbatim for a century, and the richest borrowings, whole couples renamed for the lovers, rest on journalism rather than on texts. Third, every borrower cuts the ending differently, Berkeley cutting Mercutio's despair, the squad name cutting the hero's character, the peace masque cutting the tomb. The survey's grades therefore refuse to rank invocations by fidelity alone. A borrowing can be verbatim and faithless, as the fee grant curse is, or loose and honourable, as the Sarajevo naming is. The sections that follow take the three largest cases in turn: a war that renamed two of its dead, a feud that journalism supplied with lovers, and a government that conscripted the hero's name against lovers.

## What did the Sarajevo naming do?

On 19 May 1993, in the second year of the siege of Sarajevo, a Bosniak woman named Admira Ismić and a Bosnian Serb man named Boško Brkić attempted to cross the Vrbanja bridge into Serb-held Grbavica, under an arrangement, believed on both sides, that fire would be held while they crossed. Boško Brkić was shot first and died at once. Admira Ismić, herself wounded, crawled to him, embraced him, and died beside him. Their bodies lay on the bridge for days, in a no man's land neither side would enter under fire to clear; the sources divide between four days and eight before the recovery, and no account has established with certainty who fired the shots. The photographer Mark Milstein made the image of the two bodies locked together. The Reuters correspondent Kurt Schork, who had been told what Milstein saw, wrote the dispatch that named them, published on 23 May 1993, and the name he gave them was the play's: the Romeo and Juliet of Sarajevo. Within a year the name had become the title of an international documentary, Romeo and Juliet in Sarajevo, directed by John Zaritsky and co-produced by PBS Frontline, the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation, the National Film Board of Canada, and WDR, released in May 1994. Schork continued to report the war he had named for them. He was killed on assignment in Sierra Leone in 2000, and part of his ashes were interred beside the couple in Sarajevo's Lav cemetery, where their families had brought them together after the war. Thirty years after the deaths, in July 2023, Sarajevo's Centar Municipality announced, on the initiative of its head, Srđan Mandić, and with the Ismić family's agreement, that a memorial plaque would mark the place on the bank of the Miljacka near where they died.

The documentary that carried the name deserves a sentence of its own in the survey, because it shows the name doing institutional work. Zaritsky's film did not merely repeat Schork's frame; it investigated the deaths, interviewing the families and the men who had held the bridge under their sights, and it used the lovers' names as the warrant for asking the war's hardest local question, who fired and under what orders, a question the film could not finally answer. The play's names thus licensed an inquiry they could not conclude, which is a fair emblem of the naming's whole career. As emblem, the couple entered the stock of images through which the siege is remembered abroad, alongside the burned library and the cellist in the ruins, and like those images the emblem both preserves and replaces the event. The bodies on the bridge become, in the emblem, two figures in a posture of embrace, composed and almost peaceful, while the documented facts, a failed safe conduct, an unidentified marksman, a recovery delayed for days under fire, recede. Rhetoric's defenders call this the price of remembrance. Its critics call it the mechanism by which a war with identifiable decisions becomes a tragedy with none. The survey records both judgements without splitting the difference, because the naming's own city has delivered its verdict by conduct: Sarajevo buried the couple together, buried their namer beside them, and voted the plaque.

The naming deserves analysis rather than reverence, because it is the most consequential political borrowing of the play in the modern record, and because it shows what the lovers figure can do that the feud figure cannot. The feud figure distributes blame; the lovers figure concentrates sympathy. In 1993 the siege of Sarajevo was, for distant audiences, a confusion of factions, acronyms, and disputed responsibilities, a conflict that foreign commentary kept describing as too complicated to judge. Schork's name abolished the complication. It told every reader exactly how to feel and roughly what to think: two young people who loved each other across the line had been killed by the line itself. The name was not an argument about the war's causes, its aggressors, or its remedies, and it survived precisely because it was none of those things. It could be repeated by newspapers, broadcasters, and politicians of every position on intervention, since it demanded mourning and not policy. That universality is the naming's strength as rhetoric and its limit as knowledge. The couple had not set out to unite the two sides of Sarajevo or to reconcile their communities. They had set out to leave, together, for a life on the other side of the bridge. The play's name converts an attempted escape into an emblem of union, and converts a specific, contested killing, by a sniper never identified, into the work of the divide in the abstract. The rhetoric of the feud is usually accused of simplifying blame; the Sarajevo case shows the lovers figure simplifying innocence, which is the same operation performed on the other term. Neither the couple's own families nor the city that later claimed them have treated the name as a falsification, and the memorial plaque of 2023 kept it. The name's acceptance by the bereaved is part of its record, and it complicates any verdict that the naming merely exploited the dead. Rhetoric is judged in the end by whether its audience, including the audience with the strongest right to object, keeps it. Sarajevo kept it. The keeping took local forms as well as monumental ones: the Sarajevo band Zabranjeno Pušenje set the couple's story to music in the song "Boško i Admira", carrying the names, under their own names rather than the play's, into the city's own popular memory, a reminder that the borrowed title abroad and the proper names at home have run in parallel ever since 1993, each Sarajevan audience fluent in both.

### What does the Sarajevo naming do that the feud figure cannot?

It gives a divided conflict a single pair of faces and a single feeling. The feud figure tells an audience that both sides are guilty; the lovers figure tells an audience whom to mourn, and so travels across every disagreement about blame.

## Why did the Bosnia debate run on the feud figure and the lovers at once?

The Sarajevo naming did not compete in an empty field. In the same years, the same war was being described to Western governments through the feud figure in its purest and least literary form, the doctrine that the Balkans were convulsed by ancient hatreds, immemorial and self-sustaining, for which no outsider bore responsibility and against which no outsider could prevail. The doctrine's vehicle was Robert D. Kaplan's Balkan Ghosts, published in 1993, a travel history that presented the region's peoples as prisoners of old enmities. It was widely reported in the press that President Bill Clinton had read the book, and aides were said to have described its effect as an argument against intervention in Bosnia; Kaplan himself objected to the use the administration made of it. The scholarly answer arrived in 1997 with Maria Todorova's Imagining the Balkans, published by Oxford University Press, in which the historian dissected the Western habit of treating the peninsula as Europe's repository of tribal violence. Todorova remarked that it was ironic and somewhat frightening to learn from the press that the president had drawn his information from Kaplan's book, and she stated the refutation in arithmetic form: if the region's peoples had always and only killed one another, there would be nobody left, or the region would long since have been cleansed into homogeneity, and there would be no problem to describe. The hatreds that mattered, the historians' consensus ran, were modern manufactures, built by nineteenth and twentieth century nationalisms out of older materials, which is to say they had authors, dates, and purposes, everything the word ancient exists to deny. Todorova gave the whole habit a name, Balkanism, for the discourse that casts the peninsula as Europe's internal other, violent by inheritance, and the discourse had already supplied political English with a common noun of its own: balkanization, the fragmentation of a polity into mutually hostile pieces, a word that entered the language after the Balkan Wars and performs, in a single term, the feud figure's entire argument. A speaker who calls a party, a region, or an internet balkanized is quoting the ancient grudge without knowing its source, exactly as the member in the fee grant debate quoted Mercutio without naming him. The play and the peninsula thus stand as the two great reservoirs of the same figure in European speech, one literary and one geographical, and in 1993 they were drawn on simultaneously by the same governments, the geographical reservoir supplying the excuse and the literary reservoir supplying the elegy.

The episode belongs in this survey although the play is never named in it, because it shows the feud figure operating at the level of statecraft with the literature stripped away, and it reveals what the literature contributes when it is present. Ancient hatreds is the Chorus's "ancient grudge" promoted to a theory of history, and it performs the same three services catalogued in this article: it declares the conflict causeless by declaring its causes too old to matter, it distributes the guilt symmetrically across the peoples concerned, and it exempts the speaker, in this case the watching governments, from the audit that the Prince performs on himself. Western capitals that described Bosnia as an ancient feud were borrowing Escalus's city and declining Escalus's confession, with consequences measured in years of besiegement. Against that frame, Schork's name for the Sarajevo couple can be read as a rival rhetoric built from the play's other asset. Where the grudge figure made the war illegible, a fate to be waited out, the lovers figure made it legible as an event with victims, perpetrators implied, and a bridge. Neither frame was an analysis; both were instruments for deciding what distant publics would feel, and the contest between them in the spring of 1993 was a contest between two halves of the same play. Political actors in later conflicts have reached for both halves in the same way, the ancient grudge to excuse inaction and the dead lovers to demand feeling, and the survey's lesson is that the choice between them is itself a political act disguised as a literary one. The play, characteristically, contains the refutation of both its own borrowings: its grudge is never certified as ancient in origin, only in habit, and its lovers are not an argument but a loss.

## When did American journalism give a feud its lovers?

The Hatfield and McCoy feud in the Tug Valley, on the Kentucky and West Virginia border, was reported to the nation in the late 1880s as a savage clan war, and the reporting needed a human story. The metropolitan press arrived in the valley in force after the New Year's night killings of 1888, and the feud writing that followed, in newspapers and then in the dime press and the magazines, performed for Appalachia the same service the ancient-hatreds frame would perform for Bosnia a century later: it made a region's violence legible to distant readers by declaring it a survival, a piece of the old world of clan and vendetta persisting inside a modern republic. Feud historians have traced how the romance strand entered that coverage and hardened; the New York Sun's John Spears, reporting the feud in 1888, told the Johnse and Roseanna story in a form closer to the record than the legend that succeeded it, and the full Romeo and Juliet casting belongs to the retellings that followed, as the affair was polished by each new narrator into the shape the market recognized. It found one in the affair, around 1880, between Johnse Hatfield, son of the Hatfield patriarch, and Roseanna McCoy, daughter of his enemy. The documented skeleton is bleak. Roseanna went to live with the Hatfields, returned to her own family pregnant, and was rejected in sequence by nearly everyone the story touches. Johnse Hatfield did not marry her; he married her cousin Nancy McCoy in 1881. Roseanna's child died in infancy, in the accounts that grant the child existed at all, and Roseanna herself died before the feud burned out, still in her twenties. The Encyclopaedia Britannica's account of the feud states the press history in one sentence: the legend was embellished by the brief affair, and newspapers turned it into a Romeo-and-Juliet romance. The casting hardened in book form. Feud historians trace book-length versions of the pairing to Jean Thomas's Big Sandy in 1940, and in 1948 Virgil Carrington Jones published his history of the feud with a chapter titled "The Mountain Romeo and Juliet". By the middle of the twentieth century the play's names were the feud's permanent headline, and they remain the frame through which the feud is sold to readers who know nothing else about it.

The feud that journalism simplified into a romance had, in its own decade, a politics dramatic enough to test two state governments. The killings drew in the governors of Kentucky and West Virginia over the extradition of accused Hatfields seized across the state line, posses and militia threats were exchanged between the two capitals, and the quarrel reached the Supreme Court of the United States, which decided in Mahon v. Justice, in May 1888, that Kentucky could hold the prisoners it had taken. A feud whose romance the newspapers were framing as Verona on the Tug had in fact become a constitutional incident between American states, complete with the play's missing element restored: sovereigns, two of them, competing in jurisdiction while their subjects killed one another. The romance frame deleted all of it. In the Romeo and Juliet version there are no governors, no warrants, no court, only houses and lovers, which is why the frame proved so durable in a nation happy to remember the feud as folklore rather than as a failure of its own institutions.

The scholarly record treats that frame as a manufacture. Altina Waller's Feud, the standard scholarly history of the Hatfields and McCoys published in 1988, reads the violence through the valley's economic transformation, timber and land and the collapse of older arrangements, and treats the romance as an embellishment laid over a conflict whose causes were material. Waller's verdict has not retired the romance, and the romance's screen afterlife shows why. The History channel's three-part Hatfields and McCoys, broadcast in 2012, retold the feud for a mass audience with the Johnse and Roseanna story at its emotional centre, and the same network followed in 2013 with a reality series that set present-day descendants and neighbours feuding in miniature for entertainment. A century and a quarter after the killings, the romance remains the feud's export product, the element of the record most easily detached from timber, land, and jurisdiction and shipped to audiences who will never see the Tug Fork. The survey's point is not that the romance is false in every fibre; a young couple did cross the line, and a child did die. It is that the Romeo and Juliet frame decides in advance which fibres of a conflict count as its story, and the fibres it discards are consistently the political ones, the governors, the warrants, the court, and the timber money, leaving a feud that can be loved by everyone precisely because it accuses no one. The feud's own documentary trail gives the romance thin support: the historian Leonard Roberts, annotating a McCoy family history, recorded of Roseanna's baby that its birth, age, and death are not proved. None of this has loosened the label. The case matters to this survey because it isolates the label's function in its purest form. A distant audience confronting an Appalachian conflict it cannot parse is offered the play as a translation key: two houses, one love, universal comprehension in a sentence. The translation is not free. It converts a dispute about land, timber, courts, and a stolen hog into a timeless passion, and it converts two particular people, one of whom abandoned the other, into literature's most faithful pair. The political work done by the label is the same work the feud figure does in parliament, performed by journalists rather than speakers: it retires the causes of a conflict from discussion by declaring the conflict a story everyone already knows. Where the Sarajevo naming dignified its subjects beyond their own intentions, the mountain naming traduced its subjects beyond theirs. The difference between the two cases is not in the figure, which is identical. It is in the direction of the audience's sympathy and in whether the named are in a position to be honoured or traduced, a difference the figure itself cannot register. That blindness is a property of the instrument, and the instrument is chosen anyway, which is the fact about political rhetoric that the play keeps demonstrating.

## What happens when a government borrows the hero's name?

In March 2017, within days of taking office, the new government of Uttar Pradesh, India's most populous state, fulfilled an election promise by creating police squads charged with protecting women from harassment in public places. The squads, uniformed and plainclothes officers posted near colleges, parks, and markets, were given a name drawn from Indian English street idiom, in which a roadside Romeo is a man who harasses women in public. They were called the anti-Romeo squads. The name is the only instance in this survey of the play supplying not a figure but a brand, and it shows the borrowing running in reverse. Every other invocation in the record borrows the play's prestige to dignify a political act. The squad name borrows Romeo's name to stigmatize, relying on the street idiom's older demotion of the hero into a loiterer, a sense the dictionaries of the common noun had long recorded and which the series' study of the word Romeo documents in its own territory. The state's innovation was to give the idiom a uniform, a budget, and a mandate.

The name immediately became the story, which is the definition of successful branding and the beginning of the squad's troubles. Within two weeks the Thomson Reuters Foundation reported, on 5 April 2017, that more than twenty prominent women's rights activists had called on the chief minister, Yogi Adityanath, to disband the squads, describing their work as moral policing that left people insecure and fearful. The reported record of the squads' first fortnight included young men questioned and publicly shamed, identification checks, parents telephoned, and, in one incident reported by local media, a man accused of harassment whose head was shaved by a squad. The chief minister responded inside the same news cycle by ordering guidelines: the squads were not to question, frisk, or humiliate couples, and not to hand out physical punishments. The opposition's objection reached the name itself. Akhilesh Yadav, the state's former chief minister, proposed that the government change the squad's name, pointing out that Romeo was not a molester. The literary correction came from a politician, and it was exact: the play's Romeo is guilty of many things, including two killings, but the offence the squads existed to punish is not among them. The state's own conduct in the launch's first days belongs in the row, because it shows a government discovering its borrowed name's behaviour in public. Within the month, as reports of overreach accumulated, the chief minister directed that guidelines be issued for the squads, ordering that couples not be questioned or humiliated in the drive's name and that parents be telephoned only where an offence was genuinely suspected; the orders were reported in the press on 24 March 2017. The sequence is the survey's neatest inversion of the Hansard pattern. A legislature borrows the play to dignify a speech and moves on; a government borrowed the play to brand an operation and then had to spend its own authority, in public, restraining the brand's interpretation by its own officers, since a name that means harasser to the street will be read as a licence by the patrol. The play's hero had been conscripted as the offence, the offence's officers heard the conscription as permission, and the correction had to be issued by the same voice that had named the squads. No other borrowing in the survey fed back into its borrower's conduct so quickly, which is the practical cost of the label level of appropriation that Hawkes's side of the critical quarrel describes: meanings made in the using are made by all the users, including the ones in uniform.

The squads were revived in April 2022 for a deployment of a hundred days around schools, colleges, and crossings, under the same name, the 2017 argument having changed nothing in the brand.

Set the brand against the play's own jurisprudence and the inversion sharpens. Verona's law, such as it is, hunts brawlers: the Prince's edict threatens death for disturbing the streets, and the one sentence the state actually executes in the play is Romeo's exile, imposed for a killing in a duel, not for courtship. Nobody in Verona is policed for loving, and the lovers' offence against the public order is entirely derivative; they become criminals because the feud has made their marriage a secret and their movements a trespass. A state that brands its patrols anti-Romeo has therefore adopted the position of the feud itself, treating the lover as the public problem, where the play assigns that role to the houses and to the sovereign who winked at them. The inversion is not a subtlety available only to scholars. It was available to the state's own opposition leader in a single sentence, and the survey records his sentence as the brand's most accurate review. The episode's lesson for the rhetoric of naming is general: a borrowed name imports a story whether the borrower wants it or not, and the story, given time, files its objection. Delhi's police had earlier run a comparable drive under the name Operation Majnu, drawn from the lover of the Laila and Majnun romance, so that two of the subcontinent's great lovers were conscripted by two police forces as synonyms for the harasser. The Financial Times, reporting the Uttar Pradesh launch in March 2017, glossed the target for its readers as roadside Romeos, describing the phrase as the common Indian term for men who commit acts of street harassment; the gloss matters because it shows the idiom, not the tragedy, doing the communicative work for an international audience as fluently as for a local one. Commentary in the Indian press divided over the name along a line the survey has met before. Defenders of the squads treated the name as a harmless vernacular label for a real offence, street harassment being a documented and serious harm; critics, Nilanjana Roy among them in the Financial Times of 28 March 2017, answered that a state which names its drive for a lover has announced in advance that young love itself is among the suspects, and that the squads' conduct toward couples bore the announcement out. The argument is, in the survey's terms, a dispute about what the borrowing cuts. The government held that the name cut nothing, being street idiom; the critics held that it cut the distinction between pursuit and harassment, which is the distinction the play itself observes, since its hero's suit is welcomed and its one coercive courtship, Paris's arranged claim on Juliet, is the match the tragedy refuses. The pattern is the squad section's finding. A state that names an enforcement drive for a literary lover is not making a literary judgement. It is buying instant public comprehension, and the comprehension it buys is the idiom's, not the play's: everyone addressed by the name already knows what a roadside Romeo is, and almost no one addressed by it pictures Verona. The play's prestige is spent without the play being consulted, which is why the former chief minister's objection, though correct, was useless. The name was never about the character. It was about the noun the character had already become, and the noun belonged to the street.

### Why did the squad name collapse the play's Romeo into a street harasser?

Because the borrowing never touched the play. The name drew on the established Indian English idiom of the roadside Romeo, in which the hero's name already meant a man who loiters after women, so the brand inherited the idiom's meaning entire and the character's story not at all.

## Can the play be put to work for peace?

The invocations surveyed so far are uses of the play as language: a sentence in a speech, a name in a dispatch, a brand on a police squad. A smaller and stranger class uses the play as an instrument, staging its words inside divided communities in the hope that the fiction will do negotiation work. The best documented recent instance comes from Belfast. In 2019 Terra Nova Productions, a Belfast company whose intercultural work is supported through the European Union's PEACE programme, staged A Midsummer Night's Dream with professional and amateur performers drawn across the city's communities. A scholarly account of the production, published in the journal Atlantis in December 2024, records that the celebratory masque closing the performance recited famous lines from Romeo and Juliet, and that a local actor, urging peace, drew on the lovers' words again within a programme built around reconciliation. The detail is small and exact: in a city where the two-communities figure is not a metaphor but a description of the streets, the peace work reached past the comedy being staged to the tragedy of the divided houses for its peroration. The lovers' lines were the part of the canon the room could be trusted to feel together. The instance is graded in the survey as journal scholarship rather than as rhetoric, but it belongs in this article because it states the peace use in its honest form. The play is not quoted to describe the conflict. It is quoted to rehearse the feeling of its ending, the moment when enemies stand in a tomb and discover the price, in the hope that the rehearsal substitutes for the event.

Scholarship on the cinema of the Northern Ireland conflict supplies the standing objection to that hope, and it is the sharpest form of the simplification charge this article must answer. A study of the Troubles' romantic tragedies, published under the title "Love in the Time of the Troubles", argues that the love-across-the-divide films adopt the narrative form of Romeo and Juliet and inherit the form's defect: tragedy universalizes, and universalizing a conflict dissolves its specific politics. The study's central observation is structural. In the Northern Irish case, each side of the divide experiences itself as a minority, because the conflict is triangular, running between the two communities and the two states behind them, Ireland and Britain. A two-house story cannot hold a three-cornered conflict. Casting the divide as Montague against Capulet flattens the geometry, misassigns the symmetry, and converts a contest with stated political objects, sovereignty, borders, and the constitutional future, into a mood of mutual ancient hatred for which nobody is responsible and everybody is sorry. The objection generalizes beyond cinema. It is the precise form of the error in the Sarajevo naming, which simplified agency, and in the feud figure everywhere, which simplifies cause. Peace rhetoric that borrows the lovers promises an audience the feeling of reconciliation at the price of the analysis reconciliation would need. Behind the ceremonies stands a quieter infrastructure that the survey should not pass over. The play is a school text across the divided jurisdictions in this article: it is studied in Northern Ireland's classrooms in both the controlled and maintained sectors, as it is in India's schools and in the American districts that argue over the Hatfield romance, so that the populations whose conflicts the play is borrowed to describe have usually met the play first as pupils, on the same set pages, under teachers enjoined to neutrality about everything except the text. Peace practitioners have long treated that shared schooling as a resource: a divided city whose children have all read the same feud possesses one story whose sympathies are pre-distributed, in which each side has been assigned the same lines to admire and the same deaths to regret, and the Terra Nova masque's wager was that the pre-distribution could be activated in a room. The wager's limit is the schoolroom's own limit. What is shared in the classroom is the play as literature, its sympathies literary; the masque's ceremony asked the sympathies to hold under political load, in the presence of the actual divide, and the record of the evening, as García-Periago gathers it, is of an audience willing to let them. Willingness is not a settlement, and the survey grades the occasion as it grades Schork's dispatch: as feeling honestly organized, which is a beginning of politics and not a substitute for one.

The Belfast masque and the Troubles films stand at the two ends of that bargain: in the masque the lines are a ceremony performed by the divided themselves, at first hand and in full knowledge of the ending; in the films the form is a product sold to audiences outside the conflict, for whom the simplification is the pleasure. The survey grades the two uses differently for that reason, although the figure borrowed is the same.

One more feature of the peace use deserves record, because it explains the figure's grip on negotiators and ceremonial speakers who never quote the play directly. The ending of Romeo and Juliet is the only famous reconciliation in the canon that is staged as a transaction between the bereaved rather than as a pardon from above. No prince forgives the houses; the houses, emptied of heirs, forgive each other's existence and settle the account in gold. Reconciliation rhetoric reaches for that shape instinctively whenever it wants to present peace as something the parties themselves purchase rather than something imposed on them, and the shape's appeal is democratic: it assigns the work of peace to the former enemies and the cost to their pride. What the shape omits, and what the survey's fifth cut records, is that in the play the transaction is possible only because there is nothing left to inherit. The houses reconcile as dynasties at the moment they cease to be dynasties, having buried their futures in the same tomb. A peace process that quotes the handshake borrows a reconciliation whose precondition is extinction, and the borrowing's optimism is therefore built on the one term of the original that no negotiator would accept if it were stated. The play's ending comforts ceremonies precisely because ceremonies are not required to state their terms.

## What does the rhetoric cut?

The complication the brief for this article names, the simplification the rhetoric requires, can be stated as a list of five cuts, each verifiable against the survey. The first cut is the cause. The play itself makes this cut, since it never supplies the grudge's origin, and rhetoric merely inherits the vacancy; but a conflict in politics always has causes, and a figure that requires causelessness teaches its audience to stop asking for them. The second cut is the sovereign. The Prince, who fails to prevent the deaths and confesses the failure in the play's last audit, disappears from every borrowing, because a rhetoric of division has no use for a failed referee who is also partly guilty; actual conflicts have referees, states, courts, and guarantors, and the figure trains audiences to forget them. The third cut is accident. The play's catastrophe runs on a letter stopped by plague quarantine and a message never delivered, machinery that political rhetoric, which prefers hatred as an explanation, always removes; the feud figure makes the deaths the feud's necessary product, where the play makes them the feud's contingent product, and the difference is the difference between fatalism and politics. Bound up with the accident is a sixth deletion, the mediator. The Friar is the play's one professional go-between, a man whose office crosses the line between the houses because his cell belongs to neither, and his mediation is the catastrophe's engine: the secret marriage, the sleeping potion, and the undelivered letter are all his devices. Real conflicts teem with his successors, envoys, guarantors, neighbouring states, and agencies whose schemes carry their own risks, and a rhetoric of two houses in a sealed city has no room for any of them. The deletion flatters the borrower twice over. It keeps the diagram binary, and it deletes the possibility that the catastrophe was a mediation failure, the one explanation that would assign homework to the watching world. In the Bosnia record the two deletions stand side by side: the ancient-hatreds frame removed the mediators by declaring the war unmediable, and the lovers frame removed them by declaring the war a fate, and in both frames the outsiders deciding whether to act were written out of the story they were deciding about. The fourth cut is the price list. The play counts its dead precisely, Mercutio, Tybalt, Paris, the lovers, and Lady Montague dead of grief, and its reconciliation is negotiated over the bodies with statues of gold; rhetoric keeps the lovers' two deaths and drops the count, because the count would raise the question of who else a settlement must answer for. The fifth cut is the ending's discomfort. Capulet and Montague do not embrace in reconciliation; they compete in mourning, each outbidding the other's monument, in the transaction weighed by [the study of the final reconciliation and its golden statues](/2023/12/15/final-reconciliation-golden-statues/), and the Prince closes the play without forgiving anyone, in a morning he calls gloomy. Peace rhetoric quotes the handshake and not the bidding war. Taken together, the five cuts describe a consistent operation: the rhetoric keeps the play's emotional architecture and deletes its political content, then presents the architecture as if it were the politics. Students assembling the survey's cases for a presentation can lay the Hansard texts and the press record side by side in a [presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html), and the cuts become visible at a glance, because the borrowed sentences are short and the passages they were cut from are not.

One further deletion runs underneath the five cuts and may be the deepest, because it concerns time. The feud in the play is ancient in origin and four days in action: the tragedy compresses the ending of a generations-old quarrel into a single week of marriages, duels, exiles, and tombs, and the compression is the play's political hope, the demonstration that even an immemorial feud can terminate, catastrophically and completely, inside five days of decisions. The rhetoric reverses the tense. A conflict renamed for the feud is placed in the feud's origin rather than in its action, exhibited as immemorial and therefore interminable, a feature of the landscape to be managed rather than an event to be ended. The ancient-hatreds frame for Bosnia is the pure case, but the same freezing operates in miniature wherever the allusion lands: the legislative feud is declared a permanent condition of the party system, the mountain feud a permanent condition of the valley, the divided city a permanent condition of its map. The play's borrowers thus inherit its past and decline its future, and a speaker who reaches for the ancient grudge has, in the very act of reaching, announced that the quarrel being described will outlast the speech, the speaker, and probably the audience. That announcement, dressed as a literary compliment to both houses, is the feud figure's most consequential political act, and it is performed so smoothly that audiences rarely notice a decision has been taken in their name.

### What single habit unites every borrowing in the survey?

Each borrowing keeps a moment of feeling and deletes a structure of responsibility. Berkeley keeps the despair and deletes the refusal to propose anything; the Sarajevo name keeps the pathos and deletes the chain of decisions; the brand keeps a name and deletes a character; the peace uses keep the warmth and delete the price.

## What do the critics say about the borrowing itself?

The scholarly argument about political uses of the play is really an argument about who owns a text's meaning once a culture starts spending it. Peter Mack's Elizabethan Rhetoric, the standard account of the rhetorical training behind the period's writing, establishes the mechanism at the play's origin: the playwright was formed in a grammar-school culture of deliberative exercises, arguing cases on both sides, and the play's great public speeches, the Prince's edicts, the Friar's counsel, Capulet's persuasions, are built from that training. Mack's evidence matters here because it shows the play was rhetorical equipment from birth, a set of speeches engineered to be detachable, excerpted, and re-performed by schoolboys trained to argue in other men's voices. The politicians are not violating the text's nature when they quarry it. They are using it as its first audiences were trained to use it, which is why the quarrying never stops. Mack's training also explains the play's particular fitness for legislators among all professionals. Verona is a state in miniature and nearly complete: a sovereign with an enforcement problem, two organized factions with retinues and marriage strategies, a clergy acting as an unofficial foreign office, a marriage market functioning as an alliance system, and a street public that can be roused by an airy word. A parliament is the same diagram at larger scale, and the play's speeches are sized for chambers, periodic, antithetical, built to be heard once and remembered. The borrowing is therefore not a decoration laid on politics from outside. It is one deliberative culture recognizing another across four centuries, and the recognition flatters both parties, the play certified as perennially relevant, the chamber certified as classically literate, in a single gesture of quotation.

The disagreement that adjudicates the survey is between Terence Hawkes and Jonathan Bate. In Meaning by Shakespeare, published in 1992, Hawkes argued that a text has no meaning sealed inside it that later ages merely discover; meaning is made in the using, and each generation in effect means by Shakespeare, reproducing itself in him. On Hawkes's account, Berkeley's curse, Schork's naming, and the Uttar Pradesh brand are not distortions of a stable object but the object itself at work, three moments in the play's real existence, and the question of fidelity is a category error. Bate's The Genius of Shakespeare, published in 1997, defends the opposite pole: the plays possess an exceptional and durable authority that appropriations answer to even when they rebel, and the text's survival across incompatible uses testifies to properties in the writing rather than to the ingenuity of the users. The survey's four centuries of evidence adjudicate the quarrel with some precision. Hawkes wins on the labels. The anti-Romeo brand and the Sarajevo name are meanings manufactured entire by their moments; nothing in the play dictates that its hero's name should police the streets of Lucknow or that two Bosnian civilians should be mourned under Veronese names, and both namings stuck because the moment needed them. Bate wins on the figures. What recurs across incompatible centuries and causes is not arbitrary: it is always the same three assets, the causeless feud, the symmetrical curse, and the dead lovers, and never the play's other riches, not the Queen Mab speech, not the Nurse, not the sonnets the lovers exchange. The text constrains the repertoire from which moments choose, and the constraint is exactly the play's political architecture, the Prologue's diagram of two equal houses and a buried strife. The verdict on the quarrel is therefore split and firm: appropriation is free at the level of the label and constrained at the level of the figure, and political rhetoric lives on the constrained level, which is why its products are so monotonously alike.

Marjorie Garber's chapter on the play in Shakespeare After All, published in 2004, supplies the remaining term. Garber reads the tragedy as a drama of language itself, of names that will not stay detached from things and letters that will not arrive, and her reading explains the rhetoric's deepest preference. Political speech reaches for this play because the play is already about the failure of words to do their work: a name that kills, a message that miscarries, a curse that comes true, an edict that cannot bind. Speakers borrow the play at the exact points where their own words are failing, to describe conflicts that argument has not moved, and the borrowing is therefore a confession as much as a decoration. Jonathan Dollimore's cultural materialist reading of the play, in the tradition of Political Shakespeare, would add that such confessions are never innocent, that the feud figure's both-sides symmetry can serve whoever profits from a conflict being declared eternal and causeless. The point has a documented edge in the Bosnia record examined in this article. To describe a war as an ancient grudge is not a neutral stylistic choice; it is a decision about responsibility with policy consequences, since a causeless conflict admits no remedy but exhaustion, and the governments that adopted the ancient-hatreds account of Bosnia in 1993 were, on the historians' showing, choosing the description that obligated them least. Dollimore's tradition names that choice ideology, the presentation of a motivated description as a fact of nature, and the survey's parliamentary rows give the mechanism a long pedigree: the curse in Hansard is repeatedly the instrument by which a speaker converts a decision, about the ballot, about a grant, about a colony, into a lament, and laments are not voted on. Against Dollimore, the survey sets the witnesses. Schork's naming was also a motivated description, and its motivation was accusation, aimed at the watching world's indifference; the cultural materialist account, which excels at unmasking the powerful's Shakespeare, has less to say about the powerless borrowing the same play as a weapon of appeal, except to grant the asymmetry it elsewhere denies. The adjudication stands: the feud figure serves power when it naturalizes a conflict, and serves witness when it personalizes one, and the difference is visible in the record only case by case, which is why a survey, and not a slogan, is the right instrument for judging it. The survey's evidence sustains Dollimore's suspicion in the parliamentary cases, where the curse is the refuge of speakers declining to assign responsibility, and resists it in the Sarajevo case, where the naming was the act of a witness with nothing to gain. The critics thus divide the survey between them, Mack explaining its mechanism, Hawkes and Bate its ontology, Garber its pathos, and Dollimore its danger, and no single account covers all nine rows, which is itself a finding about the play's political afterlife: it is not one instrument but a kit, and the politics lies in which tool a speaker reaches for.

## The verdict on the play in political speech

Political rhetoric took from Romeo and Juliet the one thing no other tragedy offers: a complete machine for talking about division without talking about its merits. The feud figure clears the speaker of adjudication, the curse clears the speaker of partisanship, and the lovers clear the speaker of despair, since their borrowed image insists that the divided are human and the division is not the whole story. The machine's price is printed in the play for anyone who reads to the end. The feud in Verona is ended by no speech. It is ended by corpses, an audit, and two gold statues, after the sovereign's law, the friar's scheme, and every good intention in the city have failed. A rhetoric that borrows the machine while declining the price will go on producing the effects the survey records: perorations that feel like honesty, namings that feel like knowledge, brands that feel like policy, and peace ceremonies that feel like settlements. The play survives all of it, as Bate would predict, and is remade by all of it, as Hawkes would predict. What the record of four centuries finally shows is that the borrowing is a diagnostic. A speaker's Romeo and Juliet reveals the speaker's theory of the conflict being described: whether it has causes or only a grudge, whether anyone governs it or only suffers it, whether its dead are a price or a punctuation. The survey's nine rows, graded and cut against the text, are offered as the means to perform that diagnosis on the next invocation, which is already being drafted somewhere, in a chamber, a newsroom, or a ministry, by a speaker who has found, as every speaker finds, that the play says it better. The play does say it better. It also says the rest.

That remainder is the standard this article finally proposes. An invocation of Romeo and Juliet in public speech should be read the way the survey reads its nine rows: ask which of the play's three assets is being spent, ask which of its five or six structures has been cut to make the spending painless, and ask where the borrower stands in the diagram, since the diagram's one unoccupied position, the Prince's, is the position every borrower silently claims while declining its confession. Judged by that standard, the record divides cleanly. The curse in Hansard is honest rhetoric and dishonest reading, a true report of exhaustion dressed as a quotation. The Sarajevo naming is the record's honourable summit, a borrowing that cost its author his life in the same trade and was ratified by the bereaved. The mountain romance is journalism's durable fiction, still selling a feud it misdescribes. The squad brand is the record's nadir, the hero's name spent by a state against the hero's own story, corrected on the spot by the only speaker who had read the play. And the peace ceremonies hold the record's open question, whether a reconciliation rehearsed in the lovers' words can ever be more than the feeling of one. Political speech will go on borrowing the play, because no other property in the language does the work. The survey's purpose is to make the borrowing legible, so that the next audience, hearing the ancient grudge invoked over a modern quarrel, can ask the questions the play itself asks of Verona: who governs here, what was the letter that never arrived, and who, exactly, is being asked to pay.

The audit can be shortened to a listener's checklist, and the article closes with it because the invocations will keep arriving, in next sessions and next emergencies, long after this survey's rows have dated. Ask which asset is being spent, feud, curse, or lovers, since the choice announces the speaker's purpose before the argument begins. Ask which structure has been cut, the sovereign, the accident, the mediator, the grievance, or the price, since the missing piece is ordinarily the one that would incriminate the borrower. Ask where the speaker stands in the Prologue's diagram, inside a house, between the houses, or in the Prince's seat, and whether the seat's confession has been included in the borrowing or quietly left on the page. Ask, finally, what the audience is being moved to do, reform, abstain, mourn, obey, or reconcile, since an allusion that cannot name its ask is asking for exemption, and exemption is the one commodity the play refuses to sell. Four questions, drawn from fourteen lines of prologue and one closing audit, are enough to grade any future invocation; that the play supplies the grading instrument as well as the borrowed goods is the last proof of Bate's side of the quarrel, and the fitting end of the survey.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why do politicians and commentators reach for Romeo and Juliet when they describe a political conflict?

They reach for it because the play supplies a ready-made shape for division that no other text in the language matches. Its Prologue presents two households alike in dignity, an ancient grudge with no stated cause, guilt distributed to every hand the blood touches, and an ending announced in advance, in which the lovers' deaths bury the strife. A speaker who pours a local conflict into that shape inherits its verdict without arguing it: the division is framed as causeless, symmetrical, and tragic before a single fact about the actual dispute has been mentioned. The borrowing is instant and needs no explanation to any audience, which is why it recurs across parliaments, war reporting, and state branding in the record surveyed in this article.

### Q: What did the speaker in the 1854 ballot debate mean by claiming to be neither Montague nor Capulet?

He meant that he refused both party factions in the House of Commons while supporting a reform neither front bench would grant. Henry Berkeley, the member for Bristol, closed his speech for the secret ballot on 13 June 1854 by declaring himself "neither Montague nor Capulet" and laying a plague on both houses, on the ground that between the factions the electoral rights of his countrymen were stabbed nearly to death. The claim borrowed Mercutio's position, the outsider attached to neither house, although Berkeley confessed in the same speech to seventeen years as a partisan of Liberal ministers. The borrowing converted a reformer's argument into a dying man's testimony, which is why it survives in Hansard as the type of the curse's parliamentary use.

### Q: Why does Mercutio's curse suit a speaker who wants to condemn both sides of a quarrel at once?

It suits because the curse is symmetrical, quotable, and spoken from a position no living politician can occupy. Mercutio dies of a wound taken in a quarrel between two houses to neither of which he belongs, and his "plague o' both your houses" (3.1) condemns the pair in one breath, without ranking them, proposing a remedy, or sparing the speaker's own side by implication. A partisan who condemns both camps in plain prose sounds like a defector. A speaker who condemns them in Mercutio's words borrows the dying man's credentials, his impartiality, his injury, and his urgency, and the condemnation reads as testimony. The sentence's century of service in Hansard, from the ballot debate of 1854 onward, rests on that borrowed standing.

### Q: Which parts of the play does political rhetoric almost never borrow?

It almost never borrows the play's machinery of government and accident. Prince Escalus, the sovereign whose edicts fail and who closes the play by confessing that he winked at the discords and lost kinsmen of his own, appears in no invocation in the survey; a rhetoric of division has no use for a guilty referee. The undelivered letter, stopped by a plague quarantine and turning the catastrophe on chance rather than hatred, is likewise cut, because the feud figure requires the deaths to be the feud's necessary product. The full death count, Mercutio, Tybalt, Paris, Lady Montague, and the lovers, is reduced to the lovers alone, and the reconciliation's closing scene, in which the fathers compete in gold monuments over the bodies, is softened into a handshake the text never stages.

### Q: Did Kurt Schork invent the name Romeo and Juliet of Sarajevo, and in what year?

Yes, the name in its world-travelling form comes from Schork's Reuters dispatch of 23 May 1993. Schork, a Reuters correspondent covering the siege, wrote the story of Admira Ismić and Boško Brkić after the photographer Mark Milstein described the image of their bodies embracing on the Vrbanja bridge, and the lovers' names were the frame his dispatch gave them. The frame then became a title: the international documentary Romeo and Juliet in Sarajevo, directed by John Zaritsky and released in May 1994, carried the naming to audiences who never read the wire copy. Later memorial practice in Sarajevo, including the plaque announced in 2023, kept Schork's names for the couple, which makes the naming that rare case of a journalistic label ratified by the community it described.

### Q: What happened to Admira Ismić and Boško Brkić on the Vrbanja bridge?

They were shot while crossing it on 19 May 1993, during an attempted passage from besieged Sarajevo into Serb-held Grbavica that was believed to be covered by an arrangement to hold fire. Boško Brkić, a Bosnian Serb, was hit first and died at once. Admira Ismić, a Bosniak, was wounded, crawled to him, embraced him, and died beside him. Their bodies lay in the no man's land on the bridge for days before they could be recovered; accounts divide between four days and eight, and the record has never established with certainty who fired the shots. The couple had been together since their schooldays and were attempting to leave the city for a life beyond the siege, rather than to perform any union of its sides, a distinction the famous name they received tends to erase.

### Q: Why did newspapers cast Johnse Hatfield and Roseanna McCoy as Romeo and Juliet, and what do historians say about the casting?

Newspapers cast them because the affair, around 1880, gave a bewildering clan war a story distant readers already knew. The Hatfield and McCoy feud reached national audiences as savage and causeless, and Roseanna McCoy's flight to the Hatfield home, her pregnancy, and her abandonment when Johnse married her cousin instead let editors frame the feud as a romance inside a vendetta. The Encyclopaedia Britannica records that newspapers turned the affair into a Romeo-and-Juliet romance, and the casting hardened in books, reaching a chapter titled "The Mountain Romeo and Juliet" in Virgil Carrington Jones's 1948 history. Historians answer that the romance was an embellishment: Altina Waller's scholarly history of the feud grounds the violence in land, timber, and economic change, and the historian Leonard Roberts recorded that even the existence of Roseanna's baby is not proved.

### Q: What were the anti-Romeo squads, and why did their name become the controversy?

The anti-Romeo squads were police units created in Uttar Pradesh in March 2017, posted near colleges, parks, and markets to protect women from harassment in public places, fulfilling an election promise of the incoming state government. Their name borrowed the Indian English idiom of the roadside Romeo, a man who loiters after women, and thereby turned the play's hero into the offence the squads policed. The name became the controversy because it announced the mission as a judgement on young men in public rather than on specific acts, and because the squads' reported early conduct, public shaming, identity checks, and calls to parents, matched the name's suspicion. Women's rights activists demanded the squads be disbanded within a fortnight, and the chief minister ordered guidelines against humiliating couples in the same news cycle.

### Q: What exactly did Akhilesh Yadav object to in the anti-Romeo squad name?

He objected to the literary slander inside it. Akhilesh Yadav, the former chief minister of Uttar Pradesh leading the opposition to the government that created the squads, proposed that the name be changed, pointing out that Romeo was not a molester. The objection is precise about the play: its Romeo is a killer twice over in duels and a suicide, but harassment of strangers is not among his offences, and his pursuit of Juliet is welcomed by its object. The objection failed as policy, since the squads were revived under the same name in April 2022, but it stands in the record as the one intervention in the naming debate made on the text's behalf, and it came from a politician rather than a scholar, which the survey notes as the exception that proves how completely the brand had detached from the book.

### Q: How has the play been used in peace work in Northern Ireland?

Its words have been borrowed as a common liturgy for divided rooms. In 2019 the Belfast company Terra Nova Productions, whose intercultural productions are supported through the European Union's PEACE programme, closed A Midsummer Night's Dream with a celebratory masque in which performers recited famous lines from Romeo and Juliet, and a local actor urged peace through the lovers' words again, as a scholarly account of the production in the journal Atlantis recorded in December 2024. The use is ceremonial rather than analytical. Mixed casts and audiences from the city's two communities are given the lovers' lines to hold in common, on the theory that rehearsing the tragedy's language of love across a divide, in full view of its ending, rehearses the feeling of reconciliation without requiring its politics to be settled first.

### Q: What do scholars of Troubles cinema object to in the love-across-the-divide story?

They object that the form lies about the conflict's shape. A study of the romantic tragedies of the Northern Ireland conflict, published as "Love in the Time of the Troubles", argues that films built on the Romeo and Juliet pattern inherit tragedy's habit of universalizing, which dissolves the specific politics of the situation they depict. The study's decisive point is geometrical: in Northern Ireland each community experiences itself as a minority within a triangular relationship involving the Irish and British states, while a two-house story can only represent a symmetrical pair. Casting the divide as Montague against Capulet therefore flatters the audience with a feud, ancient, causeless, and blameless in structure, where the documented conflict has stated objects, constitutional questions, borders, and sovereignty, none of which the lovers' pattern can hold.

### Q: Why does the feud figure let a speaker avoid naming a villain?

Because the figure pre-assigns the guilt to the feud itself. To call two parties Montagues and Capulets is to declare them alike in dignity and alike in blame, and to locate the offence in the quarrel's existence rather than in either party's conduct. The play supports the manoeuvre by withholding the grudge's origin entirely, so the allusion carries an accusation of causelessness that the speaker never has to defend as a factual claim. Prince Escalus distributes punishment to everyone at the close, and the borrowed figure keeps that distribution while quietly removing the Prince's own share of it, which in the play falls on the governing authority. A speaker inside the figure can therefore condemn a conflict root and branch, occupy the moral summit of the debate, and leave every question of who did what, and who governs, exactly where it lay.

### Q: What does the play's ending offer a speaker pleading for reconciliation?

It offers a reconciliation with its price tag attached, which is rarer in rhetoric than in the play. The ending shows the two fathers meeting over their children's bodies, Capulet asking for Montague's hand and calling the marriage settlement his daughter's jointure, and each patriarch promising a statue of the other's child in pure gold. A peace plea built honestly on that ending cannot present reconciliation as costless or as a return to innocence; it must present it as the purchase the survivors make when the alternative has been priced in corpses. That is why the honest uses of the ending, the Belfast masque among them, keep the tomb in view, while the dishonest uses quote the handshake and crop the scene. The golden statues, examined in this series' study of the final reconciliation, are the play's own warning that even grief becomes competitive the moment it goes public.

### Q: How does the rhetoric change the lovers when it makes them a peace symbol?

It reverses their intention. In the play the lovers do not seek to reconcile the houses; they seek to escape the houses' jurisdiction, marrying in secret, planning flight to Mantua, and treating Verona's public world as the enemy of their private one. The Friar alone drafts their bond into public service, hoping the alliance will turn rancor to love, and his scheme is the play's demonstration of the error. Political rhetoric repeats the Friar's move as if it were the lovers' own: couples across divides are renamed for them precisely when a public meaning, union, reconciliation, hope, is wanted from a private attachment. The Sarajevo naming shows the change at its most honourable, since the renamed couple were fleeing the city and not uniting its sides, yet the name made them an emblem of the union they had declined to wait for.

### Q: Can the feud figure ever describe a conflict fairly?

It can, under one condition the record rarely meets: when the speaker accepts the whole diagram and not only its flattering parts. A fair use would keep the Prince in the picture, acknowledging the governing authority's share of blame; would keep the accident in the picture, admitting that catastrophes turn on miscarried messages as well as hatred; and would keep the price list complete, counting all the dead a settlement must answer for. The figure then becomes what the play is, an audit of a divided city in which responsibility is distributed by evidence. The survey's nine cases show the condition failing in eight, because each borrower cut exactly the element that would have indicted the borrower's own position, Berkeley his partisanship, the brands their mandate, the peace ceremonies their optimism. The ninth case, the Sarajevo naming, escapes the charge only because its author was a witness rather than a party.

### Q: What does the disagreement between Terence Hawkes and Jonathan Bate mean for judging political uses of the play?

Their disagreement decides what fidelity could even mean. Hawkes, in Meaning by Shakespeare in 1992, argued that meaning is made in the using, each age meaning by Shakespeare rather than finding a sealed sense in him, so a political borrowing cannot betray the text; it can only continue it. Bate, in The Genius of Shakespeare in 1997, answered that the plays carry a durable authority that uses answer to, since the same properties survive incompatible centuries. The survey adjudicates between them on the evidence: Hawkes wins the labels, since nothing in the play dictated the Sarajevo name or the Lucknow brand, and Bate wins the figures, since every borrower across four centuries reaches for the same three assets, the causeless feud, the symmetrical curse, and the dead lovers, a repertoire the text and not the moment selected.

### Q: Why does the rhetoric need the feud to have no stated cause?

Because a cause would break the figure's portability and its symmetry together. If the Montagues and Capulets fought over land, doctrine, or an inheritance, then mapping them onto a political conflict would assert that the modern parties fight over the same thing, an argument a speaker would have to defend. The play's silence about the grudge's origin, certified by the Prince himself as an airy word, lets the figure declare a conflict causeless by allusion rather than by evidence, and keeps the two houses perfectly equal, since neither can be shown to have started anything. Real conflicts have origins, documents, and dates, and the borrowers in the survey are most misleading exactly where the vacancy does their work for them, teaching audiences to experience a dispute with stated objects as if it were a mood with none.

### Q: Has any political borrowing kept the play's whole ending, with the dead counted as the price?

Almost none in the record, and the exceptions are ceremonies rather than speeches. The Belfast peace masque of 2019, performed by and before the divided community itself, recited the lovers' lines inside a programme that staged loss as its subject, so the price stayed in the room. Parliamentary and journalistic borrowings, by contrast, cut the ending to a curse or a couple and never reach the tomb scene at all. The reason is structural. A speaker who quotes the whole ending inherits the Prince's audit, in which the governing authority confesses its own share of the catastrophe, and no borrower in the survey volunteers for that confession. The ending survives intact only where the borrowers are the conflict's own participants performing for each other, which suggests the play's full politics is usable solely by those with standing to be indicted by it.

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch rhetoric survey, and how does it grade each invocation?

It is this article's table of the play's political borrowings, assembling nine documented invocations from the ballot debate of 1854 to the Belfast masque of 2019: four from Hansard, the Sarajevo naming and its documentary, the Hatfield and McCoy romance framing, the anti-Romeo squads, and the peace performance. Each row records the invocation, its speaker or source, its year, the figure borrowed from the play, and what the borrowing cuts from the text. The grading runs on two axes kept separate throughout: the documentary tier of the record, from Hansard's verbatim transcript down through primary reporting and journal scholarship, and the size of the cut, measured against the parts of the play the borrower deleted. The separation matters because the survey's central finding is that the two axes do not move together; the most verbatim borrowing, a single sentence in Hansard, can be the most faithless to the play around it.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch rhetoric survey to argue that political speech borrows the play's blame without its causes?

Begin from the survey's rows rather than from the play in general, and let the contrast between the borrowed figure and the recorded cut carry the argument. The strongest exhibits are the 1854 peroration, in which a confessed partisan borrows a dying outsider's exemption to condemn both houses while proposing a remedy the curse's speaker never imagined, and the feud figure's general career, in which the Prince's confession and the letter's accident vanish from every borrowing. Set those against the play's own closing audit, where blame is distributed by evidence among houses, sovereign, and chance, and the essay's claim follows: political speech takes from the tragedy a machinery of shared guilt that costs the speaker nothing, while declining the tragedy's insistence that the guilt has specific addresses, including the speaker's own office. Close with the survey's exception, the Sarajevo naming, to show the machinery working honourably when its user is a witness rather than a party.
