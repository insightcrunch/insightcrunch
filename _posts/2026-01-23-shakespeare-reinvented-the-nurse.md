The Nurse of Romeo and Juliet has almost no existence before Shakespeare writes her. In Luigi da Porto's novella she is a pair of hands: she carries messages, opens a door at night, keeps a secret. In Matteo Bandello she is the same pair of hands with a price attached. In Arthur Brooke's poem she is an instrument in a moral prosecution, and at the end of the poem she is sentenced for her part in it. None of these tellers gives her a name that matters, a dead child, a husband, a body that aches, or a memory that runs eleven years deep. The play gives her all of these in her first scene, in a single prose reminiscence that no source prepares, no source hints at, and no source survives comparison with. The verdict of this study is plain: the reinvention of the Nurse is the clearest specimen of Shakespeare's method with his sources anywhere in the tragedy. The plot he borrowed almost entire. The person he invented almost entire. Where the borrowings end and the inventions begin can be measured scene by scene, and the measurement shows that the comedy and the pathos of the finished play are manufactured precisely at the points where the sources fall silent.

That measurement matters because the cliché runs the other way. Audiences and readers treat the Nurse as a given of the story, a stock figure who comes with the tale the way the balcony comes with the tale. Restoring the source record reverses the picture. The tale, across a century of retelling in Italian, French, and English, manages perfectly well without her as a person; she exists in it only as plumbing, the channel through which a secret courtship flows. Shakespeare takes the plumbing and builds a woman around it: a widow with a buried daughter, a body marked by childbearing and age, a professional pride in her errands, a bawdy memory that turns Juliet's infancy into comedy, and a failure of nerve in the third act that no teller before him had imagined for her. Each of those gifts can be traced to its absence in the chain of versions that Geoffrey Bullough printed in the first volume of Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare (1957), and each absence is evidence. What follows works through the chain in order, then through the play's additions in order, and ends by adjudicating the question the evidence forces: how much of this is expansion of something present, and how much is creation from nothing.

The chain against which the measurement is taken has five links that matter for this figure. Da Porto invents the tale's familiar shape in the 1520s; Bandello retells it at greater length in 1554; Boaistuau carries it into French in 1559 and loads it with moral commentary; Brooke Englishes the French into verse in 1562; Painter Englishes it into prose in 1567. A nurse walks through all five books. She is recognizably the same employee in each: the heroine's suckling intimate, keeper of the secret, carrier of its messages. The stability is itself the finding. Five tellers, three languages, four decades, and no drift toward personhood. Whatever pressures made other elements of the tale grow, the apothecary acquiring a scene, the tomb acquiring speeches, exerted no force on her. She was not a character awaiting development. She was a solved problem of plotting, and solved problems do not attract a storyteller's curiosity. Shakespeare's curiosity is therefore not the last step in a tradition of enlargement. It is a break with the tradition's settled indifference, and it should be described as one.

## The Nurse in the Italian Sources: A Pair of Hands

Da Porto's Giulietta e Romeo, composed in the 1520s and printed at Venice in 1530, is the true start of the story in its familiar shape, and its nurse-figure shows how little the tale originally asked of her. The balia has nursed Giulietta, holds her confidence, and serves as the court's hidden postal service. Through her, word passes between the heroine and Romeo Montecchi after the Cappelletti ball; through her connivance, Romeo reaches Giulietta's chamber during the long season of their secret marriage; to her, the marriage solemnized by Frate Lorenzo is known, as it is known to almost no one else. Every one of these services will survive into the play. What does not exist in Da Porto is any interest in the woman performing them. The novella never lingers on her speech, never reports her past, never lets her judge the lovers she serves. She is defined by the direction of her walking: from Giulietta's room outward, and back again. When the catastrophe comes, the tale disposes of her as it disposes of its other instruments; Da Porto's closing sentences send her into exile, a judgment on a function, delivered without a word about a person.

### What is a balia, and what did the office carry in an Italian household?

A balia is a wet-nurse: a woman hired to suckle another family's child, often in her own home, and frequently retained afterward as a lifelong household intimate. The office carried the child through weaning and early childhood, which made its holder the keeper of the family's earliest memories and a trusted channel for its private business.

Bandello surrounds his tales with dedicatory letters and frames of polite company, apparatus in which the story's meaning is debated by gentlemen before and after the telling, and the apparatus never once descends to the servants who make the plot move. The nurse of his tale exists below the threshold of commentary altogether.

Bandello's retelling, printed at Lucca in 1554 as the ninth novella of the second part of his Novelle, follows Da Porto's plot closely and keeps the nurse in the same office. On Bullough's showing, Bandello's handling adds a shade of calculation: the go-between of the Italian tradition is never far from her fee, and Bandello lets the mercenary coloring of the part deepen a little. The change is one of tone, not of kind. Bandello's nurse still has no biography, no reported childhood charge beyond Giulietta herself, no dead of her own to mourn, no jokes that a narrator bothers to record. She helps the lovers because helping them is her narrative employment. When the truth comes out after the double death in the Cappelletti monument, Bandello's tale puts her to the question, and the secret she kept is extracted from her as evidence. The detail is telling: in the Italian tradition, the nurse's knowledge matters at the end only as testimony. Her inner life is not a category the novella recognizes.

Pierre Boaistuau's French version, the third of the Histoires tragiques printed at Paris in 1559, carries the tale toward England and wraps it in a heavier moral frame, and the nurse passes through the translation unchanged in stature. Boaistuau expands speeches of the lovers, lingers over the tomb, and addresses his reader with judgments on youthful passion; the go-between remains what she was, a convenience of the plot whose comings and goings the narrator reports without curiosity. The French stage of the journey is instructive precisely because Boaistuau is an expander by temperament: where his original is brief he amplifies, where it is plain he ornaments, and his additions to the tale, the apothecary's scene among them, show a reteller perfectly willing to build new rooms onto the story. He built none for her. The nurse passes through the most expansionist retelling in the chain without gaining a sentence of individuality, which rules out the comfortable theory that she grew by gradual accretion as the tale traveled. She did not grow at all, in any hand, until the hand was a dramatist's. Between Da Porto's Venice and Boaistuau's Paris, the tale grew in length, in rhetoric, and in moral apparatus. The one thing it never grew was a nurse with a past.

This is the baseline against which the play must be measured, and it is worth stating with precision, because loose talk of Shakespeare "developing" his source characters can hide the size of the gap. The Italian and French nurses share four traits: they are unnamed in any way that signifies, they are pastless, they speak only as the plot requires, and they end under judgment. The play's Nurse violates all four. She is named once, in passing, by her master, and the single naming lands like a slip of the tongue in a household that calls her by her office. She has a past dense enough to fill the longest reminiscence in the tragedy. She speaks far beyond the plot's requirements, and the plot waits while she does it. And she ends not under judgment but in grief, discovering the body of the girl she nursed and lamenting in terms no court in the story ever answers. The distance between those two columns is the subject of the InsightCrunch Nurse transformation set out later in this study, and it is a distance that the word "adaptation" is too weak to describe.

Two features of the Italian handling deserve emphasis because the play will reverse both. The first is the balia's relation to the courtship's physical geography. Da Porto's lovers, after the ball, conduct their affair at Giulietta's window and then within her chamber, and every stage of that access runs through the nurse: she is the doorkeeper of the heroine's rooms, the one servant whose presence beside the girl at night excites no remark. The tale exploits her office ruthlessly and notices her never. She is, in effect, part of the architecture, a human section of the wall between the lovers and the household, and walls in Da Porto do not have memories. The second feature is her relation to the marriage itself. In Da Porto she is privy to the ceremony Lorenzo performs, one of the tiny congregation of the secret, and the tale treats her knowledge as a risk to be managed rather than a bond to be honored. When the forced match with the Count of Lodrone drives Giulietta to Lorenzo's potion, the balia's part is again carriage and silence. At no point does any Italian teller ask what the secret costs its keeper, or what she thinks of the hero she admits in the dark, or whether her loyalty has limits. Those questions are the play's entire contribution to her, and they had to wait for a dramatist, because a novella narrator who asked them would have had to stop his tale and write another one inside it. Shakespeare, working in a form that lives by entrances and speeches, simply let the second tale happen.

## Brooke's Nurse: Instrument of a Moral Poem

The Nurse who reached Shakespeare most directly was Arthur Brooke's. Brooke's The Tragical History of Romeus and Juliet, printed by Richard Tottel in 1562, is a poem of some three thousand lines in poulter's measure, Englished from Boaistuau's French, and it remained the fullest English telling of the tale for the rest of the century. Bullough prints it entire as the play's chief source, and any reader who works through it meets a nurse even thinner than Bandello's, because Brooke's poem has a prosecutorial purpose that flattens everyone in it except the moral.

Brooke's prefatory address to the reader announces the purpose without embarrassment. The lovers, he writes, pursued an "unhonest desire" and heeded the counsel of "superstitious" friars and the help of a nurse figure who belongs, in his framing, to the machinery of disobedience: the tale is exhibited as a warning to youth against lust, secrecy, and contempt of parental authority. Inside that frame, the go-between cannot be allowed charm, because charm would acquit her. Brooke's nurse carries the letters and arranges the meetings the plot needs; she is present at the clandestine marriage's edges; she serves Romeus's access to Juliet through the months of their secret union. But the poem gives her no scene of her own, no remembered life, no humor that is not swallowed by the narrator's disapproval. Where Da Porto's balia is neutral machinery, Brooke's is machinery under indictment. The poem's own habits deepen the flattening. Brooke's narrator reserves direct address for Fortune, apostrophized again and again as the tale's presiding power, and for the reader, warned and instructed at every turn. The persons of the tale are spoken about in that commanding voice and rarely allowed to answer it. A nurse in such a poem cannot vet a bridegroom, because vetting requires a judgment the narrator has monopolized; she cannot reminisce, because the past in Brooke belongs to the narrator's moral hindsight, not to any character's memory. The form itself forbids her a self. Verse narrative with a prosecuting narrator is the least hospitable medium a go-between ever worked in, and part of Shakespeare's reinvention is simply jurisdictional: he moved the figure into the one medium, drama, where a servant's voice cannot be confiscated by the telling.

The poem's ending completes the indictment in the form of sentences. Brooke closes his tale by distributing fates with a judge's tidiness: the apothecary who sold the poison is hanged, Friar Laurence withdraws to a hermitage for the remainder of his days, and the Nurse, whose errands made the marriage possible, is banished. A reader who wants the poem's whole treatment of the figure can hold it in one sentence: she runs the lovers' errands for three thousand lines without once being imagined, and then she is expelled from the city for having run them. The full study of [the poem itself, Arthur Brooke's 'Romeus and Juliet'](/2025/09/19/arthur-brooke-romeus-and-juliet-1562/) sets out the poem's structure and Brooke's career in detail; what matters here is the measure of the figure Shakespeare inherited from the version he followed most closely. It was the slightest nurse in the whole chain, and it came wrapped in a moral that forbade enlarging her.

William Painter's prose version, "Rhomeo and Julietta" in the second tome of The Palace of Pleasure (1567), Englished from the same French tradition, offers a useful control. Painter's nurse is the same functionary in prose dress, and Painter's ending repeats the tradition's judgments, banishing her alongside the rest of the catastrophe's instruments. Prose gives her slightly more room to move, since Painter's manner is ampler than Brooke's meter allows, but room to move is all she gets. No English reader before the play opened could have pointed to a passage in either book and said: there is the woman Shakespeare found. She was not there to be found.

One caution belongs in the record before the poem is left, because honest source study requires it. Brooke's preface remarks that he had seen the same argument "lately set forth on stage," better commended than his own version could hope to be, which proves a lost English play on the story existed before 1562. A lost play is a locked room in the chain of transmission: it might, in principle, have enlarged the nurse before Shakespeare did, and no evidence can either credit it or clear it. The responsible verdict is the one the documents support. The lost play left no text, no speech, no name for her, and nothing in the surviving record assigns it a single line of the figure the play of the 1590s presents. Whatever the earlier stage piece contained, the Nurse who exists, Susan, earthquake, husband, vetting, counsel and all, exists in Shakespeare's text and nowhere else, and invention must be credited where the evidence lives. Bullough, who prints Brooke's preface with the poem, treats the stage reference as a curiosity of the tale's transmission; four centuries of scholarship have found nothing behind it to set against the play's sixty lines.

Brooke's handling repays one more measure, because the poem is long and its nurse is not merely brief but positioned. Her appearances cluster where the plot's joints are: the courtship's messages, the marriage's arrangement, the lovers' continuing access to one another during the months the poem allows them. At each joint she is spoken about more than she speaks, a name in the narrator's inventory of the lovers' helpers. The poem's Romeus, on its own showing, keeps her goodwill the way the tradition taught him, with gifts and good words, and the poem registers the transaction without interest in either party to it. Compare the economy of the play's second act, where a single offered coin becomes a small drama of refusal and professional honor, and the difference in kind stands clear. Brooke also gives the figure her one moment of narrative consequence, and it is characteristic: when the truth emerges after the deaths, she is examined as a repository of facts, and what she knows helps the Prince's officers reconstruct the plot. Even her consequence is archival. She matters at Brooke's ending as a filing cabinet matters to an inquiry, and the banishment that follows is the inquiry's housekeeping, not a character's fate.

## What the Play Kept: The Errands Are the Sources' Gift

Honesty about the invention requires precision about the inheritance, because the play's Nurse does perform, nearly item for item, the office the sources assign her. She is Juliet's confidante from the first act, the one person in the Capulet household to whom the secret can be told. She carries Juliet's message to Romeo after the feast and brings back the hour of the wedding. She procures the rope ladder, the "cords" of Act 3, by which Romeo is to climb to the bride's chamber. She goes to Friar Laurence's cell in Act 3 scene 3 with Juliet's ring and pleads the weeping girl's case to the despairing husband. She knows of the marriage, celebrates it, and guards it. Strip the play down to these errands and the residue matches Da Porto's balia and Brooke's functionary almost exactly. The skeleton of the part is borrowed, and borrowed faithfully.

One kept feature deserves separate notice because the play quietly sharpens it: her position at the marriage itself. In Da Porto the balia stands inside the secret's small congregation, aware of Lorenzo's ceremony and woven into its keeping. The play sends Juliet to the cell in Act 2 scene 6 with the Nurse as escort and herdsman of the errand, yet the ceremony itself is staged without her: Friar, bridegroom, bride, and no fourth figure. The exclusion is easy to miss and impossible to unsee once marked. She procures the marriage, delivers the bride to its door, and waits outside the sacrament she made possible, a servant's place at a rite that will govern her mistress's soul. The pattern prefigures the potion plot, from which she will be excluded entirely, and it is the play's own shading of an inherited arrangement: the sources keep her at the marriage's edge because their plots do, while the play makes the edge visible and lets the audience feel its chill.

The borrowing extends to her position in the household economy. In every version she is the servant whose long intimacy with the daughter licenses her to cross thresholds closed to others: she can enter Juliet's chamber unannounced, speak to the mother as a subordinate who is half a relative, and walk the streets on errands that a lady could not perform. The play keeps that license and keeps its social logic. It also keeps, from Brooke and the tradition behind him, her attachment to the marriage plot rather than to either family: she serves the union, not the feud, and her loyalty has no politics in it.

The feast errand of Act 1 scene 5 shows the kept skeleton at its most delicate. Romeo, masked among the guests, must learn who the girl he has just kissed actually is, and in the tale tradition the knowledge reaches him through the household's machinery of service. The play assigns the identification to the Nurse in person, makes her the room's registrar, answering his questions guest by guest before she delivers the blow that Juliet is "the daughter of the rich Capulet" and then summons the girl away to her mother. Brooke narrates the equivalent discovery without ceremony. Shakespeare stages it as a transaction between the lover and the go-between, their first meeting, so that the woman who will carry the marriage is introduced to its hero as the keeper of the house's names. The errand is inherited entire. The introduction, with its quiet establishment of her as the household's memory for faces, is the invention already at work inside the inheritance, preparing the audience to believe that the keeper of the names might also be the keeper of the childhood.

Everything else is the playwright's. The distinction can be put as a rule of thumb that the rest of this study will keep testing. Wherever the Nurse does something, the sources are likely to have sent her to do it. Wherever she remembers something, judges something, jokes about her own body, grieves for her own dead, or gives advice that changes the moral weather of a scene, the sources have nothing to set beside her. Action descends from the tale. Consciousness is manufactured in the playhouse. The [full change ledger of what Shakespeare changed from Brooke](/2025/09/26/what-shakespeare-changed-from-brooke/) records the plot-level alterations; the Nurse's case is stranger than any of them, because here the plot is barely altered at all while the person performing it is new.

Two of the kept errands show the inheritance at its most exact, because the sources stage them almost as the play does. The rope ladder is the first. Da Porto's Romeo reaches Giulietta's chamber by night across the tale's long season of visits, and the tradition equips him with the means of ascent that the play's Act 3 calls the cords: the Nurse procures them, Romeo climbs, and the device passes from novella to poem to play with its function intact. What the play adds is the scene around the device, the Nurse entering with the cords in Act 3 scene 2 into a room where grief has already arrived, so that the ladder of the wedding night becomes, in her hands, an instrument she delivers too late to a girl who believes her husband dead. The second errand is the ring. In Act 3 scene 3 she comes to the Friar's cell as Juliet's ambassador, reports the girl weeping and wringing her hands, and carries back the ring that restores Romeo's will to live. Her aside on the Friar's counsel, "O Lord, I could have stayed here all the night / To hear good counsel. O, what learning is!" (3.3.159-160), is pure play: the tradition's nurse never comments on the clergy. In a dozen such touches the pattern of the whole part repeats itself. The journey is the tale's. The traveler is Shakespeare's.

## The Weaning Speech: A Biography Invented from Nothing

Act 1 scene 3 is where the invention announces itself, and it announces itself as scale. Lady Capulet has come to put the question of marriage to her daughter, and the formal business of the scene is Paris's suit. Before that business can proceed, the Nurse takes possession of the stage with a reminiscence that the Arden text, in Brian Gibbons's edition, allows to run, interruptions included, across some sixty lines. No commission in the plot requires a word of it. Juliet's age could be established in a single line, and her mother nearly establishes it in one. The speech exists because Shakespeare wanted the audience to know this woman from the inside before the tragedy began, and the way he chose to make them know her was to give her a memory more exact, more physical, and more sorrowful than any other character in the play is given.

The memory is of weaning. Juliet, the Nurse recounts, will be fourteen come Lammas Eve at night: "Even or odd, of all days in the year, / Come Lammas Eve at night shall she be fourteen" (1.3.17-18). The date is fixed not by a calendar but by a constellation of losses. "Susan and she (God rest all Christian souls!) / Were of an age. Well, Susan is with God; / She was too good for me" (1.3.19-21). In four lines the play gives its go-between what a whole tradition of novella and poem never gave her: a child of her own, born alongside her charge, and dead. The wit of the speech, and its ache, both grow from that root. She nursed another woman's daughter because her own daughter did not live to be nursed, and the profession the sources treat as a plot device becomes, in the play, a biography with a grave inside it.

### How long is the weaning speech, and how is it built?

Interrupted twice and resumed each time, the reminiscence occupies roughly sixty lines of Act 1 scene 3. It is built as a narrowing spiral: from Juliet's age, to the earthquake year, to the wormwood on the Nurse's breast, to the child's fall beneath the dove-house wall, to her dead husband's joke, which she repeats as its refrain and epitaph.

The dating runs by events, not numbers. "It is since the earthquake now eleven years; / And she was weaned (I never shall forget it) / Of all the days of the year, upon that day" (1.3.23-25). The day itself is rendered in the senses: "For I had then laid wormwood to my dug, / Sitting in the sun under the dove-house wall" (1.3.26-27). Wormwood, bitter on the breast, was the period's instrument of weaning, and the speech makes the child taste the bitterness of separation at the exact moment the earth moves: "When it did taste the wormwood on the nipple / Of my dug and felt it bitter, pretty fool, / To see it tetchy and fall out with the dug!" (1.3.30-32). Then the quake: "Shake, quoth the dove-house; 'twas no need, I trow, / To bid me trudge" (1.3.34-35). Every source in the chain knows that Juliet was nursed. Not one of them knows that she was weaned on an earthquake day, under a dove-house wall, by a woman who had smeared wormwood on her own breast and who connects the child's first bitterness with the trembling of the ground. The passage is not an amplification of anything. It is a world built in thirty lines of prose.

The details repay slow reading because each is a small act of world-building with no source warrant. Wormwood was the documented instrument of weaning in the period's household practice, a bitterness painted on the breast to teach the child refusal, and the speech embeds the custom in a remembered afternoon, sun on a dove-house wall, the child pretty and foolish in her anger at the dug. The earthquake does similar work at the scale of public history: editors have long connected her quake with the tremor that shook London in 1580, within living memory for the play's first audiences, and whether or not the allusion is topical, its function is plain. Her private calendar is pegged to a public event her hearers could check against their own memories, which makes the whole reminiscence feel witnessed rather than invented. Even her idiom of certainty is embodied. She does not say she remembers; she wagers, lays fourteen of her teeth on the fact, swears by her maidenhead, and offers her own decaying body as the archive's seal. A narrator in Da Porto or Brooke certifies events by telling them. She certifies them by staking pieces of herself, and the difference between those two kinds of authority is the difference between the tale and the play.

Her oaths extend the same system across the whole part. She swears by her maidenhead, blesses Susan with a "God rest all Christian souls," prefaces a painful admission with the archaic "to my teen be it spoken," teen being sorrow in the old tongue, and in the crisis stakes her soul itself on the counsel: asked whether she speaks from her heart, she answers "and from my soul too, or else beshrew them both" (3.5.227-228). The progression is a graph of the character's investment, oath rising with stake from body to soul, and it is plotted entirely in materials the sources do not contain. Brooke's narrator swears by nothing; he asserts, and his assertions borrow the authority of print and moral. Bandello's frame gentlemen discuss; Da Porto's narrator reports. Only the play's servant puts her own substance in escrow for her words, which is why her words, alone among the tradition's treatments of the go-between, can be broken with such cost when the soul she staked is forfeited in Act 3. An oath is a small autobiography compressed into a formula, and she is the only nurse in the story's long transmission who is allowed to have one.

The speech's second movement belongs to the dead husband. The toddler Juliet, fallen forward and cut her forehead, is comforted with a joke the Nurse quotes, repeats, and plainly treasures: "Thou wilt fall backward when thou hast more wit" (1.3.42), a prophecy of the marriage bed disguised as a consolation, answered by the child's innocent "Ay" (1.3.46). The bump on the forehead is "as big as a young cockerel's stone" (1.3.53), and the anecdote circles back to its refrain: "Thou wilt fall backward when thou comest to age; / Wilt thou not, Jule?" (1.3.57-58). The comedy is real, and so is the structure beneath it. A husband survives only as a joke told over a child's hurt; a daughter survives only as a date by which another girl's age is counted. The Nurse's memory is the play's first archive, and everything in it is shadowed by the two absences at its center.

The household around her keeps trying to close the archive. "Enough of this; I pray thee, hold thy peace" (1.3.49), says Lady Capulet, and Juliet herself adds, "And stint thou too, I pray thee, Nurse, say I" (1.3.61). The interruptions are part of the invention's meaning. Shakespeare writes a woman whose experience exceeds the errand she is on, and he writes the impatience of her betters as the measure of the excess. Brooke's nurse never had to be silenced, because she never said anything her betters had not commissioned. The play's Nurse must be silenced twice in her first scene, and the silencing fails, since the speech is what every audience carries away from the scene. Barbara Everett's essay on this material, "The Nurse's Story," collected in Young Hamlet (1989), builds its whole case on the observation: the reminiscence is not decoration on the scene but a complete story folded inside it, the record of a life that the marriage negotiation merely interrupts.

The scene around the speech sharpens the point by giving the Nurse a rival archive to defeat. Lady Capulet's own contribution to the marriage business is the famous conceit of Paris as a book: "Read o'er the volume of young Paris' face, / And find delight writ there with beauty's pen" (1.3.82-83). The mother's knowledge of marriage is bibliographic and decorative, a matter of covers, margins, and clasping gold. The Nurse's knowledge is obstetric and calendrical, a matter of breasts, earthquakes, and Lammas-tide. Shakespeare builds the scene as a contest between the two epistemologies and awards it, on any honest hearing, to the servant: the mother's metaphor decorates a husband she has barely considered, while the servant's reminiscence establishes the bride's age to the hour and prices, in its closing joke, exactly what marriage will do to her. The scene's final line belongs to the Nurse as well. After Juliet's dutiful "I'll look to like, if looking liking move" (1.3.97), it is the go-between who caps the negotiation: "Go, girl, seek happy nights to happy days" (1.3.106). Brooke's scene of proposal has no such cap, because Brooke's nurse has no such standing. In the play she closes the scene the mother opened, and the hierarchy of the household quietly inverts inside the comedy.

Everett's reading draws the largest consequence from the speech, and it deserves stating at its full strength. The reminiscence, she argues, is not merely the record of Juliet's infancy but the only complete life the play narrates: a birth shadowed by a death, a childhood dated by a natural disaster, a marriage surviving as a joke, a widowhood spent in another family's service. The lovers get five days and a tomb. The Nurse gets a whole span, compressed into prose and delivered as an interruption. On this account the invention is not local color but counterweight: the tragedy of speed is framed by a comedy of duration, and the frame is a servant's memory. Readers who find the speech excessive are measuring it against the plot's needs, as Lady Capulet does when she calls for peace. Measured against the play's design, the speech is load-bearing, and its absence from every source is the single largest gap between the tale Shakespeare borrowed and the play he wrote.

The weaning speech's bawdy is not ornament either; it is epistemology. The Nurse knows what she knows through the body: through suckling and weaning, through the calendar of a girl's growth from the breast to the marriage bed, through her own marriage remembered in a husband's joke. Her oath at the scene's opening is sworn "by my maidenhead at twelve year old" (1.3.2), a joke that compresses a whole sexual history into a discarded credential. Her reckoning of Juliet's age is exact because she administered the body the age is measured on. When she swears she can "tell her age unto an hour" (1.3.12), the claim is professional: the woman who laid wormwood on her dug keeps the household's true chronology, against a mother who must ask her how old her own daughter is.

No source nurse has a body in this sense. Da Porto's balia has hands and feet, for errands. Bandello's has a purse. Brooke's has a sentence hanging over her. None has a breast, a dead husband, four remaining teeth, or bones that ache on the stairs. The play insists on all of these. "I'll lay fourteen of my teeth," she wagers on Juliet's age, "and yet, to my teen be it spoken, I have but four" (1.3.13-14). The joke is small and the implication is large: her evidence is stored in a decaying body, and she knows the exchange rate between memory and flesh. Her bawdy works the same way. The husband's prophecy over the child's fall, her parting promise in Act 2 scene 5 that Romeo shall "bear the burden soon at night," her cry of "Now comes the wanton blood up in your cheeks" (2.5.73) at Juliet's blush: each joke translates desire into the physical vocabulary of a woman who has managed bodies all her life. The full reading of [the Nurse's speech of bawdy memory and lost Susan](/2022/11/18/the-nurse-bawdy-speech-analysis/) traces that vocabulary line by line; the source point is simpler. Bawdy in the sources, where it exists at all, belongs to narrators. In the play it belongs to a servant woman, and it is the form her knowledge takes.

Her bawdy also does structural work the sources never needed done. The lovers' language, from the first sonnet they share, runs toward the idealized: hands as pilgrims, lips as shrines, love as a religion of light. Against that register the play sets the Nurse's prose idiom, in which a wedding night is a burden borne, a blush is wanton blood, and a bride's future is a fall backward foretold over a bumped forehead. The juxtaposition is the play's honesty about what the sonnets are describing. Brooke's poem cannot produce the effect, because its nurse is a suspect functionary whose speech the narrator keeps at arm's length; there is no second register in the poem, only the narrator's single moral voice and the lovers' reported words. Shakespeare's invention of a bawdy, bodily memory for the go-between gives the tragedy its ground bass, the sound of desire as it is managed by the people who change the sheets.

Her bawdy also keeps the play's time, and this is a function no summary of the sources would lead anyone to expect. The lovers reckon in instants: a glance across a hall, a sonnet's fourteen lines, a night measured against the lark. The Nurse reckons in seasons of the body. A girl is weaned at three, falls and cuts her brow, grows toward the age at which the husband's joke will come true, and the whole sequence is stored in the servant's flesh like rings in a tree. When she jokes, the audience hears the long calendar underneath the short one, and the hearing changes the meaning of the lovers' haste. Brooke's poem, which stretches its action across months, has no need of such an instrument and no such instrument to offer; its narrator owns all the time there is. The play outsources its longest memory to its lowest-ranking major figure, and the outsourcing is the invention's deepest cut. Bawdy, in her mouth, is what a lifetime of attendance on other people's bodies sounds like when it is finally allowed to speak in its own voice.

## Professional Pride: The Go-Between Vets Her Client

The sources send the nurse to Romeo. The play sends her to inspect him. Act 2 scene 4, the street scene with Mercutio, is the clearest case of an inherited errand acquiring an invented interior. The errand itself is pure Da Porto and pure Brooke: carry Juliet's question, receive the wedding's hour, return. What Shakespeare builds on the errand is a professional ordeal and a professional judgment. Mercutio meets her with a barrage of mockery pitched at her body and her errand's smell of procurement: "the bawdy hand of the dial is now upon the prick of noon" (2.4.102-103). She endures it, demands a private word with the discipline of a woman protecting a client's confidence, and then does something no nurse in the tradition does. She vets the bridegroom.

The vetting is specific and it is severe. Romeo must be told that if he means to "lead her into a fool's paradise, as they say, it were a very gross kind of behaviour, as they say; for the gentlewoman is young" (2.4.162-165). The doubled "as they say" is the accent of a woman quoting the world's morality to a gentleman who outranks her, and the content is a threat dressed as a proverb: the go-between reserves the right to judge the transaction she serves. She also refuses, at first, the money Romeo offers, waving off the coin before she accepts it, in a small ceremony of professional self-respect that the tradition never thought to stage. Bandello's nurse is shadowed by her fee. The play's Nurse makes a point of the fee's refusal, and the point reframes the errands around it: she serves Juliet, and Romeo is admitted to the service on probation.

### What does the Nurse's vetting of Romeo add that her errands cannot?

The errands transmit information; the vetting transmits judgment, and judgment is what the sources withhold from her. By warning Romeo against trifling, she becomes the first figure in the play to treat the secret marriage as a moral fact with obligations attached, and her probation of the bridegroom sets the standard her third-act counsel will fail.

Her dignity under the mockery deserves its own sentence of record, because the play writes it into her body before it writes it into her argument. She arrives from the street scene "so vexed that every part about me quivers," as she tells Romeo, and her complaint is precise: Peter, her own man, stood by and let the gentlemen make sport of her, a dereliction she names before she will transact any business. Only when the protest is registered does she proceed to the confidence, the warning, and the fee. The sequence is a tiny etiquette of self-respect performed by a woman the tradition never allowed to insist on anything. Brooke's nurse is mocked by no one because she is noticed by no one; the play's Nurse is mocked at length and answers at length, and the answering is the character.

The pride runs through the smaller errands as well. In Act 2 scene 5 she converts the return journey into a performance of withheld news, teasing the frantic Juliet with aching bones and demands for dinner, because the message is hers to deliver at her pace and the delay is the wage of dignity the household never pays her in coin. In Act 3 scene 2 her garbled report of the street fight, grief arriving as fragments before it arrives as facts, shows the professional channel breaking under a load it was never built for: she has carried love's messages expertly and carries death's message as badly as a loving amateur. None of this is in Brooke, whose nurse reports and fetches under the narrator's eye without a flicker of occupational character. The play's invention is consistent across the scenes: give the function a worker's pride, and the worker becomes a person whose later failure will register as a fall rather than a malfunction.

The scene also gives her the tradition's most eloquent silence to break: her own earlier voicelessness. Because the sources never let her address the hero, the play can mark her arrival as an event in language. Mercutio greets her entrance as a sail on the horizon, her fan and her bulk read as a ship under canvas, and her first business is a malapropism, a request for a "confidence" with Romeo where a conference is meant (2.4). The slip is a class signature that no narrative source could have supplied, since narration corrects its servants' diction by absorbing it. On stage her diction is hers, roguery becomes "ropery" in her indignant protest, "what saucy merchant was this that was so full of his ropery?" (2.4), and the comedy of the errors is the sound of a social inferior refusing to be corrected out of her errand. She rebukes even Peter, her own attendant, for standing idle while Mercutio mocked her, a small flash of household authority inside the public humiliation. Every beat of this is invention performing the same office: the errand from the sources is being carried, visibly, by a woman with dignity to lose, and the audience is being taught to price her dignity before the play spends it.

## The Counsel of Betrayal: The Invention No Source Hands Down

The third act tests the invention to destruction, and the test is also invented. In Act 3 scene 5, with Romeo gone into exile and Capulet raging at his daughter's refusal of Paris, the Nurse first does the bravest thing in her part: she defends Juliet against the father. "God in heaven bless her! / You are to blame, my lord, to rate her so" (3.5.168-169). The household's hierarchy bends for a moment around the old servant's courage, and Capulet's contemptuous dismissal of her is the price she pays for it. Then, alone with Juliet, she gives the counsel that ends their intimacy: marry Paris. "I think it best you married with the County. / O, he's a lovely gentleman! / Romeo's a dishclout to him. An eagle, madam, / Hath not so green, so quick, so fair an eye / As Paris hath" (3.5.218-222). The argument descends from appraisal to arithmetic: "Your first is dead, or 'twere as good he were / As living here and you no use of him" (3.5.225-226). Juliet's test is quiet: "Speakst thou from thy heart?" (3.5.227). The answer seals it: "And from my soul too, / Or else beshrew them both" (3.5.227-228). "Amen!" says Juliet (3.5.229), and when the older woman has gone, the verdict falls: "Ancient damnation! O most wicked fiend! / Is it more sin to wish me thus forsworn, / Or to dispraise my lord with that same tongue / Which she hath praised him with above compare / So many thousand times?" (3.5.235-239). "Go, counsellor; / Thou and my bosom henceforth shall be twain" (3.5.240-241).

### How does the counsel speech borrow the marriage market's own language?

The speech prices both men like goods at a stall: Paris is appraised feature by feature, eye against eye, while Romeo is written down to kitchen linen, a dishclout. Its clinching term is comparative and commercial, one match excelling another as merchandise excels, and its arithmetic treats a banished husband as stock valued at nothing because it cannot be used.

No source hands this scene down. Da Porto's balia, confronted with the Lodrone match and Giulietta's despair, keeps faith with the marriage she served; the recourse she supports is Lorenzo's device. Bandello's nurse remains inside the secret she was paid to keep. Brooke's poem presses Juliet hard, but the pressure comes from her parents and the Friar's counsel answers it; the poem's nurse is never given a speech urging bigamy, because a figure without an inner life cannot change her mind on stage. Even her punishment in Brooke is for loyalty to the plot, for running the errands, not for deserting it at the crisis. Shakespeare's counsel scene is therefore the boldest of the inventions, because it is an invention against the grain of the tradition: he takes the one trait every source does assign her, fidelity to the lovers' service, and writes the moment of its collapse. The play-internal study of [the Nurse's comedy, class, and betrayal](/2022/11/11/the-nurse-character-analysis/) follows what the collapse does to Juliet's solitude in the scenes that follow; the source study's point stands apart from it. Only a character built as richly as this one could betray anyone. A function cannot fall. The fall is the proof of the reinvention, and it is why the scene wounds audiences in a way no summary of Brooke can prepare them for.

The counsel also completes the arc the vetting began. In Act 2 she warned Romeo that trifling with Juliet would be gross behavior, holding the marriage to a standard. In Act 3 she advises Juliet to treat the marriage as waste paper, holding survival to a standard instead. The two speeches are the same professional intelligence at different temperatures: the go-between who judged the bridegroom now judges the market, and finds the bridegroom bankrupt. Critics who soften the counsel into peasant practicality miss its formal daring. Shakespeare gives his invented woman an invented apostasy, and lets Juliet's response, the coldest speech she ever makes, measure exactly how much personhood the betrayal required as its fuel.

The scene's architecture shows the invention working at full stretch, because Shakespeare first raises her to the height from which the counsel drops her. Her rebuke of Capulet is the only speech in the scene that defends Juliet to her father's face, and Capulet's answer reclassifies her on the spot: "Peace, you mumbling fool! / Utter your gravity o'er a gossip's bowl, / For here we need it not" (3.5.172-174). The dismissal is a demotion in public, from intimate to gossip, and it arrives minutes before she demotes herself. No source stages anything like it; Brooke's Capulet has no exchange with the nurse in the crisis at all, and the Italian fathers likewise ignore the balia when the forced match is pressed. The play invents her standing, her courage, and her humiliation in a single sequence, so that the counsel to marry Paris reads not as a servant's reflex but as a great household figure choosing the household over the girl. Juliet's response completes the design. She does not rage at the departing counselor; she uses her, sending her to Lady Capulet with the fiction of a confession errand, and the instrument of the betrayal becomes, in the same breath, the instrument of the betrayed girl's counter-plot. A functionary could be dropped from the story at this point. The play's Nurse has to be managed, deceived, and finally excluded from the potion's secret, because the invention has made her too consequential to ignore.

## The Keeper of the Secret: Knowledge the Sources Never Gave Her to Hold

A subtler measure of the reinvention is epistemic: what the character is allowed to know, and what the story does with her knowledge. In the narrative versions, knowledge belongs to the narrator. Da Porto's narrator knows the marriage, the potion, the tomb, and distributes the facts to his reader in his own order; the balia's share of the secret is a plot condition, not a possession, and the tale never asks how holding it feels or what it obliges her to do. Bandello and Brooke work the same way. The nurse knows because the plot needs a knower inside the Capulet house, the way it needs a ladder against the wall. Knowledge, in the sources, is plumbing like everything else about her.

The play converts her knowledge into a moral biography, in three movements that have no source counterparts. In the first, she is the secret's chosen guardian: Juliet confides in her before any other soul, Romeo must pass her inspection to reach the secret's benefit, and the marriage is, in a real sense, administered from her hands, ladder, messages, ring, and all. In the second movement, she discovers the limit of her knowledge's authority: she knows the marriage is real, knows the husband is banished rather than dead, knows the second match is bigamy, and armed with the full truth she counsels the lie anyway, because knowledge in her keeping was always custodial rather than principled. She held the secret the way she held the household's keys, as an instrument of service, and when the service changed hands her custody changed with it. In the third movement, the secret is withdrawn from her. The potion plot, the play's second great confidence, is constructed entirely outside her knowledge: the Friar and Juliet conspire in a cell she is not invited to enter, and the woman who knew everything first is reduced to laying out clothes for a wedding she believes real. The arc from first knower to last dupe is the character's tragedy in miniature, and it is wholly Shakespeare's construction. Brooke's nurse is examined at the end because she knows things; the play's Nurse is ruined because of how she held what she knew, and then excluded from knowing more. The difference between those two endings for a servant's knowledge is the difference between the tale and the drama in a single figure. The arc has a coda the sources make impossible. Brooke's nurse is examined when the plot is solved, because her knowledge is the plot's property and the state reclaims it. The play's Nurse is never examined at all, though she could testify to the marriage, the ladder, the ring, and the counsel from the inside; the tomb scene's explanations are assembled from the Friar's letter-knowledge and confession, while the other great knower sits outside the record, her archive unopened. Shakespeare withholds the examination because the play has reclassified her knowledge as a life rather than a file, and lives are not evidence. The tradition's most informed servant ends its versions in a courtroom. The play's ends hers in a house that has stopped asking her anything, which is the lonelier sentence and the one no judge pronounces.

## Scene by Scene: Where Each of Her Appearances Comes From

The audit becomes undeniable when it is run appearance by appearance, because the play gives her ten scenes and the sources can account for the business of perhaps half of them. Act 1 scene 3, the weaning reminiscence, has no counterpart of any kind: the proposal interview exists in every version, conducted by the mother, but no version staffs it with a servant who commandeers it. Act 1 scene 5, the feast, restores the inheritance briefly. A go-between must identify the heroine to the hero and withdraw her at the mother's summons, and the tradition supplies the errand; the play adds only the texture of her guest-by-guest knowledge of the room, the servant as the household's living register of names. Act 2 scene 4 divides down the middle, as shown above: errand inherited, vetting invented. Act 2 scene 5 divides the same way: return inherited, delay invented. The pattern through the second act is a perfect alternation, journey by journey, between the tale's property and the playwright's.

The third act breaks the alternation, and the break is the argument. Act 3 scene 2 brings her in with the cords and the news of the street fight, grief arriving in fragments: the tradition knows that word of the killing must reach the heroine, but no narrative version stages the messenger's own collapse as the medium of the news, and the scene's agony depends on her incapacity, a professional channel drowning in what it carries. Act 3 scene 3, the cell, keeps the errand of comfort and adds her commentary on the Friar's learning, noted above. Act 3 scene 5 then departs from the tradition altogether: no source scene corresponds to the counsel, because no source nurse has the standing to give it or the intimacy to make it lethal. The fourth act belongs to the invention almost without residue. In Act 4 scene 2 she is folded into the wedding bustle she herself made impossible, fetching and approving in a household that no longer confides in her. In Act 4 scene 4 she keeps the night watch with the cooks and the logs, teasing Capulet about his younger days as a "mouse-hunt" and receiving, in his "good Angelica," the play's single use of her name. In Act 4 scene 5 she performs the office the whole part has been building toward and finds the body. Narrative sources distribute the discovery among the household's alarm; the play concentrates it in the pair of hands that washed the child, and the concentration is an inventor's decision, not an adapter's inheritance. Five acts, ten appearances, and the ledger reads the same at every line: the tale supplies her entrances' occasions; Shakespeare supplies the woman who walks through them.

Her absences belong in the ledger too, because the play marks them as carefully as her entrances. She is not inside the cell for the wedding of Act 2 scene 6, though her errands built it. She is dismissed from the chamber before the potion soliloquy of Act 4 scene 3, and Juliet names the exclusion as a theatrical fact: the Nurse is called back for a moment, then sent away with the excuse of the wedding clothes, because "my dismal scene I needs must act alone" (4.3.19). No source can mark an absence of this kind, since a figure who exists only when the plot summons her cannot be meaningfully missing. The play's Nurse can be missed, and is: her non-appearance in the scenes where the secret changes hands is the plot's way of registering that the reinvention gave her a presence large enough to leave a shaped hole. A device is either on or off. Only a person can be pointedly elsewhere.

## Expansion or Invention? Bullough, Everett, and the Editors

The complication the evidence forces can now be stated cleanly. Some of the play's Nurse is expansion: the errands exist in the sources, and Shakespeare amplifies them, adding delay, teasing, fatigue, and pride to journeys the novella and the poem make in a clause. Some of her is invention without any seed: Susan, the earthquake, the wormwood, the husband, the bump like a cockerel's stone, the vetting of Romeo, the counsel of bigamy. The honest verdict is that the two operations are not evenly mixed. Expansion accounts for her plot; invention accounts for her meaning. A source scholar counting incidents will find her mostly inherited. A reader counting what the play would lose without her will find her mostly created. The disagreement between those two countings is the real subject of the criticism, and the criticism divides over it in instructive ways.

### What does Bullough print for the Nurse, and what does he leave to summary?

Bullough prints Brooke's poem and Painter's prose entire, gives Da Porto and Bandello in translation, and reduces Boaistuau to summary, so the Nurse a reader can inspect is chiefly the English one. In every text his apparatus offers, her share stays a handful of functional exchanges, and the layout performs the argument: pages for the plot, silence for the go-between.

Geoffrey Bullough's editorial work remains the ground on which all source study of the play stands, and his accounting of the Nurse is faithful to his method: he tracks the tale's transmission, prints the texts, and registers the playwright's departures at the level of incident and structure. In that accounting the Nurse's enlargement appears as one item among the play's enrichments of a borrowed plot, companion to the enlargement of Mercutio. The pairing is itself a Bullough habit worth noticing: the two figures the tradition left emptiest, the hero's witty friend and the heroine's nurse, are yoked in the source scholarship as the twin exhibits of Shakespeare's additive method, and the yoking is just. Both stand in Brooke as names with functions; both walk out of the play as the figures audiences quote. Yet the Nurse's case is the purer of the two, because Mercutio at least owns a death the plot requires and a Queen Mab set piece attached to a court errand, while the Nurse's greatest scene serves no errand at all. Bullough's framework registers both enlargements at the same level, as enrichment. The distinction between them, one figure expanded around a plot function and the other created around a vacancy, is exactly the distinction his incident-counting cannot express and Everett's reading can. The method is not wrong; it is incomplete in a way its own evidence exposes. Bullough's volume demonstrates, page by page, that nothing in the chain of versions prepares the weaning speech, and then has no category under which to file the demonstration. Fidelity measured by incident will always underrate a character whose additions are made of memory rather than events.

Barbara Everett's essay supplies the missing category. Reading the first-act reminiscence as "the Nurse's story," she argues that the speech constitutes a parallel life inside the play: a complete, self-contained narrative of nursing, bereavement, marriage, and aging, carried by a figure the main plot treats as staff. On Everett's account, the additions are not enrichment of the borrowed structure but a second structure, the play's buried autobiography of the servant class, and the Nurse's presence redefines what the tragedy is about: not only the lovers' brief blaze but the long, ordinary, death-haunted labor that raised one of them. Where Bullough counts what the sources gave, Everett weighs what the play knows. The disagreement is genuine, and it is adjudicated here in Everett's favor on the question of function, with Bullough's evidence as the proof. Bullough's own printed chain is the strongest witness that the material on which Everett's reading rests exists nowhere before the play. The source scholar assembled the evidence for a conclusion his framework could not draw; the critic drew it. Invention is the right word for the weaning speech, the vetting, and the counsel, and expansion is the right word only for the errands that carry the plot from door to door.

The editors stand between the two countings, and Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition (2000) shows how. Levenson's introduction registers the enlarged Nurse, with Mercutio, among the play's decisive departures from its sources, and her commentary restores the material culture the speeches assume: wormwood and weaning practice, Lammas-tide reckoning, the dove-house and the quake. An editor must annotate what is on the page, and what is on the page, in the Nurse's case, is a density of lived detail that no source text on Levenson's own showing contains. The textual history adds a quiet corroboration. The fuller Nurse belongs to the play as the second quarto of 1599 printed it, a text its title page advertised as "newly corrected, augmented and amended" against the shorter first quarto of 1597. Augmentation was the play's own contemporary description of its method, and no part of the text better earns the word than the sixty lines in which a go-between remembers an earthquake.

Levenson's commentary also models how an editor should handle the invention's material basis. Her notes on the weaning speech gloss the practices the speech assumes, the wormwood and the weaning age, the Lammas-tide calendar by which a servant dates a child's growth, the dove-house as a working part of a great household's economy, until the annotation itself becomes a small social history of nursing. Nothing in that social history comes from the play's sources, which never pause over the balia's craft; the editor must import it from the records of early modern household life, because the speech she is annotating is dense with a world the tale never described. Gibbons's Arden notes work the same seam, and the cumulative effect of the modern commentary tradition is an unintended tribute to the argument of this study. To explain thirty lines of the play's Nurse, editors have had to reconstruct a profession, a calendar, and a domestic architecture. To explain the whole of Brooke's nurse, a footnote on the ending's banishment suffices. The disparity in annotation is the disparity in invention, measured in the margins of the editions themselves.

The stakes of the adjudication reach beyond one character. Taught Bullough's way, the play becomes a lesson in fidelity: a borrowed plot, faithfully followed, with local enrichments that a student can list. Taught Everett's way, it becomes a lesson in voice: a borrowed plot inhabited by persons the plot never contained, with the student's attention directed to the seams where the sources stop and the speaking starts. The Nurse is the ideal demonstration case for the second lesson precisely because her delta is measurable, errand by errand and row by row, in a way that grander claims about Shakespearean originality rarely permit. A student who has watched a function acquire a dead daughter, a dead husband, a profession's pride, and a soul to forfeit, all within scenes whose occasions a poem of 1562 supplied, has learned the difference between a source and an origin. The tale is the play's source. The Nurse is one of its origins, and origins, in this dramatist's practice, are made rather than found.

Two rows of the table carry the heaviest evidence and deserve to be read slowly. The judgment row records the tradition's most complete silence: across five tellings and four decades, no nurse ever evaluates the hero, because evaluation is a narrator's privilege and she is never the narrator. The play hands her the privilege in a street, over a fan, against a mocking gentleman, and the whole moral architecture of the second act tilts on her verdict. The ending row records the complementary fact. Every source closes her account with a sentence, because every source regards her as an item in a ledger of blame. The play, having made her a person, finds it has no ledger that fits her, and leaves her account open. Between the judgment she was never allowed to give and the judgment she is never made to receive, the whole reinvention stands measured.

Read down the table's center and the complication resolves itself. Shakespeare expanded the middle rows, the errands and the custody, from genuine source material. He invented the rows that carry weight: family, speech, body, judgment, apostasy. The transformation is not a source character developed. It is a source vacancy filled, and filled so completely that four centuries of readers have mistaken the filling for the original furniture of the tale.

A second disagreement runs beneath the first and should be adjudicated with it. Is the counsel of Act 3 in character, or is it a plot mechanism wearing the character's clothes? A plot accounting, of the kind Bullough's method encourages, can treat the counsel as machinery: Juliet must be isolated before the potion, so the confidante is switched off. Everett's way of reading refuses the reduction, and the text supports the refusal. The woman who vetted Romeo in Act 2 judged him by a standard of honest dealing; the woman of Act 3 judges Paris by a standard of usable goods. The standards are both hers, both professional, both drawn from a servant's knowledge of what the world permits, and the distance between them is exactly the distance fear travels in a household where a raging master has just called her a mumbling fool. The counsel is the vetting with its courage gone, and only an invented interior could make the continuity visible. Gibbons's Arden commentary, glossing the scene, notes how the speech's comparisons reach back to her earlier praise of Romeo, praise Juliet herself throws back at her in the "thousand times" of the dismissal speech. The echo is the playwright's signature on the invention: the betrayal is composed out of the character's own earlier materials, which is to say out of materials that exist nowhere outside the play.

## What a Reader of Brooke Brought to the Playhouse

The reinvention was not a secret kept from its first audiences; it was performed in front of readers who knew the tale. Brooke's poem had been reprinted in 1587, within a decade of the play, and Painter's prose version circulated alongside it, so the story Shakespeare staged was among the most familiar narratives in the bookshops. A spectator who had read the poem came to the theatre knowing exactly what a nurse in this story was: a few errands, a moral shadow, a banishment at the end. The play's first act would have instructed that spectator, within twenty minutes, that the familiar furniture had been rebuilt. The proposal scene in Brooke is the mother's business, briefly told. On stage it became the servant's memoir, with the mother reduced to calling for peace. The shock of scale is a designed effect, and it depends on the audience's prior knowledge of the slightness being replaced.

This matters for how the invention should be described. Adaptation usually names a negotiation between a text and its source conducted for an audience that may not know the original. Here the negotiation was public. Shakespeare was not quietly improving a book his audience had forgotten; he was conspicuously enlarging the least regarded figure in a story his audience knew by heart, and the enlargement announced the play's whole method before the lovers had exchanged a sonnet's worth of dialogue. The Nurse is the play's manifesto delivered first: what this dramatist does to a tale is not to change its events but to repopulate them, and the repopulation begins at the bottom of the household, with the character every previous teller had agreed to leave empty. Critics who treat the weaning speech as a charming delay before the real play starts have the sequence backwards. The speech is the play's thesis statement about its own sources, spoken by the evidence.

## Her Prose: The Medium of the Invention

One structural fact explains why the reinvention had to wait for a playwright, and the fact is prose. In the novella and the poem, every voice is the narrator's voice at one remove: Da Porto reports the balia's errands in his own periods, Bandello in his, Brooke in the level measure of poulter's measure, where a nurse and a prince are ground to the same meter. Narrative can lend a servant the narrator's eloquence or deny her speech, but it cannot give her a diction of her own without breaking its frame. Drama can, and Shakespeare's instrument for the gift is prose. The Nurse's great scenes run in prose while the household around her speaks verse, and the prose is not a deficiency but a signature: the medium of memory, appetite, and work, set against the verse of ceremony and desire. Her sentences accumulate the way errands accumulate, by addition rather than architecture, clauses piling on clauses as one recollection fetches the next, until Lady Capulet's call for peace acknowledges what the audience already knows, that this voice will not be concluded from outside.

The prose also certifies the invention's source status, because prose of this density cannot be a translation effect. Brooke's poem, Englished from Boaistuau, transmits plot, names, and moral; it transmits no idiom for its nurse, since the French had none to transmit. When the play's Nurse swears by her maidenhead, wagers her four teeth, quotes her husband, and prices Paris against a dishclout, the language is doing something the entire chain of transmission never attempted: it is individuating a servant by the way she talks. Later dramatists learned the trick from this play and used it everywhere, which is why the figure can feel traditional. The feeling is an optical illusion produced by her descendants. In the record Bullough assembled, she has no ancestors at all.

## The Ending She Is Denied: Sentence in the Sources, Silence in the Play

The tradition ends by judging her; the play ends by forgetting her, and the difference is the reinvention's final proof. Brooke's poem closes its accounts in a formal distribution of fates, and the banished nurse takes her place in the ledger beside the hanged apothecary and the hermit friar. Painter repeats the pattern, Da Porto's closing sentences exile her, and Bandello's tale extracts her testimony before disposing of her. In every version the go-between is an item in the catastrophe's bookkeeping, present at the audit whether she wills it or not. Shakespeare's fifth act holds an audit of its own, the Prince's reconstruction over the bodies, and the Nurse is not in it. She is not summoned, not sentenced, not named. The play simply stops spending her, after the wedding bustle of Act 4 scene 4, where Capulet in passing gives her the name the tradition never gave her, "good Angelica," and the discovery of Act 4 scene 5, where she finds the seeming corpse and delivers the lament of the woeful day.

Her last great utterance belongs to the discovery, and it shows the invention spending its capital in the formal register as confidently as in the comic one. "O woe! O woeful, woeful, woeful day! / Most lamentable day, most woeful day / That ever, ever I did yet behold!" (4.5.49-51). The lament is built from repetition the way her reminiscence was built from accumulation, the same voice under the opposite weather, and it lands with a weight the sources' discovery scenes cannot approach, because in them the grief is narrated by men who never nursed the girl. The Prince's closing words in the tomb promise a general audit, "Some shall be pardoned, and some punished" (5.3.307), and the play keeps the promise for friars, princes, and fathers. She is the one major agent the audit never reaches.

The silence is not carelessness; it is the one ending consistent with what she has become. A function can be sentenced, because a sentence is a function of the plot. A person whose inner life the play has spent three acts building cannot be closed by a line in the Prince's ledger without shrinking her back into the device she escaped, and Shakespeare declines to shrink her. She exits the play the way she exits Juliet's confidence in Act 3, by withdrawal rather than punishment, present in the audience's memory and absent from the record. Brooke's nurse is banished from Verona. Shakespeare's is banished from the plot, and the second exile is the sadder and the more truthful one.

## The Measure of the Part: Her Share of Voice

A final comparison can be made in bulk. The tragedy is crowded with servants, servingmen with their trenchers and napkins, Peter with his musicians, Gregory and Sampson with their thumb-biting, and their combined speech would not fill the weaning reminiscence. The play's allocation of voice inverts the household's allocation of rank with a completeness no source attempts: the mistress of the house speaks less, and less memorably, than her daughter's nurse, and the father who owns the house must shout to compete with her. Narrative sources cannot produce this inversion even by accident, because narration rations voice by importance and she is not important. Drama rations voice by presence, and Shakespeare kept writing her presence larger. Editors measuring the part for the stage have always known what the source record proves from the other side: this is a principal's allocation carried by a servant's name, the largest prose share in the play, built on a foundation of a few reported errands. Bulk is not quality, but in this case bulk is evidence, since every line of the surplus had to be written against a tradition that supplied nothing to copy. The comparison inside the play's own prose makes the choice visible. Shakespeare writes functional prose for the servingmen of Act 1 and Act 4, prose of trenchers, napkins, logs, and joint-stools, good for a laugh and a cleared stage; he writes brilliant prose for Mercutio, prose as display. For the Nurse he writes a third kind, prose as storage, in which a household's whole unwritten history is kept. The three kinds coexist in one play, distributed by the dramatist's judgment of what each figure is for, and only one of them had to be invented against the grain of every book he was adapting. The servingmen's prose descends from a stage tradition of clownage; Mercutio's from the wit-combat of the schools and the court. Hers descends from nothing in the library. It is the sound of a class of women the literature of the story had used for a century without once permitting to describe their own lives, and the permission, when it comes, produces the most quoted servant's speech of the age.

## What the Reinvented Nurse Contributes

What, then, does the reinvention buy the tragedy? First, a past. The feud has no history the play bothers to explain; its origins are a blank the Prologue fills with the phrase "ancient grudge" and leaves. The Nurse supplies the missing dimension from below. Her eleven years since the earthquake, her daughter dead in infancy, her husband dead after, her charge grown from the breast to the brink of marriage: this is the only fully narrated stretch of lived time in the play, and it teaches the audience how to feel the speed of the main action. The lovers live their whole story in days. The Nurse's memory is the instrument that makes those days legible as a catastrophe, because she alone can set them against the long, slow calendar of an ordinary life. The contrast is sharpened by the play's treatment of every other past. Capulet's history surfaces in Act 1 scene 5 as ballroom anecdote, a cousinly dispute about how many years have passed since they last wore masks, charming and weightless. The feud's past never surfaces at all. Only the servant's past is given the dignity of narrative, with dates, places, weather of the earth, and named dead, and the allocation is a judgment in itself: the play entrusts its memory to the figure its sources entrusted with nothing, as if to say that a household's true archive is kept by the woman who did its bodily work. No reader of Da Porto or Brooke could anticipate the distribution, because in the tale the past belongs to narrators and is spent on moralizing. In the play it belongs to the Nurse and is spent on love, grief, and a joke about falling down.

Second, the reinvention buys the comedy its dignity. The laughs in Act 1 scene 3 and Act 2 are not relief inserted into a tragedy; they are knowledge the tragedy needs, spoken in the only idiom that holds it. A reader assembling the comparison for study, whether in an essay draft or a set of seminar slides laid out with [the ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) so the two columns of the transformation stay side by side, can watch the mechanism work: every comic row in the table's right column does double duty as pathos in storage. The husband's joke is funny in Act 1 and desolating by Act 5, because the audience has learned whose mouth the play's comfort comes from and what it cost her. Brooke's poem, sentencing its nurse, can spend none of this capital. It never made the deposit.

### What does the Nurse's lament in Act 4 do that the sources' discovery scenes do not?

The sources report the discovery of the corpse as one more turn of the plot. The play gives the discovery to the woman who weaned the girl, and her refrain of woeful days makes it a bereavement with a biography behind it: the grief lands because Act 1 established who is grieving and what she has buried.

Third among these gifts, the reinvention gives the tragedy a working chorus. The tale tradition locates commentary in its narrators: Da Porto's frame voice, Bandello's dedications, Brooke's prosecuting apostrophes to Fortune. The play abolishes the narrator and must relocate the commentary somewhere, and while the sonnet Chorus takes the formal share of it at the act doors, the Nurse takes the human share inside the scenes. She evaluates the bridegroom, prices the suitors, dates the action against a longer calendar, mourns the dead before the principals can, and tells the audience, in the idiom of the servants' hall, what the verse of the great is too elevated to say. A chorus in the Greek sense stands outside the action and knows more than the actors; the play's Nurse stands inside the action and knows it from underneath, which is the only vantage the sources never occupied. That the commentary function of five narrative voices should condense, in the drama, into a widowed servant with four teeth is the reinvention's most elegant economy.

Fourth, the reinvention buys Juliet her solitude, and with it the tragedy's second half. The counsel scene works as the hinge of the play only because the intimacy it breaks was built from invented material. Had the Nurse remained Brooke's functionary, Juliet's turn to the Friar would be a change of messenger. Because the Nurse is Susan's mother, the keeper of the dove-house memory, the vetted ally of Act 2, her defection leaves Juliet alone in a way no parental rage could accomplish, and the potion plot begins in that loneliness. The sources hand Shakespeare a girl who consults a friar. The play he wrote gives us a girl abandoned by the woman who raised her, consulting a friar because no one else is left.

The reinvention also buys the audience its hindsight, and hindsight is the tragedy's cruelest instrument. Nobody leaves Act 1 scene 3 expecting the weaning speech to matter to the ending; it reads as comic surplus, the play idling in a servant's memory while the marriage plot waits. The later acts collect on it with interest. The woman who dated a childhood by an earthquake is the woman who cannot read a street fight's news in order; the voice that priced Juliet's whole past is the voice that prices her future against a dishclout; the hands that laid wormwood on a breast are the hands that draw the curtains of the seeming corpse. Each return re-reads the first scene darker, and the re-reading is available only because the first scene was invented at such length. Brooke's audience loses nothing at the poem's ending that it was given at the poem's beginning, because the poem gave its nurse nothing to lose. The play's design is the opposite: it makes a deposit of personhood in Act 1 and bankrupts it in Act 3, and the audience's grief at the bankruptcy is the interest on Shakespeare's invention.

The series thesis finds its cleanest proof here. The cliché says the Nurse is part of the story, as given and as old as the tale itself. The record says she is the tale's most conspicuous absence and the play's most instructive presence: a figure the tradition carried for a century as plumbing, reinvented in a single prose speech and its consequences into the tragedy's memory, its ground bass of bodily knowledge, and its measure of what betrayal costs. Shakespeare's method with his sources is often described as transformation, and the word can blur into compliment. In the Nurse's case it can be audited line by line, and the audit returns a precise figure: the errands are inherited, the woman is his.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Nurse transformation, and what does it set side by side?

The InsightCrunch Nurse transformation is the before-and-after table in this study, setting the nurse of the source tradition against the Nurse of the play across identity, family, speech, body, judgment, counsel, and ending. Its pattern is stark: the left column is a job description that Da Porto, Bandello, Boaistuau, and Brooke all recognize, while the right column is a biography none of them contains. The table's use is diagnostic. Any element a reader assumes belongs to the old tale can be checked against its rows, and most of what audiences love in the character falls on the play's side of the line.

### Q: Does any version before the play give the nurse a dead child of her own?

No version before the play gives her a child at all. Da Porto's balia, Bandello's go-between, Boaistuau's functionary, and Brooke's instrument are all defined entirely by their service to the heroine; no teller assigns them offspring, living or dead. Susan, born alongside Juliet and dead in infancy, is Shakespeare's gift to the character, and the whole emotional economy of the play's Nurse depends on it. Her devotion to the Capulet daughter, her authority over the girl's childhood, and the desolation inside her comedy all read differently once the audience knows she is lavishing a dead child's portion of care on the child who survived. The invention also explains the play's strangest household fact: that Juliet's emotional parent is her servant, and that the tragedy of her abandonment in Act 3 is therefore a bereavement and not merely a tactical loss.

### Q: How does the nurse enter Da Porto's novella, and what is she first asked to do?

She enters as Giulietta's balia, the nurse who suckled her and now holds her confidence inside the Cappelletti household, and her first work is carriage: to pass word between the heroine and Romeo after their meeting and to manage the secret traffic of the courtship. Da Porto gives her employment without introduction, because the novella's interest is the lovers and she is their instrument. There is no scene of her arrival, no account of her past, no speech worth the narrator's quotation. She is simply there when a message needs carrying, which is precisely the condition the play's first-act reminiscence abolishes.

### Q: Why does no Italian version give the nurse a name?

The Italian versions leave her nameless because the novella form allocates identity by rank and desire: lovers, parents, friars, and counts receive names, while servants receive functions. A balia in Da Porto or Bandello is a piece of household equipment with a voice for errands, and naming her would imply an interior the tale has no intention of opening. The play's handling exposes the convention by breaking it almost casually: Capulet calls her Angelica once, in passing, in Act 4 scene 4, and the single name in five acts measures how completely the drama has outgrown the novella's economy of persons.

### Q: Which of the Nurse's errands does the play keep from Brooke's poem in their original outline?

The play keeps the errand skeleton nearly whole: the carrying of Juliet's message to the hero and the return with the wedding's hour, the procurement of the rope ladder for the wedding night, the keeping of the marriage secret inside the household, and the journey to the Friar's cell on the despairing husband's behalf. Brooke's nurse performs the same office under his narrator's disapproval. What the play changes is never the errand but the errand-runner: delay, teasing, fatigue, professional pride, and private judgment are all loaded onto journeys that the poem accomplishes in a clause or two of reported motion.

### Q: Does any source mention the earthquake by which the play's Nurse dates the weaning?

No source mentions an earthquake, a weaning day, or any event in Juliet's infancy. Da Porto, Bandello, Boaistuau, Brooke, and Painter all begin their interest in the heroine at marriageable age, and her nursing survives in them only as the fact that explains the balia's intimacy. The earthquake of Act 1 scene 3, eleven years before the action, with the wormwood on the Nurse's breast and the dove-house wall in the sun, is an invention entire. Its absence from the chain is one of the cleanest proofs in source study that the play's memory of Juliet's childhood was manufactured in the playhouse rather than inherited from the tale. The detail has the further virtue of being checkable by any reader: open Bullough's volume at any version in the chain and search it for an earthquake, and the only tremor in the tradition is the one in the play.

### Q: How does Brooke's narrator judge the nurse's part in forwarding the secret marriage?

Brooke's narrator judges it as complicity in disobedience. The poem's preface frames the tale as a warning against unhonest desire, contempt of parents, and reliance on superstitious counselors, and the nurse who forwards the secret marriage belongs, in that framing, to the machinery of the sin rather than to its victims. Her errands are narrated under disapproval throughout, and the poem closes the account by sentencing her. The contrast with the play is total: where Brooke's voice prosecutes the go-between, Shakespeare's scenes invite the audience to love her first and judge her only at the moment Juliet does.

### Q: Does the nurse in any source warn the hero against trifling with the heroine?

No source gives her a warning to deliver. In Da Porto, Bandello, Boaistuau, and Brooke she conveys the heroine's messages and the hero's answers without ever addressing the hero on his intentions; the moral commentary belongs to narrators, not to her. The play's Act 2 scene 4 vetting, in which she tells Romeo that leading Juliet into a fool's paradise would be gross behavior, is an invention, and a consequential one. It converts the go-between into a judge of the transaction she serves, and it sets the standard of loyalty against which her third-act counsel is later measured by Juliet and found to have collapsed.

### Q: Is the Nurse's teasing delay over the news in Act 2 an invention or an inheritance?

The errand is inherited and the delay is invented. Every version sends the nurse back from the hero with the wedding arranged, and every version has her deliver the news. None has her withhold it for the length of a scene while the heroine begs, offering aching bones and dinner as her excuses and converting the message into a performance of professional dignity. Shakespeare builds the delay out of the servant's social position: news is the one asset she controls outright, and she spends it slowly. The scene's comedy is the invention; only the journey belongs to the tale.

### Q: What does Painter's prose nurse show a reader that Brooke's verse nurse does not?

Painter's prose shows the functionary at slightly greater length and in a slightly warmer medium: The Palace of Pleasure amplifies its French original in flowing narrative, and the nurse's comings and goings gain room and circumstantial ease that Brooke's poulter's measure, yoked to a prosecuting narrator, denies her. What Painter cannot show is a person. His nurse remains unnamed, pastless, and unjudging, and his ending repeats the tradition's sentence of banishment. The comparison is valuable precisely because Painter had every advantage of medium over Brooke and still produced the same vacancy the play would fill.

### Q: How much of the Nurse's first-act speech is expansion, and how much is invention outright?

All of it is invention outright, because expansion requires a seed and the sources contain none. No version before the play gives the nurse a reminiscence, a dead child, a husband, a weaning, an earthquake, or a single anecdote about Juliet's childhood; there is nothing for Shakespeare to expand. The first-act speech is the purest case in the character's dossier, and it is the right test for the whole question of her reinvention. Where the errands are concerned, expansion is the honest word. Where the speech is concerned, only creation describes the act, and the speech is what the character is remembered for.

### Q: Does the Nurse's husband exist anywhere in the sources?

He exists nowhere in the sources. No teller before the play gives the go-between a husband, a marriage, or a household of her own; she is a function suspended in the heroine's family with no family behind her. The play's husband exists only inside his wife's quotation of him, comforting the fallen toddler Juliet with the joke about falling backward when she has more wit, and the Nurse's repetition of the joke across the weaning speech turns a dead man into the speech's refrain. His absence from the entire tradition is a small, perfect measure of the invention: Shakespeare gave his go-between not just a past but a marriage inside it.

### Q: How long does the secret marriage run in Da Porto, and what part does the nurse play in sustaining it?

Da Porto's marriage runs for a season rather than a night: after Frate Lorenzo weds them, Romeo visits Giulietta repeatedly over an extended period before the killing of her cousin turns the tale, and the balia sustains the arrangement throughout. She is its infrastructure, admitting the husband, guarding the secret, and keeping the household's ordinary face intact. The play compresses the season into a single wedding night and a dawn parting, yet keeps her in the same office, procuring the cords for the one ascent it allows. The contrast shows the invention's method at its most economical: same function, same woman in outline, and yet the play's version aches with a biography the novella never wrote. Da Porto's season of visits also exposes what the play's compression costs her and buys her: with months to fill, a novella nurse might have accumulated incident, yet accumulates none, while the play's nurse, given four days, accumulates a lifetime.

### Q: What does Bullough's collection of the sources let a reader measure about the Nurse?

Bullough's first volume of Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare prints Brooke and Painter entire and gives Da Porto and Bandello in translation, and that assembly lets a reader measure the Nurse's growth with unusual exactness. Page by page, the collection shows the go-between remaining a handful of functional exchanges across a century of retelling, and then shows the play's figure exceeding the whole file at her first entrance. Bullough's own method counts departures at the level of incident, which underrates a character built from memory rather than events, but his edition supplies the evidence on which the stronger conclusion rests: the raw material for the play's Nurse is simply not in the book that contains everything else.

### Q: Which of the Nurse's set pieces have no counterpart in any source?

Four set pieces stand without any counterpart: the weaning reminiscence of Act 1 scene 3, with Susan, the earthquake, and the dove-house wall; the street vetting of Romeo in Act 2 scene 4, warning him against a fool's paradise; the teasing withholding of the news through Act 2 scene 5, built from her aching bones and professional pride; and the counsel of Act 3 scene 5 urging Juliet to marry Paris, which reverses the fidelity every source assigns her. The pattern across the four is the article's thesis in miniature. The play's Nurse is most herself exactly where the tradition is most silent, and her most famous scenes are a catalogue of the sources' omissions.

### Q: What happens to the nurse figure as the tale moves from prose novella to verse poem to stage play?

In the prose novella she is neutral machinery, an instrument the narrator reports without curiosity. The novella's economy explains the neutrality: a tale told in summary and scene mixed can always step around its servants, reporting that a message was delivered without pausing over the deliverer, and the deliverer costs the narrative nothing to ignore. In Brooke's verse poem she becomes machinery under indictment, her errands prosecuted by a moralizing narrator and her person sentenced at the close. On the stage she becomes a person: given prose of her own, a past, a body, professional pride, and a fall. The sequence is not a steady growth but a flat line followed by a leap, and the leap coincides with the change of medium, because drama must embody what narrative can merely report. A narrator can say a message was carried. A play must put a woman on the stage to carry it, and Shakespeare's woman refused to stay a device.

### Q: Why is Brooke's poem still called the play's chief source when its nurse is so slight?

Because chief source describes the plot, and the plot is Brooke's gift nearly entire: the sequence of feast, balcony, secret marriage, street fight, exile, forced match, potion, and tomb reaches Shakespeare through the poem more fully than through any other single text. The Nurse's slightness in Brooke is no objection to the title; it is the title's sharpest illustration. Source study measures what descends, and what descends from Brooke is the story's architecture. The Nurse shows the complementary truth, that a play's most beloved person can descend from nothing in its chief source at all, and both truths belong in any honest account of how the tragedy was made.

### Q: Does any earlier teller let the nurse speak at length in her own voice?

No earlier teller lets her speak at length, and most let her barely speak at all. Da Porto and Bandello report her errands in the narrator's voice, Boaistuau absorbs her into his moralized narration, and Brooke's poem gives her brief functional exchanges inside a narrative that judges her. Where the versions do quote her, the quotations are errands in verbal form, a message repeated, a consent conveyed, words whose content the plot could have carried in summary without loss. Sustained direct speech, in a voice with its own idiom, memory, and comic timing, begins with the play. The difference is not merely quantity. A reported functionary belongs to the narrator who reports her; a speaking woman belongs to herself, and the whole reinvention follows from that transfer of ownership.

### Q: How can an essay use the Nurse transformation to argue Shakespeare's method with his sources?

An essay can use the table's two columns as its structure: begin with the inherited errands, where fidelity to Brooke and the tradition is nearly complete, and then move through the rows with no left-hand counterpart, family, speech, body, judgment, and counsel, arguing that Shakespeare's originality concentrates where his sources are silent. The Nurse is the ideal exhibit because the measurement is clean: the same plot office exists on both sides of the comparison, so the delta cannot be confused with changes of story. Close with the adjudication this study reaches, that expansion names the errands while invention names the woman, and the essay has a thesis the evidence will carry.

### Q: Why does the play give its Nurse a whole childhood for Juliet when the sources begin with a marriageable girl?

The sources begin with a marriageable girl because their interest is the marriage plot, and a heroine's childhood is machinery they do not need. The play gives its Nurse Juliet's whole childhood because tragedy needs weight that plot cannot supply: an audience that has heard the weaning, the earthquake, the fall beneath the dove-house wall, and the dead husband's joke has watched Juliet be raised, and therefore knows the price of her destruction in units of lived time. The childhood also belongs to the Nurse rather than the mother for a reason the invention makes felt. The woman who performed the labor of the child's body is the one who holds its archive, and the play honors the labor by giving her the archive entire. The contrast can be put in one sentence for an essay's close: open any earlier version at its beginning and the heroine is already a marriageable girl whose past fills a clause, while the play opens its heroine's past through sixty lines of a servant's remembering, and somebody in the fiction finally knows what the girl cost to raise.
