## The Food Safety Modernization Act and the Shift From Reaction to Prevention

The operative question behind the Food Safety Modernization Act was whether federal food law should wait for contaminated food to reach consumers and cause illness before the full weight of government action applied, or whether the law should require the firms that manufacture, process, pack, and hold food to identify hazards in advance and control those hazards before distribution. That question shaped every major choice in the statute. The older framework had treated adulteration largely as a condition to be proved after food had entered commerce. The new framework treated prevention as a legal obligation in its own right, placed on the regulated facility rather than left to inspection, seizure, and recall after a failure had occurred.

The formal identity of the measure is exact. The FDA Food Safety Modernization Act, Public Law 111-353, was signed by President Barack Obama on January 4, 2011. The statute amended the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938, the central federal law governing food, drugs, and cosmetics, and it worked within that older structure rather than replacing the structure outright. The amendment changed the premise of federal food regulation under the 1938 act. Where the earlier law had supplied tools for responding to food that was already adulterated, the 2011 act added a system of mandated prevention, built around hazard analysis, preventive controls, produce standards, import verification, and a recall authority that no longer depended entirely on voluntary cooperation.

The distinction between response and prevention sounds abstract until the mechanics of the two systems are placed side by side. A reactive system asks whether a particular article of food is adulterated and whether the government can assemble the evidence and the legal process needed to remove that article from the market. A preventive system asks an earlier question. A preventive system asks what hazards are known or reasonably foreseeable in a facility, what controls will reduce or eliminate those hazards, how the controls will be monitored, what corrective action will follow when a control fails, and how the facility will verify that the whole arrangement works. The Food Safety Modernization Act moved the second set of questions to the center of federal food law.

### What problem was the Food Safety Modernization Act meant to solve?

The statute was meant to solve a structural gap in federal food law. Contaminated food could move through processing and distribution without any general legal duty to identify hazards in advance, and federal action ordinarily followed illness or a finding that food already in commerce was adulterated.

The gap was structural rather than episodic. A single outbreak can be explained by a single failure at a single facility, and a legal system built only for response can appear adequate when failures remain confined to the firm that produced the contaminated article. The record that accumulated before enactment pointed in a different direction. Contamination in an ingredient could travel through hundreds of downstream products. A hazard in fresh produce could reach consumers with no processing step capable of removing the hazard. A pathogen in shell eggs could spread through a distribution system that crossed state lines long before investigators traced illnesses back to a source. In each pattern, the decisive events occurred before any inspector found a violation and before any court considered a seizure or an injunction. Prevention addressed the point in the chain where the harm was set in motion.

The reactive character of the 1938 framework defined the limits that the new statute had to overcome. Under that framework, the Food and Drug Administration acted against food that was already adulterated. The available instruments reflected that posture. Seizure took goods out of commerce. An injunction ordered a firm to stop a practice or to refrain from distribution. Criminal referral punished conduct after the conduct had occurred. Current good manufacturing practice regulations set baseline conditions for facilities, and those regulations supplied an important floor for sanitation and process control. None of those instruments, standing alone, amounted to a general statutory duty requiring each covered facility to analyze the hazards particular to its own operation and to build a written system of controls around the hazards that the analysis found. The law intervened with force, but the force arrived late in the sequence that produced illness.

Limited exceptions proved the shape of the general rule. Hazard analysis and critical control point systems, known by the acronym HACCP, had entered federal food regulation in defined categories before the 2011 act. Seafood regulation adopted a HACCP rule in 1995, and juice regulation adopted a HACCP rule in 2001. The meat and poultry system administered by the United States Department of Agriculture rested on a separate inspection structure. Outside the seafood and juice HACCP rules and the meat and poultry system run by the Department of Agriculture, no general statutory duty required a food facility to analyze its own hazards and control those hazards in advance. A facility producing a food outside those categories could operate under general manufacturing practice rules and under the prohibition on adulterated food without a freestanding legal obligation to construct the kind of forward looking hazard system that the 2011 act later imposed.

The 1938 act had been amended many times before the food safety legislation arrived. Congress returned to the same statute whenever a gap in federal authority over a regulated product became impossible to ignore, and each major amendment redefined what the Food and Drug Administration had to prove and when the agency could act. A reader tracing [the earlier amendment of the same statute that reshaped federal drug approval](/2018/06/01/kefauver-harris-amendments-1962/) encounters the same legislative habit applied to a different product category. The food safety act followed that habit. The statute did not discard the 1938 foundation or the adulteration standard built on that foundation. The statute added a preventive layer to a law whose enforcement tools had developed around proof of a violation in a specific article of food. The facility became responsible for producing the analysis, the plan, and the records. The regulator became responsible for judging whether that preventive system satisfied the law.

Fragmentation compounded the problem of late intervention, because no single agency governed the whole food supply and the boundary between agencies followed the commodity rather than the risk. The Food Safety and Inspection Service, known as FSIS, operated inside the Department of Agriculture and regulated meat under the Federal Meat Inspection Act, poultry under the Poultry Products Inspection Act, processed egg products under the Egg Products Inspection Act, and catfish under the authority assigned to that service. The Food and Drug Administration regulated shell eggs and the rest of the food supply. The contrast inside a single food category captured the logic of the whole division. Processed egg products fell to FSIS. Shell eggs fell to the Food and Drug Administration. The pathogen did not observe the boundary. The distribution chain did not observe the boundary. Only the legal authority observed the boundary.

The scale on each side of that boundary was uneven. By the account of the Congressional Research Service, the Food and Drug Administration held responsibility for roughly 80 percent of food spending, against roughly 20 percent for products regulated by the Department of Agriculture. The percentages describe spending rather than risk, and the distinction deserves emphasis. A category accounting for a smaller share of spending can carry severe hazards, and a category accounting for a larger share can contain many low risk products. The figures nevertheless explain why the preventive gap in the part of the system governed by the Food and Drug Administration mattered at national scale. The agency with the broader portfolio, covering produce, seafood, processed foods, shell eggs, and the long remainder of the food supply, operated under the framework that lacked a general preventive mandate. The system described in [the separate account of federal rulemaking for the inspection programs on the agriculture side of the boundary](/2021/02/01/usda-food-safety-rulemaking/) rested on a different statutory base, and the 2011 act left that division in place rather than consolidating the agencies into a single food safety authority.

Oversight institutions had named the fragmentation problem in formal terms well before Congress completed the legislation. The Government Accountability Office placed federal oversight of food safety on the High-Risk List in 2007, and the food safety system remained on that list after the designation. Placement on the list did not identify a single defective rule or a single failing program. Placement identified a system level condition. Responsibility was divided across agencies, and the two largest presences in the system, the Food and Drug Administration and FSIS, operated under different statutes and different inspection traditions. A designation of that kind supplied Congress with an institutional finding rather than an anecdote. The architecture of oversight had been judged a source of risk in itself, and the judgment came from the auditing arm of Congress rather than from advocates for the legislation.

Against that institutional background, the human burden of foodborne illness supplied the baseline that gave the legislative argument its scale. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention published estimates by Scallan and coauthors in Emerging Infectious Diseases in 2011 that placed the annual toll at about 48 million illnesses in the United States, about 128,000 hospitalizations, and about 3,000 deaths. The estimate of 48 million illnesses corresponded to one in six Americans in a year. Estimates of that kind rest on modeling rather than on a count of confirmed cases, because most foodborne illness never reaches a laboratory and many confirmed cases never receive a pathogen specific diagnosis. The baseline described a routine and recurring burden rather than a rare catastrophe. Illness at that scale meant that the performance of the food safety system was measured not only in the outbreaks that reached national attention but also in a large volume of cases that never produced a headline, a recall, or a congressional hearing.

The outbreak sequence that preceded enactment converted the baseline and the institutional critique into a set of concrete failures, each with a different lesson about the limits of response. The 2006 outbreak of E. coli O157:H7 in bagged fresh spinach caused 205 illnesses and 3 deaths, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Bagged fresh spinach reached consumers without a cooking step or a processing intervention between the field and the table that could be relied on to eliminate the pathogen, and the product moved through a distribution system built for speed and national reach. A contamination event in a ready to eat produce item of that kind leaves a reactive system with narrow options. By the time illnesses appear, investigators must work backward from the memories and purchase records of ill consumers to a product with a short shelf life that many households have already consumed or discarded. The spinach outbreak demonstrated how quickly the window for effective removal of a perishable product can close.

The Salmonella Saintpaul outbreak in 2008 added a lesson about identification under pressure. The outbreak caused more than 1,400 illnesses, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Investigators first blamed tomatoes, and the trail later led to peppers grown in Mexico. A warning aimed at the wrong commodity damages producers of a food that did not cause the illnesses while leaving the actual vehicle in commerce for a longer period. A warning aimed at the right commodity, issued with confidence and supported by records that move at the speed of distribution, protects consumers without sweeping in an uninvolved sector. The Saintpaul investigation exposed the dependence of a reactive system on epidemiological inference assembled after illnesses accumulate. Traceability, recordkeeping, and preventive control at the points where produce is grown, packed, and shipped all bear on how fast an investigation can converge on the correct answer, and the 2008 outbreak showed the cost of convergence that arrives slowly and lands first on the wrong product.

No episode in the sequence damaged confidence in voluntary and reactive controls more than the Peanut Corporation of America disaster of 2008 to 2009. The company operated a plant at Blakely, Georgia, and its peanut butter and peanut paste carried Salmonella Typhimurium into a wide range of downstream foods. The final count by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recorded 714 illnesses in 46 states, with 166 people hospitalized and 9 deaths. More than 3,600 products were recalled. The scale of the recall followed from the position of the company in the supply chain. Peanut Corporation of America sold an ingredient rather than only a finished food bearing the company name on a retail shelf, so contamination at one plant spread through hundreds of products made by other firms, each of which had to identify affected lots and remove affected goods after the ingredient had already been incorporated and shipped onward. A failure at an ingredient supplier multiplies under a reactive system, because every downstream customer becomes part of the removal problem and none of those customers controlled the conditions that produced the contamination.

The internal record at Peanut Corporation of America separated the episode from an ordinary failure of sanitation. The company had shipped product after internal tests returned positive results for Salmonella, and the company had retested product until a negative result appeared, then shipped on the strength of the negative result. Retesting until a favorable outcome appears inverts the purpose of testing. A test result functions as information about a hazard in a preventive system, and a positive result calls for holding product, investigating the cause, and applying a control. In the practice documented at the Blakely plant, a positive result functioned as an obstacle to be tested around. Owner Stewart Parnell was convicted in 2014 and sentenced in September 2015 to 28 years in prison. Criminal punishment arrived years after the illnesses and deaths. The punishment answered for the conduct. The punishment could not restore the sequence of decisions that a working preventive duty, backed by inspection and records that a regulator could examine before distribution, was designed to interrupt.

The shell egg outbreak in 2010 brought the argument to the sector on the Food and Drug Administration side of the jurisdictional boundary in the months immediately before the Senate completed action on the legislation. Salmonella Enteritidis in shell eggs from Wright County Egg and Hillandale Farms of Iowa led to a recall of about 550 million eggs in August 2010. An estimated 1,939 illnesses were associated with the outbreak period in the analysis by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Shell eggs presented a distinctive version of the preventive problem. Contamination could exist inside an egg that appeared normal, and the product moved in enormous volume through ordinary retail and food service channels. A recall of about 550 million eggs also tested the practical meaning of removal at scale. Announcing a recall and completing a recall are different achievements when the article moves in that volume.

Read together, the four episodes answered the strongest defense of the older framework, which was that serious failures were rare aberrations in a system that generally worked. The spinach outbreak involved a ready to eat produce item and the speed of perishable distribution. The Saintpaul outbreak involved mistaken identification and an imported vehicle traced to peppers grown in Mexico. The peanut episode involved deliberate shipment after positive tests and an ingredient supplier whose failure radiated through more than 3,600 products. The egg outbreak involved a staple commodity in extraordinary volume under the jurisdiction of the Food and Drug Administration. Different commodities, different failure mechanisms, and different positions in the supply chain produced the same structural observation. In each episode the decisive gap opened before illness was detected, and the tools of the older framework operated on food and on firms after the hazard had already traveled. The Food Safety Modernization Act legislated against that gap. The statute answered the operative question in favor of prevention as a legal duty, imposed on the facilities and farms and importers positioned to act before distribution, and enforced through plans, standards, records, inspections, and recall authority built into the amended 1938 act.

## How the Food Safety Modernization Act Reached the Statute Book

The Food Safety Modernization Act did not travel to enactment on the bill that first carried its ambitions through a chamber. The final law, Public Law 111-353, signed by President Barack Obama on January 4, 2011, rested on a Senate text, carried at the last stage inside a House vehicle whose original subject had nothing to do with food. That unusual ending was not a decorative detail of the legislative record. The ending explained the statute itself, because the provisions that caused the procedural crisis, the fees in section 107, survived the crisis, and the compromise that had made Senate passage possible, the Tester-Hagan amendment on small farms and small facilities, survived beside the fees without alteration.

The House moved first, and moved with apparent decisiveness. H.R. 2749, titled the Food Safety Enhancement Act and sponsored by Rep. John Dingell, passed the House on July 30, 2009, by 283 to 142. The vote established that a substantial House majority accepted the premise behind the legislation, that the reactive framework inherited from the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 required a preventive replacement. The vote did not, however, make H.R. 2749 the law, and the bill never became the vehicle that finished the work. The reason lay less in rejection than in displacement. The Senate developed a separate bill, with separate sponsorship and separate committee management, and the Senate text, once passed, became the operative draft around which the closing maneuvers turned. Each chamber guards its own draft, its own compromises, and its own allocation of credit and responsibility, and a conference or an exchange of amendments becomes necessary only when both chambers have settled on language. Here, the closing settlement formed around the Senate language rather than around the House bill, so the Dingell bill remained part of the history of the statute without becoming part of the enrolled text. Its passage mattered as evidence of support and as pressure on the Senate to act. Its passage did not determine the words that reached the President.

That distinction between support and text ran through the entire passage story. Agreement that food law needed rebuilding was broad, and the House vote in the summer of 2009 demonstrated that breadth early. Agreement on the exact distribution of burdens proved harder, and the interval between the House vote and Senate passage stretched across more than a year of the same Congress. During that interval the Senate bill, S. 510, stood as the pending answer to the House action, sponsored chiefly by Sen. Richard Durbin and managed by Sen. Tom Harkin, chairman of the Committee on Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions, the HELP Committee. The stall was the central fact of the bill's middle period. S. 510 had sponsorship, committee leadership, and a House-passed companion measure demonstrating support in the other chamber, and the bill nonetheless waited. A preventive statute reaches into the daily operations of farms and facilities, and each category of operation had reason to measure the draft against its own costs and capacities. The longer the bill waited, the clearer it became that passage depended on resolving the position of small operations, for whom a uniform set of preventive duties, written with large and professionally staffed facilities in contemplation, threatened a burden out of proportion to sales.

### What compromise allowed the Senate bill to move?

The Tester-Hagan amendment, sponsored by Sen. Jon Tester and Sen. Kay Hagan and folded into the manager's amendment before the Senate vote, supplied the compromise that brought support from the small farm and local food sector and allowed S. 510 to move to passage by 73 to 25 on November 30, 2010.

The architecture of that compromise deserves attention, because its survival through every later procedural emergency showed how essential the Senate regarded the bargain. The amendment worked through a qualified test rather than through a simple exemption for operations under a stated size. An operation qualified only when two conditions met. The average annual monetary value of all food sold during the previous three years had to fall under 500,000 dollars, and more than half of that food, measured by value, had to be sold directly to qualified end users. A qualified end user meant the consumer, in the ordinary sense of a person rather than a business, or a restaurant or retail food establishment located in the same state as the farm or facility, or not more than 275 miles from it. The two conditions together tied relief to scale and to sales channel at the same time. A small operation selling into national distribution did not qualify on size alone. A larger operation selling at a farmers market did not qualify on channel alone.

The effect of qualification also differed by setting, a nuance the passage debate carried into the enacted text. A qualified facility under the preventive controls rule faced modified requirements rather than a full food safety plan, documenting its status and either its hazard analysis and controls or its compliance with state and local food safety law. A qualified farm gained an exemption from the standards of the produce rule, subject to modified requirements that included labeling identifying the farm at the point of sale. Registration duties, the inspection mandate, and the mandatory recall authority stood outside the amendment's reach, and the Food and Drug Administration retained power to withdraw qualified status when an operation was directly linked to a foodborne illness outbreak or when withdrawal served the protection of public health. Critics of the amendment, among them the Produce Marketing Association, argued in November 2010 that exemption by sales channel and distance was not risk based, in a formulation captured by the objection that food safety knows no size. Sen. Tester answered the scale objection from the other direction, describing the carve-out as a loophole to walk through with a wheelbarrow, not to drive a truck through. The exchange defined the bargain precisely. The amendment opened a narrow passage for a defined class of small, direct selling operations, and the narrowness of the passage formed part of the price of Senate agreement.

When the Senate passed S. 510 on November 30, 2010, by 73 to 25, the vote total reflected the bargain's effect. A margin of 73 votes in a chamber where contested measures often survive narrowly indicated that the manager's amendment, with the Tester-Hagan compromise folded inside it, had converted a stalled bill into a text that senators across a wide range could accept. The bill that passed was therefore not simply the bill that had waited. Passage followed amendment, and the amendment that mattered most concerned the treatment of the smallest regulated operations. The exemptions in sections 418 and 419 were a condition of enactment, negotiated before the decisive vote and defended as the difference between a statute that regulated proportionately and a statute that, in the defenders' account, would have damaged a sector out of proportion to any safety gain on offer.

Victory in the Senate, however, produced not a bill ready for the President but a constitutional obstacle that the Senate vote had done nothing to cure. The obstacle sat in section 107 of S. 510, a provision far removed from preventive controls or produce standards. Section 107 imposed fees, covering reinspection, recall noncompliance, participation in the Voluntary Qualified Importer Program, and the reexport of imported goods. In a House-originated bill, such provisions would have raised no threshold question. In a bill that had originated in the Senate and passed that chamber as its own measure, the fees carried a different legal character. The House treated the section 107 charges as revenue measures, and the Constitution, through the Origination Clause, reserves the origination of revenue bills to the House of Representatives. On that reading, S. 510 as passed could not be taken up by the House, because a Senate-originated text carrying revenue provisions arrived in the wrong chamber in the wrong form. The objection did not challenge the policy of the fees. The objection challenged the pedigree of the paper on which the policy was written.

### Why did the Senate bill need a House vehicle to become law?

Section 107 of S. 510 imposed fees for reinspection, recall noncompliance, Voluntary Qualified Importer Program participation, and importer reexport. The House treated those charges as revenue measures. Under the Origination Clause, a Senate-originated bill carrying revenue provisions could not proceed as passed, so enactment required moving identical text into a House-originated vehicle.

The derailment that followed illustrated how completely a procedural defect can displace a substantive majority. By the opening of December 2010, the policy argument had been settled in both chambers in sequence, the House through H.R. 2749 in 2009 and the Senate through S. 510 in 2010. No further vote on the merits stood between the text and enactment. What stood in the way was the form of the vehicle, and the calendar offered little room for a leisurely correction, because the Congress that had produced the agreement was approaching its end. The first attempts at rescue accepted the Senate text and sought a different legislative container already in motion. Carrying the food safety text inside an omnibus spending bill offered one route, since a large appropriations measure moving at the close of a session can absorb additional matter that lacks a viable path on its own. Carrying the text inside a continuing resolution offered another route on the same logic. Both attempts failed in December 2010. The record of failure mattered for what followed, because each collapsed alternative narrowed the options and raised the cost of further delay.

The rescue, when the rescue arrived, matched the problem with unusual precision. On Sunday, December 19, 2010, the Senate took up H.R. 2751, a bill that had originated in the House, as the Origination Clause required, and whose original subject had been the vehicle rebate program of 2009, an entirely unrelated matter from an earlier phase of the same Congress. The Senate stripped the bill of its original content and inserted the text of S. 510 in its place. The maneuver, familiar in congressional practice as the use of a shell vehicle, solved the single defect that had blocked enactment and solved nothing else, because nothing else required a solution. The fees in section 107 remained in the text, worded as the Senate had passed the fees, but the fees traveled inside a House-originated bill, and the House objection lost its object. The bill number and the chamber of origin were the whole of its contribution, and in the circumstances that contribution was decisive. A rebate program for vehicle purchases and a preventive regime for the national food supply shared a legislative shell for a single reason, that the shell had begun its life in the correct chamber.

Procedure at the moment of rescue also departed from the pattern of the earlier decisive votes. The Senate passed the amended H.R. 2751 on December 19, 2010, by unanimous consent, without a roll call. Unanimous consent in that setting did not signal that every senator preferred the text in every particular. Unanimous consent signaled that no senator chose to force a recorded vote or to block a measure whose Senate merits had already been tested three weeks earlier at 73 to 25. The November vote had settled the question of Senate support, and the December action concerned the vehicle, so the chamber completed the December action through the fastest form available on a Sunday in the closing days of a session. The House then passed the bill on December 21, 2010, by 215 to 144, a recorded vote that confirmed the chamber's acceptance of the Senate text inside the House shell. Acceptance, rather than authorship, was the act requested of the House in December, and a majority of 215 supplied that acceptance. President Barack Obama signed the bill on January 4, 2011, and the text became Public Law 111-353, codified at 124 Stat. 3885 as an amendment to the 1938 act.

An accounting of gains and losses across that passage begins with an observation that the losses were procedural rather than substantive. H.R. 2749, the Dingell bill, was lost as a vehicle, and with the bill went the House's claim to textual authorship of the statute, even though the House had acted first and by a wide margin. S. 510 was lost as an enrolled measure in its own name, even though the words of S. 510 constituted the law. What the enacted text gained, relative to the position after the Senate vote in November, was a constitutionally sound path through the House and, with that path, enactment itself before the Congress expired. The fee provisions of section 107 survived inside the House vehicle, so the derailment over revenue origination changed the container of the fees without changing the fees, and the financing elements tied to reinspection, recall noncompliance, the Voluntary Qualified Importer Program, and importer reexport entered the statute as the Senate had written the elements. The small operation compromise survived intact as well. The Tester-Hagan qualified test, with the 500,000 dollar threshold measured across all food sold and the direct sales condition measured by value, passed through the omnibus failure, the continuing resolution failure, and the shell bill substitution without renegotiation, which confirmed the status of the compromise as settled Senate business that no party to the closing maneuvers had an interest in reopening.

The passage of the Food Safety Modernization Act therefore closed on a distinction that the earlier stages of the bill had kept in shadow. Majorities for the policy existed in both chambers, first in the House in July 2009 and then, after the Tester-Hagan bargain unlocked the Senate, in November 2010. Majorities alone did not enact the statute. Enactment required a text whose origin satisfied a constitutional rule about revenue, a vehicle stripped and refilled in the final days of a Congress, a Sunday action taken without a roll call, and a House vote accepting words the House had not drafted. The gate left the substance whole, and the wholeness was the outcome of the rescue. A statute remembered for shifting federal food law from reaction to prevention arrived through a sequence in which reaction, to a constitutional objection raised after Senate passage, determined whether prevention would be enacted at all.

## The Prevention Architecture of the Food Safety Modernization Act

The Food Safety Modernization Act rebuilt federal food law around prevention. The framework inherited from the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 had been reactive in structure. Under the older arrangement, the Food and Drug Administration acted against food that was already adulterated, through seizure of goods, injunctions, and criminal referral, and the agency relied on good manufacturing practice regulations to set general expectations for facilities. Outside hazard analysis systems adopted for seafood in 1995 and for juice in 2001, and outside the separate meat and poultry system administered by the United States Department of Agriculture, no general statutory duty required a facility to analyze hazards in advance and control them before contaminated food reached commerce. The prevention provisions changed the default. Registration kept the agency informed about where food was made, written plans required facilities to identify and control hazards, standards reached back to the farm production of produce, sanitary rules followed food in transit, and food defense addressed deliberate contamination. Each provision assigned a different actor a different preventive task, and the tasks interlocked along the chain from production to distribution.

### Facility Registration and the Power to Suspend

Section 102 amended section 415 of the 1938 act, codified at 21 U.S.C. 350d, and registration supplied the foundation on which the rest of the prevention architecture rested. Facilities that manufacture, process, pack, or hold food must register with the Food and Drug Administration. The registration duty identified the establishments subject to the food provisions of the 1938 act. An agency cannot set inspection priorities or trace responsibility during an incident without an authoritative record of the facilities engaged in making and handling food, and registration maintained that record.

The statute added two features that changed the character of registration. The first was biennial renewal. Registration was no longer a single act performed once and left unchanged in agency files. A facility must renew its registration between October 1 and December 31 of each even-numbered year. The fixed window forced registration information to be refreshed on a predictable cycle, so that facilities that had closed or changed hands did not linger in the record. The three month window at the end of the year gave every facility a defined and uniform period for the task.

The second addition was enforcement power. The statute gave the Food and Drug Administration authority to suspend a registration, and a facility whose registration stood suspended was barred from distributing food. The sanction attached to the facility rather than to a particular shipment. Seizure under the older framework reached goods; suspension reached the establishment itself and halted the flow of product out of it. The agency held a tool that could stop distribution without first proving that a specific lot of food was adulterated.

### Preventive Controls and the Written Food Safety Plan

Section 103 supplied the center of the prevention architecture. It added section 418 to the 1938 act, codified at 21 U.S.C. 350g, and section 418 imposed on covered facilities a duty that the general food law had not carried before. The duty fell on a precisely identified actor. The owner, operator, or agent in charge of a covered facility bore the obligation, and the statute framed responsibility in terms of the person who controlled the operation. A food safety plan could not be treated as a filing detached from management, because the owner, the operator, or the agent in charge was the party charged with evaluating hazards, installing controls, and maintaining the records that proved the system operated.

The obligation began with evaluation. The responsible party must evaluate known or reasonably foreseeable hazards associated with the facility and the food handled there. A hazard that was reasonably foreseeable fell within the evaluation even if the operation had no history of the problem, and a known hazard fell within the evaluation regardless of how the knowledge had been acquired. Under the reactive model, hazard knowledge often mattered only after an incident, when investigators reconstructed what had gone wrong. Under section 418, hazard knowledge had to be assembled in advance, as the basis for control decisions.

Preventive controls followed from the evaluation. The written food safety plan identified the controls that the facility would apply to the hazards the evaluation had found, and the plan tied each control to the analytical step that justified it. The logic was sequential. A hazard that appeared in the evaluation required a response in the plan, and a control that appeared in the plan required a foundation in the evaluation, so that a reviewer could trace the reasoning in both directions. A plan that omitted a foreseeable hazard failed at the first step, and a plan that named a hazard without a corresponding control failed at the second.

### What must a written food safety plan contain?

A written food safety plan must rest on an evaluation of known or reasonably foreseeable hazards and must set out preventive controls for those hazards, procedures for monitoring the controls, corrective actions for failures, verification that the controls work, periodic reanalysis, and records documenting each element of the system.

Monitoring gave the plan a continuing life after adoption. The responsible party must monitor the preventive controls to establish that the controls operated as the plan intended. A control that existed only on paper offered no protection. Monitoring generated observations at the point where the control applied, and those observations showed whether performance matched the plan. A control that drifted out of specification could be detected through monitoring before adulterated product accumulated.

Corrective actions addressed failure. When monitoring or other experience showed that a control had not operated as intended, the responsible party must take corrective actions. The plan anticipated failure in advance and prescribed the response, so that deviation triggered a defined course of conduct rather than improvisation under pressure. The element served two purposes. It dealt with the immediate deviation and the food affected by it, and it addressed the conditions that had allowed the deviation to occur, so that repetition became less likely. Section 418 required the opposite of a system that planned only for success: the response to failure was itself part of the written design.

Verification tested the system as a whole. The responsible party must verify that the preventive controls worked, that monitoring was performed, and that corrective actions achieved the intended result. Verification stood apart from monitoring in function. Monitoring watched the control at the moment of application, while verification asked whether the control, as designed and as applied, actually controlled the hazard. A control could be monitored faithfully for an extended period and yet be inadequate to the hazard, and verification was the element charged with catching that deeper defect. Verification also drew on the records of the system, which made documentation part of the safety mechanism rather than an administrative appendage.

Reanalysis kept the plan from hardening into obsolescence. The responsible party must reanalyze the food safety plan at least once every three years. Production changes over time, through new products, new suppliers, new equipment, and new knowledge about hazards, and a plan written for an earlier configuration could lose its fit. The three year cycle imposed an outer limit on staleness, and reanalysis returned the facility to the hazard evaluation, with the accumulated experience of monitoring, corrective actions, and verification available as evidence. The review could confirm the earlier analysis or revise it, but the obligation to conduct it did not depend on whether the facility expected change.

Records bound the system together. The responsible party must keep records of the plan and of the procedures and results that the plan generated, covering monitoring, corrective actions, and verification. Without records, monitoring observations vanished and verification had nothing durable to examine. With records, performance could be reviewed over time, patterns could be detected, and the agency could assess compliance from evidence rather than recollection. The duty also sharpened internal discipline, because a facility that must document a control behaves differently from one that must only perform it.

A qualified individual stood behind the document. The statute required that a qualified individual prepare the food safety plan or oversee its preparation, and the qualification expectation ran through the performance of the activities the plan prescribed. A plan drafted without adequate grounding in hazard analysis could satisfy a formal checklist while missing the hazards that mattered, and the qualified individual requirement inserted competence as a structural condition of compliance. The requirement connected the legal obligation to the technical discipline of hazard analysis that had developed in the earlier seafood and juice systems and in private practice.

Two boundaries defined the reach of section 418. The first was the farm boundary. Section 418 does not apply to farms as such, and the preventive controls duty attached to covered facilities rather than to farming operations in their capacity as farms. Farm production received separate treatment in the produce safety provision, with standards written for growing and harvesting rather than for facility based processing. The second boundary ran between human food and animal food. The rulemaking under section 103 also produced the animal food preventive controls rule, so the model extended to food for animals, with requirements adapted to that sector. The final preventive controls rules for human food and for animal food appeared in the Federal Register on September 17, 2015, and the human food rule was codified at 21 C.F.R. Part 117.

### Produce Safety Standards for Growing and Harvesting

Section 105 carried prevention onto the farm. It added section 419 to the 1938 act, codified at 21 U.S.C. 350h, and section 419 established the first statutory federal standards for growing, harvesting, packing, and holding produce. Facility based provisions regulated establishments that manufactured and processed food, but a large share of produce reached consumers with little or no processing, and the hazards associated with fresh produce arose in the field, in water and soil practices, in worker hygiene, and in packing operations rather than at a manufacturing step that could remove contamination. A prevention statute that stopped at the facility gate would have left the production stage of an entire category of food outside the statutory structure, and section 419 closed that gap by writing standards for the conditions of production themselves.

### What standards govern the growing and harvesting of produce?

Section 419 established the first statutory federal standards for growing, harvesting, packing, and holding produce, covering worker training and health and hygiene, agricultural water, biological soil amendments of animal origin, domesticated and wild animals, and equipment, tools, and buildings, with special requirements reserved for sprouts.

The standards addressed five areas of production practice. The first area was worker training and health and hygiene, which treated the people who handled produce as part of the safety system. The second area was agricultural water, where quality failures could spread contamination across a field rather than confining it to a single item. The third area was biological soil amendments of animal origin, materials valued for fertility that could also carry pathogens into contact with produce. The fourth area was domesticated and wild animals, whose presence in and around growing areas presented contamination risks a grower had to manage. The fifth area was equipment, tools, and buildings, where surfaces and structures could either protect produce or become routes of contamination. Together the five areas described the production environment as a whole.

Sprouts received special requirements within the produce framework, so that a grower of sprouts could not rest on compliance with the general produce standards alone. The rule structure therefore recognized gradations of risk within the produce category itself. Section 419 did not impose an undifferentiated code on every crop; it organized standards by subject area and added a distinct layer for the one commodity named for special handling. The produce safety rule was published in final form on November 27, 2015, and it was codified at 21 C.F.R. Part 112.

The provision displaced an older and softer regime. Before section 419, federal expectations for the safe production of produce rested on guidance rather than on binding statutory standards, and guidance described recommended practice without the enforceable force of a rule grounded in the statute. Alongside guidance, private buyer requirements did much of the practical work, as purchasers imposed conditions on growers through contracts and purchasing specifications. That arrangement produced uneven protection. Buyer requirements varied with the buyer and answered to commercial negotiation as much as to public health, while guidance bound no one. Section 419 replaced that patchwork, for covered produce, with standards that carried statutory authority, applied by category rather than by contract, and could be enforced as law.

### Sanitary Transportation of Food

Section 111 addressed the movement of food between the places where food was made, grown, and received. The provision directed rulemaking on sanitary practices for shippers, carriers, receivers, and loaders moving food. The inclusion of all four classes of actor reflected the distributed character of transport. A shipper tendered the load, a carrier moved it, a loader handled the loading, and a receiver took delivery, and sanitary failure could enter at any of those points. A rule addressed to carriers alone would have left the conditions created by the other participants outside the regulatory frame, so section 111 treated transportation as a stage of food handling in its own right rather than as an interval between regulated places. A facility could execute a food safety plan with precision and a farm could meet the produce standards in full, and contamination or abuse of conditions during movement could compromise the food before delivery. Prevention that ended at the shipping dock protected production but not distribution. The sanitary transportation rule appeared in final form on April 6, 2016, and it was codified at 21 C.F.R. Part 1, subpart O.

### Food Defense Against Intentional Adulteration

Section 106 addressed a different category of risk. The hazards governed by preventive controls and produce standards arose through accident, negligence, and the ordinary biology of food production. Section 106 addressed harm that an actor intended. The provision directed science-based mitigation strategies against intentional adulteration at vulnerable points in the food supply. Mitigation was to be science-based, so that protective measures rested on analysis of how adulteration could occur and where an act could succeed, rather than on undirected vigilance. The strategies were aimed at vulnerable points, so that effort concentrated where an act could affect the greatest amount of food or reach the greatest number of consumers. The food defense rule that implemented the provision was codified at 21 C.F.R. Part 121, and the final rule appeared on May 27, 2016.

The inclusion of food defense inside a food safety statute marked a conceptual distinction drawn with care. Safety systems assume that hazards are unwanted by everyone in the system. Defense against intentional adulteration assumes an adversary who studies the system, conceals intent, and selects the point of weakest protection, and controls designed for accidents do not automatically answer that threat. Section 106 therefore required a distinct analysis, organized around vulnerability rather than around the routine hazards of a process. Prevention under section 418 stopped foreseeable accidents before harm occurred; prevention under section 106 attempted to make deliberate harm harder to accomplish and narrower in effect, by building mitigation at the points where protection mattered most.

### Fees and the Structure of Support

Section 107 supplied a narrower and more mechanical element of the architecture: fees. The provision authorized fees for reinspection, for recall non-compliance, and for participation in the Voluntary Qualified Importer Program. The structural role of the fees was cost allocation. Reinspection consumed agency resources because a compliance failure had made a further visit necessary, and a fee placed that cost on the operation whose condition generated it. Recall non-compliance fees followed the same logic, attaching cost to the party whose failure imposed added federal work. Program participation fees treated a voluntary program as a service whose beneficiaries bore a share of the expense. The fees did not fund the prevention system as a whole, which rested on annual appropriations, but section 107 aligned the costs of particular federal activities with the actors that occasioned them. The placement of the fee provisions in a Senate originated bill also carried a constitutional consequence under the Origination Clause that shaped the passage of the statute, a history treated in the account of enactment.

### How the Prevention Provisions Fit Together

The prevention provisions operated as a sequence of gates. Registration under section 415 established the population of facilities, with suspension as a sanction against the establishment itself. Section 418 governed the interior conduct of covered facilities through a documented cycle of evaluation, control, monitoring, correction, verification, and reanalysis, performed under the direction of a qualified individual and preserved in records. Section 419 extended statutory standards to the production of produce, where the facility model did not fit, and replaced guidance and private buyer requirements with enforceable standards organized by subject area. Section 111 followed food into transit, and section 106 guarded the points where a deliberate actor might strike. Section 107 attached fees to reinspection, to recall non-compliance, and to program participation. No single provision carried the design alone, and the design is best measured by the coverage of the whole: a facility known to the agency, a plan written before harm occurred, standards in the field and on the road, and a defense at the vulnerable points.

## Detection, Response, Recall, and Imports under the Food Safety Modernization Act

Detection under the Food Safety Modernization Act rests on a premise that the older system had left unaddressed in statute. A preventive law fails if hazards escape notice for long intervals, if contaminated product moves faster than investigators can trace, and if an agency can identify a danger without a reliable means to remove the product from commerce. Sections on inspection, tracing, and surveillance supplied the eyes of the new system. Sections on recall and imports supplied its hands.

### Inspection Frequency and the Limits of a Mandate

Section 201 wrote inspection frequency into law with unusual specificity. For high-risk domestic facilities, inspection was required not less often than once in the five years after enactment and not less often than once every three years thereafter. The classification mattered because the statute did not treat all registered facilities as presenting equal danger. Risk determined tempo. A facility placed in the high-risk category received a shorter initial window and a shorter recurring interval, reflecting a judgment that the probability or consequence of failure justified closer federal attention.

For other domestic facilities, the timetable ran longer. Inspection was required once in the seven years after enactment and not less often than once every five years thereafter. The distinction preserved a universal mandate while allocating the most frequent visits to the operations identified as high risk. In structure, the provision rejected both extremes. It did not authorize indefinite intervals for lower-risk facilities, and it did not impose an identical schedule regardless of risk. Coverage and prioritization operated together, with every domestic facility inside the mandate and the interval adjusted to the category assigned.

Foreign facilities received a different formulation, expressed as an annual number of inspections rather than an interval for each facility. The statute required not fewer than 600 inspections in the first year after enactment and not fewer than twice the previous year's number in each of the five following years. Compounded across that period, the schedule described a trajectory toward 19,200 inspections. The arithmetic expressed ambition with clarity. It also exposed a dependence that the text of the mandate could not remove. Appropriations never funded the trajectory, so the stated escalation remained a measure of congressional expectation rather than a description of inspection capacity built under the law.

For the domestic mandate, Congress supplied a practical mechanism in the same provision. The Food and Drug Administration received authority to rely on federal, state, or local inspections to satisfy the domestic mandate. Reliance broadened the pool of inspections that could count, acknowledged that state and local officials conducted food facility work across the country, and avoided duplicative visits where another level of government had performed an inspection suitable for the federal purpose.

### Tracing Product and Linking Illness to Source

Section 204 addressed traceability through pilot projects rather than an immediate universal tracing system. The provision required pilot projects on tracing produce and processed food and a report to Congress. The choice of pilots reflected the difficulty of the problem. Produce and processed food move through different chains. Produce may be commingled, repacked, and distributed quickly with limited shelf life. Processed food may combine ingredients from many sources and carry lot information shaped by manufacturing practice. A tracing method effective for one category may transfer poorly to the other.

The purpose of tracing is time. During an outbreak investigation, each day spent reconstructing distribution extends exposure if contaminated product remains available and widens the scope of removal if investigators cannot separate affected lots from unaffected lots. Better tracing narrows a recall and identifies a source with greater confidence. Section 204 approached that goal experimentally, testing what information participants held, how quickly the information could be assembled, and where breaks in the chain prevented a complete path from production to sale.

A separate element of section 204 concerned designation of high-risk foods and added recordkeeping, and that element ran late. The delay became the subject of distinct litigation filed in October 2018 by the Center for Food Safety and the Center for Environmental Health. That later suit concerned the high-risk foods designation and traceability recordkeeping under section 204, a different provision and a different claim from the earlier deadline litigation over the foundational rules.

Section 205 turned to surveillance, providing for enhanced surveillance through work with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and state laboratories. Surveillance supplies the human illness signal that inspection and tracing may not generate on their own. A facility inspection can reveal conditions capable of producing contamination without identifying illnesses tied to a specific product. Laboratory and epidemiological work can reveal a pattern of illness before the source is known. Surveillance points investigators toward a hazard, tracing tests whether a suspect product reached the people who became ill, and inspection examines the conditions under which the product was made.

Detection, tracing, and surveillance operate as complements rather than substitutes. Frequent inspection without tracing can find a problem without locating all affected food. Strong tracing without surveillance can reconstruct movement only after a problem is identified by another route. The statute addressed all three, though with different degrees of prescription and very different timetables for completion.

### Mandatory Recall Authority and the Voluntary System

Section 206 added the authority most plainly associated with response. It created new section 423 of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938, codified at 21 U.S.C. 350l, and gave the Food and Drug Administration power to order a food recall under defined conditions. Before that grant, the agency could request removal, publicize danger, seize product, seek an injunction, or use administrative detention under a demanding standard. Section 206 added compulsion at the recall stage itself, for the circumstance in which a responsible party declined to remove food that met the statutory test for danger.

### What triggers a mandatory food recall under the Food Safety Modernization Act?

A mandatory recall requires a reasonable probability that a food is adulterated or misbranded and that its use will cause serious adverse health consequences or death. The Food and Drug Administration must first offer a voluntary recall. An order follows only after refusal or failure to act.

The standard has two parts joined together. First, there must be a reasonable probability that an article of food is adulterated or misbranded. Second, there must be a reasonable probability that use of or exposure to the article will cause serious adverse health consequences or death. Suspicion alone does not meet the threshold, and a minor labeling defect without the required health consequence does not meet the threshold either. The formulation directs the authority toward the gravest category of food risk, where delay carries a prospect of severe illness or death and where a refusal to act leaves the public exposed to a danger that lesser tools may remove too slowly.

Procedure restrains the grant as firmly as the substantive standard defines it. If the statutory conditions are present, the Food and Drug Administration must first give the responsible party an opportunity to cease distribution and recall the article voluntarily. The mandatory order is not the opening move. Only if the party refuses or fails to carry out the voluntary action may the agency order cessation of distribution and recall. After an order, an informal hearing is available under the provision. The sequence preserves a role for cooperation at the front of the process and places a procedural check after compulsion, rather than making the order an unreviewed first response.

Accountability accompanied the power itself. The statute requires the Department of Health and Human Services to report annually to Congress on use of the authority, creating a record of how often the threshold was invoked and whether the backstop remained exceptional or became routine.

The counter-reading begins with the record of the voluntary system, and that record deserves statement at full strength. Before 2011, every Food and Drug Administration food recall was voluntary, and companies complied in the overwhelming run of cases. A request from the agency carried commercial, legal, and reputational consequences that made refusal rare. Against that background, a mandatory power could appear unnecessary, an added layer directed at conduct that market incentives and existing enforcement tools already produced in nearly every instance. The fallback tools also had real force. Seizure and injunction placed federal enforcement directly against goods and conduct, publicity could destroy demand for a product and a brand, and administrative detention held product under a demanding standard.

The failures that produced section 423 exposed the gap in that arrangement. The system depended on cooperation from the very party whose conduct or product created the danger. When that party delayed, disputed the evidence, or had already distributed a contaminated ingredient widely, indirect tools struggled to match the speed and breadth of distribution. A seizure reaches identifiable goods. An injunction requires judicial process. Publicity warns buyers but does not itself organize retrieval from every point in a chain. None of those tools supplied a direct command to the responsible party to remove a defined article from commerce wherever distribution had carried the article.

The Peanut Corporation of America supplied the defining case. Its plant at Blakely, Georgia produced peanut butter and peanut paste contaminated with Salmonella Typhimurium. The company had shipped product after internal tests returned positive results and had retested until a negative result appeared. Peanut paste is an ingredient, not solely a finished product sold under one label. Contamination at that point spread outward as downstream manufacturers incorporated the paste into separate foods. More than 3,600 products were recalled, and no one held authority to compel removal of an ingredient already inside that widening set of downstream products at the speed the hazard required. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recorded a final count for the outbreak of 714 illnesses in 46 states, with 166 people hospitalized and 9 deaths.

An ingredient supplier occupies a position of multiplied consequence. A failure at a single plant can enter hundreds of product lines, each with separate distribution and branding. Tracing identifies the connections. A recall order reaches the party at the origin of the chain. Stewart Parnell, the owner of the Peanut Corporation of America, was convicted in 2014 and sentenced in September 2015 to 28 years in prison. Criminal punishment addressed culpability after the outbreak. It did not supply a prospective removal tool for the next refusal case. Section 423 was written for that scenario.

### The Infant Formula Exception

Section 423 does not extend to infant formula, and the reason is institutional rather than a judgment that formula presents low risk. Section 412 of the 1938 act, codified at 21 U.S.C. 350a, already carried a mandatory recall procedure for infant formula. Section 412 was enacted by the Infant Formula Act of 1980. Under that earlier framework, a mandatory recall procedure applied to infant formula that failed to meet its quality and notification requirements. Congress therefore had no need to place formula inside the new general authority, because a product-specific mandatory procedure predated the Food Safety Modernization Act by three decades.

Leaving section 412 in place preserved the tailored procedure. Section 423 supplied the missing general authority for other food, while formula remained governed by the mechanism Congress had created specifically for formula.

### A Backstop Tested by Delay

The first use of section 423 arrived on April 2, 2018, when the Food and Drug Administration issued a mandatory recall order against Triangle Pharmanaturals LLC of Las Vegas for powdered kratom products contaminated with Salmonella. On March 30, 2018, the agency had requested a voluntary recall, and the company refused. The statutory sequence operated exactly as section 206 prescribed. A voluntary opportunity came first. Refusal followed. Only then did the agency issue an order, the first mandatory recall order in agency history under the new authority.

The interval between enactment and first use carries meaning. The order of April 2, 2018 came more than seven years after enactment on January 4, 2011. During that period, food recalls continued through the voluntary route, consistent with the pattern that had prevailed before 2011. A backstop can change negotiation without frequent formal use, because a company weighing refusal faces knowledge that the agency holds a further step. The record supports a narrower conclusion. The authority was reserved for a refusal, used only after the voluntary request failed, and structured so that rarity of use formed part of the design rather than evidence of failure.

### Imports and Verification in the Supply Chain

Title III of the statute moved the center of import control away from the border as the principal point of assurance. Border examination samples only a fraction of arriving food and occurs after production, packing, and shipment have already taken place. A defect created at a foreign facility may be invisible at entry. Sections 301, 302, and 307 built a different allocation of responsibility. The importer, the party with a commercial relationship to the foreign supplier and a presence within United States jurisdiction, received duties to verify that imported food met domestic standards.

Section 301 created the Foreign Supplier Verification Program through new section 805 of the 1938 act, codified at 21 U.S.C. 384a. Importers must perform risk-based foreign supplier verification showing that food was produced in compliance with section 418 or section 419 or with equivalent protection. Section 418 supplies the preventive controls standard for facilities. Section 419 supplies the produce safety standard. The reference to equivalent protection accommodates foreign systems and practices that reach the same level of public health protection through different means, while holding imported food to the protective outcome that domestic law requires.

The definition of importer determines who bears the duty. For purposes of section 805, the importer is the United States owner or consignee of the food at entry, or the United States agent of the foreign owner or consignee. The definition reaches a party inside the United States rather than resting the primary verification duty on a foreign producer beyond ordinary domestic enforcement reach. It also follows commercial control. The owner or consignee selects suppliers, sets contract terms, receives the food, and places the food into domestic distribution. Verification duties attach where leverage over the supplier and accountability within the jurisdiction coincide.

The verification itself is a program of activities, not a single certificate filed at entry. The statute describes risk-based verification that includes hazard analysis, evaluation of the supplier, verification activities such as audits or testing, corrective actions, and records. Hazard analysis identifies what could go wrong with a particular food from a particular supplier. Evaluation weighs the procedures and history of the supplier and the capacity to control the identified hazards. Verification activities then test that judgment, through an audit of production or testing of product. Corrective actions answer a failed check, and records preserve the basis for each judgment.

Section 302 added an incentive beside the duty in section 301. It created the Voluntary Qualified Importer Program through new section 806 of the 1938 act, codified at 21 U.S.C. 384b. The program gives importers that qualify expedited review and entry of food, for a fee. Qualification rests on supplier controls, use of certified facilities, and compliance history. The Food and Drug Administration began accepting applications for the program in 2018. Expedited entry rewards an importer for building the controls that section 301 requires and for using facilities certified under the third-party system in section 307, while directing examination resources toward shipments from unknown or poorly controlled sources.

Section 307 completed the import structure through third-party certification. It added new section 808 of the 1938 act, codified at 21 U.S.C. 384d. Under the provision, accreditation bodies recognized by the Food and Drug Administration accredit third-party auditors, and accredited auditors certify foreign facilities. The structure places two layers between the agency and the certification decision. The agency recognizes the accreditation body. The accreditation body accredits the auditor. The auditor examines and certifies the facility. Agency recognition at the top preserves a federal point of control over a system that performs much of its examination work outside federal employment.

Certification under section 808 has two principal uses in the import system. Certification can be required as a condition of entry for designated high-risk foods, so that a foreign facility producing such food must obtain certification before the food may enter. Certification also counts toward eligibility for the Voluntary Qualified Importer Program, supporting the expedited path in section 302 and serving as a path into the importer program. The first use is compulsory and risk-driven. The second use is voluntary and incentive-driven. In both, certification supplies evidence about a foreign facility generated through an accredited process, evidence that an importer and the agency can use without the agency conducting every examination directly.

The three import provisions operate as a connected system. Section 301 places verification on the importer. Section 307 generates accredited certification that an importer can rely upon and that the agency can require for designated high-risk foods. Section 302 rewards importers that combine strong supplier controls, certified facilities, and a sound compliance history with expedited entry. Duty, evidence, and incentive align. The border remains a point of enforcement and targeted examination, but responsibility moved upstream into the supply chain, to the parties that chose the suppliers.

Read together, these provisions give the preventive statute its enforcement depth. Inspection frequency sets a floor for direct examination at home. Traceability pilots and surveillance cooperation shorten the path from illness signal to identified source. Mandatory recall supplies a direct order when voluntary removal fails. Foreign supplier verification, qualified importer status, and third-party certification extend prevention beyond domestic facilities by assigning verification to importers and accredited auditors.

## Who the Food Safety Modernization Act Covers and Who It Leaves Outside the Full Rules

Coverage under the Food Safety Modernization Act turns on a distinction that runs through the entire statute, between a farm as a place of production and a facility as a place of manufacture, processing, packing, or holding. Section 418 of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, added by section 103 of the statute, never applied to farms as such. The preventive controls duty, with its written food safety plan, its hazard analysis, its monitoring, its corrective actions, its verification, and its reanalysis at least once every three years, was imposed on the owner, operator, or agent in charge of a covered facility. Registration under section 102, amending section 415 of the 1938 act, reached the same facility category. Facilities that manufacture, process, pack, or hold food had to register, with biennial renewal between October 1 and December 31 of each even numbered year, and with a new power in the Food and Drug Administration to suspend a registration and thereby bar the facility from distributing food.

The produce rule stood on different ground, and the difference explains where the exemption fight mattered most. Section 419 of the 1938 act, added by section 105 of the statute, set the first statutory federal standards for growing, harvesting, packing, and holding produce. The subjects of the rule reached onto the farm itself: worker training and health and hygiene, agricultural water, biological soil amendments of animal origin, domesticated and wild animals, and equipment, tools, and buildings, with special requirements for sprouts. A farm that never entered section 418 could therefore face a direct federal standard under section 419 for the way produce was grown and handled before any processing step began. The qualified exemption therefore had to operate inside the produce rule itself, while the corresponding treatment for facilities had to operate inside preventive controls. The Tester-Hagan amendment, sponsored by Senators Jon Tester and Kay Hagan and folded into the manager's amendment before Senate passage on November 30, 2010, worked in both places at once, and the legal effect differed in each place. The amendment did not redraw that boundary, and instead created a qualified status for a defined class of smaller, direct selling operations.

### Which small farms and facilities qualify for the Tester-Hagan treatment?

A farm or facility qualifies only when two tests are met together. Its average annual value of all food sold over the previous three years must fall under 500,000 dollars, and more than half of that food by value must go directly to qualified end users.

The first test measures scale, and the statute measures scale by all food sold rather than by covered food alone. The average annual monetary value of all food sold during the previous three years must be under 500,000 dollars. In the produce rule, that figure is adjusted for inflation from a 2011 baseline. The count includes products outside Food and Drug Administration jurisdiction, such as meat. An operation therefore could not reach the threshold more easily by separating out sales of meat or other products regulated elsewhere and counting only the portion of sales that the agency regulated. The denominator was the whole food business, averaged across the three year period.

The second test measures channel, and channel is measured by value rather than by volume, by number of transactions, or by number of customers. More than half of that food, by value, must be sold directly to qualified end users. The definition of a qualified end user has two branches. The first branch is the consumer, and the statute specifies the consumer rather than a business in that role. The second branch is a restaurant or a retail food establishment located in the same state as the farm or facility or not more than 275 miles from it. Distance enters the definition only through that second branch. A sale to a consumer counts under the first branch. A sale to a restaurant or retail food establishment counts only when the geographic condition is satisfied.

Read together, the two tests define a particular kind of operation rather than smallness in the abstract. An operation under the dollar figure but selling mainly through intermediaries fails the channel test. An operation selling mainly direct but at or above the dollar figure fails the scale test. Either failure ends the inquiry. The class captured by both tests is an operation of limited total food sales whose receipts come in greater part from direct relationships with consumers or with nearby restaurants and retail food establishments. That pairing of scale and channel was the compromise that brought small farm and local food support behind the bill and let the bill move. The legal language, however, uses dollars, a three year average, a fraction greater than one half, a defined buyer, a state line, and a distance of 275 miles, rather than local as a general description.

The setting for that compromise included a much longer history of federal farm legislation shaping which operations sold through which channels, a history traced in [the long development of farm bill law and its basic structure](/2020/11/01/farm-bill-history-and-structure/). Direct marketing did not arise inside the Food Safety Modernization Act. The statute encountered an existing sector of farms and small facilities that sold to consumers and to nearby establishments, and the amendment decided how the new prevention duties would treat that sector. The decision was not to remove the sector from the food safety system, but to change the form of the duty for operations meeting both tests.

For a qualified facility under preventive controls, the effect was modified requirements rather than exemption from the rule and rather than the full food safety plan required of other covered facilities. The facility had to document its status as qualified. Documentation was not a formality, because status depended on the sales figure and the direct sales fraction, and a claim without a record of its basis would leave nothing for an inspector or for the agency to test. After documentation, the facility had to satisfy one of two substantive routes. The facility could document its hazard analysis and its controls, so that the analytical core of preventive controls remained in a modified form without the full plan structure. Or the facility could document compliance with state and local food safety law, so that an existing body of nonfederal requirements supplied the substantive reference point. Neither route treated qualification as a finding that the food presented no hazard.

For a qualified farm under the produce rule, the language runs the other way. A qualified farm is exempt from the standards of the produce rule but must comply with modified requirements. The growing, harvesting, packing, and holding standards that section 419 authorized, covering water, soil amendments, animals, worker hygiene, and buildings and equipment, do not apply to a qualified farm in the same manner as to a farm without the exemption. The modified requirements then qualify the exemption. Chief among them is the labeling duty. Where food from a qualified farm is sold, labeling must identify the farm. Identification preserves a link between the food and its source at the point of sale even though the full set of produce standards does not apply.

Withdrawal is the limit built into both forms of qualified treatment. The Food and Drug Administration may withdraw the exemption or the qualified status if the operation is directly linked to a foodborne illness outbreak or to protect public health. The two grounds are stated in the alternative, and the second ground reaches beyond a completed link to a particular outbreak. Loss of the status returns the operation to the full requirements of the rule that otherwise applies, the full preventive controls plan for a facility or the full produce standards for a farm. The withdrawal power restores the full duty when the stated grounds are present.

Separate from Tester-Hagan qualification, the produce rule carries its own set of exemptions, and the categories should not be merged with the qualified exemption. The rule fully exempts farms averaging 25,000 dollars or less in annual produce sales. That exemption rests on produce sales alone and on a far lower figure than the 500,000 dollar all food figure in the qualified test, and the exemption is full, without the modified requirements structure that accompanies qualified status. The rule also exempts produce rarely consumed raw, as placed on the list established under the rule. The rule draws that line by list rather than by case by case argument. The rule also exempts produce receiving commercial processing that adequately reduces pathogens. The rule also exempts produce for personal or on farm consumption. Food grown for the household of the farm or for consumption on the farm does not enter the commercial supply that the produce standards govern.

Those four categories answer different questions from the question answered by Tester-Hagan, which combines total food scale with sales channel for operations that remain in commerce and sell, in substantial part, direct. An operation could fall outside the four produce categories and hold qualified status, or fall inside one of the four categories without any need to establish qualified status. The grounds differ, the conditions differ, and the labeling and documentation consequences differ.

The boundaries of the carve out matter as much as its center, and the excluded class is as definite as the class it includes. Tester-Hagan does not cover any operation at or above the sales test. The line is under 500,000 dollars as an average over the previous three years, and an average at the figure does not satisfy a test written as under the figure. Tester-Hagan does not cover an operation selling half or more of its food through distributors and brokers. The direct sales fraction must be more than half, so a fraction of exactly one half fails the channel test even for an operation well under the dollar figure. Large processors and manufacturers stand outside the carve out. The scale test alone excludes operations of that size in the ordinary case, and the modified requirements for a facility were not written as an alternative compliance system for large scale manufacture. The practical field is small farms under the produce rule and small facilities under preventive controls.

Three further limits follow. Tester-Hagan exempts no one from facility registration. A facility that must register under section 415, as amended, must register whether or not the facility holds qualified status, and registration carries the biennial renewal obligation and exposure to suspension. Tester-Hagan exempts no one from the inspection mandate. Section 201 set inspection frequencies for domestic facilities, high risk facilities not less often than once in the five years after enactment and not less often than once every three years thereafter, and other domestic facilities once in the seven years after enactment and not less often than once every five years thereafter, with the agency permitted to rely on federal, state, or local inspections. Qualified status changes the substantive rule against which an operation is measured, without removing the operation from the inspection system. Tester-Hagan exempts no one from mandatory recall. Section 206, adding section 423 of the 1938 act, authorizes a mandatory recall order, after an opportunity for voluntary action, where there is a reasonable probability that food is adulterated or misbranded and that use of the food will cause serious adverse health consequences or death.

The Produce Marketing Association argued in November 2010 that exemption by sales channel and distance is not risk based. A pathogen does not measure the distance between a farm and a restaurant, and a pathogen does not measure the share of sales made direct. On that view, a standard that varies with channel and mileage varies with facts that bear no relation to the hazard in the food itself. The association's formulation was that food safety knows no size. Stated at full strength, the objection is not that small operations are more dangerous than large operations. The objection is that danger does not sort by the criteria the amendment uses, so an operation presenting a serious hazard could receive lighter treatment because its sales were direct and its receipts were under a dollar figure, while an operation presenting a lower hazard could receive full treatment because its sales passed through a distributor. On the association's position, the prevention duty should follow the hazard and the process, and departures should rest on a showing about the food and its handling rather than on a showing about marketing.

The National Sustainable Agriculture Coalition defended the amendment on a different measure, the incidence of compliance cost. A written plan, a hazard analysis, monitoring records, verification steps, and management time carry costs that do not shrink in proportion to sales. Spread across the receipts of a large operation, a given compliance cost amounts to a small fraction of each dollar of sales. Spread across the receipts of a small direct market operation, the same cost amounts to a far larger fraction of each dollar. The coalition argued that compliance costs per dollar of sales fall hardest on small direct market operations and that the amendment prevented the statute from crushing the local food sector. Stated at full strength, the defense is not that small operations present no hazard. The defense is that a uniform duty, identical in form regardless of scale, can destroy the class of operations to which the duty is applied without adding protection in proportion to the added cost, and that a modified duty, with documentation, hazard consideration or state and local compliance, labeling on the farm side, and a withdrawal power held by the agency, preserves a level of control suited to the scale of the operation. Earlier farm level regimes, including the framework discussed in [the history of the federal organic production statute](/2021/03/01/organic-foods-production-act-1990/), had posed the problem of fitting national standards to smaller producers before, although the food safety statute posed the problem in sharper form.

Senator Tester gave the compromise its most quoted measure of intended scale in 2010. The senator described the carve out as "a loophole to walk through with a wheelbarrow, not to drive a truck through." A wheelbarrow belongs to the scale of a small farm operation moving produce by direct sale. A truck belongs to the scale of distribution through intermediaries and of manufacture at volume. The formulation concedes that an opening exists and insists that the opening was sized for the first kind of operation and not for the second, and that insistence matches the operative language. The dollar test blocks the truck by scale. The channel test blocks the truck by method of sale. The withdrawal power addresses the operation that enters through the opening and then becomes linked to an outbreak or presents a ground for action to protect public health. The statute as enacted holds the competing answers inside a single structure, full duties as the rule, qualified treatment as the defined exception, and the full duties restored on withdrawal.

## How the Food Safety Modernization Act Rules Fell Behind Schedule

The Food Safety Modernization Act set a demanding timetable for its own implementation. Congress wrote the principal rulemaking duties into the statute with deadlines of roughly 18 months from enactment, and the Food and Drug Administration missed all seven foundational rulemaking deadlines. A law designed to move federal food regulation from reaction toward prevention depended, for most of its practical effect, on rules that defined what prevention required in concrete terms. Until those rules issued in final form, the detailed standards for hazard analysis, produce growing conditions, supplier verification, transportation practices, and protection against intentional adulteration had not taken operative form.

The gap between enactment and operative rules was not a quiet administrative interval. Proposed rules appeared in 2013, well past the statutory dates, and supplemental proposals followed in September 2014 after heavy public comment. The produce safety proposal alone drew more than 15,000 comments in its first round. Litigation followed, producing rulings for the plaintiffs in 2013, an appeal by the agency to the Ninth Circuit, and a settlement embodied in a consent decree filed on February 20, 2014. That decree set the submission deadlines that governed the final phase of the foundational rulemaking. Final rules then published across a span running from September 2015 to May 2016, with compliance dates staggered by business size and, for one set of requirements, extended by a later rulemaking.

### Why did the Food and Drug Administration miss the rulemaking deadlines?

The statute set deadlines of roughly eighteen months for the principal rules, and the Food and Drug Administration missed all seven foundational deadlines. The agency faced new scientific and operational work, heavy public comment, and authorizations that depended on annual appropriations that did not match the Congressional Budget Office estimate for added spending.

The explanation begins with the character of the work the statute assigned. The seven foundational rules were not minor amendments to an existing regulatory program. The preventive controls rules required the agency to translate a statutory duty to evaluate hazards and implement a written food safety plan into enforceable regulatory text for human food and for animal food. The produce safety rule required the first statutory federal standards for growing, harvesting, packing, and holding produce, covering agricultural water, biological soil amendments of animal origin, worker training and health and hygiene, animals in growing areas, and equipment, tools, and buildings, with special requirements for sprouts. The foreign supplier verification rule required a system under which importers performed risk based verification of foreign suppliers. The rule on accredited third party certification required a structure of recognized accreditation bodies and accredited auditors. The sanitary transportation rule addressed practices for shippers, carriers, receivers, and loaders moving food. The intentional adulteration rule required science based mitigation strategies at vulnerable points in the food supply. Each rule rested on a different statutory provision, addressed a different segment of the food system, and raised distinct questions of scope, exemption, and compliance burden.

Rulemaking on that scale moves through proposal, comment, revision, and final action, and the comment record in this program was substantial. The produce proposal drew more than 15,000 comments in its first round, a volume that reflected the breadth of the affected community, from large produce operations to small farms concerned about the reach of federal standards into growing practices. Heavy comment does not by itself explain a missed statutory deadline, and the plaintiffs in the deadline litigation treated the missed dates as a legal failure rather than an administrative inconvenience. The comment record does explain part of what happened after the proposals appeared. Supplemental proposals issued in September 2014, adding another round of public input on revised approaches before final rules could issue. The sequence from proposal in 2013 to supplemental proposal in September 2014 to final publication in late 2015 and 2016 shows an agency revising its initial positions in response to the record, and it also shows how far the program had drifted from the timetable Congress had written.

The funding structure forms the second part of the explanation, and it receives fuller treatment in the discussion of appropriations below. The statute did not itself raise inspection funding, and its authorizations depended on annual appropriations. The Congressional Budget Office estimated in 2010 that implementation would involve roughly 1.4 billion dollars in additional federal spending over the first five years. An authorization of that kind states what Congress may appropriate. It does not appropriate the money. A rulemaking program written on an 18 month clock and financed through annual decisions faced a structural mismatch between the deadline and the resources assigned to meet it.

### The Deadline Suit in the Northern District of California

The litigation that converted missed deadlines into a court supervised schedule began in August 2012. The Center for Food Safety and the Center for Environmental Health sued the Food and Drug Administration in the United States District Court for the Northern District of California. The case was Center for Food Safety v. Hamburg, reported at 954 F. Supp. 2d 965 (N.D. Cal. 2013). The plaintiffs argued unlawful withholding under the Administrative Procedure Act. The theory was that agency action required by statute had been withheld past the dates Congress had set, and that a court could compel the agency to act on a defined schedule. The seven missed foundational deadlines supplied the factual core of the claim. The dispute was not about the content of any particular standard. The plaintiffs did not ask the district court to write produce standards or preventive controls. The dispute was about time, and about whether statutory deadlines retained legal force after the dates passed without final rules.

Judge Phyllis J. Hamilton ruled for the plaintiffs in 2013 and set court ordered deadlines. The rulings gave the implementation program a judicial timetable in place of the statutory timetable that had lapsed. A court ordered schedule changes the position of an agency in a practical sense even when the underlying rulemaking tasks remain the same. Priorities inside the agency have to accommodate a date enforceable by the court. The Food and Drug Administration appealed to the Ninth Circuit. While the appeal was pending, the parties settled. The settlement took the form of a consent decree, filed on February 20, 2014, which set submission deadlines for the seven foundational rules and resolved the deadline dispute without a Ninth Circuit decision on the merits of the withholding claim.

That procedural path determined the form of accountability for the remainder of the program. The consent decree was a negotiated instrument filed with the court, not an appellate ruling that defined the law of statutory deadlines for other agencies or other statutes. Its force lay in the specific dates it fixed for specific rules. A court can order an agency to submit rules by dates certain. A court does not supply the scientific record, the comment analysis, or the appropriated funds that rulemaking requires, and the decree dates therefore represented a judgment about feasible timing reached through negotiation rather than a judicial finding that the original 18 month schedule had been realistic. The parallel problem of an agency rulemaking program constrained by forces outside the text of its own statute appears in [the account of Federal Trade Commission consumer protection rulemaking](/2019/01/15/ftc-consumer-protection-rulemaking/), where procedural and institutional constraints likewise shaped what rulemaking could deliver and on what timetable.

### The Consent Decree and the Submission Schedule

The consent decree filed on February 20, 2014 set submission deadlines for the seven foundational rules. Submission meant delivery of the final rules within the process that precedes publication, and the decree dates and the Federal Register publication dates that followed were close but not identical. Keeping the two sets of dates separate prevents a common error in accounts of the program, which treat the decree schedule as if it were the publication schedule. The decree required the preventive controls rules for human food and for animal food by August 30, 2015. The decree required the foreign supplier verification rule, the produce safety rule, and the third party accreditation rule by October 31, 2015. The decree required the sanitary transportation rule by March 31, 2016. The decree required the intentional adulteration rule by May 31, 2016. Counted as rule submissions, the schedule covered seven foundational rules, because the human food and animal food preventive controls rules counted separately within the first date and the October 31, 2015 date carried three rules.

The structure of the schedule reflected the state of the rulemakings at the time of settlement. The preventive controls rules came first. The import related rules and the produce rule followed two months afterward, grouped on a single date. Transportation and food defense closed the schedule in the first half of 2016. The decree therefore ordered the completion sequence of the foundational program and gave regulated parties and the agency a fixed framework for planning compliance work. For a program that had operated without an enforceable timetable since the statutory dates passed, the decree restored predictability, even though its dates fell years after enactment for rules Congress had ordered within roughly 18 months.

### What Issued, and Where the Final Rules Sit

Measured against the decree schedule, the final rules published close to the submission dates. The preventive controls rule for human food and the preventive controls rule for animal food published in the Federal Register on September 17, 2015, weeks after the August 30, 2015 submission deadline. The produce safety rule, the foreign supplier verification rule, and the accredited third party certification rule published on November 27, 2015, within a month of the October 31, 2015 submission deadline. The sanitary transportation rule published on April 6, 2016, days after the March 31, 2016 submission deadline. The intentional adulteration rule, the food defense rule, published on May 27, 2016, days before the May 31, 2016 submission deadline. Read together, the two chronologies show a program that missed its statutory deadlines by years and then met, in substance, the negotiated deadlines that replaced them. Congress set the first timetable and the agency failed to meet any part of it. Litigation produced a second timetable and the agency completed the foundational program on that timetable.

The codifications placed the new program inside the existing structure of the Code of Federal Regulations. The preventive controls rule for human food sits at 21 C.F.R. Part 117. The produce safety rule sits at 21 C.F.R. Part 112. The foreign supplier verification program sits at 21 C.F.R. Part 1, subpart L. The accredited third party certification program sits at Part 1, subpart M. The sanitary transportation rule sits at Part 1, subpart O. The food defense rule sits at 21 C.F.R. Part 121. The foundational rules do not occupy a single unified part. The dispersed codification mirrors the structure of the statute itself, which built the prevention program by amending and adding to the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 provision by provision rather than by enacting a freestanding food code.

Publication did not end the implementation timetable. A final rule states the standard and sets the dates by which categories of operations must comply, and in this program those dates extended the story of sequencing well beyond 2016. The length of that extension was deliberate design rather than drift, and the design logic requires separate explanation.

### The Logic of Staggered Compliance

Every foundational rule staggered its compliance dates by the size of the operation. Businesses generally faced the earliest dates. Small businesses faced later dates. Very small businesses faced dates later than both other groups. The foreign supplier verification program keyed later dates to supplier size and rule coverage rather than to a single date for all importers. The design rested on a judgment about capacity. Compliance with a preventive controls rule required an operation to develop or revise a written food safety plan, to have a qualified individual prepare or oversee that plan, and to build monitoring, corrective action, verification, and recordkeeping practices around the plan. Compliance with the produce standards required changes in practices on farms, in areas from water use to worker training. Those tasks fell on operations with very different administrative depth. A large facility with dedicated food safety staff could absorb planning and documentation duties on a shorter schedule than a small operation in which the owner performed several functions. Staggering translated that difference into law. It gave smaller operations more time without exempting them from the standard.

Staggering also carried costs that the design accepted. For a period of years after publication, the prevention program applied in full to some operations and had not yet attached to others in the same supply chain. The Tester Hagan qualified exemptions operated alongside the staggered dates and should not be confused with them. An exemption or a set of modified requirements removed or reduced the standard itself for an operation that met the statutory test. A staggered date left the standard intact and moved the date by which the operation had to meet it.

One set of requirements moved even after the final produce rule published. The agricultural water requirements carried later dates set by a 2019 extension rule, and the compliance dates for those provisions separated from the dates for the remainder of the produce rule. Publication of a final rule in November 2015 did not produce a single moment at which produce safety obligations became complete. The rule arrived in layers, with general compliance phased by farm size and one major component deferred by a later extension rulemaking.

### Money as a Cause of Delay

The appropriations gap ran underneath the entire chronology. The Food Safety Modernization Act did not itself raise inspection funding. Its authorizations depended on annual appropriations, which meant that each year of implementation required Congress to provide money through the appropriations process before the authorized activities could proceed at the authorized scale. The Congressional Budget Office estimated in 2010 roughly 1.4 billion dollars in additional federal spending over the first five years. That estimate described the cost of the program Congress had designed. It did not guarantee that appropriations would match the design.

Section 201 supplied that instance. The section set inspection frequencies for domestic facilities, with high risk facilities inspected on a shorter cycle than other facilities, and the agency could rely on federal, state, or local inspections for the domestic mandate. For foreign facilities, the section set a numerical trajectory. The statute called for not fewer than 600 foreign inspections in the first year after enactment, and not fewer than twice the previous year number in each of the five following years. Doubling on that path produced a trajectory to 19,200 inspections by the sixth year. Appropriations never funded that trajectory. The foreign inspection mandate therefore stood in the statute as an enacted instruction that the funded program never carried out at the stated scale.

The gap mattered to the rulemaking delay through a less mechanical channel. Rulemaking competed within the same constrained budget environment as inspection and the rest of the implementation program. Congress ordered seven foundational rules within roughly 18 months, financed the act through annual appropriations rather than through the statute itself, and left at least one numerical mandate in section 201 entirely unfunded at its stated scale. Deadline litigation could and did produce a schedule. It could not produce appropriations, and the decree schedule that resulted was a schedule for completing rules, not a remedy for the funding structure that had contributed to the delay.

### A Separate and Later Case

In October 2018, the Center for Food Safety and the Center for Environmental Health filed a separate suit concerning section 204 and its high risk foods designation and traceability recordkeeping requirements. That action concerned a different provision and a different claim from the 2012 deadline case, and the two actions stand as distinct proceedings on distinct records.

## The Food Safety Modernization Act in Enforcement and in Effect

Enforcement under the Food Safety Modernization Act rested on a premise that differed from the premise of the 1938 framework. The older framework had allowed the Food and Drug Administration to act against food that was already adulterated, through seizure of goods, injunctions, and criminal referral, and to rely on current good manufacturing practice regulations. Prevention plans, hazard analysis, and produce standards changed what a facility had to do before contamination occurred. The enforcement tools added by the statute determined what followed when a facility failed to meet those duties or a responsible party refused to remove a product. The design joined a small number of forceful sanctions to a larger structure of inspections, reports, and records, a structure that depended on partners and on appropriations the statute did not itself supply.

### How did registration suspension change enforcement against a facility?

Section 102 gave the Food and Drug Administration power to suspend the registration of a facility that manufactured, processed, packed, or held food. Suspension barred the facility from distributing food. The sanction operated against the registration itself, so loss of registration functioned as a shutdown of lawful distribution rather than as a fine alone.

Registration had existed before the statute as an identification requirement. The statute kept that duty and added biennial renewal between October 1 and December 31 of each even numbered year. The addition of suspension transformed registration from a roster into a condition of operation. A facility that lost its registration lost the legal basis for distributing food, even though suspension did not by itself seize product or resolve a criminal case. The agency could stop the flow of food from a facility through an administrative act directed at status, rather than proceeding only against particular lots of adulterated product after the product had entered commerce. That sequence reversed the older pattern, which had required action against goods. Suspension also supplied a consequence beyond an adverse inspection report, because a facility faced loss of the registration on which distribution depended if the statutory grounds were met.

Inspection frequency under section 201 supplied the cadence against which that sanction operated. High risk domestic facilities were to be inspected not less often than once in the five years after enactment and not less often than once every three years thereafter. Other domestic facilities were to be inspected once in the seven years after enactment and not less often than once every five years thereafter. The same section allowed the Food and Drug Administration to rely on federal, state, or local inspections for the domestic mandate, a reliance written into the statute because the inspection numbers could not be met by federal inspectors alone. Partner inspections could extend coverage, at the price that quality and depth depended in part on systems outside direct federal control.

Mandatory recall under section 206 operated on a different trigger and through a different sequence. The section added section 423 of the 1938 act. If there was a reasonable probability that an article of food was adulterated or misbranded and that use of the article would cause serious adverse health consequences or death, the agency first had to give the responsible party an opportunity to cease distribution and to recall the product voluntarily. Only if the party refused or failed to act could the agency order the recall, with an informal hearing available. The order of steps preserved voluntary recall as the first route, and the new power changed the negotiation that preceded removal because refusal no longer ended the matter. Section 423 did not extend to infant formula, because section 412 of the 1938 act, enacted by the Infant Formula Act of 1980, already carried a mandatory recall procedure for infant formula that failed to meet quality and notification requirements.

Accountability for use of the recall power took the form of reporting. The statute required the Department of Health and Human Services to report annually to Congress on use of the mandatory recall authority. An annual report compelled the department to place each use into a congressional record, and Congress could compare the authority granted in 2011 with the authority exercised in each later year. The first mandatory recall order illustrated the reserve character of the power. On April 2, 2018, the Food and Drug Administration issued a mandatory recall order against Triangle Pharmanaturals LLC of Las Vegas for powdered kratom products contaminated with Salmonella, after the company refused a request for a voluntary recall made on March 30, 2018. The order arrived more than seven years after enactment. The authority had changed the consequences of refusal from enactment, because every voluntary negotiation occurred under the possibility of compulsion, yet its practical force during that long interval rested on deterrence rather than on demonstrated orders.

Section 402 added protection for the people positioned to see violations first. The section provided whistleblower protection for employees of covered entities who reported violations. Inspectors visited on a schedule set by section 201, and even the most frequent domestic cycle left long intervals between visits. Employees worked inside the process each day and could observe practices that an inspection might miss, including failures to follow a food safety plan. Protection for reporting reduced the personal cost of bringing such information to attention, and the statute treated employee knowledge as a channel that enforcement needed alongside inspection and records. Read together, sections 102, 201, 206, and 402 described an enforcement posture that combined periodic outside inspection, an internal reporting channel, a status sanction against facilities, and a product sanction against responsible parties that refused to act.

## The Gap Between Enactment and Operative Law

A statute changes legal duties on the date of enactment only to the extent that its provisions operate without further rulemaking. Much of the Food Safety Modernization Act required rules before facilities, farms, and importers could comply with detailed standards, and the rulemaking record determined when prevention became operative in specific sectors. The statute ordered the principal rules on deadlines of roughly eighteen months, with preventive controls and produce safety among them. The Food and Drug Administration missed all seven foundational rulemaking deadlines. The Center for Food Safety and the Center for Environmental Health sued the agency in August 2012 in the Northern District of California, arguing unlawful withholding under the Administrative Procedure Act. Judge Phyllis J. Hamilton ruled for the plaintiffs in 2013, and a consent decree filed on February 20, 2014 set submission deadlines for the seven foundational rules. Final rules then appeared on staggered dates, with preventive controls published on September 17, 2015, produce safety, foreign supplier verification, and accredited third party certification published on November 27, 2015, sanitary transportation published on April 6, 2016, and intentional adulteration published on May 27, 2016. Compliance dates extended further, staggered by business size and by rule, with preventive controls for human food reaching businesses generally on September 19, 2016 and very small businesses on September 17, 2018, and produce safety reaching large farms on January 27, 2020. A signature in 2011 therefore did not describe the date on which a grower, a processor, or an importer faced a complete set of detailed duties.

The Jensen Farms outbreak gave that interval a precise and severe illustration. In September 2011, Listeria contamination in cantaloupes from Jensen Farms produced an outbreak that the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention counted at 147 illnesses and 33 deaths. The outbreak occurred after enactment of the statute and before any produce rule existed. Produce safety rulemaking under section 105 would later establish the first statutory federal standards for growing, harvesting, packing, and holding produce. None of that detailed framework governed the 2011 cantaloupe season as an operative final rule. The point is chronological rather than speculative. The outbreak did not prove that a finished produce rule would have prevented the contamination at a particular operation. The outbreak demonstrated a narrower proposition with full force, that enactment and operation are different events, and assessment that begins the compliance clock in January 2011 misstates the record for every sector whose duties depended on a final rule.

## Measured Effect and the Contest Over Attribution

By the staggered compliance dates, the compliance architecture was in place. Facilities subject to preventive controls had duties to analyze hazards and to maintain written food safety plans prepared or overseen by a qualified individual. Farms subject to the produce rule faced standards for water, soil amendments, animals, workers, and equipment. Importers faced foreign supplier verification duties, and rules on sanitary transportation and intentional adulteration completed the foundational set. Architecture of that kind can be verified directly, through plans, monitoring records, corrective actions, and verification. Outcome measurement presented a harder problem, because illness incidence reflects contamination, consumption patterns, detection, laboratory practice, and reporting as well as facility compliance.

FoodNet surveillance supplied the principal outcome record available by the article date. FoodNet data through 2019 showed no decline in several major pathogen incidence rates against the 2016 to 2018 comparison period, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in its 2020 FoodNet report recorded increases for several pathogens. That record limited the claims that evidence could support. A claim that the statute had measurably cut illness by 2019 exceeded the strength of the surveillance data. Outcome attribution remained contested among analysts, with the dispute centered on the meaning of flat or rising incidence during a period when the compliance architecture had reached full staggered application only toward the end of the decade.

Analysts who defended the statute on architecture first grounds held that prevention systems take years to register in incidence data. The strongest version of that position began with the design choice itself. Congress replaced a reactive model, under which the agency acted against adulterated food already in commerce, with duties to identify hazards before release and to control them through monitored plans. A hazard analysis could remove a failure mode that would otherwise have produced sporadic contamination, and such avoided events leave no case count, because surveillance counts detected illness rather than contamination events that never reached a consumer. On that reasoning, incidence data lagged the intervention, especially where compliance dates were staggered by size. Incidence that held steady might also represent deterioration avoided rather than failure, although the surveillance record alone could not prove that counterfactual.

Analysts who pressed an outcome first criticism held that a decade produced rules and plans without measured reductions. The strongest version of that position treated incidence as the purpose against which the statute had asked to be judged. The burden estimates that built the case for enactment counted illness on a large scale, with about 48 million Americans affected each year, about 128,000 hospitalizations, and about 3,000 deaths in the estimate by Scallan and coauthors published in Emerging Infectious Diseases in 2011. Against a burden of that scale, that line of criticism held, a system of plans, records, and inspections had to show movement in illness rates within a reasonable period, or the claim of prevention remained an assertion about process. The criticism also drew on delay and underfunding, because a prevention system that arrives late prevents less than the enacted design promised. Neither position could be closed with the data available by November 15, 2020.

## What the Statute Left Unchanged

The limits of the statute were structural as well as temporal. The act did not consolidate the fragmented agency structure that the Government Accountability Office had placed on its High Risk List in 2007 and had kept there. It did not move meat inspection or poultry inspection from the United States Department of Agriculture to the Food and Drug Administration. It did not create a single food safety agency. The boundary between the two largest regulators remained the boundary drawn by older statutes. The Food Safety and Inspection Service regulated meat, poultry, processed egg products, and catfish under separate statutes. The Food and Drug Administration regulated shell eggs and the rest of the food supply, roughly 80 percent of food spending by the account of the Congressional Research Service, against roughly 20 percent for products regulated by the Department of Agriculture. The statute strengthened the larger side of that division and left the division itself in place.

Funding formed the second structural limit. The statute did not fund itself, and its authorizations depended on annual appropriations. The Congressional Budget Office estimated roughly 1.4 billion dollars in additional federal spending over the first five years, stated as an estimate made in 2010. Only appropriations could pay inspectors and support the partner systems on which domestic inspection reliance depended. Foreign inspection exposed the gap between written scale and funded scale with unusual clarity. Section 201 required not fewer than 600 foreign facility inspections in the first year after enactment and not fewer than twice the previous year number in each of the five following years, a trajectory that reached 19,200 inspections by the sixth year. Appropriations never funded that trajectory. Any assessment that quotes the trajectory as an achievement misreads authorization as operation.

## The Closing Assessment

The namable claim at the center of the Food Safety Modernization Act was specific enough to state in one sentence. For more than seventy years, from the 1938 framework to the signing on January 4, 2011, the food agency could not compel removal of contaminated food from the general food supply, and every recall before 2011 was voluntary. Companies complied in the overwhelming run of cases. The cases that produced the statute were the refusals, the delays, and the ingredient suppliers whose contamination spread through hundreds of downstream products before removal could be compelled. The statute changed the premise of regulation in response to that record. Prevention replaced reaction as the organizing principle, hazard analysis became a legal duty rather than a voluntary practice for covered facilities, produce acquired federal standards for the first time, importers acquired verification duties for foreign suppliers, registration acquired a suspension sanction, and voluntary recall acquired a mandatory order behind refusal. The new premise held that government could require a regulated party to find hazards first, to document controls, to submit to inspection on a risk based cycle, and to remove product under compulsion when the statutory standard for serious harm was met.

Measured against that premise, the record through November 15, 2020 was mixed in identifiable ways. The foundational rules existed, compliance dates had run for most covered operations, and the first mandatory recall order had demonstrated that section 423 could operate when a company refused a voluntary request. The same record contained missed deadlines corrected only after litigation, a foreign inspection trajectory that appropriations never matched, an agency structure that remained fragmented, and FoodNet incidence that showed no decline in several major pathogens against the 2016 to 2018 comparison period. The statute succeeded in changing what the law required and in changing the consequences of refusal. The statute had not, by the evidence available at the article date, demonstrated a measured national reduction in foodborne illness, and it had not removed the structural divisions or funding dependence that limited federal food oversight before enactment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the Food Safety Modernization Act change?

The Food Safety Modernization Act replaced a reactive framework with a preventive framework under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938. Before enactment, the Food and Drug Administration acted mainly against food that was already adulterated, through seizure, injunctions, and criminal referral. The statute added a written food safety plan duty for covered facilities, science based standards for produce production, verification duties for importers, sanitary transportation rulemaking, mitigation against intentional adulteration, biennial facility registration renewal with suspension power, mandated inspection frequencies, and a mandatory recall authority for defined cases of serious risk. Prevention became a legal duty assigned to facilities, farms, and importers before distribution.

### Q: Did the Food Safety Modernization Act create mandatory recall authority?

Yes. Section 206 added section 423 of the 1938 act, codified at 21 U.S.C. 350l, and gave the Food and Drug Administration authority to order a recall. The authority applied where a reasonable probability existed that food was adulterated or misbranded and that use of the food would cause serious adverse health consequences or death. The agency first had to give the responsible party an opportunity to cease distribution and recall the food voluntarily. An order could follow only after refusal or failure to act, with an informal hearing available. Section 423 did not extend to infant formula, because section 412 already carried a mandatory recall procedure for formula.

### Q: What is a preventive controls plan under the Food Safety Modernization Act?

A preventive controls plan is the written food safety plan required by section 418 of the 1938 act, added by section 103 and codified at 21 U.S.C. 350g. The owner, operator, or agent in charge of a covered facility must evaluate known or reasonably foreseeable hazards, prepare and implement a plan with preventive controls for those hazards, monitor the controls, take corrective actions when a control fails, verify that the controls work, reanalyze the plan at least once every three years, and keep records. A qualified individual must prepare the plan or oversee preparation. The rule for human food was codified at 21 C.F.R. Part 117.

### Q: What does the Food Safety Modernization Act produce rule cover?

The produce rule implemented section 419 of the 1938 act, added by section 105 and codified at 21 U.S.C. 350h. Section 419 established the first statutory federal standards for growing, harvesting, packing, and holding covered produce. The standards addressed worker training and health and hygiene, agricultural water, biological soil amendments of animal origin, domesticated and wild animals, and equipment, tools, and buildings. Sprouts received special requirements beyond the general standards. The final produce safety rule published on November 27, 2015 and was codified at 21 C.F.R. Part 112. Compliance dates were staggered by farm size, beginning on January 27, 2020 for large farms.

### Q: Who is exempt under the Food Safety Modernization Act?

Exemption depended on the rule. Farms averaging 25,000 dollars or less in annual produce sales were fully exempt from the produce rule, and produce rarely consumed raw, produce receiving commercial processing that adequately reduces pathogens, and produce for personal or on farm consumption were also exempt. A separate qualified status applied under the Tester-Hagan amendment where average annual sales of all food over three years fell under 500,000 dollars and more than half of that food by value went directly to qualified end users. Qualified farms followed modified requirements, including labeling, and qualified facilities followed modified requirements under preventive controls. Registration, inspection, and mandatory recall were not exempted.

### Q: How does the Food Safety Modernization Act treat imported food?

The statute placed verification duties on importers rather than resting assurance on border examination alone. Section 301 added section 805 of the 1938 act, requiring risk based foreign supplier verification that imported food complied with section 418 or section 419 or with equivalent protection. Section 302 created the Voluntary Qualified Importer Program for expedited review and entry. Section 307 provided for certification of foreign facilities by auditors accredited through accreditation bodies recognized by the Food and Drug Administration. Certification could be required for designated foods and counted toward qualified importer eligibility. The foreign supplier verification rule published on November 27, 2015.

### Q: Why did Food Safety Modernization Act rules take years to finalize?

The principal rules carried statutory deadlines of roughly 18 months, and the Food and Drug Administration missed all seven foundational rulemaking deadlines. Proposed rules appeared in 2013, and supplemental proposals followed in September 2014 after heavy comment, with the produce proposal drawing more than 15,000 comments in a first round. The Center for Food Safety and the Center for Environmental Health sued in August 2012, Judge Phyllis J. Hamilton ruled for the plaintiffs in 2013, and a consent decree filed on February 20, 2014 set submission deadlines. Final rules published between September 17, 2015 and May 27, 2016, years after the statutory dates.

### Q: How often must facilities be inspected under the Food Safety Modernization Act?

Section 201 set inspection frequencies by risk category. High risk domestic facilities were to be inspected not less often than once in the five years after enactment and not less often than once every three years thereafter. Other domestic facilities were to be inspected once in the seven years after enactment and not less often than once every five years thereafter. The Food and Drug Administration could rely on federal, state, or local inspections for the domestic mandate. For foreign facilities, the statute required not fewer than 600 inspections in the first year after enactment and not fewer than twice the previous year number in each of the five following years.

### Q: What is the public law number of the Food Safety Modernization Act?

The public law number is Public Law 111-353, published at 124 Stat. 3885. President Barack Obama signed the measure on January 4, 2011. The enacted text was the Senate bill S. 510 from the 111th Congress, sponsored chiefly by Senator Richard Durbin and managed by Senator Tom Harkin. Because section 107 fee provisions in a Senate originated bill raised an Origination Clause objection in the House, the Senate stripped a House originated vehicle, H.R. 2751, on December 19, 2010 and inserted the S. 510 text. The House passed that vehicle on December 21, 2010 by 215 to 144.

### Q: Does the Food Safety Modernization Act cover meat and poultry?

No. The statute did not move meat or poultry regulation to the Food and Drug Administration and did not consolidate the federal food safety agencies. The Food Safety and Inspection Service in the Department of Agriculture retained regulation of meat under the Federal Meat Inspection Act, poultry under the Poultry Products Inspection Act, processed egg products under the Egg Products Inspection Act, and catfish. The Food and Drug Administration regulated shell eggs and the rest of the food supply. By the account of the Congressional Research Service, that remainder represented roughly 80 percent of food spending, against roughly 20 percent for Department of Agriculture regulated products.

### Q: Which outbreaks led to the Food Safety Modernization Act?

Four episodes built the case in the record before enactment. In 2006, E. coli O157:H7 in bagged fresh spinach caused 205 illnesses and 3 deaths. In 2008, a Salmonella Saintpaul outbreak caused more than 1,400 illnesses and was traced to peppers grown in Mexico after tomatoes were first blamed. From 2008 to 2009, Salmonella Typhimurium in Peanut Corporation of America peanut products caused 714 illnesses in 46 states and 9 deaths, with more than 3,600 products recalled. In 2010, Salmonella Enteritidis in shell eggs from Iowa producers led to a recall of about 550 million eggs, with an estimated 1,939 illnesses associated with the outbreak period.

### Q: What must importers verify under the foreign supplier verification program?

Under section 805 of the 1938 act, an importer must perform risk based foreign supplier verification activities showing that food was produced in compliance with section 418 or section 419 or with equivalent protection. The program described in the statute includes hazard analysis, evaluation of the supplier, verification activities such as audits or testing, corrective actions, and records. The importer is the United States owner or consignee of the food at entry, or the United States agent of the foreign owner or consignee. Initial compliance with the foreign supplier verification program fell on May 30, 2017, with later dates keyed to supplier size and rule coverage.

### Q: What is the Voluntary Qualified Importer Program under the Food Safety Modernization Act?

The Voluntary Qualified Importer Program is the program created by section 302 through section 806 of the 1938 act, codified at 21 U.S.C. 384b. The program gives importers that qualify expedited review and entry of food, for a fee. Qualification rests on supplier controls, use of certified facilities, and compliance history. Certification under the accredited third party system in section 307 counts toward eligibility for the program. The Food and Drug Administration began accepting applications for the program in 2018. The program placed an incentive beside the verification duty in section 301, rewarding importers that built documented supplier controls and used certified facilities.

### Q: What does the Food Safety Modernization Act food defense rule require?

The food defense rule implemented section 106, which directed science based mitigation strategies against intentional adulteration at vulnerable points in the food supply. The rule, codified at 21 C.F.R. Part 121 and published in final form on May 27, 2016, required a written food defense plan with mitigation strategies at actionable process steps for registered facilities that produce food for consumption in the United States. Modified requirements applied by business size. Compliance fell on July 26, 2019 for covered facilities generally and on July 27, 2020 for small businesses, and the rule set July 26, 2021 for very small businesses.

### Q: Has the mandatory recall authority in the Food Safety Modernization Act been used?

Yes, in one documented instance by the article date of November 15, 2020. On April 2, 2018, the Food and Drug Administration issued a mandatory recall order against Triangle Pharmanaturals LLC of Las Vegas for powdered kratom products contaminated with Salmonella. The agency had requested a voluntary recall on March 30, 2018, and the company refused that request. The order was the first mandatory recall order issued under section 423, and the order arrived more than seven years after enactment on January 4, 2011. The statute also required annual reports to Congress on use of the authority.

### Q: What is the difference between a farm and a facility under the Food Safety Modernization Act?

A farm and a facility occupy different positions under the statute. Section 418, the preventive controls provision, does not apply to farms as such, and the written food safety plan duty attaches to a covered facility through its owner, operator, or agent in charge. A facility is an establishment that manufactures, processes, packs, or holds food and must register under section 415, with renewal in each even numbered year. A farm grows produce and can be covered directly by section 419, which sets standards for growing, harvesting, packing, and holding produce. An operation can therefore stand outside section 418 as a farm and face produce standards as a farm.

### Q: What records can the Food and Drug Administration inspect under the Food Safety Modernization Act?

Records form part of the preventive controls duty in section 418. The owner, operator, or agent in charge of a covered facility must keep records of the written food safety plan and of the procedures and results generated by that plan, covering monitoring of preventive controls, corrective actions taken when a control failed, and verification that the controls worked. Reanalysis of the plan at least once every three years also rests on that record. Under the foreign supplier verification program in section 805, importers likewise must keep records of hazard analysis, supplier evaluation, verification activities, and corrective actions for imported food.

### Q: Does the Food Safety Modernization Act cover animal food?

Yes. Section 103 directed the preventive controls rulemaking that produced rules for both human food and animal food. The final preventive controls rules for human food and for animal food published together in the Federal Register on September 17, 2015. The animal food rule imposed good manufacturing practice and preventive controls for animal food on facilities that manufacture, process, pack, or hold animal food, with qualified facilities following modified requirements. Compliance for animal food was phased in by business size between September 2016 and September 2019. The human food rule was codified at 21 C.F.R. Part 117.

### Q: When did the Food Safety Modernization Act requirements take effect?

Requirements took effect through staggered compliance dates set by each final rule rather than on a single date after enactment in 2011. Preventive controls for human food applied on September 19, 2016, with small businesses on September 18, 2017 and very small businesses on September 17, 2018. Foreign supplier verification began on May 30, 2017. Sanitary transportation applied on April 6, 2017 and April 6, 2018 for small businesses. Food defense applied on July 26, 2019, with later dates in 2020 and 2021 by size. Produce safety applied on January 27, 2020 for large farms, and the rule set January 26, 2021 for small farms and January 26, 2022 for very small farms.

### Q: What is the legal status of the Food Safety Modernization Act?

The statute is an enacted public law that amended the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938. Enacted as Public Law 111-353 on January 4, 2011, the measure added sections 418, 419, and 423 and sections 805, 806, and 808 to the 1938 act, among other provisions. The foundational rules issued under the statute in 2015 and 2016 were codified in the Code of Federal Regulations, including Part 117 for preventive controls for human food, Part 112 for produce safety, and Part 121 for food defense. No repeal of the statute had been enacted by any Congress through 2020.
