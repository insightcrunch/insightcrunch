The dictatorship of the proletariat is the most quoted phrase in Marxism and the least examined. It is quoted by opponents as proof of what Marxism always intended, and by defenders as a term of art that hostile readers wilfully misunderstand, and in both uses the phrase does its work before anyone has asked the prior questions: what did the words mean when Marx used them, how often did he use them at all, and what was later done under their authority by people who claimed his inheritance. Those are three different questions, with three different kinds of answer, and the confusion of the phrase's career begins whenever they are merged into one. This article keeps them apart from its first page to its last. The meaning question is a question about texts and about the vocabulary of a century, and it can be answered with some precision. The verdict question is a question about regimes, institutions and human consequences, and it belongs to a different evidentiary world, where the records are archives, demographic reconstructions and the testimony of survivors. An article that answers the first question and behaves as though the second had been answered too has cheated its reader, whichever direction the cheating runs.

Begin with the fact that surprises most readers when they first meet it plainly stated. Marx used the phrase rarely. It is not a refrain running through his work, not the centrepiece of the Communist Manifesto, not a concept he defined at length in a treatise devoted to it. It appears a handful of times across a writing life of four decades, in a history of French politics written in the aftermath of revolution, in a private letter summarising his own contribution to the theory of class struggle, and in a set of marginal notes on a party programme that he never published in his lifetime. Everything else is commentary: Engels restating it, Lenin weaponising it, Kautsky contesting it, Luxemburg qualifying it, party programmes codifying it, constitutions inscribing it, and Western parties eventually striking it from their platforms as unusable. The phrase has a biography far larger than its birthplace, and a glossary article that confined itself to the birthplace would leave the reader unprepared for every argument in which the phrase actually figures.

The reader this article serves is the one who has met the phrase in an argument and wants to know what can responsibly be said about it. Perhaps a speaker has claimed that Marx advocated dictatorship and that the matter is closed. Perhaps a defender has replied that Marx meant something entirely different by the word and that the matter is equally closed. Both closures are premature, and they are premature in symmetrical ways, which is the first hint that the article's organising instrument will be a symmetry. The defender is right about the older meaning and wrong if the older meaning is treated as a verdict on the twentieth century. The critic is right that regimes ruling under the phrase built systems of one party dominance and repression, and wrong if that record is treated as a dictionary, as though later practice could retroactively fix what a word meant in 1850 or 1875. Meaning and verdict travel separately. The phrase meant class rule. Regimes acting in its name did things that have to be described in their own terms, neither softened by the etymology nor needed to establish it.

## Dictatorship of the proletariat: the two questions separated

Every dispute about this phrase is two disputes wearing one coat. The first asks what the term meant: what its component words carried in the middle of the nineteenth century, how Marx deployed them in the few places he deployed them, and what distinctions the term was built to draw. The second asks what followed: how the term was taken up, codified, fought over and eventually abandoned, and what institutions and outcomes stand in the historical record under its banner. Call the first the meaning question and the second the verdict question. They are not independent, since a term's meaning shapes what can plausibly be claimed in its name, but neither settles the other. A term can mean something comparatively precise and still be available for uses its coiner never described. A practice can be indefensible on its own record without altering what a dead author's word meant in its own decade. Readers who hold the two questions apart can follow every stage of the argument that follows. Readers who merge them will find each section of this article answering a question they did not ask, because each section addresses one question at a time and says which one it is addressing.

The separation also disciplines the article's own procedure. When the subject is Marx's texts, the evidence is textual: editions, letters, dates, the company the phrase keeps in the sentences where it occurs. When the subject is the Soviet or Chinese or Cambodian record, the evidence is the historical scholarship on those states, and figures appear as attributed scholarly ranges rather than as single numbers, because the scholarship itself works in ranges and an honest summary has to preserve that shape. When the subject is the dispute of 1918, the evidence is two pamphlets and a prison manuscript, each answering the others point by point. Nothing in the article asks the reader to accept a conclusion about one question as a premise for another. Where the two questions genuinely touch, at the point where critics argue that the concept itself lacked an internal limit and thereby invited its later career, the article gives that argument a section of its own, states it at full strength, and answers it with the strongest material available, including the concessions the material forces.

A word about what this article declines to do. It does not retell the general Marxist theory of the state, which has its own exposition in the [marxist theory of the state guide](/2012/12/01/marxist-theory-of-the-state-guide/), and it touches doctrine only where a doctrine bears directly on the meaning of one phrase. It does not try the case for or against Marxism as a whole, and it does not conclude that any political arrangement, named in these pages or absent from them, is preferable to another. Its ambition is narrower and, for a phrase this loaded, harder: to leave the reader able to test any use of the term, in a speech, a textbook, an examination answer or an editorial, against the evidence of what the words meant and what the record shows, and to notice immediately when a speaker is answering one of the two questions while claiming to have answered both.

## The symmetry test: the instrument the reader keeps

The article's central claim can be named at once, in the manner of this series, so that the reader can watch it work rather than wait for it to be announced. The symmetry test is this. In Marx's usage, dictatorship names the rule of a class, any class, and it names that rule whatever constitutional clothing the rule wears. A monarchy resting on landed property is, in this vocabulary, the dictatorship of an aristocracy. A parliamentary republic resting on the political dominance of property owners is the dictatorship of the bourgeoisie. A transitional order in which the working class holds state power is the dictatorship of the proletariat. The word does not change its meaning as it crosses from one case to the others. It marks which class rules, and it is silent, deliberately silent, about the form through which the ruling is done. A use of the phrase passes the symmetry test when it applies the one meaning evenly across all the cases. A use fails the test when the word is allowed to mean class rule in one sentence and personal tyranny in the next, or when the proletarian case is described with the vocabulary of despotism while the bourgeois case is described with the vocabulary of constitutional normality, without any acknowledgement that the term itself draws no such distinction.

Run the test on the commonest claim made about the phrase, the claim that it proves Marx wanted a dictatorship in the sense that a later century attached to that word: a single ruler, a single party, suspended law, terror as an instrument of government. The test asks whether the claimant applies the same reading to Marx's description of the parliamentary republic as a bourgeois dictatorship. If republican France, with its elections, its assembly, its press and its property franchise, counts in Marx's sentence as a dictatorship, then the word in that sentence cannot simultaneously mean rule by one man through terror, because the French case exhibits neither. The claimant who insists on the terror reading for the proletarian phrase and the class reading for the bourgeois phrase has not interpreted Marx. He has translated one occurrence and refused to translate the other, and the refusal, not the text, generates the conclusion. That is the symmetry test doing its work: it does not decide what the reader should think about Marxism, and it does not acquit or convict anyone. It enforces one meaning per word per author, which is the minimum condition of reading at all.

The test also cuts in the opposite direction, against a defence that has become as routine as the attack. A defender sometimes argues that because the phrase meant class rule, nothing that later called itself a dictatorship of the proletariat can be laid at the concept's door, since class rule by the immense majority is a different thing from the party states that history produced. The symmetry test asks the defender a matching question. If the word identifies which class rules and is silent about form, then a regime's claim to embody the phrase stands or falls on evidence about who actually ruled: the class in whose name power was held, or a party and state apparatus ruling over that class among others. Where the evidence shows the second, the defender cannot rescue the regime by etymology, and where the evidence shows the second, the concept's own criterion is what exposes the gap. Symmetric reading is uncomfortable for both camps, which is the sign that it is a reading discipline and not a debating device.

Hold on to a third consequence, because the article's second half depends on it. If the term in Marx marks class content and not form, then the great shift in its career can be stated precisely. Across the twentieth century the phrase came to name a form: a type of regime, recognisable by its institutional features, one party, fused party and state, suppressed opposition, a security apparatus answerable to the leadership. The shift from content-word to form-word is the single most important event in the phrase's biography, more important than any individual dispute, because it changed what the phrase could be used to do. As a content-word it analysed. As a form-word it legitimated, identified friends and enemies, and eventually embarrassed the parties that inherited it, until several of them formally discarded it. The usage timeline set out later in the article arranges that shift in a row of stages, and the sections on Kautsky, Lenin and Luxemburg show the shift beginning, contested from the first moment it appeared.

### Did Marx mean dictatorship in the modern sense?

No, and the textual evidence is unusually direct. He used dictatorship in the older sense of class rule, and described the parliamentary republic as a dictatorship of the bourgeoisie within the same analysis, applying one word to both cases. The term registers which class holds state power, not whether that power runs through one party or one person.

That answer needs its qualification immediately, because a bare no invites the reader to close the subject at exactly the point where it becomes serious. The older sense of dictatorship was not a gentle sense. Class rule, in the analysis Marx inherited and sharpened, meant that one class's dominance was secured by the whole apparatus of law, property and armed force, and that the dominated classes experienced that apparatus as coercion whether or not a single despot sat at its head. To say the word meant class rule is not to say it meant something mild. It is to say where the word pointed: at the identity of the ruling class rather than at the personal character of a ruler or the constitutional shape of a regime. A parliamentary republic could be, in this vocabulary, a dictatorship with elections. A revolutionary government could be, in this vocabulary, a dictatorship with universal suffrage and recallable officials. The combinations sound paradoxical only because a later century taught the word to point somewhere else.

The word's later pointing is itself a historical fact that the glossary has to register rather than resent. Words change their centres of gravity under the pressure of events, and dictatorship changed under the pressure of the regimes of the nineteen twenties and thirties, fascist and communist alike, which made the personal and party senses of the word the ones every newspaper reader knew. By the time Western communist parties debated whether to keep the phrase in the nineteen seventies, they were debating a word whose public meaning they no longer controlled. A glossary article written honestly has to hold both facts at once: the meaning Marx used, recoverable from his texts and his century, and the meaning the phrase acquired, recoverable from the record of its career. The sections that follow take the two in order, and the verdict sections never pretend that recovering the first disposes of the second.

## The word before Marx: Rome, emergency and a borrowed vocabulary

Dictatorship entered European political language from Rome, and the Roman institution explains the word's older centre of gravity better than any dictionary. The dictator of the Roman republic was a magistrate appointed in an emergency, charged with a specific task, and expected to lay the office down when the task was done, with six months as the conventional outer limit. The office concentrated power to an extraordinary degree while it lasted: the dictator's imperium overrode the ordinary magistrates, and the usual checks of collegiality and appeal were suspended. But the institution was defined by its commission and its term, not by the personal character of its holder, and the exemplum every schoolboy in Europe once knew was Cincinnatus, summoned from his plough, victorious in a matter of weeks, and back at the plough before the season turned. The point of the legend was not that Rome loved strongmen. The point was that concentrated power could be an instrument of the republic, held briefly and surrendered on schedule.

The institution's degeneration was equally part of the inherited picture, and nineteenth-century readers knew that too. Sulla took the dictatorship on an open-ended commission to remake the state, and Caesar took it for life, at which point the office had ceased to be the republic's instrument and had become its replacement. The word therefore carried a double inheritance into modern politics: an emergency concentration of power that was temporary and commissioned by definition, and the memory that such commissions could be stretched into permanence by the men who held them. Both halves of the inheritance matter for understanding what happened to Marx's phrase later. The first half explains how nineteenth-century writers could use the word without meaning a tyranny. The second half explains why critics of the twentieth-century regimes could argue, with some force, that the Roman danger was built into the word from the start, and that a commission without an enforceable term is an invitation to the Sulla outcome.

Between Rome and Marx the word lived a busy life in the revolutionary vocabulary of France, and that life is the immediate source of Marx's usage. The French Revolution had governed through emergency bodies and had debated dictatorship as a revolutionary necessity; the conspiratorial tradition that followed, above all Auguste Blanqui and his followers, made the dictatorship of a revolutionary minority a standing element of insurrectionary doctrine, the bridge between the overthrow of the old order and the education of the new. When Marx wrote about France around 1850, the word was already in circulation among the Parisian revolutionaries he knew, argued with and sometimes organised beside. He did not mint it. He took a term from the working vocabulary of the revolutionary movement and bent it to a different referent: not the rule of a conspiratorial committee, and not the emergency magistracy of a republic, but the rule of a class. That bending is the original act this article has to explain, because everything in the phrase's later career is a bending back or a bending further.

### What did dictatorship mean in Rome?

In the Roman republic it named a temporary emergency magistracy, commissioned for one task and bounded by a term of months, in which ordinary checks were suspended so a single magistrate could act with undivided authority. The office was an instrument of the republic, not its replacement, until Sulla and Caesar stretched the commission into permanence.

Notice what the Roman sense does and does not contain. It contains concentration: one will, temporarily unimpeded. It contains exception: the normal rules are in abeyance for the duration. It does not contain a theory of who should hold the office beyond the commission's purpose, and it does not describe a permanent form of state. When nineteenth-century writers reached for the word, they reached for the concentration and the exception, and they argued about the rest. Marx's distinctive move was to attach the word to a class subject and a historical process rather than to a magistrate and a commission, and that move changed the word's grammar. A magistrate can resign. A class cannot resign, and a process has no term fixed by law. The question of how a class dictatorship ends, which the Roman institution answered with a calendar, becomes in Marx's usage a question about the transformation of society itself, and the difficulty of that question is one the article will meet again when it reaches the strongest objection to the concept.

The nineteenth century also knew a second, looser usage that matters for the symmetry test. Writers across the political spectrum spoke of the dictatorship of a class, a party, a press, even of public opinion, meaning by it the effective dominance of one social force over the conditions under which others lived. The usage was analytical rather than constitutional: it named where power actually lay, behind or beneath the formal arrangements. A landed interest could be said to dictate to a parliament that formally answered to voters; a moneyed interest could be said to dictate the terms on which governments borrowed and newspapers survived. Marx's usage belongs to this family and radicalises it. Where the loose usage was a metaphor for influence, Marx's was a theoretical claim about the state: that every state, whatever its form, is the organised power of one class for the suppression of another, and that calling this arrangement dictatorship in all its instances is not rhetoric but classification. The parliamentary republic does not escape the classification by holding elections, because the classification concerns the class character of the power the elections staff.

### How did the word's meaning shift across its career?

The shift was from a word that identified which class ruled to a word that identified a type of regime. In Rome it named an office, in the revolutionary nineteenth century a class relation analysed through any constitution, and in the twentieth century a form of government recognisable by its institutions, one party, fused party and state, suppressed opposition.

That single sentence summarises the usage timeline the article sets out in full below, and the reader who grasps it early will find the rest of the article easier to hold. Each stage of the timeline preserves something of the stage before and discards something else, and the disputes the article recounts are mostly disputes about which preservation counts. Kautsky insisted that the class-relation meaning was the only one Marx's texts licensed, and that the Bolsheviks had quietly substituted the regime meaning while keeping the word. Lenin, answering him, effectively embraced the regime meaning and gave it a definition of his own, insisting that dictatorship meant power unrestricted by law, whoever held it. Luxemburg tried to hold the class meaning and graft democratic form onto it, and said the graft was not optional. The Soviet constitutions then codified the regime meaning in law while continuing to speak the language of class. The Eurocommunist parties, two generations later, concluded that the word could no longer be heard in its class sense at all, and dropped it. A word that began by naming an office ended by naming an embarrassment, and the stages between are the article's subject.

## The phrase in Marx: three anchor texts and a long silence

The single most useful fact a reader can carry into any argument about this phrase is the thinness of its textual base. Marx wrote for forty years, on economics, history, philosophy, journalism and party politics, and the expression dictatorship of the proletariat surfaces in only a few places, each of which can be described in a paragraph. The Class Struggles in France, his running analysis of the revolutionary years from 1848 to 1850, written for a journal audience while the events were still warm, contains the phrase in its revolutionary form: the class dictatorship of the proletariat named as the necessary transit point toward the abolition of class distinctions themselves. The letter to Joseph Weydemeyer of March 1852 contains it in its most deliberate form, as the second of three propositions Marx offered as his own distinctive contribution to the theory of classes. The Critique of the Gotha Programme, marginal notes written in 1875 on the draft programme of a unifying German workers' party and circulated privately, contains it in its most consequential form, as the name of the state during the political transition period between capitalist and communist society. Three anchors, three genres, three decades of a career otherwise spent on other vocabularies. The enormous literature that treats the phrase as the keystone of Marx's politics is a literature about those three passages and about what later movements built on them.

### What did Marx actually write, and where?

He wrote the phrase in three principal places: in his analysis of the French revolutions around 1850, where class dictatorship names the transit point toward a classless order; in the letter to Weydemeyer of 1852, where it summarises his contribution; and in the Critique of the Gotha Programme, where it names the transitional state.

The Weydemeyer letter deserves its prominence examined, because it is the one place where Marx himself ranked the phrase among his claims to originality. Writing to an old associate who had become a journalist and officer in America, Marx distinguished what he had found from what he had added. That classes exist, he said, he had not discovered; bourgeois historians and economists had described the anatomy of classes before him. His own contribution was threefold: to show that the existence of classes is bound to particular historical phases of production, that the class struggle leads necessarily to the dictatorship of the proletariat, and that this dictatorship itself is only the transition to the abolition of all classes. The sequence matters as much as any element in it. The phrase enters Marx's own summary not as a destination but as a middle term, the second step of three, defined by what it leads to. Whatever else the concept became in other hands, in the author's own accounting it was instrumental and transitional, a bridge whose whole significance lay in the far bank.

The Gotha passage is the one on which the largest structures were later built, and its setting is worth the reader's attention precisely because the setting is so unceremonious. The notes were a private rebuke, sent to party leaders in confidence, protesting a programme Marx thought theoretically slovenly and politically concessive. In the fourth section, asking what transformation the state undergoes in the transition, he answered that between capitalist and communist society lies a period of revolutionary transformation, that its political form can be nothing but the revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat, and that the question of the state's withering belongs to the society beyond. The sentence is an answer to a drafting question, not the climax of a treatise, and the notes themselves went unpublished until Engels brought them out in 1891, against the wishes of party leaders who found them embarrassing, in the journal Die Neue Zeit, with a preface insisting on their relevance. A phrase that would be inscribed in constitutions entered public life as an editorial act of defiance fourteen years after it was written and eight years after its author died.

Against these three anchors stands the silence of everything else, and the silence is evidence. The Communist Manifesto of 1848, the text in which the phrase is most often imagined to stand, does not contain it; the Manifesto speaks of raising the proletariat to the position of ruling class and of winning the battle of democracy, and its programme of measures is radical without the word. Capital, the work of Marx's maturity, builds its analysis of the state only intermittently and never rests it on the phrase. The vast journalism, the letters to Engels running to thousands of pages, the drafts and notebooks: the phrase is not a habit of this writer. Scholars who have combed the corpus for its occurrences, among them Hal Draper, whose multi-volume study of Marx's theory of revolution devoted a full treatment to the term's rarity, report a small number of uses in print and manuscript, concentrated in the years around 1850 and the single late recurrence of 1875. The responsible way to state the frequency is the brief's way: a handful of times. Any page that gives the reader a precise-sounding count without a defined corpus and a defined search is selling false precision, because the total moves with every decision about translations, variants like the German Diktatur des Proletariats, and unpublished drafts.

### Why does the rarity of the phrase discipline its interpreters?

Rarity disciplines interpretation because a phrase used a handful of times cannot bear the weight of a detailed institutional doctrine. Every elaborate account of what the dictatorship of the proletariat requires, permits or forbids is built by inference from a few passages, and honest reading has to mark which parts are text and which parts are construction.
The thought carried in that answer organises the rest of this section. The passages themselves, read without supplementation, tell the reader that a transition exists, that the state in that transition is the rule of the proletariat as a class, and that the rule is directed toward the abolition of classes and therefore toward its own supersession. They do not specify a party form, a franchise, a legal code, a duration or a mechanism of accountability. Readers who find those specifications in the phrase are importing them, from the Commune writings in the best case and from later party practice in the worst, and the imports differ so widely that the history of the phrase is largely a history of competing importations claiming the same customs stamp.

One more textual feature deserves emphasis before the article leaves Marx's desk, because it is the feature the symmetry test formalises. In the French writings, the dictatorship vocabulary is applied to the other side first and most concretely. The bourgeois republic that emerged from the June Days of 1848, with its constitution, its assembly and its property order defended by General Cavaignac's emergency powers, is analysed as the dictatorship of the bourgeoisie in republican dress: the pure rule of one class, no longer mediated by a monarchy standing above classes in appearance. The proletarian dictatorship enters the analysis as the symmetrical counterpart, the rule of the other great class of modern society, exercised in the open rather than behind a throne. Whatever a reader concludes about the merits of the analysis, its structure is unmistakable. The word does the same work on both sides of the barricade. An interpretation that reads terror into the word only when the proletariat holds it has broken the structure it claims to expound, and the breakage, not Marx's sentence, is doing the arguing.

## The usage timeline: the artifact of this article

A glossary exists to be consulted in the middle of other reading, and the findable artifact of this article is built for that use. The usage timeline arranges the phrase's career in six stages, stating for each what the term meant at that stage, who used it, and what changed in the passage to the next. A reader who has time for nothing else in these pages can take the timeline away whole, and a reader who stays will find each row expanded in the sections around it. The timeline's discipline is the article's discipline in miniature: meaning and verdict in separate columns of the mind, the word's sense recorded for each stage before any judgement about the stage is formed.

| Stage | What the term meant | Who used it | What changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Roman origin | A temporary emergency magistracy, commissioned for a task and bounded by a term | The Roman republic and its magistrates | The word entered European languages carrying concentration of power together with a built-in expiry |
| Marx's usage | The rule of a class, applied symmetrically to bourgeois republic and proletarian transition alike | Marx, in the French writings, the Weydemeyer letter and the Gotha notes; Engels restating it | The subject of dictatorship became a class and its measure became the abolition of classes, not a magistrate's calendar |
| The Second International dispute | A contested inheritance: class rule compatible with democracy, or class rule defined against it | Kautsky and Lenin in rival pamphlets, Luxemburg qualifying from prison and manuscript | The same phrase was made to authorise two opposed accounts of democracy within one movement |
| The Bolshevik usage | The rule of the proletariat exercised through soviets and, in practice, through the party that led them | Lenin, Trotsky, the Communist International and its parties | The term began its slide from naming a class content to naming a form of regime |
| The Soviet constitutional usage | A form of state inscribed in constitutions and party programmes, succeeded on paper by the state of the whole people | The Soviet state in its constitutions and the CPSU programme, and allied parties abroad | The phrase became a constitutional category while actual rule rested with a party apparatus |
| The Eurocommunist abandonment | A liability: a word whose public meaning its users no longer controlled | The French, Spanish and Italian communist parties in their congresses and programmes | Western parties formally dropped the phrase, judging the class meaning unrecoverable in public argument |

Read the table from left to right and the career tells itself as a story of drift under pressure. The Roman row contributes the grammar of exception and expiry. The Marx row replaces the magistrate with a class and the calendar with a social transformation, keeping the exception and losing the expiry date, a loss whose consequences the objection section examines without flinching. The dispute row shows the phrase becoming a battlefield inside its own movement within a generation of its author's death, which should cure any reader of the idea that its meaning was ever simply possessed by anyone. The Bolshevik row records the hinge: a class content claimed through an institutional form that increasingly did the claiming itself. The constitutional row shows the endpoint of the hinge, the phrase as official self-description of states whose ruling institutions it no longer described. The abandonment row closes the arc with the judgement of the phrase's own heirs in the West, who concluded that a word means what its hearers hear, and that what hearers heard could no longer be argued away.

## What the term leaves unspecified: the honest inventory

A precision glossary earns its keep by stating not only what a term contains but what it does not, and this term's silences are extensive and consequential. The phrase, in Marx's uses of it, does not specify a party. No passage in which it appears mentions a single party, a vanguard organisation, or a prohibition on other parties; the party question, which dominates the phrase's later career, is simply not addressed in the texts where the phrase lives. It does not specify a leader. No personal ruler is named, implied or required by anything in the three anchor texts. It does not specify a franchise: whether the transitional order votes, whom it enfranchises, and what it does with the votes of the dispossessed classes are questions Marx argued about in other connections, fiercely and in detail, but the phrase itself answers none of them. It does not specify a legal order, a court system, a press regime or a police. It does not specify a duration in any unit a calendar can measure. Set against the weight later placed on the phrase, the inventory of its silences is the most sobering exhibit in this article, because nearly everything that made the twentieth-century regimes what they were had to be supplied from elsewhere: from the theory of the vanguard party, from civil war emergency, from the administrative inheritance of the old regimes, from the choices of identifiable men at identifiable congresses. The phrase supplied a name and a legitimation. The institutions were built by hands the name did not guide in any detail a blueprint could be checked against.

Two of the silences deserve separate handling because arguments constantly fill them with smuggled content. The first is the silence about parties. Readers formed by the later record assume a one party state the way they assume a flag, and are then surprised, or suspicious, when told the texts do not contain one. The surprise is instructive. Marx's political world contained competing workers' parties, factions, newspapers and internationals; his own practice was a lifelong war of position inside a plural movement, conducted with pamphlets rather than prohibitions. Nothing in that practice proves what a Marxist transition would institutionally require, and the article asserts no such proof. What the silence establishes is narrower and firmer: the one party form cannot be derived from the phrase, and anyone who claims the derivation owes the reader the intermediate premises, which are Lenin's and his successors', not Marx's sentences. The second silence concerns duration, and it is dangerous in a different way, because a transition without a term invites the question of who declares it over. That question gets its own treatment below, in the section on the strongest objection, where it belongs: it is the point at which the concept's critics have always pressed hardest, and they have not pressed without reason.

### Does the phrase require a single party?

Nothing in the passages where Marx uses the phrase mentions a party at all, single or otherwise. The term names the class holding state power during a transition. The identification of that rule with one party's monopoly belongs to the Bolshevik settlement and its theorists, and reading it back into the phrase is an anachronism the texts do not license.

The brevity of that answer is itself the finding: on this question the texts permit a short answer because they contain nothing to summarise. Contrast the labour required to answer what the phrase does say, which has occupied the article for pages. The asymmetry between the crowded column of the term's later associations and the empty column of its textual warrants is the recurring discovery of precision work on this phrase, and readers who perform the same exercise on neighbouring terms, revolution, socialism, even democracy in some mouths, will find the asymmetry depressingly familiar. A vocabulary for keeping the columns apart, entry by entry, is part of what the [marxism glossary key terms](/2010/05/15/marxism-glossary-key-terms/) resource in this series exists to supply.

## The Commune measures: the institutional content Marx actually praised

If the phrase itself is institutionally silent, Marx's writing about the Paris Commune of 1871 is not, and the connection between the two bodies of writing is the strongest thread a reader can pull when asking what institutional content the concept carried for its author. The Commune governed Paris for two months in the spring of 1871, elected by universal male suffrage in a city at war, and Marx's account of it in The Civil War in France treated its arrangements as the discovered form of working class government, the political shape he had been unable to specify in advance. Engels, introducing a new edition twenty years later, made the link to the phrase explicit, telling readers who wanted to know what the dictatorship of the proletariat looked like to look at the Commune, and that retrospective identification, by the author's closest collaborator, is the nearest thing the tradition has to an authorised illustration. The full analysis of Marx's Commune writings belongs to the companion study of the [paris commune marx analysis](/2013/02/15/paris-commune-marx-analysis/), and what follows here is only the inventory of measures, because the inventory is what the argument about limits will need.

The measures were these. Public functions were filled by election, with every official, from councillors to judges to administrators, subject to recall at any time by the electors who had chosen them; the mandate was imperative in spirit and revocable in fact. Officials were paid at workmen's wages, collapsing the material distance between the governed and the governing and removing office as a route to fortune. The standing army was abolished in favour of the armed population, the National Guard of all citizens, so that the means of coercion was not a separate caste's monopoly. The police were stripped of political functions and made responsible to the Commune. Church and state were separated and church property disestablished. Education was opened free of charge. The Commune itself was a working body, executive and legislative in one, sitting in public, rather than a parliamentary talking shop delegating power to ministers beyond reach. Marx's enthusiasm fastened precisely on the anti-bureaucratic machinery: here, he argued, was a state form that began dismantling the state as a separate power standing over society, returning functions to the society that performed them.

Two disciplines are required when this inventory is used in argument, and the article insists on both. First, the Commune measures answer the question of what Marx praised, not the question of what the phrase logically contains. The connection between the Gotha sentence and the Commune arrangements is a connection drawn by Engels after the fact and by the tradition after him; it is strong evidence about the concept's intended content in its founding circle, and it is not a deduction. Second, the Commune must be described whole or not at all. The same two months that produced election, recall and wage equality also produced, in their final crisis, a revived Committee of Public Safety, hostages taken under decree, and the shooting of the Archbishop of Paris and other hostages during the suppression, in a week when the Versailles forces entering the city killed Communards in numbers historians estimate in the thousands and by some reckonings well beyond ten thousand. A glossary that lists the recall provision and omits the hostages has stopped being a glossary. The Commune record, like every record this article touches, is reported here in full outline: the institutional inventions that Marx celebrated and the emergency violence that accompanied their collapse, neither used to cancel the other.

With the inventory stated whole, its argumentative weight can be fixed exactly. When critics say the concept contained no internal limit, the Commune measures are the strongest reply the tradition possesses: election, recall, wage equality and a dispersed armed force are limits, and limits of a structural kind rather than parchment promises. When defenders deploy the measures as an acquittal, the reply's weakness has to be stated with equal clarity: the measures appear in a different text, about a different event, under emergency conditions that consumed them within weeks, and the tradition that canonised the phrase gave the measures a fraction of the emphasis it gave the phrase itself. Party programmes quoted the dictatorship and footnoted the recall. That imbalance is a fact about the reception, not about Marx's desk, and the article keeps it in that column, but it is the reception, as the next sections show, that governed what the phrase went on to do in the world.

## The duration question: a transition measured in transformations, not years

How long the dictatorship of the proletariat is meant to last is the question every curious reader asks and no text of Marx answers in the form the question expects. The anchor passages tie the end of the transition not to a date or a number of years but to a social condition: the abolition of classes themselves. In the Weydemeyer summary the dictatorship is the transition to a classless order. In the Gotha notes the transition period lasts as long as the transformation between capitalist and communist society lasts, which the same notes describe in terms of a first phase still stamped with the birthmarks of the old society. The state's disappearance, the process for which this series keeps a separate entry on the [withering away of the state](/2013/01/15/withering-away-of-the-state/), belongs to the far side of that transformation. Nothing in the schema offers a lever by which a calendar could be attached, and the absence is structural rather than an oversight: a theory that measures periods by modes of production does not experience a missing decade count as a gap.

The reader should nonetheless register what the absence does once the phrase leaves the study and enters a revolution. A transition defined by a condition rather than a term hands the power of certification to whoever rules in the transition's name: the condition's arrival is a judgement, the judges are the rulers, and no external clock overrules them. Defenders of the concept reply that the same is true of many respectable political notions, that democratisation, reconstruction and development are all condition-defined and all certified by interested parties, and the reply is fair as far as it goes. Critics answer that the combination is what matters: a condition-defined transition, plus a state doctrine that identifies the ruling party with the class in whose name the condition is awaited, plus the suspension of the very institutions, contested elections, independent courts, a critical press, by which a false certification could be challenged. Each element separately has analogues elsewhere. The compound is the historically operative thing, and the compound was assembled by the phrase's inheritors in specific circumstances, which the article now reaches. The Second International had already split over the phrase's meaning before any Bolshevik held power, and the split of 1918 is where the modern argument about the term truly begins.

## The dispute of the Second International's heirs: Kautsky against Lenin

By 1918 the phrase belonged to a movement that had split over a world war, and the two men who fought its most famous duel over the term were both, in their own estimation, Marx's faithful executors. Karl Kautsky had been the German Social Democratic Party's chief theorist for a generation, the editor of Marx's posthumous manuscripts, the man Engels had trusted with the literary estate of the tradition. Vladimir Lenin had built the Bolshevik organisation, seized power in Russia in October 1917 in the name of the soviets, and dispersed the Constituent Assembly in January 1918 when its elections returned a majority for other parties. In the summer of 1918 Kautsky published The Dictatorship of the Proletariat, a pamphlet arguing that what the Bolsheviks had built was not the rule of a class but the rule of a party, and that the substitution violated the concept it invoked. Lenin answered within months with The Proletarian Revolution and the Renegade Kautsky, among the most vituperative texts he ever wrote. The duel is the phrase's first great reception crisis, and it maps, with uncanny completeness, the positions every later argument would occupy.

Kautsky's case rested on a distinction between a condition and a form of government. Marx, he argued, had used the phrase to name a condition: the state of affairs in which the proletariat holds political power, a condition that could exist under various forms and that, in a country with developed democratic institutions, would naturally express itself through them. Democracy, for Kautsky, was not one optional clothing for class rule among others; it was the form in which a majority class, and the proletariat in a developed society was the majority, could actually rule as a class, since a class of millions rules through representation, suffrage and public contest or it does not rule at all, and a committee rules in its place. The Bolshevik practice, as he catalogued it: the dispersal of an elected assembly, the progressive restriction of the suffrage, the suppression of rival socialist newspapers, the elevation of the soviets from organs of the working class into organs of one party's administration, amounted on his account to the dictatorship not of the proletariat but over it. He pressed the textual point with an editor's malice, noting how thin the phrase's presence in Marx was, and how the single Gotha sentence had been made to carry a state doctrine it could not bear. Readers who want the framework within which Kautsky was arguing, the theory of the state as class rule that both duelists shared even while drawing opposite conclusions, will find it set out in the [marxist theory of the state guide](/2012/12/01/marxist-theory-of-the-state-guide/).

Lenin's reply conceded nothing to the distinction and redefined the term in the act of defending it. Dictatorship, he wrote, is rule based directly upon force and unrestricted by any laws, and the revolutionary dictatorship of the proletariat is violence against the bourgeoisie not bound by rules because its historical task, the suppression of the exploiters' resistance, cannot be accomplished within the legal forms the exploiters built for their own protection. Democracy, in his counter-analysis, was itself a form of class dictatorship wherever it existed: bourgeois democracy was the dictatorship of the bourgeoisie wearing parliamentary dress, and the symmetry that this article has drawn from Marx's French writings stands in Lenin's pamphlet fully armed and facing the other way. The difference between the two duelists can be located with precision. Kautsky used the symmetry to argue that since class rule was compatible with democratic form, the Bolshevik form stood condemned by the concept. Lenin used the symmetry to argue that since all class rule was dictatorship anyway, dispensing with democratic form cost the concept nothing. The symmetry test cannot by itself choose between them, and the article does not pretend otherwise. What the test shows is that both men were fighting over the term's silence about form, the one treating the silence as an opening democracy must fill, the other treating it as a permission that necessity may fill.

The historical setting of the duel is not decoration, and a precision article falsifies its subject if it floats the pamphlets free of the year that produced them. Russia in 1918 was disintegrating into civil war; the Brest-Litovsk treaty had amputated its western territories; foreign armies were landing at its ports; the Bolshevik government faced armed revolt from the right and, after July, from former allies on the left. Kautsky wrote from a Germany months away from its own revolution and collapse, where the question of councils against parliament was about to be argued with artillery in Berlin. Both men knew that the pamphlet war was a proxy for decisions being taken in real capitals about which institutions the European labour movement would build where it took power. None of that context decides who read Marx better, and the article offers it for a different reason: to show that the phrase's meaning was never going to be settled by philology alone, because the phrase had become a warrant in a struggle over institutions, and warrants are interpreted by the needs of the struggle. The philology still matters, and this article has done it first and done it carefully, because a struggle over a text that nobody reads closely is a struggle in which any warrant can be forged. But the reader who understands 1918 understands why the forgery question kept arising.

### What did Kautsky say about the phrase?

Kautsky argued in 1918 that Marx had used the term for a condition, the proletariat's holding of political power, and not for a form of government. On that reading the Bolshevik system, rule by one party over a restricted suffrage, was the dictatorship of a party rather than of a class, and stood condemned by the concept it claimed.

That short answer compresses a pamphlet whose patience is part of its force, and the compression should not hide how much of Kautsky's case was predictive rather than textual. He argued that a dictatorship in the Bolshevik form could not be a temporary bridge, because the apparatus it created, a party fused with administration, a press reduced to one voice, a political police, generated interests of its own that no proclaimed transition timetable would dissolve. He argued that suppressing democracy to reach socialism would deform the socialism reached, since a class trained by its rulers in obedience was being unfit, daily, for the self-government the end state required. And he argued the economic corollary, that a modern economy could not be administered by decree through a monopoly party without the decree system hardening into the permanent management of scarcity by privilege. Posterity can grade these predictions, and historians across the spectrum have graded several of them highly. What matters for the glossary is that the predictions were framed as an interpretation of a phrase, and that the interpretation, class condition versus regime form, remains the cleanest statement of the fork at which the phrase's career divided.

Trotsky entered the same dispute from the other side two years later, in Terrorism and Communism, answering Kautsky with a defence of revolutionary terror as an instrument and of party rule as the proletariat's necessary form, and his candour is useful to the glossary precisely because it states what Lenin's pamphlet sometimes leaves in the folds of polemic. In Trotsky's account the party does not merely serve the class; in the decisive respects it acts for the class, judges the class's real interests against its momentary opinions, and is entitled to override the second in the name of the first. A reader tracking the phrase's slide along the usage timeline can watch it happen inside that argument: the content-word, which class rules, acquires an institutional answer, this party, and the party answer, once given, does not need the class question asked again. The substitution of party for class, which Kautsky had named as the Bolshevik error and Luxemburg, in a manuscript neither duellist had read, had named as the revolution's mortal danger, is the hinge on which the whole twentieth-century career of the phrase turns. The next section takes up the manuscript.

## Luxemburg's criticism from inside the revolutionary camp

Rosa Luxemburg wrote her assessment of the Russian Revolution in the autumn of 1918, in a German prison cell where she was held for her opposition to the war, and the manuscript's route into print is part of its authority. She had welcomed the October Revolution as the salvation of the socialist idea in a Europe of slaughter, and she wrote as a partisan of the revolution against its enemies on the right. Her criticism was therefore the criticism of a co-believer, aimed at the Bolsheviks' handling of democracy, and it was not published in her lifetime: she was murdered in Berlin in January 1919, in the suppression of the Spartacus rising, and the manuscript appeared only in 1922, brought out by Paul Levi, the former Communist leader expelled for publicising it against the party's wishes. A text suppressed by its author's own movement, written by the movement's most famous martyr, occupies a peculiar place in the phrase's dossier. Neither side of the 1918 duel could quote it at the time. Every later argument has had to reckon with it.

Her targets were specific, and a glossary should name them rather than summarise her as a voice of democratic conscience in the abstract. She objected to the dispersal of the Constituent Assembly, not from reverence for that body but because she held that its defects called for new elections rather than abolition of election as a principle. She objected to the Bolshevik land settlement and the nationalities policy on tactical grounds the article passes over. Her central objection concerned the suffrage and the public sphere: the restriction of voting rights to those who lived by their own labour, which in a ruined economy disfranchised shifting masses of people arbitrarily; the suppression of press freedom and of freedom of assembly and association for opponents; and the transformation of the soviets, which she had celebrated as the revolution's creative form, into bodies that met rarely and ratified rather than ruled, once the life of the other institutions around them had been extinguished. Her reasoning ran through a distinction this article has already used. A class, she argued, rules only through the broadest public life of its members; suppress the public life and what rules is not the class but a clique, a dozen party leaders and the apparatus beneath them, and a dictatorship in that sense, however revolutionary its personnel, was the negation of the thing the phrase had named.

The sentence of hers that has outlived the manuscript's other arguments deserves its fame and its place here, quoted within the limits this series observes for quotation. Freedom, she wrote in its usual English rendering, is always the freedom of the one who thinks differently. Eleven words, and the argument they compress is the one this section exists to record: that freedom reserved for government supporters and party members is not freedom in abridged form but a different substance altogether, because the corrective that freedom exists to supply comes precisely from those who think differently, and a revolution that silences its correctives blinds itself at the moment it most needs sight. Luxemburg paired the sentence with a prognosis. Without general elections, without unrestricted freedom of press and assembly, she warned, life dies out in every public institution and the bureaucracy remains the only active element, while public life gradually falls asleep and a dictatorship of functionaries replaces the rule of the working class in any sense the words can bear. Readers grading predictions, as the Kautsky section invited, will find hers among the most exact the century produced about the system then being born.

Place her on the usage timeline and her position sharpens the map. Kautsky had defended the class meaning by binding it to democratic form, from a position outside the Russian Revolution and increasingly outside the revolutionary tradition as it re-formed. Lenin had embraced the form meaning and defined it as unrestrained force. Luxemburg defended the class meaning from inside the revolution, against the revolution's own government, insisting that the form was not an ornament the content could survive without. The three positions of 1918 exhaust, between them, most of the logical space the phrase has occupied since, and the parties and states that later invoked the phrase chose, knowingly or not, among those three inheritances. The Soviet settlement chose Lenin's. The Western social democratic tradition, where it engaged the phrase at all, chose Kautsky's reading and rejected the practice. The dissident Marxist traditions that kept returning to Luxemburg's manuscript, generation after generation, were choosing the third: the claim that the phrase's meaning and democracy's forms stand or fall together, and that a movement which sacrifices the second in the first's name will lose both.

### What did Luxemburg criticise about the Bolshevik practice?

She criticised, from within the revolutionary camp, the dispersal of the assembly, the restricted suffrage, and the suppression of press and assembly freedoms for opponents. Her argument was that a class rules only through the broadest public life, and that without elections and open contest the soviets would wither into ratifying bodies while an apparatus ruled in the class's name.

The manuscript matters to the glossary for a further reason: it is the earliest full statement, by a revolutionary Marxist of unimpeachable standing, of the class-party distinction as an empirical test rather than a polemical slogan. Luxemburg does not merely assert that party rule differs from class rule. She specifies the observable conditions under which the difference can be detected: whether elections occur and matter, whether hostile newspapers appear, whether assemblies debate outcomes not fixed in advance, whether the class in whose name power is held can organise, speak and vote against its governors and survive the experience. That testability is what lifts her criticism out of the pamphlet war and into the toolkit this article is assembling. The symmetry test tells the reader what the phrase means. Luxemburg's conditions tell the reader how to check whether a claimant regime matches the meaning. The article will combine the two instruments in its later section on the evidence that separates class rule from party rule, and the combination is, in the judgement of this series, the most useful thing a reader can take away from the whole dossier: a meaning and a measuring instrument, both drawn from inside the tradition the phrase belongs to, neither requiring the reader to import a foreign standard to apply them.

## The Bolshevik usage and the slide from content to form

The Bolsheviks did not merely inherit the phrase; they rebuilt the state around a set of doctrines in which the phrase was assigned a definite institutional address, and that rebuilding is the stage of the timeline at which the content-word began its conversion into a form-word. Lenin's State and Revolution, written in hiding in the summer of 1917 and published in 1918, remains the canonical exposition: the bourgeois state machine is to be smashed, not taken over; its replacement, on the Commune model, is a state of armed workers with elected and recallable officials, a state that is already, in Lenin's deliberate paradox, no longer a state in the proper sense because the majority suppresses the minority rather than the reverse. The book's own reader's guide in this series, the [state and revolution readers guide](/2012/12/15/state-and-revolution-readers-guide/), unpacks its argument chapter by chapter, and the present article needs only the book's function in the phrase's career: it attached the phrase to the soviets, the councils of workers' and soldiers' deputies that had arisen in 1917, as the phrase's institutional embodiment. After October, the formula was fixed: the dictatorship of the proletariat in Russia takes the form of the soviet republic, and the soviets were the discovery, as the Commune had been for Marx, that made the abstract phrase concrete.

The civil war then did to the soviets what emergencies do to representative bodies in most regimes, and the record must be stated as plainly for this side of the argument as for any other. Between 1918 and 1921 the government banned rival socialist parties in stages, subordinated the soviets to the party's committees, built the Cheka into a political police operating outside court control, and dispersed or arrested striking workers who invoked the soviet idea against the soviet state. The Tenth Party Congress of 1921, meeting as sailors from the Kronstadt naval base rose in revolt demanding freer soviets and were crushed by force, banned organised factions inside the ruling party itself, closing the last legal channel through which the class in whose name the state ruled could contest the party that ruled it. Whatever interpretation a reader places on these events, and interpretations range from tragic necessity under invasion and famine to the working out of an authoritarian logic present from the start, the institutional facts are the common ground of the scholarship: by the early nineteen twenties, power in Russia was held by a single party, its internal debate disciplined, its apparatus fused with the state's, and the soviets functioning as transmission and ratification rather than as sovereign bodies. The phrase continued to name the arrangement. The arrangement had ceased to be describable, in the phrase's original class vocabulary, without strain, and the strain is precisely what Kautsky had predicted the vocabulary would show.

Stalin's codification completed the conversion. Under his rule the phrase became a fixture of doctrine with a fixed exposition in textbooks and party schools, paired with an account of its functions: the suppression of the exploiters, the direction of the economy, the alliance of workers and peasants, each function assigned to the state that bore the phrase as its official description. The 1936 constitution, promulgated at the height of the purges, described the Soviet Union in its preamble and structure as a state of workers and peasants, and the doctrine of the dictatorship of the proletariat remained the state's theoretical self-description until the party programme of 1961 announced that the dictatorship had fulfilled its historical mission within the Soviet Union and that the state had become a state of the whole people. The announcement is one of the timeline's most revealing rows compressed into a single act: a party declaring, by resolution, that the class condition named by the phrase had ended, while the form of rule built under the phrase continued unchanged in every particular a citizen could touch. Content declared complete, form continuing entire: the distance between the phrase's first meaning and its constitutional career could hardly be illustrated more cleanly, and the illustration is supplied by the phrase's own custodians.

## The record of the regimes that ruled under the phrase

The verdict question has its own evidence, and the article now changes register deliberately, from texts to records. States that described themselves, in constitutions, programmes and doctrine, as dictatorships of the proletariat or as states building socialism under that doctrine's authority governed a large part of the twentieth century's population, and their records include repression on a scale that no honest glossary may summarise in a euphemism or pass over as a distraction from semantics. What follows is an outline, with the figures the scholarship supports stated as the ranges the scholarship gives, because the record is part of what the phrase now means in public life and because the article's neutrality consists in accuracy, not in silence. The fuller question of how such regimes relate to the doctrine they invoked is treated in this series under [marxism and authoritarianism](/2019/07/01/marxism-and-authoritarianism/), and the present section's narrower duty is the record itself, attached to the phrase whose career is under examination.

In the Soviet Union, the state founded under the phrase carried out, across the decades of its existence, campaigns of coercion that historians have documented in the archives opened after 1991. The Red Terror of the civil war years executed political opponents and hostages in numbers that remain contested in range but run to the tens of thousands at minimum. Collectivisation at the turn of the nineteen thirties deported millions of peasants designated as kulaks to exile settlements where mortality was severe, and the famine of 1932 and 1933 that accompanied the drive killed, on the estimates most widely cited by demographic historians, in the region of four to five million people in Ukraine and well over a million more elsewhere in the Union, with the all-Union toll commonly placed between five and seven million. The Great Terror of 1937 and 1938, the purge campaigns driven by quotas and lists from the centre, produced documented executions that the archival record supports at roughly three quarters of a million, alongside millions arrested and a camp system whose population peaked in the millions and whose deaths across its existence are counted by historians in the low millions. After the Second World War the pattern moderated in scale without changing in kind: psychiatric confinement of dissenters, exile of national groups in the war years, the suppression of the Hungarian rising of 1956 and the Czechoslovak reform of 1968 by invasion. The scholarly literature disputes margins, responsibilities and interpretations, including how far famine policy was intended as famine. It does not dispute that coercion of this order was a standing instrument of a state that called itself, in its doctrine, the dictatorship of the proletariat.

The phrase's other great theatre was China, where the victorious Communist Party inscribed the people's democratic dictatorship, its adaptation of the formula, in the constitutional order of the state founded in 1949. The early years brought land reform campaigns in which landlords were tried before mass meetings and executed in numbers historians place in the range of one to two million. The Anti-Rightist campaign of 1957 consigned hundreds of thousands of intellectuals and officials to disgrace, labour and exile on the strength of opinions invited months earlier. The Great Leap Forward of 1958 to 1962, the crash programme of collectivisation and industrialisation directed from the centre, produced a famine whose excess deaths demographic scholarship estimates across a wide and terrible range, from fifteen million at the conservative end to figures above forty million in the most extensive reconstructions, with much of the literature converging on a toll in the tens of millions that makes it the deadliest famine in recorded history. The Cultural Revolution decade that followed added its own toll of persecution, deaths in factional fighting and suicide, commonly estimated between one and two million, and a generation's education sacrificed. In Cambodia, the Khmer Rouge regime of 1975 to 1979, ruling in the name of a peasantry it claimed to embody, emptied the cities, abolished money and markets, and killed, by execution, starvation and disease under its policies, a share of the population that demographic estimates place around a quarter, between one and a half and two million people. Smaller states ruling under the same doctrine, in Eastern Europe, in North Korea, in Ethiopia under the Derg, in Afghanistan, accumulated records of their own, each documented, each differing in scale, none negligible to the people inside them.

Three cautions belong to this record as firmly as the figures do, and the article states them as part of the record rather than as softening. First, the ranges are real: demographic reconstruction from incomplete archives yields intervals, not integers, and any writer who selects a single figure from a range, high or low, to suit an argument is mishandling evidence. Second, the states named were not the only twentieth-century regimes to practise mass repression, and the article's confinement of its catalogue to those ruling under one phrase reflects its subject, not a comparative acquittal of colonial empires, fascist states or counter-revolutionary terror, whose records stand in their own dossiers. Third, the causal question, what share of this record the phrase and its parent doctrine explain, is a separate question from the descriptive one, and it is exactly the question the article's later sections take up with arguments rather than with the catalogue. The catalogue establishes what happened under the phrase's authority. It does not, by itself, establish why, and a reader who has been given the catalogue honestly is entitled to have the why argued, not assumed.

## Two fallacies that guard the exits

Between the meaning question and the verdict question stand two fallacies, one at each exit from the evidence, and naming them is part of this article's assigned work because both are committed daily by fluent speakers. The first is the exculpatory etymology: the argument that because the phrase originally meant class rule, and because class rule by the majority sounds inoffensive, the later record is a misunderstanding for which the concept bears no responsibility. The fallacy lies in treating a word's origin as a verdict on its career. Meanings do not govern their inheritors at that distance; a concept that enters the world without institutional specifications, as the inventory of silences showed this one did, cannot claim its silences as a defence when others fill them, any more than it can be blamed for every filling. The etymology acquits Marx of having advocated one man tyranny, a specific historical correction worth making. It does not acquit the concept of having been, in its unspecified state, available for the uses that followed, and the availability is a property of the concept that its history has now made impossible to unsee.

The second fallacy is the retrospective dictionary: the argument that because regimes ruling under the phrase built one party states and practised terror, the phrase must always have meant one party rule and terror, and that Marx's sentences are to be read as the blueprint of the Lubyanka. The fallacy lies in treating later practice as a definition of earlier words, which would make all textual history impossible: by that method the Commune's recall provisions, the Weydemeyer transition and the description of a parliamentary republic as a bourgeois dictatorship become inexplicable decorations on a text that secretly meant the Cheka all along. The record of the regimes is evidence about the regimes, about their founders' doctrines, about the circumstances of civil war and encirclement, about the compound described earlier, transition without term plus party identified with class plus institutions of contest dismantled. It is not a dictionary. A reader who keeps the fallacies labelled can hold the two truths the phrase's dossier actually contains: that Marx meant class rule and used the phrase a handful of times, and that states ruling under the phrase killed and imprisoned on an enormous scale. Neither truth dissolves the other. The argument worth having begins after both are on the table, and it concerns the connective tissue between them, which is where the strongest objection lives.

### Where did the meaning change: in the word, or in the practice?

In both, and the sequence matters. The practice changed first, as revolutionary governments built one party states under civil war conditions while keeping the phrase. The word then followed the practice, acquiring its regime sense from the states that bore it, until the new sense was the one every audience heard and the older sense needed a glossary to recover.

That two-step sequence explains a feature of the phrase's career that single-step accounts miss. If the word had changed first, as a deliberate doctrinal revision, the revision would have a date, a text and an author, and later generations could argue with the reviser. What happened instead was drift with custody: institutions built under emergency justified themselves in the phrase's language, textbooks then codified the institutions as the phrase's meaning, and by the time the codification was complete the drift was invisible to those raised inside it, who learned the regime sense as the word's only sense and experienced Kautsky's or Luxemburg's readings as eccentric antiquarianism. Drift of that kind is how most politically loaded words change, and recognising the mechanism is a transferable skill: whenever a term's current meaning is defended as its eternal meaning, the reader should ask whether an institution had custody of the term during the years the meaning moved.

## The strongest objection: a concept without an internal limit

The objection this article is required to state at full strength does not depend on mistranslating Marx, and it is not answered by the symmetry test. It runs as follows. Grant everything the philology establishes: the phrase meant class rule, Marx used it rarely, no party and no terror are specified in his sentences. The concept still names a period in which ordinary political rights, the rights of the defeated classes and, in the logic of transition, of anyone the transition's managers classify with them, are suspended by design rather than by emergency, for a duration defined by a condition the rulers themselves certify, under a legitimation that treats opposition to the transition as allegiance to the old order. A concept of that shape contains no internal limit: no provision by which those living under it can call it to an end, no criterion independent of the rulers by which its necessity can be audited, no protected space from which a correction could be organised. The defect, on this objection, is not that bad men later abused a good idea. The defect is in the specification itself, in what the concept omits at the exact points where political concepts earn their safety, and the twentieth century's record is not an ironic accident that befell the phrase but the predictable filling of a specified vacuum by the only agents positioned to fill it, the armed organisations that made the revolutions. Stated at that strength, the objection survives every etymological correction this article has offered, and it deserves to be met on its own ground.

The strongest answer available begins with the Commune inventory, and it is a real answer as far as it reaches. Marx's one concrete illustration of the concept in operation was an order built from limits: universal election, recall at any time, officials on workmen's wages, coercion dispersed in an armed population rather than concentrated in a separate apparatus, public bodies working in the open. If the concept's institutional content is taken from its author's own exemplar, then the content includes internal limits of a structural kind, and the objection's picture of an unlimited suspension describes a departure from the exemplar rather than its execution. A defender can add that the objection's logic, applied impartially, would convict many political concepts whose transitional suspensions are accepted in ordinary judgement: founding constitutions written by unelected conventions, wartime governments ruling by decree under conditions their own cabinets certify, reconstruction regimes imposed after civil wars. The absence of a self-executing expiry is a feature of emergency and founding politics as such, not a signature unique to this phrase.

Then the concessions, which the article's instructions to itself require to be made without varnish, because an answer that concedes nothing has answered nothing. The Commune content reaches the concept by association, through Engels's retrospective identification, not by specification in the passages where the phrase appears; a reader who confines the concept to its anchor texts will not find a single limit in them. The tradition that transmitted the phrase transmitted the limits feebly: the party schools taught the dictatorship as a chapter of doctrine and the recall provision as a detail of Parisian history, and the imbalance of emphasis is a fact about the concept's real historical existence, which is the existence its readers and rulers actually encountered. And the objection's central mechanism, certification of the transition's end by the transition's beneficiaries, was not refuted by the phrase's career but illustrated by it, most cleanly in 1961, when a party congress declared the condition fulfilled by its own resolution while changing nothing else. The article's verdict on the exchange, if verdict is the word for a glossary's finding, is that the objection identifies a genuine defect of the concept as specified, that the Commune material supplies the materials of a limit without ever having been assembled into one by the concept's custodians, and that the distance between those two findings is the exact size of the tragedy the phrase's name became attached to. Readers who find that too harsh on the concept, and readers who find it too lenient, are both referred to the evidence assembled in the preceding sections, which is where this article insists the argument be conducted.

## The abandonment: how the phrase's own heirs retired it

The final stage of the timeline is the one that tells the reader most about the phrase's condition in the language, because the judges were the phrase's proprietors. During the nineteen seventies the large communist parties of Western Europe, operating legally in democracies and competing for voters who associated the phrase with the regimes of the East, concluded one after another that the term could no longer be carried. The French Communist Party, at its twenty-second congress in 1976, formally removed the dictatorship of the proletariat from its programme, the clearest single act of abandonment and the one historians of the phrase treat as its Western death certificate. The Spanish party under Santiago Carrillo, articulating the strategy that came to be called Eurocommunism, argued that socialism in developed democracies had to be built with democracy and not against it, and treated the phrase as an obstacle to that argument's credibility. The Italian party, the largest in the West, had already travelled farthest in practice toward a pluralist self-understanding and let the phrase lapse from its working vocabulary. Each party had its own electorate and its own history, and the article does not merge their motives into one. The shared judgement beneath the motives is the significant fact: that in public argument the word dictatorship now meant what the century had taught it to mean, and no quantity of glossary work by party intellectuals could make an audience hear class rule in it again.

Read the abandonment with the symmetry test in hand and it yields one more finding. The Western parties did not drop the phrase because they had ceased to analyse states in class terms; their programmes continued to speak of monopoly capital, of the class character of state power, of the need to transform rather than merely administer the existing order. They dropped the phrase because the phrase had ceased to perform that analysis in public. It performed an identification instead: with the Eastern regimes, with one party rule, with the security apparatus. A content-word had become, in the ears of every audience the parties needed, a form-word, and the parties' own decision is the strongest evidence in the whole dossier that the conversion described in the timeline's later rows was real and irreversible by stipulation. A word, the abandonment teaches, is not owned by its definers. It is owned, in the end, by the history of its use, and the history of this word's use had been written by its most powerful users, in institutions and in archives that no congress resolution could edit after the fact.

### What judgement did the Western parties pass on their own inheritance?

Western parties dropped it because their electorates heard the word dictatorship in its acquired regime sense, and no restatement of the older class meaning could be heard over the record of the states that had ruled under it. The French party's congress decision of 1976, the formal landmark, ratified a judgement the Spanish and Italian parties reached in their programmes.

The abandonment also closes the article's long argument about the relationship between meaning and verdict with an empirical observation rather than a slogan. The parties that dropped the phrase were not conceding that Marx had meant tyranny; their own intellectuals continued to publish the philology, correctly, in party journals. They were conceding that the verdict question had annexed the meaning question in the only arena where phrases live public lives, the hearing of non-specialist audiences, and that a movement addressing those audiences had to choose between its inherited vocabulary and being understood. The choice they made is the phrase's epitaph as a living political term: it survives in scholarship, in the internal literatures of small organisations that retain it as a badge, and in the rhetoric of the phrase's opponents, for whom its acquired sense is a permanent asset. The glossary reader meets it now almost exclusively in one of those three custodies, and knowing which custody a given use comes from is half of understanding the use.

## Does the term explain twentieth century repression?

The question is the one the whole article has been approaching with its two columns kept apart, and it can now be asked in a form precise enough to answer. Does the phrase, and the doctrine condensed in it, explain the repression catalogued in the record section: is the terror of the regimes a consequence of the concept, such that a reader who understands the concept understands why the terror occurred? The honest answer is layered, and each layer has to be given its own weight. At the level of legitimation, the connection is direct and documented. The regimes justified the suspension of rights, the banning of parties, the subordination of courts and the operations of their security organs in the vocabulary of class rule and transition: enemies were defined by class location, resistance to the transition was defined as the old order's death struggle, and the phrase supplied the master category under which particular acts of repression were filed as historical necessity. A vocabulary that classifies opponents as a doomed class resisting its abolition does not merely describe repression after the fact. It prepares the classification in advance, and the preparation is part of the explanation of why repression, when it came, took the targets and the justifications it did.

At the level of sufficient cause, the connection weakens, and precision requires the weakening to be stated. Repression on a mass scale has been practised by regimes of many doctrines and of none, under legitimations nationalist, racial, religious, developmental and purely praetorian, which shows that the human and institutional materials of terror, a monopoly party or junta, an emergency, a security apparatus, a dehumanising classification of enemies, are not this phrase's invention and do not require it. The specific doctrines that did the heaviest institutional work in the communist cases, the vanguard party entitled to substitute itself for the class, the ban on factions, the theory of sharpening class struggle under socialism that licensed the purges of the nineteen thirties, were constructions of the phrase's inheritors, several of them contested at the time by other Marxists in exactly the phrase's name. Historians who weigh the causal shares variously emphasise ideology, circumstance, institutional dynamics and individual rulers, and the article does not pretend to settle their dispute. What a glossary can settle is the form of the answer: the phrase functioned as legitimation and classification, the compound built around it functioned as machinery, and machinery plus legitimation, in conditions of war, scarcity and encirclement, proved sufficient in case after case. Explanation that stops at the phrase explains the filing system and misses the machine. Explanation that skips the phrase misses why the machine's operators believed, or could say they believed, that history had authorised the filing.

There is a final refinement, and it returns the reader to the symmetry test one last time before the personas. The phrase's defenders sometimes argue that bourgeois states also repress, that colonial violence and counter-insurgency belong in any honest catalogue, and that singling out this phrase's record is itself an asymmetry. The observation about other states is true and belongs in wider histories; the series' treatment of the charge at full length is the [marxism and authoritarianism](/2019/07/01/marxism-and-authoritarianism/) entry already cited. But the observation does not touch the question this section asked, which was not comparative. It asked whether a term explains the repression carried out under the term, and the answer does not change because other terms have their own repressions to explain. Symmetry in this article has always meant one standard applied evenly, and one standard applied evenly requires each dossier to be opened on its own evidence, as this one has been, without borrowing acquittals or convictions from the dossiers of others.

## Class rule and party rule: the evidence that separates them

The article promised the reader two instruments, a meaning and a measuring device, and this section assembles the second from the materials already laid out. Given any regime that claims, or is claimed, to instantiate the dictatorship of the proletariat in Marx's sense, what evidence would distinguish the rule of a class from the rule of a party over a class? Luxemburg's conditions supply the core of the test, and the Commune inventory supplies the rest. Are officials at every level elected in contests whose outcomes are not arranged, and recallable by their electors in practice rather than on paper? Can the class in whose name power is held organise independently of the ruling organisation, publish its disagreements, strike, assemble and vote against incumbents without those acts being classified as counter-revolution? Is the coercive apparatus dispersed and accountable, or is it a separate hierarchy answerable upward to the leadership alone? Are the material privileges of office limited to something like the workmen's wage principle, or does office confer a standard of life, access and security visibly removed from the class's own? Can the transition's end be demanded through institutions the rulers do not control, and has any ruling group under the phrase ever submitted its mandate to a vote it could lose?

The questions are empirical, and their virtue is that they do not require the examiner to share any party's theory. They were all, in substance, asked by Marxists about Marxist regimes within the tradition's own lifetime: by Luxemburg in 1918, by Kautsky in the same year from his different ground, by the Workers' Opposition and the Kronstadt sailors inside Russia in 1921, by dissident communists in every decade since, and the regimes' answers are matters of record rather than of interpretation. Where the answers run one way, the regime, whatever its constitution says, is the rule of a party and an apparatus over a class whose name it bears, and the phrase's own original criterion, which class rules, is what licenses the finding. Where the answers were to run the other way, the finding would differ, and the test would have done the same work. This is what it is for a glossary instrument to be real rather than rhetorical: it specifies the evidence before the case is chosen. Readers who carry away only one page of method from this long article should carry away this one, because the same separation, between a doctrine's self-description and the institutional evidence for it, is the master skill for reading every political vocabulary that claims a class, a nation or a people as its subject.

### How does the symmetry test read a parliamentary republic?

It would show a state whose class character the test describes without consulting its constitution. A republic in which property, law and administration secure the dominance of owners over non-owners is, in Marx's usage, the dictatorship of the bourgeoisie with elections, and the test insists that the same word be used, with the same meaning, for the proletarian case.

The answer's economy conceals a subtlety the reader should not miss, because it marks the boundary of what the test can do. The symmetry test governs the use of a word; it does not govern the evaluation of the arrangements the word describes. A parliamentary republic and a one party state both fall, on Marx's usage, under class dictatorships of their respective classes, and nothing in the test ranks them, excuses them or equates their records, their liberties or their body counts. The ranking question is real and unavoidable, and it is conducted with other instruments: with evidence about rights, about coercion, about the possibilities of correction and removal, of exactly the kind the previous section assembled. Readers who suspect the test of smuggling in an equivalence have mistaken a dictionary discipline for a moral one. The test says the word means one thing in both cases. What the reader concludes about the cases remains the reader's work, and the article has supplied evidence for it in both columns without performing it.

## Misreadings this article corrects

Precision work ends by collecting its corrections in one place, and five misreadings account for most of the confusion the phrase generates. The first is that Marx advocated dictatorship as the word is ordinarily understood after the twentieth century: a single ruler, a single party, terror as method. The texts support none of the three elements, and the symmetry of his usage contradicts the reading at its root. The second is that the phrase is central to Marx's work, the keystone without which the arch falls. A concept stated in three passages across forty years, absent from the Manifesto and from Capital's architecture, is a real element of the theory of transition and nothing like its centre, and interpretations that make it the centre are reading the reception back into the corpus. The third is the simplest error of fact: that the phrase appears in the Communist Manifesto. It does not, and the correction is worth making pedantically because the Manifesto is the one text many claimants have actually opened, and the phrase's absence there, in a document that does speak of the proletariat raised to the ruling class and of despotic inroads on property rights, shows how deliberately the vocabulary of the phrase belongs to the later, French-focused writings rather than to the famous pamphlet.

The fourth misreading is that the concept requires a single party, which the article has addressed at length: the requirement enters history with the Bolshevik settlement and its theorists, and attributing it to the phrase confuses the concept with one of its custodians. The fifth is the subtlest and the most respectable: that establishing the nineteenth-century meaning settles the twentieth-century question, that once the reader knows the word meant class rule the argument about the regimes is over. The article has treated that move as a fallacy with a name, and it repeats the correction here because the move is the one educated defenders of the phrase reach for first. The meaning question and the verdict question are both real, both answerable, and answerable only separately. A glossary that taught the reader to perform one and forget the other would have failed at the exact point where this phrase does its damage in public argument, which is the point where one of the two questions is used to smother the other.

## Engels: the phrase's second author and first editor

No account of the phrase is complete that treats Marx's three passages as self-propelling, because between the passages and the century stands Engels, performing the three operations by which a manuscript phrase becomes a doctrine: publication, glossing and repetition. It was Engels who published the Gotha notes in 1891, prefacing them with the insistence that the party needed their candour, and thereby converted a private rebuke into a public proof text. It was Engels who, in the same year, attached the phrase to the Commune in the introduction already quoted, supplying the illustration that anchored the abstraction. It was Engels whose letters, articles and late summaries restated the transition doctrine in the systematic form that party educators could teach, and whose authority, as the surviving founder, made the restatements canonical for the generation of Kautsky and Lenin alike. The phrase's career cannot be understood as Marx's intentions unfolding; it is, from 1883 onward, an editorial construction, built by the most capable and loyal of editors, and later builders worked from the edition as much as from the author.

None of this is an accusation, and the glossary registers it as a mechanism rather than a scandal. Every durable doctrine in the history of political thought passed through editors, systematists and popularisers whose selections decided what the doctrine would be for its mass readership, and the selections are rarely recoverable as pure transmissions. What the reader should take from the Engels stage is a habit of attribution. When a claim about the phrase is quoted from Marx, ask whether the sentence is Marx's or Engels's gloss on Marx; the two are usually allied and sometimes identical, but the Commune identification, the most institutionally specific content the phrase ever received from its founding circle, is Engels's sentence about Marx's analysis, and the difference matters exactly where the argument about internal limits turns on that identification. Attribution is not pedantry here. It is the difference between knowing what a text says and knowing what a tradition, however faithfully, has made of it, and this phrase's whole career is contained in that difference.

## The phrase and democracy: the versus question examined

Searchers ask whether the dictatorship of the proletariat is compatible with democracy more often than they ask any other question about it, and the article owes them a structured answer rather than a verdict. The question conceals three questions, which separate cleanly once the article's two instruments are applied. The textual question asks whether Marx's usage opposed the phrase to democratic forms, and the answer is that it did not: the usage is symmetrical, applying dictatorship to republics with elections and reserving no special hostility for suffrage, and Marx's one praised instance of the concept's embodiment was an elected, recallable, publicly sitting body chosen by universal suffrage in its city. The conceptual question asks whether rule by a class as a class is compatible with the procedures by which majorities govern themselves, and here the tradition split along the 1918 fork: Kautsky held that a majority class can rule only through those procedures and that dispensing with them voids the class claim; Lenin held that the procedures were themselves a class form and dispensable where they obstructed the transition; Luxemburg held Kautsky's premise with Lenin's revolution, insisting the procedures were the content's life support. The historical question asks what the regimes that ruled under the phrase did with democratic procedures, and the record section has answered it: they subordinated, stage-managed or abolished them, in every case durably, and no state ruling under the phrase ever submitted its continuation to an election it could lose.

A reader who keeps the three questions separate can locate any participant in the versus debate in seconds, and can spot the standard manoeuvre the debate runs on, which is answering the historical question and presenting the answer as the textual one, or answering the textual question and presenting it as the historical one. The manoeuvre is committed in good faith as often as in bad, because the three questions share a vocabulary and feel like one. They are not one, and the discipline of this article has been to refuse, at each stage, the feeling. It remains only to add that the versus framing itself, dictatorship against democracy as two regime types on a shelf, is already the form-word's framing, imported from the phrase's later career. In the class vocabulary the contrast does not parse: a democracy, for Marx's analysis, is a form through which a class dictatorship may be exercised, and the live question inside that vocabulary is never whether to have class rule but which class rules and through which forms, with what possibilities of correction. Recovering that framing does not oblige the reader to adopt it. It obliges the reader who argues with Marx to argue with the position he held, rather than with the shelf the twentieth century built for him.

## Examples: what would count, and why the tradition offers so few

The query that brings many readers to the phrase asks for examples, and the honest answer begins with a refusal that is itself informative. Marx named no state as an example of the dictatorship of the proletariat, for the sufficient reason that he did not believe one had existed; the Commune, his and Engels's chosen illustration, was a besieged city government of two months that never held a country, and Marx's praise for it was praise for a form discovered in embryo, hedged in his own correspondence with doubts about its chances and its leadership's errors. Every fuller example on offer belongs to the reception: the Soviet state as its founders presented it, the Chinese state under its adapted formula, and the smaller states that copied their constitutions. A glossary that lists those states as examples without the framing would be performing the retrospective dictionary fallacy in tabular form. The states are examples of regimes that ruled under the phrase, which is a true and important statement. Whether they are examples of the phrase's referent, the rule of the proletariat as a class, is the contested question the measuring instrument of the previous sections was built to examine, and the examination's findings, case by case, belong to history writing rather than to a list.

The scarcity of agreed examples is not an accident of the record; it follows from the concept's structure, and seeing why protects the reader against a common rhetorical use of the scarcity from both directions. A concept defined by class content, with its form unspecified and its duration certified by a social transformation, cannot generate a checklist by which an instance could be certified to everyone's satisfaction, because the certifying questions, who rules, has the transformation occurred, are themselves the contested political questions of each case. Opponents of the concept treat the missing checklist as a confession: a political programme that cannot say when it has been instantiated cannot be falsified by any failure, since every failure can be reclassified as not yet, or no longer, the genuine article. Defenders treat the same openness as the normal condition of orienting concepts, pointing to democracy itself, whose instances are contested in every generation without the concept being discarded. The article records both readings without choosing, and notes only that the choice between them is the deepest form of the argument about the phrase, the point at which the dispute about a term becomes a dispute about what political concepts are for. Readers who reach that point have exhausted the glossary's competence, and the article's remaining sections return to its proper work: the phrase's life in the language of public argument, and the counsel owed to the three audiences of this series.

## The phrase in public argument: a field guide for the perplexed

Outside scholarship the phrase lives as a move in argument, and its moves are few enough to catalogue, which this section does as a service to readers who will meet them in speeches, broadcasts and comment threads and want to know what has just been done in front of them. The first move is the quotation ambush: the phrase produced, without context, as a self-sufficient proof of tyrannical intent, relying on the audience's acquired sense of the word dictatorship to convict before the defence can be called. The counter is the symmetry test, applied aloud: ask what the speaker makes of the parliamentary republic sentence, and the ambush either becomes a discussion or dissolves. The second move is the etymological acquittal: the phrase defended, without remainder, by its nineteenth-century sense, as though establishing the sense disposed of the century. The counter is the two fallacies section in miniature: origin is not verdict, and the regimes' record is not erased by a dictionary, however accurate the dictionary is. The third move is the badge: the phrase retained or revived by small organisations as a test of seriousness, where its function is not analytical at all but tribal, marking those willing to say the hard word as the genuine heirs. The counter is not an argument but a classification: a badge is doing identity work, and it should be read as one reads a uniform, for information about the wearer, not as a proposition about 1875.

The fourth move is the euphemism charge, the mirror of the badge: the claim that any Marxist who avoids the phrase is concealing it, that the doctrine requires the word whatever its current speakers say, and that abandonment was therefore a confidence trick. The Eurocommunist record defeats the charge in its strong form, since the parties that dropped the phrase altered their programmes, their strategies and eventually their names in public and at cost, which is not how confidence tricks are usually conducted, and the weaker form of the charge, that the underlying analysis of class power survived the word's retirement, is true and was openly avowed by the retirees themselves. The fifth move is the transfer: the phrase's acquired sense applied to any assertive government, any emergency law, any executive the speaker dislikes, until dictatorship means nothing more precise than power used against the speaker's preferences. The transfer is the final stage of the form-word's drift, the point at which the term describes a mood, and the symmetry test's last service to the reader is to refuse it in every direction at once: a word that has been made to mean everything a speaker dislikes has stopped meaning the specific, arguable thing it meant in Marx, in Lenin and in the constitutions, and the reader loses nothing by retiring it from that speaker's sentences and returning to the dossiers.

Running under all five moves is a single fact about the phrase's public life that the journalist layer of this article's personas will develop: the phrase is almost never used by anyone who has read the passages where it appears, and it is used most confidently in proportion to the distance from those passages. That is not an ad hominem; it is a finding about the phrase's career, continuous with everything the timeline recorded. The phrase escaped its texts early, was carried by movements and states that had institutional reasons for their readings, and entered general circulation as a token whose value was set by the largest events of the century rather than by three passages in a French history, a private letter and a set of marginal notes. A glossary cannot reverse that escape. It can return the reader to the passages equipped, warn the reader where the tokens were minted, and leave the reader harder to ambush, acquit or badge than before. That is the service attempted here, and the personas now take up its application to the three working audiences of this series, with the study habit that this whole series recommends for loaded terms, the keeping of dated notes against primary passages, supported where the reader wants a structured notebook by the [VaultBook study notes for Marxism](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), which several articles in this series pair with their glossaries.

## The Blanquist shadow: what Marx took from the conspirators and what he refused

The phrase's prehistory inside the revolutionary movement carries a tension the reader should see plainly, because opponents of Marx have used it as a shortcut to the personal rule reading and defenders have sometimes denied a connection that the record supports. The dictatorship vocabulary circulated in the Parisian revolutionary underground before Marx used it, above all among the followers of Auguste Blanqui, for whom it named the rule of a disciplined revolutionary minority governing on behalf of a population not yet ready to govern itself, an educational dictatorship of the enlightened few. Marx knew this milieu intimately, organised in its exile circles, fought its factions, and absorbed its vocabulary in the years around 1850 when his own phrase appears. The borrowing is real. What he did with the borrowed word is the point at issue, and the texts show a systematic substitution of subject: where the Blanquist formula placed a committee, Marx's sentences place a class; where the conspiratorial version justified minority rule by the majority's unreadiness, Marx's version derived its authority from the proletariat's position as the immense majority of society, in the Manifesto's phrase for the movement he served. The substitution is exactly the kind that the class-party evidence test later formalises, and it is why Kautsky and Luxemburg, who knew the prehistory as well as anyone, could charge the Bolshevik settlement with reverting to the Blanquist subject while keeping the Marxist word. A glossary does the reader no service by hiding the shadow. The word came to Marx with a minority-rule usage attached, his texts reattach it to a class, and the subsequent career of the phrase can be read, from one angle, as a long contest between the word's two subjects, the committee and the class, with the committee winning the institutions and the class keeping the title.

## The franchise question: who votes inside the transition

No silence of the concept was fought over more concretely than the franchise, because suffrage is where the abstract question of class rule becomes a list of names at a polling station. Marx's own practice supplies the background: he had treated universal suffrage in England as a potential instrument of working class power in his journalism of the eighteen fifties, and the Commune he praised had been elected by universal male suffrage and had enfranchised, in effect, a whole besieged city. Nothing in the anchor passages withdraws the vote from anyone. The Bolshevik constitution of 1918, by contrast, wrote class disfranchisement into law, excluding employers, traders, clergy and other categories from the suffrage and weighting workers' votes above peasants' in the soviet structure, and defended the exclusions as the dictatorship's very substance: the old classes were to be ruled, not consulted. Luxemburg's manuscript attacked the exclusions on the concept's own ground, arguing that a suffrage restricted by administrative category in a shattered economy would disfranchise shifting masses arbitrarily and would destroy the public life through which alone a class, as opposed to its self-appointed trustees, could be said to rule at all. The argument generalises beyond its occasion, and it is the franchise form of the article's central finding. Every later constitution that bore the phrase had to publish its answer to the question of who votes, and the answers are among the most legible documents in the phrase's dossier, because a franchise clause cannot hide behind a genitive. Readers examining any claimant regime can begin there, with the electoral law's exclusions and weightings, before they reach the proclamations, and they will usually find that the distance between the phrase's class meaning and the regime's form is measurable in the franchise alone.

## The soviets as a claimed form: councils between embodiment and façade

The phrase's Bolshevik career cannot be understood without the institution that was supposed to embody it, because the soviets were the answer the revolution gave to the question the phrase leaves open: through what bodies does a class rule? The councils had arisen spontaneously in 1905 and again in 1917, in factories, regiments and districts, as organs of strike organisation and then of dual power, and their early life had the features the concept's defenders prized: delegates elected at the workplace, mandates revocable, sessions open, authority rising from the bench and the barracks rather than descending from a ministry. Lenin's theoretical writing of 1917 fastened on exactly these features and generalised them into the Commune-state, a republic of councils in which the working population administered itself and the old bureaucratic machine was broken rather than inherited. For a brief period the claim had institutional substance behind it, and historians who dismiss the soviet idea as a façade from birth flatten a record that includes genuine contests, shifting majorities and rival socialist parties competing inside the councils through the first half of 1918.

The conversion of the councils into the façade their critics described was a process with dates, and dating it is the glossary's contribution to an argument often conducted at the level of essences. Through 1918 the Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries were progressively excluded from the soviet executive bodies, first by manoeuvre and then by decree after the Left Socialist Revolutionary rising of July. Through 1919 and 1920 the appointment of officials from above replaced election in one branch of administration after another, a substitution the civil war's emergencies were said to require and which the emergency's end did not reverse. By 1921, when the Kronstadt sailors, many of them veterans of 1917, demanded renewed soviet elections without party monopoly and were suppressed by assault across the ice, the demand itself was being treated as counter-revolutionary, which is as precise a marker as the record offers of the distance travelled: the institution in whose name the dictatorship of the proletariat was exercised now required protection against the class in whose name it ruled, when that class attempted to use the institution. Defenders of the settlement reply, in the scholarship as in the polemics of the time, that a besieged revolution choosing between its councils' freedom and its own survival chose as every government at war chooses, and that the choice's tragedy does not make it a fraud. The article takes no side in that reply. It records the sequence because the sequence is what the phrase's timeline means by the slide from content to form, rendered in institutional detail: a form invented to embody a class's rule, retained as the rule's description after the embodying had ceased, and defended thereafter as the description's own justification.

## The phrase in the Cold War's courtroom: the blueprint reading examined

For roughly four decades of the twentieth century the phrase was read, in the West's public argument, almost exclusively through one interpretive frame, and a precision article should describe the frame as carefully as it describes the phrase, because the frame is still the default setting of much commentary. The frame held that Marxism contained a fixed political destination, that the destination was legible in advance from the canonical texts, and that the regimes of the East were the destination's implementation, so that the phrase functioned in argument as a synecdoche for the whole claim: quote the phrase and the blueprint was proved. Scholarship inside the frame varied in quality from archival rigour to pamphleteering, and its strongest practitioners did real work, tracing doctrinal continuities between Lenin's texts and the party states that a purely circumstantial account could not explain. The frame's weakness, which the revisionist historiography of the Soviet period pressed from the nineteen seventies onward, was its handling of contingency: civil wars, invasions, famines, successions and the sheer improvisation of governments were discounted as scenery, and every outcome was read back into the founding intention, which is the retrospective dictionary fallacy operating at the scale of a historiography. The phrase suffered the frame's fate in miniature. Read as a blueprint, its sparse textual existence was an embarrassment to be explained away; read as a term whose career included drift, custody and conversion, the sparseness became the central datum. The post-Cold War opening of archives strengthened the second reading's materials without abolishing the first's questions, since the archives documented both the improvisation and the doctrinal self-understanding of the improvisers, often in the same files. The reader's takeaway is methodological and by now familiar: when a writer presents the phrase as a blueprint, ask for the specification the blueprint contains, and watch whether the answer cites Marx's passages, Lenin's constructions or the regimes' constitutions, because the three are different documents and the argument changes completely depending on which is actually on the table.

## Three specimens from public argument, examined with the instruments

Abstract instruments earn their keep on specimens, and this section runs the article's two tests over three stylised uses of the phrase that any reader of commentary will recognise, stylised because the article examines moves rather than persons. The first specimen is the editorial sentence that runs, in effect: Marx called for a dictatorship of the proletariat, and the gulag was the result. The symmetry test fails the sentence at its first clause, since the call it attributes to Marx uses the word in a sense his own application of it to a parliamentary republic contradicts. The evidence test then does the sentence's second half a service the sentence did not intend: it separates the true claim available nearby, that regimes invoking the phrase built camp systems, from the false economy of deriving the camps from the call, and shows the derivation skipping the party constructions, the civil war, the franchise exclusions and the certification problem where the actual explanatory work waits. The second specimen is the movement defence: the phrase meant majority class rule, the Eastern regimes were therefore not instances of it, and nothing in their record touches the concept. The symmetry test passes this specimen's definition and the evidence test then takes its measure: the separation of concept from instances is legitimate as far as the concept's meaning goes, but the specimen usually proceeds to spend the separation as an acquittal, and at that point the exculpatory etymology has been committed, since a concept whose unspecified silences were filled by its most powerful custodians, in its name and under its banner, for seventy years, has a reception that belongs to its dossier whether or not any instance matched its definition. The third specimen is the scholarly sentence at its best: the phrase named class rule in Marx, was converted by its inheritors into the name of a regime form, and the states built under the converted meaning accumulated the record the archives document. That specimen passes both tests because it keeps the columns apart and gives each its evidence, and the article offers it, without ornament, as the pattern a reader should demand from any treatment of the phrase, including this one. The specimen's three clauses correspond to the article's three great divisions, meaning, career and record, and a treatment missing any one of the clauses has failed in a way the reader can now name.

## The phrase beside its neighbours: a vocabulary map

A term is understood partly by the company it does not keep, and the phrase sits inside a cluster of Marxist expressions with which it is constantly confused in hurried writing. Political power is the cluster's genus: Marx speaks throughout his work of the proletariat conquering political power, and the phrase under examination is one specification of that conquest, naming the class character of the power during the transition rather than the conquest itself. Ruling class is the analytical noun the phrase presupposes; the symmetry test works because the analysis treats every state as a ruling class's instrument, and the phrase is simply the verb form of that noun for the proletarian case. Transition period is the phrase's temporal frame, the interval the Gotha notes place between two social orders, and it should not be collapsed into the phrase, since the period is defined by the transformation of production and property while the phrase names the period's political form. Revolutionary dictatorship, the Gotha sentence's full expression, adds the manner of the transition's birth, in revolution rather than in gradual accession, and interpreters who quote the shorter form alone lose an adjective its author included. The Commune-state, finally, is not a synonym but a proposed embodiment, the institutional shape Engels pointed to when asked what the phrase looked like in practice, and treating embodiment and concept as one term is the small confusion from which several large ones grow. A reader who can sort a paragraph's vocabulary into these five neighbours has done half the work of reading any document in the phrase's dossier, because most documents in that dossier exploit at least one of the confusions, innocently or otherwise.

## Verifying the anchors: a protocol for readers who check

Because the phrase's textual base is small, it is one of the few major political concepts whose foundations a lay reader can verify entire, and this section sets out the verification as a protocol, in keeping with the series' habit of leaving the reader with an instrument rather than an impression. Take the Weydemeyer letter first, dated 5 March 1852 and available in the standard collections of the Marx-Engels correspondence: check that the three propositions appear in the order the article reported, that the dictatorship is the second and is defined by its destination, and that the letter is a private communication rather than a programme. Take the Gotha notes second, in any edition that prints them as the marginal comments they are: check the setting, a draft party programme under negotiation, the private circulation, the 1891 publication by Engels, and the transition sentence's position as an answer to a question about the state's transformation. Take the French writings third, and check the symmetry in the sentences themselves, the application of dictatorship language to the bourgeois republic before or beside its application to the proletariat. Then perform the two negative checks, which are the protocol's most instructive steps: search the Communist Manifesto for the phrase and confirm its absence, and search a concordance or a digital edition of the collected works for the phrase's occurrences, noting how the total you get depends on the corpus and the language you search, which is the experiential proof of this article's refusal to print an occurrence count. A reader who has run the protocol owns the phrase's foundations in a way no summary can confer, and will read the enormous secondary literature, Lenin and Kautsky included, with the salutary instinct to ask of every claim the question the protocol teaches: which passage, in which genre, of what date, carrying how much of the structure being hung on it.

## The people's democracies: the phrase in its coalition form

Between the Bolshevik original and the Chinese adaptation stands a third family of usages that the timeline compresses and that a full account should open, because it shows the phrase being deliberately diluted for coalition politics and then reconcentrated. In the states established in Eastern Europe after 1945, the ruling parties initially governed under the formula of people's democracy, a transitional form described in the doctrine of the time as distinct from the Soviet model: coalition in appearance, multi-party in façade, and defined by its theorists as a stage on the road toward the dictatorship of the proletariat rather than as that dictatorship itself. The distinction was not merely decorative. It allowed the parties to present their rule as a national and popular stage while the underlying transformation, the elimination of rival parties, the fusion of the coercive apparatus with the party, the alignment of courts and press, proceeded by the sequence historians call salami tactics, each slice too thin to provoke the resistance the whole would have met. By the end of the nineteen forties the doctrinal description caught up with the practice, and the people's democracies were reclassified in the movement's theory as states exercising the dictatorship of the proletariat in a national form, a reclassification that illustrates, in a single doctrinal gesture, the form-word's elasticity: the phrase had become a status to be conferred on a regime by the movement's centre when the regime's construction was complete, rather than a description of who ruled that anyone could test. Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968 then supplied the verdict column for this family, in uprisings and reforms suppressed by the centre's armies, and the phrase's Western custodians drew the lesson the abandonment section recorded. For the glossary's reader the family matters because it is the clearest case of the phrase being used as a grade in a hierarchy of regimes, awarded from outside, which is as far from Marx's analytical usage, a classification any reader could apply to any state including one's own, as the word's long career ever travelled.

## Why the phrase repays study by readers who want nothing from it

A closing word is owed to the reader who has no stake in the phrase's vindication or condemnation and has followed the article this far on the strength of the dossier's intrinsic interest. Such a reader has been given, under the cover of one term's biography, a worked example of the general process by which political vocabularies decay and convert: a precise analytical term, sparsely used by its author, edited into doctrine by a loyal lieutenant, left unspecified at exactly the points where specification governs safety, seized as a warrant by the first government that needed one, converted by that government's practice into the name of a form, codified in constitutions, defended by fallacies at both exits, and finally retired by its own heirs when its public meaning escaped their custody beyond recall. The process is not unique to this phrase or to this tradition. It is the standard biography of load-bearing political words, and a reader who has watched it happen slowly, stage by stage, with the evidence separated at each stage into meaning and verdict, owns a template that transfers to every other term in the public vocabulary whose history is doing unexamined work in current argument. That transfer, more than any finding about 1852 or 1875, is the article's reason for its length, and it is why this series gives a phrase of a handful of occurrences an entry of this size: the occurrences are few, but the career is the century's, and the instruments the career taught, the symmetry test and the evidence test, are the reader's to keep.

The two instruments also tell the reader how to approach the neighbouring entries in this cluster of the series, each of which guards one border of the territory surveyed here. The theory of the state supplies the analysis of class rule within which the phrase is a single term; the Gotha guide supplies the transition sentence in its full textual setting; the Commune study supplies the institutional inventory in its full historical setting; the withering away entry supplies the far side of the border the phrase never crosses; and the authoritarianism entry tries the causal case at the length it deserves. Read in that order, the cluster performs the same separation at series scale that this article performs within its own pages, and a reader who works through it will have met the phrase in every relation that matters: as a word in a vocabulary, as a sentence in a text, as a warrant in a struggle, as a name over a record, and as a problem in the theory of political language itself. That is the whole of what a glossary can do with such a phrase, and it is enough.

## After the regimes: the phrase since the fall of its states

The timeline's last row in the artifact table ends with the Western abandonment, but the career has a coda that belongs in the text, since the states that carried the phrase have now themselves passed into history. Between 1989 and 1991 the Eastern European party states collapsed in sequence and the Soviet Union dissolved, and the phrase lost, within three years, every government that had borne it in Europe, together with the institutional ecosystem, the party schools, the academies, the publishing houses, that had reproduced its regime sense as common knowledge for two generations. The successor parties of the region renamed themselves, rewrote their programmes and competed in elections under vocabularies from which the phrase was absent, completing at the level of the East the retirement the West's parties had performed in the nineteen seventies, though from opposite causes: the Western parties had dropped the word because their voters rejected what it had come to mean, and the Eastern parties dropped it because the states that defined its meaning had ceased to exist. China, Vietnam, Cuba, Laos and North Korea retained states whose constitutions descend from the phrase's family, under adaptations and, in the Chinese case, under an official formula that subordinates the old term to the people's democratic dictatorship and to later doctrinal layers, so that the phrase's governmental career survives in Asia in forms its European career no longer exhibits.

For the glossary reader the coda's significance is the change in the phrase's custody that followed the collapse. A term whose public meaning was set, for seventy years, by the states that bore it is now a term whose meaning is set by texts, by scholarship and by memory politics, and the contest over it has accordingly changed character: it is fought in textbooks, in lustration debates, in the historiography of the regimes, and in the internal arguments of the small movements that still claim the inheritance, rather than in the programmes of governing parties. That change returns the phrase, by an irony its biography has earned, to something like its condition in Marx's lifetime: a term of analysis and argument, used by minorities, detached from the machinery of any European state. The acquired sense has not evaporated; no reader of the phrase now can fail to hear the century in it, and the article has insisted throughout that the hearing is part of the evidence. But the balance between the meaning question and the verdict question has shifted for the first time since 1917, because the verdict's exhibits are closed archives now rather than standing governments, and the phrase's future uses will be scholarly and polemical rather than constitutional. A glossary written in that condition has the duty this article has attempted: to fix the record of both questions while both can still be examined in full, and to hand the reader the instruments before the phrase's next custodians, whoever they prove to be, begin their own work on it.

## The quiet decades: the phrase inside the Second International before the war

Between Engels's publications of the early eighteen nineties and the duel of 1918, the phrase lived the life of a doctrinal heirloom: carried in programmes, cited in May Day oratory, expounded in party schools, and rarely examined, because the movement that owned it was growing by every measure its members tracked and the transition the phrase named seemed a question for a future that legal mass parties could afford to leave vague. The German party's Erfurt Programme of 1891, the movement's model charter, did not rest its demands on the phrase, and the theoretical commentary Kautsky supplied for that programme treated the conquest of political power by the proletariat as the hinge of history without making dictatorship its headline. The vagueness was not accidental and it was not cowardice alone. A party contesting elections, building unions, newspapers and insurance funds inside states that prosecuted sedition, had institutional reasons to speak of power in the grammar of majorities, and the phrase's revolutionary grammar sat in the programme's reserve, acknowledged as inheritance and seldom drawn upon. Bernstein's revisionist challenge at the decade's end, arguing that the movement was everything and the final goal nothing, forced the phrase's defenders to say what the transition involved, and the orthodox replies, Luxemburg's among them, defended the goal's necessity in terms of class power while still leaving the phrase's institutional content at the level of generality Marx had left it. The quiet decades thus bequeathed 1918 a phrase universally owned and nowhere specified, which is why the specification, when it came, could be supplied by the men who held a state rather than by the movement that held the texts. Reception history rarely offers so clean a lesson: a doctrine's unspecified centre does not stay unspecified. It waits for its first government.

## The phrase in translation: the German original and its travels

The German form, Diktatur des Proletariats, deserves a paragraph of its own in any precision treatment, because translation choices have quietly steered the phrase's reception in the languages where most of its arguments are conducted. Diktatur in nineteenth-century German political usage shared the Roman inheritance and the class usage described earlier in this article, and German readers of Marx's century could hear in it the register of emergency authority that English readers, after the twentieth century, cannot hear in dictatorship without effort. The genitive construction matters too: the dictatorship of the proletariat, the rule belonging to and exercised by the class, a construction that sits awkwardly with the later picture of rule exercised over the class by its trustees, and translators and commentators in several languages have noted how the grammar itself keeps posing the question the Bolshevik settlement answered by substitution. In Russian the phrase passed into a vocabulary already shaped by debates about revolutionary authority under tsarism, and in Chinese its adapted successor, the people's democratic dictatorship announced in Mao's essay of 1949, openly re-engineered the concept for a coalition of classes under party leadership, dictatorship over enemies combined with democracy among the people, a formula whose candour about the two registers at least stated in public what other custodians of the phrase practised without a formula. Each language's career is a small reception history of its own, and the comparative study of the translations, which words were chosen, which constitutions adopted which rendering, remains one of the underworked fields the researcher persona below commends. For the glossary reader the practical lesson is modest and firm: when the phrase is quoted in translation, the quotation carries the translator's century as well as the author's, and checking the century is part of checking the quotation.

## The phrase abroad: adaptations and their candour

The phrase's career outside Europe tests the article's instruments on fresh material, because the adaptations were explicit in ways the European originals often were not. The Chinese formula just mentioned defined itself as a joint dictatorship of several revolutionary classes led by the working class through its party, and its founding essay stated the two faces of the state, democracy for the people and dictatorship for the reactionaries, with a frankness that makes the document unusually valuable to the analyst: the categories of friend and enemy are named, the party's leading position is asserted rather than implied, and the reader can watch the content-word and the form-word operating in one sentence, class alliance as content, party leadership as form. Vietnamese and Cuban adaptations varied the formula toward their own coalitions and histories, national liberation in the one case supplying the phrase with an anti-colonial register largely absent from its European career, and in each case the local party's congresses and constitutions document the adaptation in dated texts that reception historians can actually work on. What the adaptations collectively show is that the phrase functioned, by the middle of the twentieth century, less as a quotation from Marx than as a constitutional genre, a recognised way for a revolutionary party to describe the state it intended to run, with the Marxist genealogy supplying legitimacy and the local text supplying the institutions. A genre can be studied as the timeline studied the original: by what it meant at each stage, who used it, and what changed, and the genre's stages are written in the constitutions of states whose records the verdict column of any honest account must also carry.

## Where the phrase hands over: the boundary with the withering away

Every account of the phrase eventually reaches the border it shares with the doctrine of the state's disappearance, and the border is worth marking precisely because visitors constantly cross it without noticing they have changed subjects. The dictatorship of the proletariat names a state, the last state in the theory's sequence, still an apparatus of class coercion and still, in the theory's own terms, a state in the full sense. The withering away names the process beyond that state's far side, in which the functions of rule are progressively absorbed by the society itself and the apparatus loses its reason to exist. Confusing the two produces the common muddle in which the phrase is accused of promising an immediate statelessness it never promised, or credited with a libertarianism its transitional coercion contradicts. The separate entry on the [withering away of the state](/2013/01/15/withering-away-of-the-state/) treats the far side of the border in full. For this article's purposes the border itself is the point: the phrase stakes out the territory on the near side, the territory of power, transition and coercion, and its silences about that territory's government are the silences the preceding sections inventoried. A reader who keeps the border in view will also see why the certification problem concentrates exactly on it. The crossing of the border is the event the transition exists to reach, the event no calendar schedules and no institution outside the ruling power is positioned to declare, and the history of the phrase's regimes can be written, without distortion, as the history of that declaration endlessly deferred by the only authority competent, under the doctrine, to make it.

## For students and exam candidates

For students and exam candidates this phrase is a gift disguised as a hazard, because it rewards exactly the skills examinations exist to detect and punishes exactly the habits they exist to expose. The mark-carrying move is the symmetry test stated early and applied once: define the term as class rule, note that Marx applies it to the parliamentary republic in the same analysis, and conclude that the term identifies which class holds power rather than the form of its rule. That single paragraph, which can be written in four sentences, separates a script from the cohort more reliably than any quantity of narrative, because the cohort's scripts, in the main, will either convict Marx of advocating tyranny or acquit him by etymology, and both errors are visible to a marker within a page. Support the definition with the three anchors and their genres: the French writings, the Weydemeyer letter of 1852 with its three propositions, and the Gotha notes of 1875 with the transition sentence. State the rarity plainly, since rarity is evidence and markers reward candidates who use it. Then, and only then, earn the evaluation marks by separating the questions: the Commune measures as the concept's associated content, the no internal limit objection at full strength, and the concession that the limits were never given the phrase's own emphasis in transmission. A script that holds meaning and verdict apart to the last line will outscore a script twice its length that merges them, and the student who wants the structure in a reusable form will find the key text's own exposition in the [critique of the gotha programme guide](/2013/09/01/critique-of-the-gotha-programme-guide/).

## For teachers and seminar leaders

For teachers and seminar leaders the phrase arrives pre-loaded in a way few syllabus terms do: students meet it with a verdict already installed, from family memory, from media usage, from the ambient politics of the classroom's country, and the verdict fires before the first text is opened. The sequence that works is the sequence of this article, and it works because it postpones every occasion for the installed verdict to discharge. Begin with Rome, not with Russia: the emergency magistracy, its commission and its term, Cincinnatus and then Sulla, so that the class acquires the older sense as a piece of institutional history before the word can be heard as an insult or a programme. Move to the nineteenth-century class usage and to the symmetry in Marx's French writings, letting the parliamentary republic sentence land while the class is still thinking philologically. Only then introduce the anchor passages and the rarity, and only after the meaning is secure open the dossier of 1918, Kautsky, Lenin and Luxemburg as a three-cornered seminar exercise in which students must argue the position furthest from the one they arrived with. Leave the record of the regimes to the final session, presented as this article presents it, with ranges and with the two fallacies named in advance, so that the class's moral energy is spent on evidence rather than on the smothering moves. The teacher's discipline throughout is the article's: never let a meaning claim do verdict work, never let a verdict do dictionary work, and return every intervention, patiently, to the question of which of the two the speaker is addressing.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

For researchers, historians, and journalists the phrase is a professional hazard of a specific kind: it is a term whose scholarly sense and public sense have diverged so completely that using it without a gloss misinforms whichever audience the writer did not have in mind, and the working rules follow from that divergence. The historian's rule is attribution discipline at the level of the sentence: never let the phrase appear in one's own voice as a description of a regime without marking whose description it is, the regime's constitution, its opponents' polemic or the analyst's considered classification, because the three uses carry different evidence and collapse into one another in careless prose. When the subject is Marx, cite the passage, the genre and the date; the corpus is small enough that no claim about the phrase need ever rest on paraphrase of a paraphrase. When the subject is a regime, prefer the institutional inventory to the label: party monopoly, franchise, courts, press, security apparatus, succession, since the inventory is checkable and the label is an argument. The journalist's rule is the hearing rule the Eurocommunists learned at cost: audiences hear the acquired sense, so a quotation containing the phrase will be heard as a statement about one party rule whatever the speaker intended, and reporting it fairly means reporting that hearing as part of the story, together with any gloss the speaker supplies. Researchers working across the phrase's languages should note the German Diktatur des Proletariats and the Russian and Chinese adaptations as distinct exhibits rather than as transparent equivalents, since each national career bent the term toward its own institutions. Finally, the scholarly frontier on the phrase is not a new reading of the three passages, which are short and well combed, but the reception archives: party school curricula, translation histories, the congress records of the abandonment years, where the drift from content-word to form-word can be documented in dated paper rather than inferred. That is where the next solid work on the term will be done, and this article's timeline is offered as the scaffolding for it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does dictatorship of the proletariat mean?

In Marx's usage it means the rule of the working class as a class during the transition between capitalist and communist society. Dictatorship here carries its older sense of class rule, the same sense in which he called a parliamentary republic a dictatorship of the bourgeoisie. The phrase identifies which class holds state power. It does not, in his texts, specify a single party, a personal ruler or a machinery of terror, and its whole point in his own summary was transitional: the bridge toward a society in which classes, and with them class rule itself, have disappeared.

### Q: Did Marx mean dictatorship in the modern sense?

No. The sense later attached to the word, rule by one person or one party through suppressed law and organised fear, belongs to the century after his death and to the regimes that taught the word its new meaning. Marx used dictatorship as his century used it in class analysis: the dominance of one class over the state, whatever the constitutional form. He applied it to republican France with its assembly and elections. Recovering his sense does not settle what later regimes did under the phrase, and this article treats those as separate questions throughout, but as a matter of textual fact his meaning was class rule, not personal tyranny.

### Q: How often does the phrase dictatorship of the proletariat appear in Marx?

A handful of times across forty years of writing, and any more precise figure should be distrusted unless it defines its corpus and its search terms. The secure anchors are three: his analysis of the French revolutions around 1850, the letter to Weydemeyer of March 1852 where he lists the idea among his own contributions, and the Critique of the Gotha Programme of 1875 where the phrase names the transitional state. It does not appear in the Communist Manifesto or in Capital. Scholars who have combed the published and unpublished writings report a small number of occurrences, concentrated in the French period with the single late recurrence, which is why this article states the frequency as a handful rather than as a count.

### Q: Does the dictatorship of the proletariat mean a one party state?

Not in the texts where the phrase appears. Those passages mention no party at all; they name a class and a transition. The one party form entered the phrase's history through the Bolshevik settlement after 1917, when rival parties were banned in stages, factions inside the ruling party were prohibited in 1921, and the party's apparatus fused with the state's. Lenin's theory supplied the justification, arguing that the party embodies the class's real interests. Whether that substitution was a legitimate development or a betrayal of the concept was the substance of the dispute between Kautsky, Lenin and Luxemburg, but its date and its authorship are not in doubt: it is a twentieth-century construction, not a nineteenth-century definition.

### Q: What did Kautsky say about the dictatorship of the proletariat?

In his pamphlet of 1918 Karl Kautsky argued that Marx had used the term for a condition and not for a form of government: the condition in which the proletariat holds political power, which in a developed country would express itself through democracy, since a class of millions can rule only through suffrage, representation and public contest. On that basis he charged the Bolsheviks with building the dictatorship of a party rather than of a class, disfranchising and suppressing the very class in whose name they ruled. He also predicted that an apparatus built that way would not dissolve on any proclaimed schedule, because it would acquire interests of its own. Lenin's furious reply, which redefined dictatorship as rule unrestricted by law, confirmed for most readers that the two men were no longer using the phrase in the same sense.

### Q: What did Luxemburg criticise about the Bolshevik dictatorship?

Writing from prison in 1918, as a supporter of the revolution, Rosa Luxemburg criticised the dispersal of the Constituent Assembly, the restriction of the suffrage, and the suppression of freedom of the press, assembly and association for the Bolsheviks' opponents. Her argument was institutional rather than sentimental: a class rules only through the broadest public life, and once elections, newspapers and assemblies are extinguished, the soviets themselves wither into bodies that ratify decisions taken elsewhere, while a bureaucracy becomes the only active element in public life. Her manuscript, unpublished until 1922, also contains the sentence for which she is most quoted in this connection, that freedom is always the freedom of the one who thinks differently. She intended the criticism as a defence of the revolution against its own methods.

### Q: Why did communist parties drop the phrase?

The large Western communist parties dropped it during the nineteen seventies because the word had become unusable in democratic politics. Their electorates heard dictatorship in the sense the century's regimes had given it, as one party rule and repression, and no explanation of the older class meaning could be heard over that association. The French party removed the phrase from its programme at its congress of 1976, the formal landmark; the Spanish party under Carrillo built its Eurocommunist strategy on distance from the term, and the Italian party let it lapse from working use. Notably, the parties kept much of their class analysis of the state. What they abandoned was the word, on the judgement that a phrase means what its hearers hear, and what hearers heard was the Eastern record.

### Q: Is every state a class dictatorship in Marx's usage?

Yes, that is the usage's central consequence and the basis of the symmetry test used throughout this article. In Marx's analysis every state is the organised power of a dominant class: the monarchy resting on landed property expresses the rule of an aristocracy, the parliamentary republic the rule of the bourgeoisie, and the transitional order the rule of the proletariat. The term therefore does not distinguish states that are dictatorships from states that are not; it distinguishes which class's dictatorship a given state is. This is why the word cannot mean personal tyranny in one of his sentences and class rule in another. What the usage does not do is rank the forms: it classifies by class content and leaves the evaluation of liberties, rights and coercion under each form to be made on separate evidence.

### Q: How long is the dictatorship of the proletariat meant to last?

Marx gives no term in years, and the absence is built into the concept: the transition lasts as long as the transformation between capitalist and communist society lasts, ending when classes themselves have been abolished and the state, in the phrase of the tradition, withers away. The Weydemeyer letter defines the dictatorship by its destination, as the transition to the abolition of all classes; the Gotha notes describe a first phase of the new society still stamped with the old society's birthmarks. Because the end is defined by a social condition rather than a date, the question of who certifies that the condition has arrived becomes politically decisive, and critics of the concept have always pressed exactly there. The Soviet party's declaration in 1961 that the dictatorship had fulfilled its mission, issued while its form of rule continued unchanged, is the standing illustration of the difficulty.

### Q: Does the term explain twentieth century repression?

Partly, at the level of legitimation and classification, and insufficiently at the level of cause. Regimes ruling under the phrase justified bans, purges and police operations in its vocabulary, classifying opponents as members of a doomed class whose resistance the transition entitled the state to break; the vocabulary prepared the categories into which victims were filed. But mass repression has been practised under many doctrines, and the heaviest institutional machinery in the communist cases, the substitutionist party, the ban on factions, the security apparatus fused with the state, was built by the phrase's inheritors rather than specified by the phrase. Historians divide the causal shares among ideology, war, scarcity, institutions and rulers. The term explains the filing system and the self-understanding of the operators better than it explains the machine, and this article keeps that distinction rather than letting one word carry the whole century.

### Q: How should a teacher introduce the phrase without importing later associations?

Start where the word started, not where it ended. Open with the Roman magistracy, its commission and its six-month horizon, using Cincinnatus and Sulla to show an office defined by expiry and then abused past it. Move next to the nineteenth-century habit of speaking about the dominance of a class, and let students meet Marx's application of the word to a parliamentary republic before they meet his application of it to the proletariat, so the symmetry registers before the controversy does. Introduce the three anchor passages and their rarity after the older sense is secure, then stage the 1918 dispute as a three-cornered exercise, and leave the regimes' record for the end, with figures given as scholarly ranges. The ordering is the pedagogy: each stage is understood before the next one's associations can contaminate it.

### Q: What would the symmetry test show about a parliamentary republic?

It would classify the republic by its class content rather than by its constitution. In Marx's usage a parliamentary republic in which property, law and administration systematically secure the dominance of owners over those who live by wages is the dictatorship of the bourgeoisie conducted through elections, and the test then requires the identical word, with the identical meaning, for the proletarian transition. The test governs the word only. It does not equate the arrangements morally or equate their records, and it leaves every question about rights, coercion and the possibility of removing a government to be answered on evidence, where this article insists such questions belong.

### Q: Why does the phrase's rarity in Marx matter for interpretation?

Because a phrase used a handful of times cannot contain a detailed doctrine, and acknowledging the rarity forces every interpreter to show their working. The anchor passages establish a transition, a ruling class and a destination; they do not establish a party form, a franchise, a legal code or a duration. Every richer account is a construction from materials elsewhere, from the Commune writings, from Lenin's theory of the party, from the practice of states, and honest argument marks the joins between text and construction. Rarity also cuts against the phrase's enemies: a concept this marginal in the corpus cannot be the secret key to everything Marx wrote. Interpretations that present it as either the blueprint or the acquittal are both reading the reception back into three passages.

### Q: How does the term differ from dictatorship as personal rule?

In nearly every particular except the concentration of power they both name. Personal rule is the power of an individual, held without a term, exercised through a court and an apparatus answerable to the ruler, and legitimised by the ruler's person, mission or blood. The dictatorship of the proletariat, in its original usage, is the power of a class, a collective subject of millions, held in principle for a transition whose end is defined by the abolition of classes, and legitimised by a theory of history rather than by a person. The two concepts share the word because both suspend the ordinary distribution of power, and the entire confusion of the phrase's career comes from the shared word outliving the difference. Twentieth-century regimes repeatedly fused the two, producing personal rule that spoke the language of class rule, which is why the distinction has to be re-drawn by each generation of readers.

### Q: What evidence separates class rule from party rule in a given case?

The evidence is institutional and observable, and this article assembles it from Luxemburg's conditions and the Commune inventory. Look at whether officials are elected in contests with unfixed outcomes and recallable in practice; whether the class in whose name power is held can organise independently, publish disagreements, strike and vote against incumbents without being reclassified as enemies; whether coercion is dispersed and accountable or concentrated in an apparatus answerable only upward; whether office brings a visibly separate standard of life; and whether the transition's continuation has ever been submitted to a decision the rulers could lose. Where the answers run one way, a party rules over a class while bearing its name, whatever the constitution proclaims. Where they run the other, the class claim has evidence behind it. The test comes from inside the tradition, which is why regimes of the tradition have never been able to dismiss it as a foreign standard.
