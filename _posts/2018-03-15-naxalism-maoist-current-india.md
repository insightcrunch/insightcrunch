Naxalism names the armed communist movements that began with a peasant uprising in 1967 in the Naxalbari area of northern West Bengal, broke away from India's parliamentary communist parties over the question of armed struggle, splintered into a large family of rival organisations, and ended by operating principally among tribal populations in the forested districts of central and eastern India. A reader who meets the word in a headline usually meets it as a security problem, a matter of ambushes, bans, and deployments. That framing is the last chapter of the story mistaken for the whole book. The movement began as a quarrel about land inside the communist movement itself, between comrades who had organised the same villages and read the same texts, and everything that followed, the split, the founding line, the fragmentation, the strange later map, descends from that quarrel and from the ground on which it was fought.

![A relief map of forested hill districts beside a bundle of village land records tied with string, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Hold the strangeness of the outcome in view from the start, because it is the puzzle this article exists to solve. A movement launched in the name of proletarian revolution, guided by a doctrine elaborated for peasant war in China and industrial insurrection in Europe, came to rest on a social base that classical Marxism had never identified as revolutionary: Adivasi communities in forest districts, many of them only lightly connected to wage labour, markets, or the written word. Its heartland was never the factory city or the rich peasant belt. It settled where the Indian state's administrative presence was thinnest, where rights over forest land were contested between villages and the forest department, and where displacement for mines, dams, and industrial projects had been concentrated. The explanation offered here, named the geography of dispossession, holds that the movement's persistence maps onto that configuration of forest, mineral wealth, weak administration, and contested land, rather than onto any distribution of ideological conviction. Where those conditions combined, an armed organisation could establish itself and find grievance ready-made. Where they did not, the same doctrine, preached by the same kind of cadres, withered.

That inversion deserves emphasis because it reverses the usual order of explanation. Accounts of revolutionary movements typically begin with ideas and treat geography as scenery. Here the sequence runs the other way. Doctrine explains the movement's founding choices, its vocabulary, its internal quarrels, and its catastrophic early strategy. Doctrine does not explain its address. The address was chosen by terrain, by the location of minerals under tribal land, by the reach or non-reach of the tehsil office and the police station, and by the long history of forest law that had converted customary users of the forest into encroachers on it. A student who grasps that distinction, between what a movement believes and where a movement can live, holds the key not only to this subject but to a general pattern the series meets repeatedly: a movement's social base is explained by where it can operate at least as much as by whom its doctrine addresses.

## What Naxalism is, and what the word covers

Begin with the name, because the name is a place. Naxalbari is a small area in the Siliguri subdivision of Darjeeling district, in the narrow northern corridor of West Bengal, where the plains rise toward the Himalayan foothills and tea gardens alternate with peasant villages. In May 1967, peasants organised by local communist cadres moved to seize land and harvests they held to be theirs under the law, and the resulting confrontation, and its suppression, gave the Indian language a new word. Adherents and newspapers alike began calling the rebels Naxalites, and the tendency Naxalism, and the labels stuck to every organisation that claimed descent from that rising, including organisations that later repudiated much of what the founders had done. The word therefore denotes a lineage rather than a single body. At almost no point in the half century after 1967 was there one Naxalite movement in the organisational sense. There was a family, quarrelsome and multiplying, whose members shared an origin myth, a set of founding texts, and a refusal of the parliamentary road, while disagreeing about nearly everything else.

Precision about the lineage matters because coverage routinely violates it. The armed organisations that merged in the 2000s, the electoral party that descends from the same founding, the small groups that survive as study circles with famous letterheads, all get compressed into a single menacing noun. Compression flatters the security framing, since a single enemy is easier to describe than a genealogy, and it flatters the movement's own propaganda, which has always claimed a unity the record contradicts. The streams table set out later in this article is the corrective instrument: one row per major current, with its period of formation, its position on armed struggle, its social base, whether it contests elections, and its organisational standing described in general terms. Read the table before reading any headline about the subject, and the headline will usually be found to be describing one row while implying all of them.

The canonical definition can now be stated plainly. Naxalism denotes the armed communist movements originating in the 1967 rising in northern West Bengal, which broke from the parliamentary communist parties over strategy, adopted the doctrine of protracted armed struggle in the countryside, fragmented extensively within a few years of their founding, and in their surviving armed form came to operate principally among Adivasi populations in central and eastern India. Each clause of that definition carries an argument. The origin was a land conflict, not a conspiracy imported from abroad. The break was over the road to power, not over the goal. The fragmentation was structural, produced by the founding strategy itself. The later tribal base was an outcome to be explained, not a founding intention. The sections that follow take the clauses in order, giving roughly a fifth of the article to the origin and the split, a fifth to the founding line and its consequences, a fifth to fragmentation and the divergent roads that followed, a quarter to the structural explanation of the later geography, and the remainder to the criticisms levelled from inside Marxism and to the movement's decline.

Two disciplines govern the telling throughout. The movement's account of itself is reported from its own documents and attributed to them, never absorbed into the narration as fact, and the security establishment's account is handled the same way, attributed to the analysts and officials who produced it. These are the two great interested literatures on the subject, and between them they have crowded out the structural explanation that a reader most needs. The second discipline concerns evidence of scale. Every number in this field, of people killed, of supporters, of villages or districts touched, originates with a party to the conflict or with agencies reporting to one, and the estimates diverge wildly. This article therefore states no casualty figures of any kind, no counts of districts, cadres, or areas, and no membership claims, for any side, at any point. Scale is described in words: extensive, contracted, concentrated, thin. A reader who wants the rival figures can find them in the interested literatures, where they belong, labelled with their sources.

## A land conflict inside the communist movement

To understand why a village dispute could split a national movement, begin with the material the dispute was made of. The Bengal countryside the rebels inherited was a layered structure of claims. At the top stood landlords and tenure holders, the jotedars, who controlled substantial blocks of land. Below them worked sharecroppers, the bargadars or adhiars, who tilled plots they did not own and surrendered a customary share of the harvest, often half, for the privilege, with no security against eviction and no record of their rights that a court would recognise. Below them again stood agricultural labourers with no land at all, and around the villages stood the tea gardens, whose workers were largely Adivasi and Nepali migrants held in a separate, enclosed regime. Post independence land reform legislation had promised ceilings on holdings and protection for sharecroppers, and the promises had been extensively evaded: holdings were partitioned among relatives on paper, sharecroppers were rotated between plots so that no continuous occupancy could be proved, and the record of rights remained, in village after village, a fiction maintained by those it benefited.

The communist movement in Bengal had grown up organising precisely these grievances. The Tebhaga movement of the mid 1940s, led by the communist peasant front, had demanded that the sharecropper retain two thirds of the harvest rather than half, and had shaken the northern districts before being beaten back. In Hyderabad's Telangana region, communist led peasants had gone further still, redistributing land and defending liberated villages by arms until the Indian army ended the experiment after independence. These campaigns left the party with deep village roots in exactly the districts where land hunger was sharpest, with cadres whose authority rested on their record in such struggles, and with a permanent internal argument about what the organisation was for. Was the party a machine for winning elections and administering reforms within the republic's constitution, using its rural base as a vote bank and a bargaining counter? Or was it the embryo of a revolution that would one day take up arms against the Indian state itself, with every legal struggle a preparation for that day? The question was never abstract in Bengal. It was asked at village level, by organisers who had buried comrades from the earlier struggles and who watched the reformed law being mocked in the fields they walked each morning.

The parliamentary road acquired imposing evidence during the 1960s. The communists had already formed a government in Kerala in 1957, the first such ministry to win office through the ballot anywhere in the world on that scale, and though it was dismissed by the centre within two years, the lesson many drew was that office was attainable and worth having. The party itself split in 1964, for reasons examined in the series history of the [communist movement in India](/2017/12/15/communist-movement-india-history/), into a party that leaned toward cooperation with the Congress and a larger breakaway party, the Communist Party of India (Marxist), that kept a more combative posture while remaining committed to contesting elections. By 1967 the second of these stood at the head of a coalition government in West Bengal itself, holding the levers of a state administration, including its police, and pledged to carry out land reform through legal machinery. The men who would lead the Naxalbari rising were members of that party, working its peasant front in the Siliguri area, and their rebellion, when it came, was aimed as much at their own leadership in Calcutta as at any landlord. They held that the party had traded the revolution for office, and that the proof would be visible the moment its government was asked to choose between the sharecropper and public order.

### Why did land, rather than the factory, become the movement's first question?

Because the exploitation its cadres confronted daily was agrarian: sharecroppers surrendering half a harvest without legal record, labourers without plots, and reform statutes evaded through paper partitions of holdings. The industrial working class in the region was small and enclosed in tea gardens, while the village offered both the grievance and the organised following that made action possible.

The local leadership around Naxalbari had been preparing its ground for years. Cadres such as Kanu Sanyal, a tireless organiser in the Terai villages, and Jangal Santhal, drawn from the Adivasi community itself, had built peasant associations that pressed claims plot by plot: recording sharecroppers, resisting evictions, recovering land held above the ceiling, harvesting crops on disputed fields. Charu Majumdar, the theoretician who would soon give the revolt its doctrine, was a party organiser from Siliguri town whose writings circulated among these cadres, arguing that India was ripe for an agrarian revolution on the Chinese model and that the parliamentary leadership was suppressing that ripeness out of attachment to its own respectability. The relationship between the village organisers and the party centre had been deteriorating through the mid 1960s as the centre disciplined radicals and the radicals accused the centre of revisionism, the period's term for abandoning revolution while keeping its vocabulary. When the United Front ministry took office in Calcutta in the spring of 1967, the Terai cadres read the event through their doctrine: a communist led government, they predicted, would defend the existing order in the countryside, and its doing so would expose the parliamentary road before the whole movement. The rising that followed was, among other things, an experiment staged to prove that prediction.

## The rising in the Terai and its suppression

The sequence in the spring of 1967 can be told without ornament. Peasant associations in the Naxalbari, Kharibari, and Phansidewa areas moved from pressing claims through petitions and conferences to direct action: occupying land identified as held above the ceiling or as landlord property, harvesting standing crops on disputed plots, arming village defence groups with bows, spears, and a few firearms, and setting up parallel authority in pockets of the countryside. A police party sent into the area was ambushed and an officer killed. The state government, after hesitation that itself became part of the legend, ordered suppression, and police fired on a gathering of villagers at Prasad Jote, killing villagers, among them women. Armed police and then paramilitary columns combed the area through the monsoon months, the leadership was arrested or driven underground, and by late summer the organised rising in its birthplace had been broken. The whole episode, from first seizure to suppression, occupied a single season.

Measured by any military standard the rising was negligible, a few hundred activists in one subdivision, suppressed by a provincial police force without difficulty. Its consequences were entirely disproportionate, and they flowed from a single fact that the brief's cold fact pattern states and that every account since has had to confront: the order to suppress was given by a government in which the communist party of the rebels themselves was the leading partner. The home portfolio, responsible for the police, sat with that party's own leadership. For the radicals, the event proved their doctrine in the most public way imaginable: communists in office had fired on communists in the fields, in defence of the property relations both had sworn to abolish. For the party leadership, the episode was an indiscipline that had embarrassed a fragile coalition and invited repression on the whole movement, and it expelled the rebels. Both readings were honestly held, both were argued in print for years afterward, and the quarrel between them is the true founding document of Naxalism. Readers who want the subsequent history of that governing party in Bengal should follow the series analysis of the [Left Front in Bengal](/2018/01/15/west-bengal-left-front-analysis/), which takes up the record of the long ministry that followed; the concern here is with those who walked out of it.

### What happened when the state answered the uprising?

Suppression was swift and was ordered by a coalition ministry in which the rebels' own party held the leading place and the police portfolio. Village organisations were dismantled, leaders jailed or driven underground, and the rising in its home subdivision was extinguished within a season, while the political argument it had staged escaped suppression entirely.

The expelled and the sympathetic did not disperse. Through the months after the suppression, rebel cadres across several states corresponded, published, and organised, encouraged from abroad by the Chinese party's press, which praised the rising in famous editorials as a thunderclap of revolution in India, a characterisation the Indian rebels treasured and their opponents never forgot. Coordination committees of revolutionaries formed inside and outside the parent party, gathering the Bengal radicals with like minded groups in Andhra Pradesh, where a parallel agrarian struggle in the Srikakulam hills was moving toward armed action, and in other states where local movements had reached similar conclusions. The organisational question was whether these scattered revolts constituted a tendency within the old party to be fought for, or a new party to be founded against it. Majumdar's answer, pressed with increasing insistence through his series of programmatic articles known as the Historic Eight Documents, was that the old parties were beyond capture and that a genuine communist party had to be built anew, on a line that repudiated parliamentarism root and branch.

## The split: two answers to the question of the state

Strip the polemic away and the split of the late 1960s turns on one question: what is the Indian state, and how does it fall? The parliamentary parties, in their different dialects, held that the republic's constitution, however compromised by landlord and capitalist influence, was a real arena: elections could be won, ministries formed, reforms enacted, and the balance of forces shifted by organisation among workers and peasants using legal rights. Their critics within the movement answered that this mistook the furniture for the house. On the rebel analysis, the Indian state was the instrument of a ruling bloc of landlords, big capitalists tied to foreign capital, and a bureaucracy inherited from colonial rule; its elections were a mechanism for rotating managers of that bloc; and a party that entered its ministries became, whatever its intentions, an administrator of the order it existed to destroy, as the events in the Terai had just demonstrated. From that diagnosis the strategy followed of itself: build liberated bases in the countryside where the state's writ was weakest, raise a people's army by stages, encircle the cities from the villages as the Chinese revolution had done, and treat every parliamentary engagement as a snare. The doctrine of Maoism in its general form, its theory of protracted war and of the peasantry as the main revolutionary force in a backward country, belongs to the series treatment of [Maoism and the Soviet path](/2018/09/01/maoism-vs-soviet-marxism/); what matters here is the use Indian rebels made of it, as a warrant for decisions already reached on Bengal evidence.

### What did the two sides of the split disagree about?

About the character of the Indian state and how it could be overcome. The parliamentary parties treated elections, ministries, and legal mass organisation as a genuine road for shifting the balance of forces. The rebels treated the state as a landlord and capitalist instrument whose parliamentary forms were a snare, leaving armed agrarian war as the only road.

Each side's case had a body of evidence, and fairness requires the weight of both to be felt. The parliamentary case pointed to reforms actually delivered through office, to the protection legality afforded mass organisations, and to the fate of every armed adventure attempted in the republic's short history, each of which had been crushed at heavy cost to the very villagers it claimed to serve. The rebel case pointed to the hollowness of land reform as administered, to ministries dismissed at the centre's pleasure, to the police firing ordered by a communist led government itself, and asked what further proof could be needed that the state would never legislate its own supersession. Underneath the strategic dispute ran a temperamental one that memoirs from both camps record: between men who had come to experience organisation as an end worth preserving and younger cadres for whom preservation had become indistinguishable from betrayal. The split was formalised by expulsions and rival conferences, and its product, in April 1969, was a new party.

## Thunder from abroad: the international reception

The rising's second life began in foreign print before its first life had ended in the Terai. The Chinese party's press greeted the Bengal events in the summer of 1967 with an editorial salute that compared the rising to a spring thunder over India, and the phrase, reprinted in the rebels' journals and remembered by every participant, performed real work: it certified the revolt to its makers as an event in world history, assured them that the centre of world revolution, as they then reckoned it, had weighed their village struggle and found it significant, and it fixed the movement's orientation toward Beijing at the moment of its birth. Indian governments of the period read the same editorials as evidence of foreign instigation, and the instigation charge entered the official account of the movement at its origin, where it has remained available ever since. The record assembled by historians supports a more precise division of the matter. The rising was conceived, organised, and led from within the Indian movement's own quarrels, on Indian evidence, before any foreign salute arrived; the salute's effect was not to cause the revolt but to confirm its leaders in a line they had already chosen, and to raise the price of their reconsidering it, since a course praised by the world's revolutionary capital could not easily be confessed, a season later, to have been an error.

The relationship that followed was less comfortable than the salute promised. Channels between the Indian party and its Chinese counterpart carried, alongside solidarity, counsel that the recipients found indigestible: advice, conveyed as the annihilation campaign intensified, that the Indian comrades were neglecting mass work, dispersing their forces, and mistaking assassination for revolution, advice whose substance anticipated the internal Indian criticism almost exactly. The founder's circle received the counsel as interference shading into the revisionism it existed to oppose, and the exchange cooled the connection it was meant to warm. Within a few years the Chinese party's own course changed beyond recognition, its revolutionary foreign policy gave way to statecraft, and the Indian movement was left in the peculiar position of a Beijing oriented party in a world where Beijing had moved on. The fragments handled the abandonment variously: some quietly retired the international scaffolding and rebuilt on Indian materials, some preserved the orientation as liturgy, and the consolidated organisation of the 2000s emerged with a doctrine formally Maoist and a practice answerable to no foreign centre, the first generation of the family to be entirely its own. For the structural argument of this article, the episode carries a moral the security literature, with its instigation theory, and the movement literature, with its fraternity theory, both resist: foreign encouragement amplified the founding choices and foreign counsel failed to correct them, which locates the causes of both the choice and the failure where this article has placed them throughout, inside the Indian movement's institutions and on Indian ground.

## A new party founded on a repudiation

The Communist Party of India (Marxist-Leninist) was proclaimed in April 1969, on the anniversary of Lenin's birth, by the coordination of revolutionary groups that had broken with both existing communist parties. Its founding programme characterised India as a semi colonial and semi feudal society, ruled by a comprador bureaucrat bourgeoisie in alliance with feudal landlords and subservient to rival foreign imperialisms, and concluded that the stage of the revolution was a people's democratic revolution whose axis was agrarian revolution in the countryside. Elections were to be boycotted, not merely deprioritised: participation in them was defined as betrayal, and the party's cadres in legislatures elected earlier were called upon to resign. Trade unions and peasant associations of the traditional kind, open bodies conducting legal struggles, were viewed with deep suspicion as schools of compromise, and the emphasis fell on secret organisation, armed squads, and the establishment of base areas. Charu Majumdar was elected general secretary, and his eight documents, written before the founding, acquired the standing of scripture within the new party, their conclusions not open to the kind of argument that party congresses exist to conduct.

### How did the founding party define the stage of the Indian revolution?

It characterised the country as semi colonial and semi feudal, ruled by foreign aligned big capital and landlords through a bureaucratic state, and held the revolution's stage to be a people's democratic one whose axis was agrarian war in the countryside. Boycott of elections and rejection of legal mass organisations followed from that characterisation as matters of principle.

Assess the founding fairly, as its participants experienced it, before judging what it produced. The new party gathered people of demonstrable courage and sacrifice: organisers who had spent years in the villages of the Terai and Srikakulam, students who abandoned degrees at Calcutta's colleges, workers and teachers who accepted illegality, poverty, and the near certainty of prison as the price of their wager. Its critique of the parent parties' ministerialism was not foolishness; it fastened on real compromises and real evasions, and its insistence that land relations in the countryside remained semi feudal in character described the lived experience of the sharecropper more accurately than the parent parties' talk of a maturing capitalism did. The founding generation also carried a genuine internationalism of the period: the Chinese revolution was recent, the Vietnamese resistance was at its height, and the proposition that Asia's countryside would decide the fate of its cities seemed to many serious people to be the lesson of the age. What the founding lacked was not sincerity or even analysis, in parts, but a mechanism for correcting error. A party that defines disagreement as treason to the line, treats its founder's articles as settled doctrine, and dissolves the open mass organisations through which feedback from the peasantry might travel, has disabled its own instruments before the first test arrives. The test arrived quickly, and it was administered by the founder himself.

## Who joined: students, schoolteachers, and the sociology of the first hour

The founding organisation's human material shaped its fate as surely as its doctrine did, and the composition is worth stating exactly. The rural cadre came disproportionately from two strata. The first was the village and small town schoolteacher, the characteristic intellectual of the Indian countryside: educated beyond his neighbours, salaried below his status, in daily contact with the state's promises and its defaults, and positioned by his literacy to keep the records, read the statutes, and draft the petitions on which grievance organisation depends. The Srikakulam sangam was a teachers' creation; the Terai organisation rested on the same stratum; the pattern repeats in the movement's later theatres, where the schoolteacher, the health worker, and the returned migrant with a smattering of education recur as its local founders. The second stratum was the student, drawn in the first phase overwhelmingly from the colleges of Calcutta and the provincial towns, young people of the educated castes for whom the movement offered an exit from the examination queue and the clerk's desk into a life whose meaning was not in question. The party sent these students to the countryside under the slogan of integrating with the poorest, and the encounter between the student's doctrine and the peasant's arithmetic, recorded in a shelf of memoirs, was the founding generation's real education: some students stayed and became organisers of the first quality, some broke, some died in the annihilation campaign their ardour had demanded, and the survivors' retrospective literature is unsparing about the ignorance in which the ardour had been formed.

Two absences in the composition matter as much. Industrial workers, the doctrine's designated vanguard, were present in the first phase mainly as individuals; the organised working class of the mill towns and railways remained with its unions and its parties, and the movement's attempts on the cities in the campaign's later stage met the indifference or hostility of a class that had institutions of its own and declined to be liberated by students with pistols. And the rural poor themselves, sharecroppers and labourers, joined in their thousands as the base of every phase, yet rarely in its direction, so that the movement reproduced internally the structure of the society it fought: the educated commanding, the poor supplying the numbers and the dead. Later phases altered the proportions without abolishing the pattern. The forest organisation's rank and file became Adivasi in a sense the founding never was, its middle leadership increasingly drawn from the population itself, while its apex remained, to the end of the period this article covers, substantially the product of the plains classroom. A revolutionary movement's sociology is not a detail of its biography. It is the mechanism by which doctrine is filtered into practice, and much of what the record shows, the brilliance of the propaganda, the textual ferocity of the internal quarrels, the recurring deafness to the base's own priorities, becomes intelligible once the composition is kept in view.

## The founding line: annihilation of class enemies

The doctrine by which the new party is remembered, and by which it destroyed itself, emerged in the months after the founding and hardened into orthodoxy during the following year. Majumdar argued that the agrarian revolution would not be built primarily by mass organisation, land seizure, or the patient construction of base areas, all of which he increasingly treated as economism, a preoccupation with immediate demands that deferred the political question. Instead, revolutionary consciousness and authority would be created through the annihilation of class enemies: the physical killing, by small secret squads, of individual landlords, moneylenders, police informers, and officials, selected as symbols of the old order in their localities. Each killing, on the theory, would shatter the aura of invulnerability surrounding the rural power structure, draw the poorest peasants into the party's orbit, train cadres in the act that defined commitment, and render the countryside ungovernable for the old order plot by plot. The line was pressed to conclusions its author stated without flinching: that the class struggle would be measured by the number of enemies annihilated, and that a cadre who had not participated in such an act had not yet crossed into revolution. The squads were also directed into the cities in the campaign's later phase, where statues were beheaded, traffic police and teachers and rival political workers were attacked, and Calcutta's student movement consumed itself in a frenzy of exemplary violence.

### Why did the annihilation line appeal to its first recruits?

It promised immediacy to young people formed by a decade of ministerial compromise: one act that proved commitment, broke the landlord's aura of untouchability, and replaced the slow humiliations of petition and procession with direct mastery over the local symbols of oppression. For landless youth, the doctrine converted expendability into significance, and secrecy conferred the dignity of an elect.

The appeal is unintelligible unless the humiliations it answered are kept in view. A sharecropper's son who joined a squad in the Srikakulam hills or the Terai had typically watched his father removed from a plot by a landlord's word, watched a moneylender take the harvest at the threshing floor, and watched the party of his father's generation explain that the time was not ripe. The annihilation line addressed that biography directly. It located the enemy as a named man rather than a structure, offered the recruit a role in that man's destruction rather than a leaflet about it, and defined the deed as the very method by which peasant consciousness and party authority would grow. Recruiters did not need to promise victory on a schedule. The act was the politics, complete in itself, and each squad carried its doctrine in its pocket. That so many of the first recruits were students rather than peasants, young urban idealists dispatched to the countryside to integrate with the poor, sharpened the pattern: for a student burning with guilt at his own privilege, annihilation offered a baptism that no study circle could match, a deed after which retreat into ordinary life became psychologically impossible. The line thus selected for the most committed, consumed them fastest, and left behind a legend that recruitment posters in later decades continued to draw upon long after the doctrine itself was disowned.

## The first year: conferences, expulsions, and the road not taken

The founding party's first year repays a closer look, because the catastrophe that followed was chosen within it, at identifiable forks where the record shows alternatives alive and argued. The organisation proclaimed in April 1969 was from birth a coalition of the convinced and the unconvinced: alongside the founder's circle stood regional leaderships, above all in Andhra Pradesh, whose commitment to armed struggle was genuine and whose commitment to the founder's developing method was not, and who joined the founding expecting the party's congresses and committees to arbitrate the difference. Through the months that followed, the arbitration failed by design. The founder's articles were circulated as the line; objections were answered by characterisation of the objector; the Andhra leadership's insistence on mass organisations and on the primacy of the land struggle was progressively redefined as the right deviation the founding existed to extirpate; and the party's energies turned inward, to purification, at the moment its enemies were preparing its destruction. Expulsions and parallel committees multiplied through the following year, so that the repression, when it came in force, fell on an organisation already consuming itself, and several of the fragments that later rebuilt the movement trace their separate existences to these first quarrels rather than to the police. The road not taken is visible in the record with unusual clarity, since it was signposted at the time by named participants and later walked, successfully, by the electoral stream and, in its own fashion, by the Andhra construction of the following decade: build the mass organisation first, subordinate the gun to it absolutely, and let the question of insurrection be posed by the movement's strength rather than by its impatience. That the founding party could not take it was not a failure of information. The information was on the table, in the party's own canon and in its own members' memoranda. It was a failure of structure: a party founded on the repudiation of correction had no organ with which to correct itself, and the founder's line, examined next in its consequences, is what an uncorrectable organisation does with a catastrophe it has theorised as a triumph.

## What the line produced

The consequences followed the logic of the method with a fidelity that its critics, inside and outside the movement, catalogued at the time and that later successor organisations have largely accepted as accurate. Detached from mass organisation, the squads had no constituency to shelter them and no political work to fall back on when the state replied. Annihilation without a surrounding movement did not terrorise the rural power structure into paralysis; it identified the party's best cadres to the police, invited overwhelming retaliation against the villages that had hosted them, and left the peasantry to choose between an organisation that brought repression down on their homes and a state that promised to remove it. Selection of targets decayed as the campaign generalised: schoolteachers, small traders, minor officials, and workers of rival parties were killed under a category elastic enough to cover anyone the squad could reach, and each such killing severed the party further from the population in whose name it claimed to act. The urban phase compounded the damage. The attacks in Calcutta handed the state and the press a portrait of the movement as nihilist violence, alienated the working class districts the party had hoped to ignite, and set off a cycle of retaliation between the squads and the police in which the squads, small, hunted, and cut off, were bound to lose.

### What did the annihilation line cost the organisation that practised it?

It cost the party its shelter and its future. Squads without mass organisations around them were identified and destroyed, villages that hosted them absorbed the retaliation, elastic target selection alienated the very classes the party claimed to serve, and the cadre generation of the founding was killed, jailed, or scattered within roughly three years of the founding congress.

Inside the party, doubt existed from early on and was suppressed as deviation. Peasant organisers in Andhra Pradesh questioned a method that substituted the squad's deed for the slow building of village committees and armed self defence tied to land struggles, and some leading cadres argued that annihilation divorced from mass movements violated the very Maoist canon the party claimed to follow, in which political work among the masses precedes and directs military action. Such objections were answered by expulsion or by the objectors' marginalisation, and the founder's authority, resting on his authorship of the founding documents, proved unanswerable inside the structure he had built. The Chinese party, whose praise had launched the enterprise, counselled moderation through the channels available to it, urging the Indian comrades toward mass work and against adventurism, and was ignored. By the time the campaign reached its height, the party was a besieged sect conducting a war of assassinations against a state whose coercive resources it could not begin to match, while its founders' analysis of a countryside on the verge of general insurrection went untested by any mechanism that could have reported its falsity. The peasants of the Terai and Srikakulam, the constituencies in whose name everything had been undertaken, voted on the strategy in the only way open to them: they withdrew, accommodated themselves to the returning landlords and police, and waited for quieter times.

## The urban front: the capital's season of fear

The annihilation campaign's urban chapter deserves separate record, because it was there that the movement lost the audience it could least afford to lose and there that the state's licence for unlimited reply was signed. Through the turn of the decade the squads carried the campaign into Calcutta: statues of the national movement's saints beheaded in the night, traffic policemen and police informers shot at their posts, teachers and examination halls attacked as instruments of the old culture, workers of rival left parties killed in the para wars by which neighbourhoods changed hands. The city's student movement, which had supplied the party with its most ardent recruits, consumed itself in the escalation; colleges closed, examinations collapsed, and a generation of the Bengali educated class acquired its defining political memory, of a season in which the revolution arrived in the lane as a boy with a pipe gun and a list. Memoirists of the period, including the movement's own, describe the atmosphere without disagreement about its content: fear, exhilaration, denunciation, and the steady withdrawal of the uncommitted, the tram conductor, the schoolmaster, the union organiser, whose tolerance the party needed and whose doors closed one by one.

The political consequence followed the emotional one with mechanical fidelity. A movement that frightens the city it hopes to encircle last and liberate first has inverted its own strategy's sequence, and the Calcutta campaign united against the party every force it needed divided: the parliamentary left, whose workers were among the targets and whose government answered with the police; the Congress administration at the centre, which acquired in Bengal a universally intelligible justification for emergency methods; and the urban public, whose consent to repression, once given, extended far beyond the squads to the villages and the universities. Participants later judged the urban turn the founding's single most destructive decision after the annihilation doctrine itself, and the judgement is difficult to improve upon. In the countryside the line had at least addressed a population with a grievance against the men it targeted. In the city it addressed a population whose grievances ran through institutions, unions, parties, courts, newspapers, that the squads treated as scenery, and it taught that population to experience the movement as crime. Repertoires matter: the Indian city's memory of the season entered the national imagination as the movement's portrait, and the organisation's subsequent half century in the forest has never quite escaped the frame the capital's fear constructed for it.

## Repression and the end of the first organisation

The state's reply, once the urban campaign had unified opinion behind it, was comprehensive. Joint operations by police and army combed the countryside districts where the party had worked, most elaborately in the campaign remembered as Operation Steeplechase across the border districts of Bengal, Bihar, and Odisha, while in the cities the police conducted raids, encounters, and mass arrests under emergency legislation inherited and extended for the purpose. Majumdar was arrested in Calcutta in July 1972 and died in custody within days, in circumstances the movement has always described as killing by neglect or worse and the record leaves as a death in a police lock up of a sick man denied the standing of an ordinary prisoner; the fact of the death in custody is not in dispute, and this article states it without further ornament. With the founder dead, the central leadership imprisoned or killed, and the squads destroyed, the party as a functioning all India organisation ceased to exist. What survived was the inheritance: the founding texts, the martyr legends, the scattered local groups that had never fully accepted the annihilation line, and thousands of released prisoners who emerged from the jails of the mid 1970s carrying the experience into every subsequent formation.

The balance sheet of the first phase should be drawn with the evenness the subject demands. The movement's violence in these years was real and was directed, as its doctrine prescribed, at landlords, officials, police, and also at teachers, traders, and political opponents whose designation as class enemies was a function of the squads' reach. The state's violence was also real, conducted through encounters in which prisoners were shot, through deaths in custody, and through the collective punishment of villages that had sheltered the squads, and it disposed of resources beyond comparison with its opponent. Neither ledger excuses the other, and neither is diminished by stating both. What the record supports, and what the movement's own later self criticism concluded, is narrower and more useful: the founding strategy made the outcome inevitable. An organisation that announces itself as the executioner of the rural order, while possessing neither the mass base to defend its people nor the arms to contest the state's forces, guarantees its own destruction and visits the costs of that destruction on the population it recruited. The lesson was available in the tradition's own canon before 1969. The second generation of the movement, the subject of the next sections, is the story of organisations that absorbed it in different measures.

## The agency hills: Srikakulam and the Girijan rising

The Bengal rising had a southern twin whose history corrects any account that treats Naxalism as a Calcutta export. In the agency tracts of Srikakulam district, in the northern hills of Andhra Pradesh, the Girijan tribal population had been organised since the early 1960s by communist schoolteachers working among them, men who learned the local language, lived in the villages, and built a sangam, an association, that fought the plains moneylender and trader on the concrete terrain of debt, land alienation, and the forest contractor's scales. Girijan land had been passing for generations into the hands of plains creditors through debts the illiterate borrower could not audit, in defiance of protective legislation that existed largely as a rumour. The sangam's method was the opposite of the annihilation doctrine it would later be conscripted into: petitions, debt strikes, occupation of alienated land, and the patient documentation of each family's claim. By the time the Bengal news arrived, the agency organisation was among the strongest rural movements in the state, and its radicalisation was driven as much by the state's answer to its legal phase, prosecutions, police camps, the protection of the creditors, as by any doctrine imported from the Terai.

### What did the Girijan rising demand?

Restoration of tribal land alienated to plains moneylenders and traders through unauditable debt, an end to the forest contractor's cheating scales and forced labour, and recognition of the Girijan association as the community's voice. The demands were concrete, documented family by family, and framed in the language of protective statutes the state itself had enacted and declined to enforce.

When the Andhra radicals aligned with the new party at the end of the 1960s, the agency movement was drawn into the founding line's orbit, and the collision between its own method and the imported one played out there in miniature. The sangam's strength had been built by organisers whose authority rested on years of documented grievance work; the line demanded that such work yield to the squad and the exemplary killing, and the organisers who complied traded a defensible mass movement for a hunted conspiracy within a few campaigning seasons. The state, for its part, answered the agency rising with the full apparatus it had refined in Telangana a generation before: combing operations, village regroupment in the worst period, and a police presence that outlasted the squads. By the middle of the 1970s the Srikakulam movement was broken as an armed force, though its sangam tradition persisted underground and in memory, and its survivors carried the double lesson into every later formation: the agency country could sustain a movement rooted in documented grievance for years, and could not sustain one that substituted the deed for the document. Students of the family's geography should mark the case carefully, since the agency tracts are hill and forest country of the same formation as the later strongholds, inhabited by a tribal population with the same structure of grievance, and the movement nevertheless failed there in its first incarnation, because the state arrived early, in force, and stayed. Terrain proposes; administration disposes. The configuration requires both terms, and Srikakulam in those years lacked the second: an administration too thin to hold what it had cleared.

## Fragmentation: one revolt becomes many organisations

The parent party had begun dividing before the repression finished it. Disputes over the annihilation line, over the founder's authority, over relations with the Chinese party, and over regional assessments of the Indian revolution split the organisation into competing central committees while the campaign still raged, and the destruction of the centre after 1972 left each fragment to develop in isolation, under local leaderships, in dissimilar countrysides. Through the later 1970s the field reassembled itself into a spectrum. Some groups concluded that the founder's line had been the error and rebuilt on mass organisation, land struggle, and the slow construction of peasant associations, keeping the armed option in doctrine while subordinating it in practice. Some maintained the founding orthodoxy intact and dwindled into sects guarding the founder's texts. Some rebuilt in particular regions, Bihar's plains, Andhra's Telangana districts, the forest country of the central Indian hills, adapting the inherited doctrine to agrarian structures very different from the Terai's: landlord militias and caste armies in Bihar, a long guerrilla tradition in Telangana, tribal dispossession in the forests. The Emergency of the mid 1970s, during which the parliamentary parties were also jailed wholesale, briefly levelled the field, and the releases that followed it released a generation of Naxalite prisoners into a changed political landscape, many of them determined to correct the founding errors and some to repeat them.

### How did a fragmented field still share one genealogy?

Every fragment claimed descent from the rising of the 1960s and treated the founding texts and martyrs as a common inheritance, even when repudiating the founding strategy. Shared origin, a shared vocabulary of stage and class enemy, and personnel formed in the same prisons bound the field together as a family whose members recognised one another across their quarrels.

The fragmentation is usually narrated as farce, dozens of parties with nearly identical names distinguished by initials and the surnames of dead leaders. Read structurally, it is better understood as adaptation under selection pressure. The fragments that survived the 1970s were those that solved, in their own territories, the problem the founding had failed: how to root an illegal revolutionary organisation in a population that must be able to protect it, feed it, and find its presence useful enough to justify the risks of association. In the Bihar plains, survival meant building caste and class organisations among landless labourers facing private landlord armies, and the struggle there took on the character of a low intensity caste war in which the Marxist vocabulary overlay a much older conflict over wages, dignity, and common land. In Telangana, survival meant inheriting a living memory of the earlier armed struggle and embedding squads within peasant associations that fought over waste land, forced labour, and the exactions of village landlords. In the forest districts, as the later sections describe, survival meant something else again: becoming, in effect, an alternative authority among tribal communities for whom the Indian state appeared mainly as forest guard, contractor, and policeman. Each adaptation preserved the genealogy while transforming the content, which is why the streams table that follows must be read as an evolutionary chart rather than an organisational one.

## Bihar: the plains war of caste and class

No account of the family's divergent roads makes sense without Bihar, the state where the movement's second life was led and where its categories suffered their most instructive bending. The Bihar plains present an agrarian structure unlike either Bengal's or the forest belt's: a dense, settled countryside of small holdings and vast landlessness, where the line between caste and class runs close enough to the surface that a struggle over wages or a patch of common land is simultaneously a struggle between named castes, and where the landlords of the dominant castes maintained, in the decades of the movement's growth, private armies of their own. The senas, caste militias raised by landholding groups to defend rents, wages, and honour as they defined them, made the Bihar countryside a place where violence was already privatised and distributed before any revolutionary arrived. Into this armed equilibrium the reorganised fragments of the movement entered in the 1970s, organising agricultural labourers, overwhelmingly of the oppressed castes, around demands that were modest in formulation and revolutionary in implication: the statutory minimum wage, an end to begar and hereditary servitude, possession of the homestead plot, and the right of labourers' women to be safe from the landlord's household.

### Why did caste armies change the character of the Bihar struggle?

Because the enemy arrived already organised as caste. Where the landlord's power wore the form of a militia recruited by birth and sanctified by honour, class organisation among labourers was answered as caste war, village massacres were answered in kind, and the movement's class vocabulary had to survive inside a conflict the countryside insisted on reading in the older language.

The Bihar record is the grimmest in the family's history, and it must be summarised with the restraint this article applies everywhere to violence: a decades long cycle in which squads of the movement killed landlords and their retainers, senas answered by falling on labourers' hamlets, and the state intervened episodically, usually against whichever side lacked protection in the district's politics. The massacres whose names became bywords in the state's politics belong to both columns of that ledger, and this article enters them in neither, beyond recording that the cycle occurred, that its victims were drawn overwhelmingly from the poorest, and that it formed the environment in which the electoral stream's decision for open politics was taken and defended. That decision reads differently against this background than against the forest's. In Bihar, armed organisation had not produced parallel power; it had produced a symmetry of atrocity in which the labourers' organisation could neither protect its people nor advance their demands, while its legal fronts, unions, and cultural organisations demonstrably could and did, winning wage agreements, land possessions, and eventually seats. The lesson the Bihar cadres drew, that the gun had become the obstacle to the politics it claimed to serve, was not the parliamentary parties' doctrine recited back. It was a conclusion purchased in their own districts, and it explains why the electoral turn, when it came, came from the plains of Bihar rather than from any theoretical centre, and why its proponents defended the turn with the authority of undertakers rather than of converts.

Bihar also supplies the control observation for the article's treatment of caste. The parliamentary critics had long argued that the movement's class categories could not digest the caste order; the forest organisation would later argue, in effect, that tribe superseded the question. Bihar's labourers refused both evasions in practice: they organised as classes and fought as castes in the same week, struck for wages under red flags and buried their dead under caste names, and built the family's most durable mass organisations out of the refusal to choose. The streams that survived Bihar were those that learned to hold the two registers together; those that insisted on the purity of either were consumed by the senas or by irrelevance, a selection pressure that shaped the electoral party's character permanently and that the doctrinal histories, written from the texts, systematically understate.

## Release and reckoning: the prisoners return

Between the destruction of the first organisation and the divergences charted in the streams table stands an experience that formed the family's second generation as surely as the rising formed its first: prison, and the great release that followed the Emergency. The jails of the early 1970s held Naxalite prisoners in large numbers, cadres of every fragment alongside one another, with years of enforced leisure in which the founding catastrophe could be argued over cell by cell. Memoirs and the movement's own retrospective literature describe the prison debates as the family's true second congress, unminuted and unending: the annihilation line arraigned by the men who had practised it, the founder's authority weighed against his doctrine, the parliamentary parties' critique answered and partly absorbed, and the regional questions, Bihar's caste, the agency's land records, the forest's possibilities, promoted from local knowledge to general strategy. When the Emergency ended and the prisoners emerged in the later 1970s, they carried a shared formation that cut across the fragments they rejoined: a determination that the mass organisation question outranked every other, and a suspicion of charismatic authority that the founder's fate had burned into the family's institutional memory, visible ever afterward in its preference for collective leadership, its suspicion of personality, and its multiplication of committees.

The reckoning also produced the family's historians, and their work shaped what could be thought about the movement thereafter. Participant histories of the rising, documentary collections on the split, and a shelf of memoirs in Bengali, Telugu, and English established the record on which all later accounts, including the security literature's, silently depend. The parliamentary parties produced their own official reckonings in the same decades, and the two literatures, opposed in their verdicts, agree on a surprising portion of the facts, an agreement the researcher can use as bedrock. What neither literature could supply was the structural explanation of the map, for a reason internal to both: each was written by a party to the quarrel, for whom the map was a verdict on a strategy, the rebels' vindication or their refutation, and neither could afford the possibility this article defends, that the map chiefly records the state's own unevenness, and that both the vindication and the refutation are, in large part, readings of a geography neither side chose.

## The minor streams: a note on the family's small print

The streams table omits, by design, the family's minor members, and a paragraph is owed to them, because their collective biography carries a lesson the majors cannot teach. Across the decades the field threw off dozens of small organisations: district level groups that seceded over a leader's insult or a line's comma, study circles that proclaimed themselves central committees, regional formations that lived a decade on one valley's grievances and died with their founder's arrest, and letterhead parties whose entire territory was a post office box and a journal. Polemicists of the parent parties collected their names as comedy. The comedy misses the phenomenon's information. Each minor stream is a data point in the selection experiment this article has been running: an organisation with the doctrine, the genealogy, and the will, placed in a countryside that lacked the configuration, expiring on schedule. The minor streams flourished exactly where pamphlets flourish, in the towns, among students and the educated unemployed, and their life cycle, founding conference, journal, split, oblivion, is the biography of an idea without a habitat. Their persistence as a literary phenomenon, long after their military nullity was established, testifies to the founding's cultural capital: the rising of 1967 remained, for generations of the Indian radical young, the purest available symbol of refusal, and symbols require only printers, not strongholds. The researcher should nonetheless resist the survivorship bias the majors induce. The family's history written from the consolidated organisation reads as destiny; written from the small print, it reads as a lottery in which almost every ticket lost, and in which the winning tickets share, on inspection, not a superior doctrine but an address. That is the geography of dispossession stated from the family's own tail of the distribution.

## The electoral turn: the stream that laid down the gun

The fact most often omitted from accounts of this subject, and the one journalists researching the family should carry away first, is that a significant stream of the movement abandoned armed struggle and has contested elections for decades. The lineage runs through the reorganisations of the 1970s in Bihar, where one formation rebuilt itself around open mass organisation among agricultural labourers and the rural poor, formed a political front at the start of the 1980s through which it entered electoral contests while its parent body remained formally underground, and completed its emergence into open politics in the years after, eventually standing before the electorate as a declared party. Its candidates have won seats in state assemblies and in parliament, its mass fronts organise labourers and peasants in the open, and its rallies are indistinguishable in form from those of any other party of the Indian left. It continues to describe itself as revolutionary, continues to honour the rising of 1967 as its origin, and continues to be denounced by the armed streams as renegade, a quarrel the family has conducted in print for forty years.

### How did one stream pass from armed struggle to the ballot?

By stages its own documents describe as correction: first subordinating the gun to mass organisation among labourers and peasants, then contesting elections through a political front while the parent body stayed underground, and finally emerging as an open party. Each stage was justified internally as rescuing the founding revolt's aims from the founding strategy that had destroyed them.

The electoral stream matters to the analysis out of proportion to its size, because it functions as the control case in an experiment the family ran on itself. It began from the same founding texts, the same martyrs, the same assessment of the Indian state, and a social base, the landless of Bihar, at least as oppressed as any in the country. It differed in one variable: the decision, taken under the pressure of the first phase's catastrophic failure, to treat mass organisation as primary and armed action as dispensable. The result was survival, growth, representation, and a permanent seat at the table of the region's politics, alongside the absorption of its energies into the ordinary bargaining of a poor state's democracy and the steady moderation that such bargaining imposes. Whether that outcome vindicates the parliamentary road against the rebels of 1967, or merely shows that a revolutionary party can be digested by the arena it enters, is the argument the parliamentary parties and the electoral stream's own left critics continue to conduct. What cannot be argued is the fact itself, and its absence from security framed coverage, which has no use for a Naxalite who files nomination papers, is a measure of that coverage's priorities.

## Telangana's long shadow and the Andhra construction

The Andhra stream that anchors the consolidation story carried the deepest institutional memory in the family, because its countryside had already made one armed revolution within living memory. The Telangana struggle of the late 1940s, in which communist led peasants of the princely state of Hyderabad abolished forced labour, redistributed land across a wide liberated zone, and held it against first the Nizam's forces and then the Indian army, ended in withdrawal and defeat, yet left behind villages where the party's authority had once been total and grandfathers who had governed. When the reorganised movement rebuilt in Telangana at the end of the 1970s, it rebuilt on that sediment. The new formation's method, learned from both the earlier struggle and the annihilation catastrophe, was a deliberate inversion of the founding line: village by village construction of peasant associations, campaigns over waste land occupation, forced labour, and the exactions of the doras, the rural magnates, with armed squads formed from the associations' own youth and held under the discipline that the gun defends the organisation and never substitutes for it. Through the following decade the construction spread across the northern Telangana districts until the squads operated as the military arm of a genuine rural movement, and the state government of the day alternated, in the rhythm that became characteristic of the Andhra theatre, between negotiation and bans, talks and encounters, each phase instructing the other side.

The Andhra theatre also staged the family's clearest demonstration of the limits of plains guerrilla war under a modernising state. The government that took office in Andhra Pradesh in the middle of the 1990s combined, with unusual coherence, the elements that separately had failed elsewhere: a specialised police intelligence and commando capacity recruited and trained for this single war, a political leadership that treated the conflict as its signature project, development spending targeted at the contested districts, and the exploitation of every seam between the movement and the population, including surrenders cultivated as public theatre. Within a decade the formation that had seemed, at its height, to be building a liberated zone in Telangana had been driven from its home districts, its squads killed, captured, or withdrawn across the border into the forest country of Chhattisgarh and Odisha. The withdrawal was the making of the consolidated organisation's geography. Andhra's loss was Dandakaranya's gain: experienced cadres, a tested method, and a strategic conclusion, absorbed at the highest level, that the plains could no longer hold what the forest still might. The merger of the 2000s joined the Andhra stream's method to the central Indian stream's ground, and the product, for a period, was the most formidable armed organisation the family had produced. Its subsequent history is the history of that ground's conditions changing, which the decline section takes up.

## Consolidation in the forest belt

While one branch of the family was entering legislatures, the armed branches were converging on the terrain where the second half of this article is set. Two formations did most of the converging. The Andhra stream, consolidated around 1980 from cadres formed in the Telangana tradition, built guerrilla organisation patiently: squads sheltered by peasant associations, mass fronts for youth, women, and cultural work, and a declared strategy of protracted people's war in which armed action served the construction of base areas rather than substituting for it. Its stronghold districts in northern Telangana gave it a peasant base in the older style, and from there its squads and organisers moved, through the following decades, into the adjoining forest country of the Adivasi belt. The second formation descended from a Bengal grouping of the late 1960s that had kept its independence from the founding party, had been sceptical of the annihilation line from the start, and had settled early among the tribal populations of the central districts, building slowly, prioritising the patient acquisition of trust over spectacular action. The two streams negotiated unity for years, interrupted by the armed conflict that their rivalry in some districts produced, since the family's quarrels were not always confined to paper.

In the middle of the 2000s the two merged into a single organisation, the Communist Party of India (Maoist), adopting a common programme of new democratic revolution through protracted people's war, a unified military formation, and the boycott of elections. The Indian state responded by proscribing the organisation under its anti terrorism legislation, and senior officials of the period described it in public statements as the gravest internal security challenge the country faced. That description is recorded here as what it is: the official designation, a fact about how the state classified its opponent, which governments used to justify the scale of the deployments that followed. The merged organisation, for its part, described itself in its documents as the vanguard of an ongoing people's war and its strongholds as base areas in construction, characterisations this article attributes to its literature rather than adopts. Between the designation and the self description lies the territory of the next sections: what the organisation actually was, where it could live, and why the ground, more than the doctrine, dictated both.

A word on method is needed before the geography is examined, because the merger tempts writers into treating the consolidated organisation as the movement, full stop, and the temptation distorts everything downstream. The armed organisation of the 2000s was the family's largest single member and its most consequential, but the electoral party continued its separate life, the regional mass line groups continued theirs, and the doctrinal debates among all of them continued to shape recruitment and schism. Likewise, the consolidated organisation's own practice varied enormously by district: in some forest divisions it operated as a near government, taxing contractors, running dispute forums, and regulating the tendu leaf and bamboo trades on which the local cash economy ran; in others it was a visiting squad and a network of sympathisers; in others still, a memory. The structural explanation that follows is pitched at the level these variations share: the conditions that made some districts habitable for an armed organisation and others barren, whatever the local leadership's skill or the doctrine's local colour.

## The map moved: from the plains of Bengal to the forests of the centre

Lay the movement's addresses across the decades side by side and a migration stands out that no doctrinal history can explain. The founding rising belonged to the settled agricultural plains of northern Bengal, a landscape of paddy fields, sharecroppers, jotedars, and market towns, where the state's presence was dense and old. The parallel rising in Srikakulam belonged to a hill and agency country of tribal cultivators pressed by plains moneylenders and traders, a transitional landscape between plain and forest. The Bihar struggles of the middle decades belonged to the plains again, to the flat, crowded, caste ridden countryside south of the Ganges. The consolidated movement of the 2000s belonged overwhelmingly to neither plain. Its durable strongholds sat in the forested hill country that runs across the waist of India, through the districts where Chhattisgarh, Odisha, Jharkhand, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, and Madhya Pradesh meet, a region of sal and teak forest, of iron ore and bauxite and coal beneath the trees, of villages that appear in the administrative record as names attached to forest compartments, and of Adivasi communities, Gond, Muria, Dorla, Koya, Santhal, Ho, and many others, whose ancestors had held the land before any of the states that now govern it existed.

Doctrine did not change enough across those decades to explain the change of address. The programme of the merged organisation descended in a recognisable line from the programme of 1969: same characterisation of the Indian state, same stage of revolution, same strategic grammar of countryside encircling city. Nor did the movement choose the forest freely, as a theorist chooses a laboratory. It was driven there by defeat in the plains, where dense administration, landlord organisation, and the sheer visibility of open country made armed survival impossible, and it was kept there by what the forest offered: concealment, a population with grievances no other political force was organising, and an economy of forest produce and mining contracts from which funds could be drawn. The migration is therefore the first exhibit for the geography of dispossession. A movement's map, over fifty years, traced the outline of the places where the Indian state's writ ran thinnest and its extractive demands ran deepest, and it did so under the guidance of cadres who believed, sincerely and completely, that they were following a doctrine. They were. They were also following the terrain, and only the terrain explains why the doctrine bore fruit in Dandakaranya and died in the districts where it was born.

Consider what the plains had taught. In Bengal, the founding organisation was annihilated in three years. In Bihar, armed organisation survived longer, but as one armed actor among several, facing landlord senas that matched its violence and a caste politics that bent its class vocabulary out of shape, and the stream that flourished there did so by abandoning the armed road for elections. In the Telangana districts, a genuine guerrilla movement with deep peasant roots rose high in the 1980s and was then ground down by a state government that combined an efficient police intelligence apparatus with just enough political flexibility to isolate the squads. Each plains failure had local causes, yet the pattern across them is hard to unsee: wherever the state's administrative and coercive machinery was thick on the ground, and wherever rival political organisations contested the same villages, armed persistence failed. The forest inverted every term of that pattern. Administration there was a rumour. Rival parties were absent or confined to the block headquarters town. The population's antagonists, forest officials, contractors, and distant mining interests, were few, visible, and unloved. An armed organisation did not need to win the forest population's ideological allegiance. It needed to be useful against those antagonists, and to be less predatory than the forces it displaced.

## Forests and minerals: the material basis of the map

The forest belt's suitability begins with physical geography. The districts in question carry some of the subcontinent's densest remaining forest cover, broken hill country clothed in sal, teak, and bamboo, cut by rivers that run impassable in the monsoon and by few roads in any season. For an organisation whose military problem is to avoid battle except on its own terms, such country offers what strategists of guerrilla war have always prized: sanctuary that cannot be permanently occupied at acceptable cost, movement corridors invisible from the air and road, and a hinterland across state borders, so that pressure in one jurisdiction can be relieved by slipping into another whose police answer to a different capital. The three and four state junctions of the central belt multiplied this advantage, since coordinated operations across rival state administrations are chronically difficult to arrange, and the seams between jurisdictions became, in effect, part of the movement's defensive architecture. None of this is ideology. A movement of any doctrine, or none, placed under military pressure in India would have been driven toward the same hills, and other insurgencies of other inspirations have found the same shelter in the same trees.

Beneath the trees lies the second term of the configuration: minerals. The forest districts sit atop a large share of the country's iron ore, coal, bauxite, manganese, and other deposits, a coincidence of geology and vegetation that has structured the region's modern history. The Adivasi population holds, by custom and by constitutional protection in the scheduled areas, the surface; the state holds, by law, the subsurface and the forest itself; and mining capital requires, to reach the one, the clearing of the other and the removal or subordination of the people on it. Every element of the later conflict is present in that triangle. Extraction demands roads, townships, police presence, and the conversion of forest villages into labour catchments or obstacles. The population experiences development, the word under which extraction travels, as a sequence of surveys, acquisitions, blasting schedules, and promises redeemed unevenly or not at all. An armed organisation arriving in such a district does not need to manufacture a grievance or import a doctrine to explain it. It needs only to name the antagonist the population already meets, the mine, the contractor, the forest guard, and to offer the one service no one else offers: the capacity to make that antagonist negotiate.

The forest economy itself supplied the third term. For households whose agriculture is thin and seasonal, cash comes from forest produce: tendu leaf for the bidi trade, mahua flower, bamboo, sal seed, and a dozen lesser items, gathered and sold into chains of contractors whose prices the gatherers have historically had no power to contest. Whoever can raise the rate paid for a bundle of tendu leaf, or compel a contractor to honour a weighing, delivers a measurable improvement in a family's year, paid in currency the family can count. The armed organisations learned to deliver exactly that, intervening in the produce trades, fixing minimum rates, taxing the contractors rather than the gatherers where their strength allowed, and converting a commercial grievance into the material foundation of allegiance. Observers who describe the movement's forest base as ideological commitment have usually not priced a season of tendu. The allegiance was real, but it was built the way allegiances in poor country are generally built: by being present, by being useful, and by being there still when the useful act needed repeating.

## How a squad becomes a government: the first years in a forest district

The process by which an armed presence ripened into parallel power followed, across districts that had no contact with one another, a sequence regular enough to count as the movement's real constitution, and reconstructing it from the record explains the strongholds better than any battle history. The entry was quiet: a handful of cadres, often including speakers of the local language or returnees from the population's own migration, living on the villages' margin, learning the grievance map family by family, and performing small services, a letter written, a dispute mediated, a sick child carried to the road. The first public act was chosen for its audience: the punishment of a figure the village itself had indicted, a cheating contractor's agent, a forest guard whose exactions were proverbial, a moneylender whose seizures had emptied houses, announced in advance as justice and executed as theatre. Recruitment followed the act, drawing the young men whose families had suffered the indicted figure's attentions, and with recruits came the militia, part time villagers with country weapons, whose existence converted the squad's visits into a standing fact. The organisation of production came next, the produce rates and wage settlements described elsewhere in this article, then the court, then the levy that regularised the whole, and only at the end of the sequence, sometimes years in, the apparatus of committees and the vocabulary of the programme, laid over a structure of allegiance already built from services rendered and enemies punished.

Each stage of the sequence was reversible, and the state's counter method, once it matured, consisted precisely in running the film backward: breaking the levy with protected contractors, emptying the court by making the state's own forums marginally usable, detaching the militia by amnesty and employment, and hunting the squad in a countryside whose calculations had been altered beneath it. The sequence also explains the movement's unevenness at fine grain, the phenomenon the corridor map conceals. Districts differed in how far the ripening proceeded, and the difference was rarely a matter of doctrine or zeal. It turned on accidents of the first act, whether the indicted figure was genuinely hated or merely available; on the quality of the first commander, whose reputation became the organisation's local constitution; and on the state's response time, the interval between the squad's arrival and the administration's discovery that a rival sovereignty had been under construction in its jurisdiction. Where the interval was long, the roots went deep enough to survive decades of pressure. Where a vigilant officer or a rival organisation closed it early, the same cadres with the same texts left behind a police file and a cautionary tale. Structure set the odds. Biography dealt the hands.

## Thin administration: what an absent state leaves behind

The phrase weak state presence, used throughout this article, needs unpacking, because the state in the forest districts is not absent in every sense. It is present as extraction and absent as provision, and the asymmetry is the substance of the matter. The state arrives in the Adivasi village wearing a small number of faces. It arrives as the forest department, which claims the surrounding land as its estate, licenses or forbids the gathering of produce, and prosecutes the villager who clears a patch for cultivation as an encroacher on government land. It arrives as the police, whose stations are few, distant, and historically associated in local memory with the suppression of revolts and the protection of traders and landlords rather than with the redress of complaints. It arrives periodically as the contractor and the mining survey, as the revenue official measuring for acquisition, and, at election time, as the candidate's convoy. It does not arrive, or arrives so intermittently as to be a rumour, as the school with a teacher present, the clinic with a doctor, the court within reach, the ration shop honestly stocked, or the land record that corresponds to the ground. Scholars of the region have described the pattern as a state that is simultaneously overbearing and absent, heavy where it takes and weightless where it gives, and the description has survived every attempt to better it.

Into that asymmetry an armed organisation inserts itself not as an idea but as a substitute administration. Where its strength sufficed, the movement built the apparatus the sociologists of insurgency call parallel power: people's courts that settled disputes over land, marriage, theft, and debt in sittings the villagers could attend without travel or fees; levies on contractors, mining operations, and traders that functioned as taxation and were often less arbitrary than the exactions they replaced; regulation of the produce trades; enforcement, sometimes brutal, of its own codes regarding drink, forest cutting, and the treatment of women; and a standing claim to punish the informer and the abusive official. The quality of this governance varied from the rough justice of a respected local commander to racketeering by squads the centre could not discipline, and the population distinguished carefully between the two, sheltering the one and evading the other, as populations under every dual power have always done. The analytical point is that the competition in the forest districts was never between the Indian state and an ideology. It was between two administrations, one present and one absent, and the present one won the allegiance that presence earns, at the price of the obedience that armed presence also commands.

Historians reaching for comparisons have noted that the pattern, an insurgency consolidating where the state's service frontier fails while its extractive frontier advances, recurs across the postcolonial world and long predates Marxism in any of its dialects. The Indian forest belt adds a specific twist: the extractive frontier there is guarded by law of great antiquity and moral confidence. Forest reservation in India descends from colonial legislation that declared vast tracts state property in the name of scientific management, extinguishing by notification customary rights that had never been recorded because they had never needed recording. The independent republic retained the structure and the doctrine, adding development as a second justification where the colonial power had pleaded timber revenue. The Adivasi villager thus confronts a state whose claim on his landscape is older than the republic, older than the movement that would later organise him, and enforced by a department that is landlord, police, and judge in its own cause. When the movement's squads first burned a forest depot or drove out a contractor's camp, the act landed in a moral world already prepared for it by a century of forest administration. The doctrine the cadres carried explained the act to the cadres. The forest law explained it to the village.

## Arms and their supply: the material culture of the squads

The movement's military character is easily overstated by its imagery and understated by its logistics, and a sober account of its armament corrects both literatures. For most of its history and in most of its theatres, the squads fought with the weapons of the countryside itself: country made firearms of the kind every Indian district produces illicitly, muzzle loaders and shotguns taken from landlords and licensees, and the axes, bows, and blades of the first rising, supplemented by explosives adapted from the mining economy whose geology the movement shared, gelatin and detonators diverted from the quarries and collieries that honeycombed its territory. Captured police weapons formed the prized upper tier of the arsenal, which made the ambush of isolated posts and patrols an economic as much as a military operation, a means of re-equipment, and made the defence of the state's own armouries in the belt a permanent anxiety of its commanders. In the consolidation period the arsenal modernised unevenly: assault rifles and landmines of manufactured origin appeared in the stronghold units, sourced through the region's grey markets and the country's porous eastern borders, while the militia base, the organisation's true mass, continued to carry the countryside's traditional weapons into its ambushes and its guard duties. The disparity between the tiers mattered politically. A movement whose documents promised a people's army progressing through stages of regularisation commanded, in material fact, a small core of modernly armed guerrillas floating on a sea of village militia whose armament differed little from that of the tribal risings of the century before, and the state's saturation strategy was designed against precisely that structure: the core could be hunted by commandos, the sea drained by administration, and the two operations, conducted simultaneously, dissolved the compound that neither could dissolve alone.

The explosives deserve a sentence of their own, because they bind the movement's war to the belt's economy in the most literal way available. The landmine, the strongholds' signature weapon in the war's hardest years, was assembled substantially from materials the mining industry itself supplied to the district, diverted through the same contractor networks the levy taxed, so that the mineral economy armed its own obstruction in a closed loop the security literature noted with bitterness. Casualties from these devices fell on the security forces' vehicles and patrols and, in the nature of buried weapons, on the population's paths and cattle as well, a fact the movement's critics entered prominently in the ledger against it and its own reviews acknowledged obliquely. This article describes the arsenal at the level of its political economy and declines, in keeping with the discipline applied to every operational matter in these pages, the technical and tactical particulars that belong to the manuals of the belligerents rather than to a work of reference.

## The tendu season: an economy in a leaf

To feel the strongholds' economy in the hand, follow one commodity through one season. The tendu leaf, plucked in the hot weeks before the monsoon, is rolled into the bidi cigarettes smoked across poor India, and its harvest is the forest household's principal cash event of the year. The chain begins with the state or its licensed contractors purchasing leaf from gatherers at rates fixed administratively, proceeds through layers of agents whose scales and classifications shave the gatherer's return, and ends in warehouses far from the forest, in an industry whose profits the gatherer never sees. For a family whose grain lasts eight months, the tendu weeks decide the year: school fees, debt service, a roof repaired, a daughter's wedding, all ride on the rate per bundle and the honesty of the weighing. The grievance the leaf carries is therefore not abstract exploitation. It is a number the gatherer can compute, announced each season by an authority that also classifies her father's plot as encroachment, and the comparison between the labour's price and the leaf's destination is a political education delivered annually without any cadre's assistance.

The movement's intervention in the tendu trade was its most successful economic act, repeated across the belt with local variations: a rate demanded and enforced for the season, contractors compelled to weigh honestly and pay in the village, a levy taken by the organisation in return for the enforcement, and, in stronghold districts, the effective transfer of the season's bargaining from the forest department's calendar to the organisation's. Gatherers' testimony about the change survives in every oral history of the period, and its terms are strikingly consistent: the leaf paid more, the weighing was watched, the agent who had cheated for a generation calculated differently. Set beside the movement's grander claims, the tendu settlement is modest to the point of bathos, a few rupees on a bundle of leaves. Set beside the alternatives the population had known, it was the only redistribution most forest households ever received from any authority, and it recurred every year, in cash, at the season of greatest need. Insurgencies are not sustained by their programmes. They are sustained by the settlements their presence enforces in the transactions of ordinary life, and a reader who wants the materialist explanation of the corridor in a single image should take this one: a woman tying bundles at dawn, in a forest the record calls the state's, selling at a rate a rifle she never touches has raised, in the few weeks on which her year depends.

## The levy economy: how parallel power fed itself

An armed organisation that endures must solve the problem of supply, and the forest movement's solution deserves examination in its own right, because the two interested literatures describe the same transactions in incompatible vocabularies and the truth is recoverable from the overlap. The movement's income in its strongholds derived principally from levies on the cash economy that passed through its territory: contractors executing government works, mining and transport firms, traders in forest produce, and, in the earlier Telangana phase, landlords and rich peasants assessed by village committees. Rates in the produce trades were, at the movement's most disciplined, standardised and receipted; the levy on works contractors functioned as a percentage understood by both sides and built into bids, a second exchequer shadowing the state's. Against this income stood the organisation's expenditures: the keep of full time cadres and squads, arms and ammunition purchased through the region's illicit markets, medical care, propaganda, and the support of prisoners' and martyrs' families, an obligation its documents treat as sacred and its practice generally honoured. The security literature names the whole system extortion, and at its undisciplined edge, where local commanders assessed arbitrarily, punished default with violence, and could not be appealed against, the name is accurate. The movement's literature names it taxation by a state in embryo, and where the levy purchased the services described elsewhere in this article, functioning courts, regulated produce prices, protection against worse predators, the name has a claim as well.

### How did a levy differ from a tax in the strongholds?

Imperfectly, and the imperfection is the finding. At its most regular the levy was assessed by known rates, receipted, and returned in valued services, resembling taxation by an unrecognised authority. At its worst it was arbitrary, enforced by the gun, and unappealable, resembling the extortion the security literature describes. The face a village met depended on the commander's discipline.

The analytical value of the levy system lies in what it reveals about the strongholds' political economy. A movement financed by taxing extraction is structurally bound to the extraction it opposes: the mine it obstructs in the name of the displaced also pays, when it operates, for the squads that obstruct it, and the contractor whose rake off the movement denounces is the same contractor whose bid quietly includes the levy. Participants on all sides understood the symbiosis, and it generated, in the movement's own internal discussions as reported by surrendered cadres, a recurring unease about whether the organisation was fighting the mineral economy or had become one of its costs, priced in like transport and security. The population made its own version of the calculation with characteristic precision, distinguishing in interview after interview between the levy that came with receipts and services and the demand that came with neither, and withdrawing cooperation from commanders who crossed the line, sometimes at terrible risk. Consent, in the strongholds, was not a founding endowment. It was a current account, audited continuously by the people who paid, and the movement's decline can be written, in one of its truest versions, as the history of that account's overdrafts.

## The forest department: portrait of an antagonist

Every insurgency is shaped by the specific adversary it meets daily, and the forest movement's daily adversary was not, in the first instance, the soldier or the policeman but the forest official, a figure whose history explains the conflict's peculiar bitterness. The department descends from the colonial forest service, created to reserve the subcontinent's timber wealth for imperial purposes, railways, shipbuilding, war, and staffed from the beginning as a uniformed, hierarchical estate within the state, landlord of the reserved forests and prosecutor of their offences. Its working philosophy, scientific forestry, valued the forest as a crop of commercial species on a rotation, a valuation in which the Adivasi's mixed forest of food, fodder, medicine, and gods registered as degradation to be corrected. Independence changed the department's masters without changing its doctrine or its law; the republic's forest policy added the language of national development to the colonial language of revenue, and the guard at the forest's edge, the lowest and most visible rung of the whole apparatus, continued to collect the small rents of his position from the people whose lives his classification governed. When the movement's squads first punished a forest guard or burned a department depot, the act required no explanation to its audience: it was the settling of an account older than the republic, and the squads' popularity in their first seasons can be dated from such settlements as precisely as from any document.

The department was not a monolith, and fairness requires its internal record to be entered. Its officers included, across the decades, men who documented the injustice of the classification system from inside, who argued the community's case in the language of working plans and settlement reports, and whose evidence the reform legislation of later years silently used. Its guards were themselves, in the main, local men of modest origin, posted between their own people's claims and their service's law, and the war placed them in its front line with a vulnerability their uniforms advertised and their authority could not protect. The movement's documents treat the department as feudalism's local franchise; the department's own literature treats the movement as the final obstacle to the forest's scientific management; and the population, once again, kept its own ledger, distinguishing the officer who measured honestly from the one who measured for a consideration, and mourning, on occasion, men of the first kind whom the war's categories had marked as the second. The structural point survives the particulars. A state that meets its forest citizens exclusively through a landlord department should not be surprised when those citizens experience the state as a landlord, nor when a rival landlord, offering better terms and a quicker court, finds the tenancy transferable.

## Contested rights: the forest as a legal battlefield

Dispossession in the forest districts rarely announces itself as theft. It proceeds by classification. Land a community has cultivated, grazed, and gathered from for generations exists in the record, if at all, as reserved forest, as government waste, as unsurveyed, categories under which the cultivator is an encroacher and his crop a trespass. Rights that were recorded during the colonial settlements were recorded for the plains and the revenue village; the forest village, administered by a different department under a different philosophy, often stands outside the revenue record altogether, its inhabitants tenants at will on land their grandfathers cleared. Add the protected area notifications, which convert lived landscapes into sanctuaries and parks from which residence and use are excluded, and the development project, mine, dam, industrial estate, or firing range, each acquiring under eminent domain what classification had already devalued, and the mechanism stands complete: a population can be dispossessed in perfect legality, repeatedly, by instruments none of which name it as an enemy. The series analysis of [primitive accumulation](/2011/01/01/primitive-accumulation-explained/) treats the general process by which producers are separated from the means of production as the founding act of capital; in the Indian forest the process never ended, and its unfinished character is the deep background of everything this article describes.

### What does a contested forest right look like from inside a village?

It looks like a family cultivating a plot its grandfather cleared, classified in the record as encroachment on reserved forest, liable to eviction and destruction of the standing crop, negotiating each season with the guard who can enforce or overlook the classification. The right exists in custom and use, nowhere the family can point to when the notice arrives.

Against this machinery the available legal remedies have been real but slow, and their slowness is part of the explanation of the armed alternative. Constitutional provisions for the scheduled areas promise protection and self governance that state administrations have implemented patchily for generations. A national forest rights law, enacted in the middle of the 2000s after long campaigning by tribal movements and their allies in open politics, recognised individual and community rights over forest land and produce on paper, and its implementation then passed into the hands of the same departments whose claims it curtailed, with predictable friction: claims rejected in large numbers, community rights recognised more slowly than individual ones, and the mining and conservation lobbies pressing for dilution. The point for this article is not the scorecard of a statute, which belongs to other treatments, but the structural lesson the forest population drew across decades. Every peaceful instrument, petition, litigation, legislation, delivered recognition slowly, partially, and reversibly, while the bulldozer and the survey party arrived on schedule. An organisation promising to raise the cost of dispossession by force was answering a calculation the population had already made from experience. That the promise carried its own terrible price is the subject of the objection sections below. That it was intelligible, given the record, is the premise of the geography of dispossession.

## Rivers, roads, and railways: infrastructure as a belligerent

The forest belt's infrastructure is usually described as the development the region lacks. In the history of this conflict it functioned as a weapon system whose ownership changed the war's terms, and the movement understood the fact earlier than the state did. A road into forest country is, in the first instance, an extraction instrument: it carries the surveyor, the leaseholder's trucks, the timber and ore out and the contractor's camp in, and its arrival in a previously roadless tract has preceded, in district after district, the population's first experience of dispossession at scale. The same road is, in the second instance, a military instrument: it moves the patrol in hours where the footpath took days, delivers the camp's supplies, and converts sanctuary into traversable ground. The movement accordingly treated road building in its strongholds as an act of war by other means, obstructing alignments, destroying machinery, and taxing or forbidding the works, while the state's engineers, in the saturation phase, learned to build roads as operations, surveyed under guard, constructed behind camps, and prized in the official literature in frankly strategic language. Bridges and culverts acquired tactical biographies. The railway lines that evacuate the belt's ore crossed the strongholds as exposed arteries, guarded and occasionally cut, their economics quietly incorporating the levy and the risk as the road contracts did.

For the population the infrastructure question was never so clean. The road that brings the patrol also brings the ambulance, the bus to the district town, the trader who pays more for produce than the contractor's agent at the forest's edge, and the route by which a daughter reaches the secondary school whose absence the movement's own courts could not remedy. Villages divided over particular alignments with a sophistication no outsider's categories capture, welcoming the road to the market and resisting the road to the mine, and the movement's authority was taxed hardest exactly here, where its strategic interest in isolation collided with its constituents' interest in connection. The long arc is legible in retrospect. The forest's inaccessibility was a wasting asset from the moment the mineral economy priced the belt's contents, because the wealth beneath the trees guaranteed that the roads would come, under one flag or another, within the lifetime of the trees themselves. The movement's stronghold period occupies the interval between the pricing and the arrival, the decades when the ore was valuable, the roads unbuilt, and the state that owned the one had not yet paid for the other. Saturation was the payment. Students of the decline should date it not from any battle but from the completion certificates of the road programmes, which altered the terrain variable in the configuration as permanently as a change of climate.

## Displacement: the grievance that renews itself

Among the conditions that made the forest districts habitable for an armed movement, displacement holds a special place, because unlike a static grievance it renews itself with every project cycle. The central Indian mineral belt has been surveyed, leased, and opened in waves since independence: iron ore mines feeding steel plants old and new, coal blocks for power stations, bauxite plateaus coveted by alumina refineries, dams on the forest rivers, and the roads, railways, and townships each project drags behind it. The legal machinery of acquisition compensates, where it compensates, the recorded title holder for the recorded plot. It does not compensate the community for the forest it gathered from, the stream it fished, the grazing it shared, or the sacred geography its dead inhabit, because none of those exist in the record from which compensation is calculated. Resettlement colonies, where provided, characteristically offer house plots without livelihoods to people whose livelihood was a landscape. A population can absorb such a blow once and remain a citizenry petitioning for redress. Subjected to the sequence across generations, mine after dam after sanctuary after firing range, it becomes a population for whom the state's arrival in any form predicts loss, and for whom an organisation that obstructs the sequence, even at the price of living under arms, is not an abstraction but a hedge.

### How does displacement turn a grievance into a constituency?

By repeating itself. A single acquisition can be petitioned against and absorbed; a standing sequence of mines, dams, and exclusions teaches a community that the state's arrival predicts loss, and every peaceful remedy arrives after the fact. An organisation able to raise the cost of the next acquisition, by presence and by arms, converts accumulated expectation into durable allegiance.

The movement understood this dynamic and organised toward it with deliberation. Its documents and the testimony of surrendered cadres agree on the method, whatever else they dispute: identify the project or the contractor whose arrival threatens the villages, obstruct the survey, punish the intermediary who recruits labour or land on false promises, demand the raising of wages and produce prices around the works, and present the organisation as the reason the terms improved or the project stalled. Where projects stalled for years in the belt, for reasons that mixed local resistance, litigation, market conditions, and official hesitation, the movement claimed the credit and was often believed, and belief, compounded across a district, is the currency in which insurgent authority is denominated. Fairness requires the other side of the ledger to be entered here as plainly. The same presence that obstructed acquisition also taxed the works that proceeded, sometimes heavily; obstructed schools, roads, and health posts it suspected, or whose contractors it feuded with; punished villagers who cooperated with the administration; and made the district's development hostage to its own strategic requirements, which did not always coincide with the inhabitants' wishes. A villager could find the movement's court quicker than the state's, its tax lighter than the contractor's rake off, and its protection real, and still reckon the total price of its sovereignty, in closed schools, in sons recruited, in the retaliation its presence invited upon the village, as more than the dispossession it prevented. Both reckonings existed, often in the same household, and the movement's decline, when it came, grew from the second.

## Land reform's second act: what the revolt forced the republic to finish

The revolt's most paradoxical consequence lies outside its own territory, in the plains it failed to hold, and a balanced account should register it before turning to the inversion. The parliamentary party that suppressed the rising in Bengal went on, a decade later and restored to office at the head of the Left Front, to execute the most extensive tenancy reform in the republic's history: the registration of sharecroppers in their millions in the celebrated operation that walked village to village recording the bargadar's name and right, the distribution of ceiling surplus land, and the panchayat led delivery that made the reform administratively real. The reform's architects were the rebels' former comrades and gaolers, and the reform's content was, in substantial part, the programme the rebels had been expelled for demanding be implemented without delay. Historians of the Front treat the sequence with the delicacy it requires. The reform's timing owed much to the party's own evolution and to the electoral arithmetic of the late 1970s; yet the pressure of the intervening decade, the demonstration that the countryside contained a constituency prepared to kill and die over the unrecorded sharecropper, concentrated official minds in a way that a generation of resolutions had not, and the Front's organisers in the registration camps included men radicalised by the revolt they were now, in office, answering. The series analysis of [the Left Front's Bengal](/2018/01/15/west-bengal-left-front-analysis/) gives that ministry its full treatment; the point here is the dialectic the sequence establishes against both literatures' preferred moral. Against the movement's account, the parliamentary road delivered, in the end, the largest single instalment of the founding programme, by the method the founders died repudiating. Against the parliamentary account, the delivery arrived a decade late, over the graves of the generation that had demanded it on time, and under a compulsion the revolt itself had supplied. Which of those sentences a reader underlines is the reader's politics. The sequence belongs to the record either way.

## One programme, two agrarian questions

A thread worth pulling, before the inversion is examined, is the quiet transformation of the movement's agrarian content as its address changed, since the transformation explains features of the forest organisation that its programme, unrevised in its formulae, conceals. The agrarian question of the Bengal plains was a question of tenancy: who holds the land, on what terms, with what security, and the movement's founding demands, record the sharecropper, fix the share, seize the ceiling surplus, were drafted for that question and answered it directly. The agrarian question of the forest is a question of sovereignty over a landscape: who may classify the land, license its produce, lease its depths, and exclude its people, and on those questions the founding programme, written by plainsmen for plains conditions, had almost nothing to say. The forest organisation consequently improvised its real programme in practice, and the improvisation is what the population experienced as the movement: defence of cultivation classified as encroachment, regulation of the produce trades, obstruction of acquisition, punishment of the forest guard's and contractor's exactions, and the assertion, through its people's committees, of a counter sovereignty that treated the community, not the department, as the land's proprietor. Doctrinally this was heterodoxy of a high order, a programme of tribal autonomy administered by a party of class revolution, and the movement's documents handled the tension by silence and by the reclassification described in the next section. Practically it was the source of the organisation's strength, since it aligned the party's offer with the population's actual grievance rather than with the grievance the party's theory assigned them.

The improvisation had a second consequence that historians of the wider Indian left have begun to trace. Forest politics of the kind the movement practised did not remain inside the movement. The demands it armed, community rights over forest, consent of the village assembly to acquisition and diversion, the recognition of the gram sabha as an authority in the scheduled areas, travelled outward into the open politics of the tribal belt, into legislation campaigned for by movements that rejected the gun entirely, and into the platforms of parties that had never read the founding documents. Separating the armed organisation's contribution to that diffusion from the work of the open tribal movements is probably impossible and certainly invidious; both drew on the same grievances and the same older traditions of refusal. What can be said is that the agenda of forest rights in Indian politics bears the imprint of the decades in which the only effective defender of those rights, in district after district, carried a rifle, and that the open movements inherited both the agenda and the burden of dissociating it from the rifle. The parliamentary critics' objection, recorded later in this article, that the gun crowded out every other instrument, meets here its most concrete rejoinder and its most concrete confirmation at once: the gun advanced the agenda and monopolised its credit, and the population that paid for the advance was left, when the gun withdrew, to defend the gains with instruments it had been discouraged from building.

## The inversion: a proletarian doctrine on an Adivasi base

Pause over the strangeness announced at the outset, because it is the article's centre and repays exact statement. The doctrine the cadres carried into the forest districts was a theory of history in which the industrial proletariat, created by capitalism itself, becomes the agent of capitalism's supersession, allied with the peasantry under proletarian leadership. The population the movement actually rested on was, by that doctrine's own categories, among the least proletarian in India: tribal communities organised by kinship and clan, practising shifting or settled cultivation on customary land, worshipping in their own traditions, speaking languages the cadres often had to learn, and standing largely outside the wage relation except as seasonal migrants to brick kilns and construction sites. The movement's leadership, in its upper reaches, remained disproportionately drawn from the educated castes of the plains, students and intellectuals formed in Calcutta, Patna, Hyderabad, and Delhi. The organisation thus reproduced, inside itself, the structure of the puzzle: a doctrine of the industrial class, carried by the educated, resting on a base of the pre industrial, and held together by the one thing all three shared, which was enmity toward the state that confronted each of them in a different costume.

### Why is the movement based among tribal populations?

because of terrain and dispossession rather than ideology. Its areas of operation are forested, mineral-rich districts with thin administrative presence, contested forest rights, and displacement for mining and infrastructure, which are the conditions under which an armed organisation can establish itself and find grievance.

The movement's own literature handles the anomaly by reclassification. In its documents, the Adivasi cultivator appears as a poor peasant, the forest struggle as agrarian revolution, and the whole configuration as the semi feudal order the programme predicts, with the forest department and the mining company cast in the roles the landlord and the moneylender played in the plains. The reclassification is not empty: the Adivasi cultivator is indeed exploited, does indeed lose land and produce to more powerful outsiders, and the parallel with the sharecropper's world is genuine as far as it goes. What it cannot capture is the specific content of the tribal grievance, which is as much about autonomy, custom, and the forest as a civilisation as about rent and wages, and which has repeatedly overflowed the movement's categories: in demands for separate tribal statehood, in the assertion of traditional self governing institutions against both the state and the party's committees, and in a long history, predating any communist presence, of tribal revolts against outside authority conducted under tribal leadership and tribal gods. The older rebellions of the Santhals, the Mundas, and others stand in the region's memory as proof that resistance to dispossession in the forest is an indigenous tradition into which the Marxist organisation inserted itself, not a fire the organisation kindled from cold ground. The movement that arrived proclaiming itself the vanguard of a class found itself, in the forest, the junior partner of a much older refusal, and its durability there is best explained by its willingness, at its most effective, to serve that refusal rather than lecture it.

### Why does forest terrain suit an armed organisation?

Forest and hill country multiplies a small force. Concealment negates the state's superiority in numbers and firepower, poor roads slow every response, monsoon rivers cut districts into islands, and borders between states fracture the pursuer's command while sheltering the pursued. An organisation that must avoid battle except at advantage finds its conditions of survival supplied by the landscape itself.

## The middle peasantry: the class that would not play its part

One further structural fact explains the plains failures from within the movement's own categories, and it has been oddly underweighted in accounts that prefer their explanations doctrinal or moral. The programme assigned the leading role in the countryside to an alliance of the poor peasantry and agricultural labourers against landlords and rich peasants, with the middle peasantry, the owner cultivators who work their own land and hire little labour, to be won or neutralised. In the India the cadres actually entered, that middle stratum was not a residual category but the countryside's centre of gravity: numerous, armed with caste and kinship networks, courted by every party, and possessed of a programme of its own, cheap inputs, remunerative prices, protection of its small property, that neither landlordism nor revolution addressed. The parliamentary parties, and in time the farmers' movements, harvested this stratum for generations. The revolutionary organisation, whose documents alternately courted and threatened it, and whose squads in the first phase sometimes robbed or killed its members as class enemies, never solved the relationship, and in the plains districts the middle peasantry became, in consequence, the state's most reliable rural ally, supplying the information, the militia recruits, and the electoral majorities that made armed persistence impossible. The forest inverted this variable along with the others. Adivasi society has its own differentiations, of clan seniority, of the headman's line, of the settled against the shifting cultivator, yet it lacks the plains' structure of a propertied middle stratum standing between landlord and labourer, and the organisation that entered the forest faced a comparatively undifferentiated community whose divisions it could address one by one. Class analysis, applied honestly, thus predicts its own displacement: the doctrine travelled to where the class structure it described least obtained, because that was where a movement organised in its name could survive contact with the state. The irony is not a refutation of the method. It is the method, turned upon the movement itself, and the tradition's better historians have not flinched from printing it.

## Coal country: the negative case with a skyline

The comparison gains a control from the one extractive region the movement never conquered. The coalfields of the Damodar valley, among the oldest industrial landscapes in Asia, present extraction in its most concentrated form: pits and washeries, company towns, a vast proletariat organised for a century in trade unions of every political colour, mafia economies of coal and contract, and a state present in density, in the pithead police station, the labour court, the union office, and the parliamentary constituency fought over by national parties. Revolutionary politics existed there in abundance; the coalfields' unions were communist led for generations, their strikes general, their martyrs honoured. What never appeared was a Naxalite stronghold of the forest type, though the doctrine's carriers worked the coalfields as they worked everywhere and the grievances, dangerous work, stolen wages, company raj, were as documented as any in India. The variables the configuration requires were absent in instructive combination: no forest to vanish into, no administrative thinness, a population legible and organised, its grievances already monopolised by institutions that could bargain, strike, and sue. Extraction alone, the case proves, does not produce the movement; the coal beneath a governed countryside generated unions and parties, while the ore beneath an ungoverned forest generated a parallel state, and the difference between the two landscapes is the whole of this article's argument drawn in a single contrast. The movement's own literature recognises the coalfields obliquely, in its relative silence about its weakest theatre, and the security literature, which maps threats rather than absences, has never had occasion to explain why the reddest workforce in India declined to become the corridor. The structural explanation explains it without effort, which is what explanations are for.

## Why Bengal did not return: the founding ground revisited

The selection argument can be tested a last time on the ground where the story opened. Bengal after the first phase possessed everything the movement's mythology required: the founding's own districts, a rural population with living memory of the rising, a communist tradition of unmatched depth, and, under the long Left Front ministry, a government of the very party the rebels had indicted, whose compromises supplied fresh evidence for the old charge in every decade. Successor organisations attempted the return repeatedly, in the Terai itself and in the western districts where the state's writ thins toward the Jharkhand border, and the attempts produced, at their height, a genuine armed presence in the western jungle mahal country that flared, drew saturation in reply, and subsided within years. The durable re rooting the mythology promised never occurred, and the configuration explains the failure without remainder. Bengal's countryside is the most densely administered in India, its villages saturated for three decades by the governing party's own machinery, its panchayats, unions, and committees occupying every organisational niche a rival might enter, its land question substantially transformed by the registration reform the Front had executed. The rebels of the return faced not an absent state and an unorganised peasantry but a present party and a recorded one, and the lesson completes the plains trilogy the article has assembled: Bihar's armed road dissolved into caste war and the ballot, Andhra's construction was dismantled by a modernising state, and Bengal's return was refused by a countryside already organised to the last lane. Where the movement endured, it endured where no one, neither state nor party nor union, had arrived before it. The forest was not its preference. It was its vacancy.

## The comparison that tests the claim: where the movement never lived

A structural explanation earns its keep on the negative cases, and the negative cases here are eloquent. India has never lacked for districts of desperate poverty, landlord domination, and agrarian distress outside the forest belt, and the movement's doctrine was carried into many of them by cadres as committed as any who entered the forest. The dry Deccan districts, the sugarcane belt's migrant labour camps, the brick kiln country of the northern plains, the distressed agrarian regions whose farmer suicides became a national scandal: in none of these did an armed movement root durably, though peasant organisations of other traditions, unions, and parties of the open left worked several of them for generations. Poverty was present; so were landlords, moneylenders, and corrupt administration. What was absent was the configuration. The country was open, the police station near, the population legible to the state and divided among competing political organisations that monopolised the representation of grievance, and an armed squad in such country was a police problem of a fortnight. The doctrine's failure there is the strongest single evidence that the doctrine was not the variable doing the work in the forest.

### Why did Kerala never produce an armed movement of this kind?

Kerala had the doctrine, the cadres, and the grievances, and lacked the configuration. Its countryside is dense, administered, literate, and saturated with competing political organisations that organised every grievance the communists themselves had not; its forests are small and enclosed; and its communist movement held office early and repeatedly, absorbing its radicals into the responsibilities and compromises of government.

The positive comparisons cut the same way. Within the belt itself, presence tracked the configuration at fine grain: the same organisation, with the same doctrine and often the same commanders, held one district durably and failed in the adjoining one where a highway, a district headquarters town, or a longer history of revenue administration thickened the state's presence. Across the state borders inside the forest, the movement's strength followed the seams between administrations with a fidelity no theory of ideological diffusion can reproduce, strengthening where two jurisdictions each treated the border villages as the other's problem. And the historical comparison within the forest completes the triangulation: the region's earlier revolts, the tribal risings of the colonial period conducted without a page of Marxist theory, chose the same refuges, assembled the same constituencies, and were broken by the same method, the penetration of the forest by roads, camps, and record. The pattern is older than the doctrine that inherited it, and any explanation of the movement that begins with its beliefs will fail on all three comparisons, while the explanation that begins with its conditions passes them without strain. That is the sense in which this article's namable claim should be understood: not as a denial that ideas mattered, since ideas chose the founding strategy and its catastrophic first form, but as a ranking. Ideas decided what the movement attempted. The ground decided what survived the attempt.

## The monsoon calendar: the war's seasons

The conflict kept a calendar dictated by water, and both sides' histories are unintelligible without it. The forest year divides at the monsoon: the rains that turn the belt's rivers into walls, dissolve its tracks, and ground every wheeled ambition, followed by the long dry season in which movement, and therefore war, becomes possible. The organisation's rhythm followed the water. The dry months were for operations, conferences, recruitment drives, and the great movements of squads between districts; the monsoon was for political education in the sheltered villages, for the repair of networks, for the tendu and mahua seasons' aftermath and the harvest's demands, and for enduring the sickness that the rains brought to camps without sanitation. The state's calendar was the inverse image of the same facts: its offensives concentrated in the dry season when its vehicles could move, its camps stocked and fortified against the months of isolation, its intelligence cycles timed to the enemy's predictable concentrations. Journalists who visited the belt learned to date their journeys by the same reckoning, and the population, whose agricultural year the war's calendar overlaid without displacing, read the seasons in triplicate: the sowing and the harvest, the leaf and the flower, and the arrival and withdrawal of the armed men of both sovereignties, each on a schedule the village could recite.

The calendar's strategic consequence was a war of pulses rather than of fronts, in which territory was not so much held as visited in rhythm, and in which the state's saturation method can be understood as a refusal of the calendar itself: the permanent camp, supplied by air and by all weather road, was an instrument for abolishing the monsoon's protection, for making the state's presence as continuous as the population's. Where the camps and roads arrived, the year flattened; the organisation lost the season in which it had renewed itself, and the decline, measured in the state's reporting as operations tempo and in the movement's as survival, can be graphed against the road building programme as faithfully as against any battle. Climate was the forest's oldest ally of the weak, older than any doctrine, and the risings of the colonial period had kept the same calendar for the same reasons. What changed in the final phase was not the rain. It was the adversary's decision to pay the price of ignoring it.

## The border country: sovereignty in the seams

The strongholds' geography has a juridical dimension that the relief map understates. The forest belt lies across the internal borders of half a dozen states, and within states across district boundaries whose jurisdictions were drawn for revenue convenience in another century. Every such border is, for an armed organisation, a resource: pursuit halts at it, intelligence files do not cross it automatically, operations require a coordination between administrations that competes with every other demand on their attention, and the population of the border villages learns to live under a double absence, each capital treating the seam as the other's margin. The movement's regional structure adapted itself to the seams with an exactness that cannot be coincidence: its divisions and committees were organised along the border country, its sanctuaries placed where jurisdictions meet, and its retreat routes surveyed across the lines the pursuers could not cross without paperwork. The state's counter adaptation, joint operations, unified commands, and in the saturation phase the deliberate effacement of the seams by central coordination, was correspondingly among the most consequential and least publicised of its methods, a bureaucratic reform with battlefield effects.

Beneath the tactical layer lies a demographic one. The border country's population is itself transjurisdictional: Adivasi communities whose kin, markets, sacred sites, and seasonal migrations ignore boundaries drawn through their homeland by distant secretariats, and whose experience of the state has always been of a plurality of masters, forest departments of two states claiming the same hillside, police of two districts disclaiming the same crime. A movement that organised the homeland as one theatre was, in this respect, more congruent with the population's lived geography than the republic that partitioned it, and the congruence was felt. The point generalises the article's thesis once more. The state whose unevenness explains the movement was uneven not only in its services but in its very architecture, a federation of jurisdictions laid over older countries, and the insurgency grew in the joints of the architecture as naturally as damp in a wall. Sealing the joints was possible, and in the decline years was partly accomplished, at the price of a centralisation the federation's ordinary politics resisted for every purpose except this one, which is among the quieter constitutional consequences the movement can claim to have forced.

## The corridor: a label and its discontents

Security commentary long ago named the movement's geography the red corridor, a phrase picturing a continuous band of insurgent territory running down the eastern and central spine of the country, from the Nepal border country to the southern peninsula. The phrase has journalistic force and analytical defects that a student should be able to enumerate. It pictures continuity where the record shows an archipelago: districts of durable presence separated by districts of mere transit and others of none, the whole less a corridor than a scatter of islands whose coastlines shift with pressure. It pictures a single command directing the band, where the reality, even after the merger of the 2000s, was of regional committees exercising wide autonomy across difficult communications. And it implies, by its colour and its sweep, a territorial claim of the kind a state makes, where the organisation's actual presence in much of the shaded map amounted to taxation visits, a militia network, and the population's uncertainty about which authority would arrive next. Estimates of the corridor's extent, in the official and journalistic literatures, diverge from one another by very large factors, each source counting differently: districts with a single incident, districts with a squad, districts with a parallel administration, all shaded alike. That divergence is itself information, of the kind this article's evidence discipline predicts: in a field where every measurer is a party, measurement becomes a weapon, and the honest response is to describe the configuration and decline the arithmetic.

### Why is the corridor label resisted by historians of the movement?

Because it mistakes an archipelago for a territory. Durable presence, episodic transit, and sympathy shade into one another on the security map, regional commands answer to no single headquarters, and the one colour implies a unified power the record does not show. The label serves the needs of those who coined it more faithfully than it describes the ground.

Beneath the argument about maps lies an argument about audiences. The corridor image addresses the citizen of the plains city, for whom the forest districts are a blank to be filled with a threat, and the official who must extract budgets and battalions from legislatures by pointing at the filled blank. The archipelago image addresses the district, where the questions that decide outcomes are small and concrete: which villages feed the squads, which contractor pays, which official is feared, which court sits. Both images circulate because both have uses, and a reader of the subject learns early to ask, of any map of the movement, who drew it and for what meeting. The structural explanation offered in these pages is an attempt to draw the third kind of map, organised not by incidents or by sympathy but by conditions: forest cover, mineral endowment, administrative thinness, and the history of contested rights, plotted together. On that map the movement's presence requires no corridor and no conspiracy. It appears where the four conditions overlap, fades where any one of them is withdrawn, and the pattern holds across regions whose populations share no language, no doctrine, and no contact with one another, which is the strongest evidence that conditions, rather than contagion, drew it.

## The language question: a doctrine learns new tongues

The movement's programme was written in the languages of the plains, in Bengali and Telugu and Hindi and English, in a vocabulary of class and stage formed in other countrysides. Its forest life required translation of a depth the founding never anticipated, and the translation's difficulties are a record of the inversion in miniature. Cadres arriving in the belt faced populations whose languages, Gondi, Koya, Dorli, Halbi, and a dozen others, had in several cases no standard written form, whose categories for land, kinship, and obligation mapped imperfectly onto the doctrine's, and whose political vocabulary centred on concepts, the village's autonomy, the clan's honour, the forest as ancestor, that the programme had no words for at all. The organisation's solution, evolved over years and documented in its own publications' history, was to build the translation into its structure: local recruits trained as the movement's teachers, primers and songbooks composed in the local languages, political education conducted through the idioms of the audience's own history, its risings and heroes, until the doctrine reached the village substantially re authored. What arrived was consequently not the programme of 1969 with tribal illustrations. It was a local politics of dignity, land, and refusal, wearing the programme's organisational forms, and the gap between the two, which the movement's central literature persistently understates, is the space in which the forest population actually lived with its protectors.

Language also marked the movement's internal hierarchy as plainly as any insignia. Command of the plains languages and of the texts was command of the doctrine's authorised interpretation, and the belt's own cadres, however senior in the valley, encountered a ceiling where the organisation's business passed into the written word of the centre. Memoirs and surrendered cadres' accounts return to the friction: the village commander whose authority among his people was absolute and whose reports were rewritten at every level above him, the conference where the forest's experience was minuted in a language its protagonists could not read. The movement that had indicted the Indian state for ruling the Adivasi through an alien tongue reproduced the arrangement in its own house, with better intentions and similar effects, and its historians' silence on the point is among the archive's most eloquent. For the series' wider concerns, the episode stands as the general law of travelling doctrines: a theory survives its journey by being rewritten in the vernacular of each new ground, and the rewriting, not the original, is what the ground's inhabitants ever believed.

## Women in the squads and in the villages

Any account of the forest base that omits its women describes half a society, and the movement's relation to them is among the most contested parts of its record, which this article reports by attribution on every side. The movement's own literature presents its record as emancipatory: women recruited in large proportion into its squads and militias, in some formations approaching the parity its documents celebrate; women's organisations among its mass fronts campaigning against forced marriage, witch branding, and the liquor trade; women commanders leading units and people's courts enforcing, sometimes severely, codes against domestic violence and sexual predation, including by its own cadres. Journalists and anthropologists who worked in the strongholds corroborate portions of the picture from observation: the sight of armed Adivasi women was the single feature of the strongholds that most struck outside visitors, and village testimony collected across decades credits the movement with suppressing specific abuses, the contractor's and official's sexual demands prominent among them, that the absent state had never policed. For many young women of the belt, enlistment also offered an exit from early marriage and household subordination that no other institution in their world provided, and the organisation's own recruitment understood and used the fact.

The critical record, assembled by feminist scholars, by surrender and rehabilitation accounts, and by the testimony of former women cadres, complicates every clause. Leadership at the organisation's summit remained overwhelmingly male and drawn from outside the tribal population, so that the emancipation on offer was designed by the same educated plains cadre that designed everything else. Women cadres' accounts describe the postponement of marriage and childbearing as organisational discipline rather than choice, the punishment of relationships the committees disapproved, and the exposure of women in the ranks to pressures the official literature does not record. Village women outside the ranks met the movement as taxpayers, as mothers of recruits, and as the population on whom the war's retaliation fell with particular weight, since the men of both armed parties were so often absent, dead, or in flight. Weighing these records against each other is beyond the competence of any summary, and this article does not attempt a verdict the sources will not carry. What the structural argument requires is narrower and can be stated: the movement's durability among the forest population cannot be explained without the allegiance of its women, who gathered the produce, carried the intelligence, fed the squads, and staffed the militias, and that allegiance, like the men's, was purchased by a mixture of protection, opportunity, discipline, and fear whose proportions varied by district and commander. An insurgency is held up by the people its histories photograph least, and the corridor's true infrastructure was a network of grandmothers who knew which paths the patrols used.

## The school and the clinic: the services of sovereignty

Parallel power in the strongholds was not only coercive and judicial; at its most ambitious it was provident, and its provisions repay attention because they reveal what the population asked of a state when a state finally asked them. The organisation's own documents describe a welfare apparatus in the base areas: primary schools staffed by its teachers and teaching, alongside literacy, the movement's account of the world; health workers trained in basic treatment and sanitation, carrying the first regular medical presence many hamlets had known; campaigns against the liquor stills and the sorcery accusations that scarred village life; and the enforcement of a rough egalitarianism in the distribution of seized grain and confiscated goods during the movement's redistributive phases. Researchers who visited the strongholds across the decades confirm the existence of these provisions while disputing their scale and quality, and the state's saturation strategy tacitly confirmed their importance by competing on exactly this ground, measuring its success in schools opened and clinics staffed behind the camps as proudly as in operations conducted. The competition in services is the cleanest evidence for the article's central claim about what sovereignty in the belt consisted in. Two authorities that agreed on nothing else agreed that allegiance followed provision, and conducted their war accordingly, in classrooms and dispensaries as much as in ambushes.

The provisions also expose the strongholds' ceiling, and the population's judgement of it, with a clarity the military record lacks. A movement school could teach a child to read the movement's primers; it could not issue a certificate the outside world honoured, train the nurse the clinic needed, or carry the brightest student one mile beyond the valley's rim. The organisation's health workers could dress wounds and dose fevers; the complicated case still required the district hospital, the road, and the money, all lying through the adversary's territory. Parents calculated accordingly, and the ethnographic record of the decline years is full of the calculation's consequences: children sent out to relatives beyond the belt for schooling, families quietly relocating toward the roads, a population voting with its feet for the wider world's credentials while continuing, often sincerely, to honour the authority that had first taught it to want them. Sovereignty that cannot certify its own services discovers, in the end, that it has been running a waiting room for the sovereignty that can, and the movement's educational success contained its supersession folded inside it, a pattern the founders, who despised the examination system their students died storming, would have appreciated in any movement but their own.

## Recruitment and its discontents: the young in the ranks

The movement's recruitment is the point where its account of itself and its critics' accounts diverge most bitterly, and the subject must be handled with the same attribution discipline as the violence. The organisation's documents describe an army of volunteers, drawn by conviction from the oppressed, admitted at an age its rules prescribe, and educated in its schools before bearing arms; its anniversary assessments celebrate the selflessness of successive generations of the forest's youth. The critical record, assembled by journalists, by the republic's child rights and human rights bodies, and in international reporting on children in armed conflict, describes a recruitment that shaded, in the stronghold years, from persuasion through social pressure to outright compulsion: village quotas expected of each community, families parting with a son or daughter as the price of the organisation's protection, and minors present in the militia and, in some accounts, in the squads themselves. Surrendered cadres' testimony supports elements of both descriptions, often in the same interview: the volunteer of sixteen who experienced enlistment as escape and emancipation, and the village meeting at which refusal was made unmistakably expensive. The state's record in the same theatre is not clean enough to simplify the judgement, its own forces having raised village defence structures that armed the young of the other side and its schools in the camps having militarised childhood from the opposite direction.

What the structural account contributes to this quarrel is an explanation of the pressure's source. An organisation whose strategy requires a standing force in country that offers a young person few other futures will recruit the young as reliably as water finds a slope, whatever its rulebook says, and an organisation losing its war will recruit them younger, as every belligerent in the comparative record has done when its adult manpower thinned. The moral responsibility for the practice rests with the commanders who ordered and normalised it, and the movement's literature, which finds space for extensive self criticism on extortion and on wrongful punishment, is at its most evasive on this subject, a silence the critics have rightly treated as an answer. For the population, recruitment was the tax that could not be receipted, the levy on the future itself, and the decline's literature suggests it was among the heaviest counts in the villages' audit when the audit finally turned.

## Justice in the strongholds: the people's court examined

The institution through which most inhabitants of the strongholds actually met the movement's sovereignty was neither the squad nor the committee but the people's court, the jan adalat of the movement's vocabulary, and it deserves the same unsentimental examination as the levy. In form it was an assembly: a dispute brought before the local committee or commander, the parties heard in the open, witnesses from the village, a judgement announced and executed by the organisation's authority. Its docket, reconstructed from journalists' and researchers' accounts, was overwhelmingly the docket of any rural jurisdiction starved of courts: land boundaries and inheritance among brothers, debt and its instruments, theft, assault, marriage and desertion, the sexual offences the state's police had never reached. Its advertised virtues were the virtues of presence: it sat in the village, charged nothing, spoke the language, decided in days, and enforced its decisions, against the background of a state judiciary whose nearest seat was a district town, whose language was a file, and whose timeline was a generation. Participants' testimony divides predictably on the institution's justice and unpredictably on its fairness in the particular case, and the same hearing could be, for the wife whose complaint was heard for the first time by any authority, an emancipation, and for the husband fined and shamed before his neighbours, the face of a tyranny.

The court's darker register is part of the record and belongs in any honest account. The same assembly form, convened against the alleged informer, the alleged sorcerer, the cadre accused of corruption or the villager accused of collaboration, could impose punishments up to death, without appeal, defence, or record, and the movement's critics, including its own internal reviewers, have catalogued cases in which accusation and interest coincided too conveniently for the verdict to be trusted. The institution thus concentrated the strongholds' whole ambiguity in one practice: a justice nearer, quicker, and more intelligible than the state's, wielded by an authority responsible to no one outside itself, in a society whose experience of the distant, procedural, appealable alternative was of a machine that ground the poor for fees and died of old age before deciding. Political scientists who study rebel governance comparatively place the case in a general finding: populations under dual power judge courts less by their jurisprudence than by their presence and predictability, and an authority that decides, even harshly, accumulates a legitimacy that an authority which never arrives cannot contest. The finding explains the strongholds' durability better than any doctrine, and it implicates the absent administration, whose empty chair at the village's disputes was the movement's first and most renewable resource.

## What the movement says about itself

Steelman discipline requires the movement's account of its own base and purposes to be given in its own terms before any criticism is weighed, and the account, drawn from its programmes, its anniversary assessments, and the writings of its leading committees, runs as follows. India, on this account, remains a semi colonial and semi feudal country in which the principal contradiction pits the broad masses against imperialism, feudalism, and comprador bureaucrat capital, and in which the countryside, where feudal relations and state absence coincide, is therefore the revolution's natural theatre. The Adivasi regions are not, in the movement's telling, an accidental refuge but the sharpest expression of the general condition: there feudal and pre feudal exactions survive in the forest department's tyranny, there imperialist and comprador capital reaches directly for the minerals, and there the people's war has accordingly built its most advanced base areas, governed through revolutionary people's committees that its documents present as the embryo of a new state. The movement describes its courts, its taxes, its schools and health work in the strongholds as the substance of an alternative power, its boycotts and strikes as the political education of the masses, and its military actions as the defence of that power against a state it characterises as the armed committee of the exploiting classes. Its setbacks it attributes to the enemy's superiority in arms and to its own errors of overreach or of insufficient political work, never to the strategy itself, and its persistence across five decades it offers as the strategy's vindication: parties that took the parliamentary road, its literature notes with satisfaction, have administered the old order; the people's war alone has refused it.

### What do the movement's own documents claim it is building?

A new democratic order in embryo: revolutionary people's committees exercising power in the base areas, courts, taxes, and schools of its own, an army built by stages from the people's militia, and beyond the strongholds a united front of classes against imperialism, feudalism, and comprador capital. The claim stands as the movement states it, to be weighed against the record.

Two silences inside that account deserve noting, because later critics from within the broader left have pressed on both. The first concerns agency. In the movement's telling, the Adivasi population appears as the masses, the raw material and beneficiary of a revolution whose theory, leadership, and historical script arrive from outside the forest, and the possibility that the population's own traditions of refusal might constitute a politics not reducible to the programme is one the documents entertain rarely. The second concerns the endpoint. The base areas of the documents are always in construction, the war always protracted, and the question of what political form the forest districts would take in a settlement short of total victory, a form their inhabitants might actually choose and revise, is deferred to a future that the strategy's own logic postpones indefinitely. Sympathetic anthropologists and journalists who lived in the strongholds have recorded both the genuine popularity of particular commanders and services and the population's weariness with a war without a visible terminus, and the movement's literature, to a degree unusual among insurgencies, has occasionally printed self criticism acknowledging extortionate levies, wrongful punishments, and the alienation produced by both, while attributing them to the movement's immaturity rather than to its structure. The reader should hold the account whole: a self understanding of real coherence and explanatory power over the movement's own choices, which explains the forest base as the revolution's chosen ground, and which the structural explanation of this article accepts as an account of intention while disputing as an account of cause.

## What security analysis says about the same ground

The opposing interested literature, produced by police and intelligence professionals, by the research institutes attached to the security establishment, and by the journalists who work that beat, explains the same map with a different centre of gravity. In the security account, the movement is at bottom an armed organisation sustained by extortion: a levy economy imposed on contractors, mining and infrastructure firms, forest produce traders, and government works, which finances a military structure whose ideological superstructure rationalises what is substantially a system of armed rent collection in districts where the state cannot protect commerce. Its tribal base, on this reading, is a compound of coercion and the absence of alternatives, its people's courts a theatre of intimidation, its taxation indistinguishable from the protection rackets of organised crime, and its persistence a function of terrain, of interstate seams, and of the political constraints that prevent the state from applying its full strength. The remedy that follows from the account is the remedy the state has in fact pursued in waves: security led clearing of strongholds, holding of cleared ground by permanent camps, and the delivery of administration and development behind the security frontier, in the classic sequence of counter insurgency doctrine. Senior officials stated the designation plainly in the period of the merger: the organisation, they said, constituted the gravest internal security challenge before the country, a judgement offered as the premise for the deployments that followed and recorded in this article as a fact about official classification.

The security account has genuine strengths that fairness requires registering. The levy economy is not an invention; contractors paid, firms calculated the levy as a cost of operating, and the movement's own self criticisms concede extortionate exactions by its units. Coercion is not an invention either: villagers who defied the organisation faced punishments that its documents justify and its victims remember, and the line between allegiance and prudence, in a village visited by armed men of two sovereignties, is one the population itself draws with care. Where the account weakens, as sympathetic and hostile analysts alike have noted, is at the question of origins and of variation. Extortion explains how an armed organisation finances itself once established; it does not explain why establishment succeeded in the forest districts and failed in the plains districts where commerce was richer and rackets more lucrative, nor why the organisation's arrival in a district so reliably followed the population's collisions with forest law and displacement rather than preceding them. The account's own practitioners, in their more reflective writings, concede the point by reaching for the vocabulary of governance vacuum and unaddressed grievance to explain recruitment, which is the structural explanation of this article arriving by another road, wearing a uniform. The two interested literatures thus converge, at their most honest, on the ground this article occupies: the movement lives where dispossession is organised and administration is not. They diverge on what follows, the one prescribing the deepening of the war, the other its indefinite prolongation, and the divergence is a political choice that no map can make for the reader.

## The district town: the movement's penumbra

Between the stronghold and the state capital lies the district town, and no map of the conflict is complete without it, because the town was where the two sovereignties met, traded, and spied on one another in civilian dress. The town held the courts where the movement's arrested were tried and its sympathisers defended by a small, brave profession of local lawyers; the press clubs and newspaper stringers through whom the war's facts were negotiated into print; the traders who stocked the shops the squads' families used and paid the levies the contracts concealed; the hospitals to which both sides' wounded were carried on different nights; and the colleges whose students supplied, in every generation, a trickle of recruits and a larger trickle of the movement's urban intelligence. The state's presence in the town was of a different order from its absence in the forest, continuous, staffed, and watchful, and its instruments there were legal as much as military: arrests of alleged couriers and sympathisers under the security legislation, surveillance of the professions named above, and periodic, widely publicised detentions of intellectuals accused of fronting for the banned organisation, episodes that the republic's civil liberties organisations documented and contested as they occurred. The town thus hosted a second, quieter conflict over the boundaries of legality itself, in which the question was whether association, advocacy, and professional service could be policed as warfare by other means, a question the courts answered variously across the decades and the belt's lawyers answered daily at personal risk.

For the movement, the town was simultaneously lifeline and trap. Its supply lines ran through the town's markets, its communications through the town's networks, its wounded and its funds and its printed word all passed along the same streets the police watched, and the organisation's urban apparatus, couriers, safe houses, fund collectors, lived by rules of caution that its forest cadres found contemptible and its own survival required. Arrests in the towns periodically decapitated networks the forest could not quickly replace, since the skills involved, languages, accounts, the management of false identities, were the educated strata's contribution and the educated strata were thin on the ground in the belt. The two geographies of the movement, forest and town, thus depended on the two populations its sociology joined: the Adivasi base that sheltered the squads and the educated intermediaries who connected the shelter to the world. The state's strategists understood the junction as clearly as the movement did, and the pressure applied at the town end of the line, legal, financial, and carceral, belongs in any account of the decline alongside the camps in the forest, as the tightening of the same vice at its other jaw.

## The parliamentary criticism: substitution and its price

Turn now to the criticisms levelled from within Marxism, which the brief for this article rightly treats as the most consequential, since they were made by people who shared the movement's goals and much of its analysis and rejected its method from inside the tradition. The parliamentary parties' case, argued from 1967 onward in party journals, in the polemics surrounding the split, and in the considered histories their intellectuals later wrote, rests on the charge of substitution. The rebels, on this charge, substituted the armed deed of the few for the organised activity of the many: the squad for the union, the assassination for the strike, the liberated zone proclaimed by a committee for the slow conquest of power in society by classes acting for themselves. Substitution, the critics argued, is not a shortcut to the same destination but a road to a different one, because a movement's method forms its membership, its leadership, and its habits of rule; an organisation built on clandestine violence will reproduce those habits in any power it wins, and will meanwhile have traded away the one asset the working population actually possesses, its numbers, organised openly and defensibly, for the one asset the state possesses in superabundance, firepower. The prediction the critics attached to the charge was tested early and, in their telling, confirmed: the first organisation destroyed within three years, its cadres dead or imprisoned, the villages that sheltered it punished, and the parliamentary parties left to rebuild among a traumatised peasantry the mass organisations the rebels had dissolved as reformist. The fuller history of the accusing parties, and of the state ministries through which they pursued their alternative, belongs to the series account of [the Indian communist movement's own past](/2017/12/15/communist-movement-india-history/) and to the guide to [Marxism in India](/2017/12/01/marxism-in-india-complete-guide/), where the reader can weigh the accusers' own record with the same severity.

### Why did the parliamentary left call the method substitutionist?

Because it replaced the organised activity of the classes themselves, unions, peasant associations, strikes, and elections, with the armed deeds of a small clandestine minority acting in their name. The critics argued that method forms outcome: a movement built on secrecy and violence would reproduce both in power, while surrendering the population's one real asset, its organised numbers.

To the strategic charge the parliamentary critics added a political one that cut deeper in the Indian context. Armed struggle against the Indian republic, they argued, objectively served the republic's most authoritarian tendencies: it furnished the pretext for emergency powers, for the militarisation of administration in the affected districts, for the suspension of the very civil liberties on which all mass organisation, including the rebels' own sympathisers, depended, and it divided the left at every juncture where united action was available. The rebels' boycott of elections, maintained as a point of honour, abandoned the parliamentary arena to the parties of the right and centre, while the rebels' attacks on rival left workers, a recurring feature of the first phase and a sporadic one later, made the movement, in the critics' phrase, an enemy of the movement. There is a harder edge beneath the polemic that a fair account should not blunt. The parliamentary parties were not neutral observers of the repression that destroyed the first organisation. In Bengal it was their own government that ordered the suppression of 1967, their own police who conducted much of it, and their own publicists who defended the firing on the villagers of Prasad Jote as the defence of order. The criticism of substitution therefore comes to the reader from hands that are not clean of the conflict it describes, and the rebels' counter charge, that the accusers had themselves become an arm of the state they claimed to oppose, is part of the record and was believed, on the evidence of that spring, by many who never joined a squad. Both charges can be true. The Indian left's tragedy in this period is that they were.

## The criticism from within: the founder's line judged by his own house

Sharper than the parliamentary parties' polemic, because it could not be dismissed as the complaint of rivals, was the criticism that arose inside the revolutionary camp itself, much of it while the founder lived and the rest across the decades of reconstruction. Its core was a charge about method drawn from the tradition's own canon. Maoist doctrine, as the internal critics read it, subordinates military action to political work absolutely: the party builds mass organisations, the mass organisations generate the struggle's demands and its recruits, the army grows out of that soil and remains its instrument, and the killing of individuals, divorced from a movement of the people concerned, is the definition of the adventurism the canon condemns. The annihilation line, on this reading, was not an application of Maoism but its inversion, a theory of terror as propaganda in the lineage of nineteenth century conspiratorial movements, dressed in quotations. Kanu Sanyal, who had organised the Terai rising itself and spent the campaign's years in prison, emerged to make the criticism in the most damaging form available to it: as the report of a founder upon the founding. His assessments, circulated within the movement and later published, held that the rising he had helped lead was a peasant struggle for land which the annihilation doctrine had diverted and destroyed, that the founder's cult had replaced collective leadership, and that the party's contempt for mass organisation had left the peasantry defenceless before the retaliation the squads provoked. Regional committees that had resisted the line at the time, in Andhra Pradesh and elsewhere, supplied their own testimony to the same effect: where annihilation was practised without mass cover, the organisation died; where organisers quietly subordinated the line to land struggle and association building, something survived to be rebuilt.

The successor organisations absorbed the criticism so completely that it is now difficult to find, in the literature of any major stream including the consolidated armed organisation, a defence of annihilation as method. The merged organisation's documents treat the founding period's errors under the rubric of left adventurism, credit the founder with the founding while burying his doctrine, and ground their own practice in the mass organisations and protracted construction the critics had demanded. Historians of the movement have noted the irony without needing to underline it: the internal criticism won the argument inside the family so thoroughly that the family's largest heir is built on the critic's premises, while continuing to display the founder's portrait. What the internal criticism did not settle, because it shared the premise it would have needed to question, was whether armed struggle itself, however correctly subordinated to mass work, was a road open in the Indian conditions. On that question the critics of the annihilation line divided again into those who concluded, with the electoral stream, that it was not, and those who concluded, with the forest organisation, that it was, provided the ground was right. The geography of this article suggests that the second answer was half right in a sense neither party intended: the ground decided, and where the ground was right, the organisation endured; endurance, however, is not victory, and the distance between them is the measure the final sections take.

## What the cadres read: the canon underground

The organisation's inner life ran on texts, and the canon that formed its cadres across five decades is worth inventorying, because a movement's reading list is the truest map of its intentions. At the centre stood the founder's eight documents, preserved by every stream including those that buried his method, read as the family's birth certificate and argued over as scripture is argued over, for what they authorised rather than for what they said. Around them stood the Chinese canon in translation, the military and philosophical writings of the Chinese revolution's founder, the Yenan texts on protracted war and mass work, from which the forest organisation drew its operating system and its confidence that time was a weapon the weak could wield. A third shelf held the Indian materials proper: the programmes and self criticisms of the successive parties, the documents of the splits, the anniversary assessments in which the organisation audited itself for its own members, a literature of self examination whose existence distinguishes this family from most insurgencies and whose candour, within its limits, the researcher learns to use. And a fourth, unofficial shelf grew in the camps and safe houses: the novels and poems of the movement's own writers, the prison memoirs, the histories of Telangana and the Terai written by participants, through which a recruit in the forest learned the family's past as story rather than as line.

Study was organised with a seriousness that outsiders consistently underestimate. Political classes in the strongholds followed a curriculum; new recruits were taught to read where necessary, in the movement's own primers, and promoted through stages of education that mirrored the state's schools the organisation had displaced; commanders were examined, in effect, by the quality of their teaching. The purposes were practical as much as ideological. A clandestine organisation cannot supervise its distant units; it can only form them, and the canon was its instrument of remote government, the means by which a committee in one valley assured itself that a squad in another would tax, judge, and fight in the approved manner. Where the canon failed of its purpose, in the levy that became extortion and the court that became a settling of scores, the failure was one of formation's reach, and the organisation's own remedies, rectification campaigns, criticism sessions, the rotation of cadres, were educational in form. To describe the movement as an army is accurate and insufficient. It was also a school system with an army attached, and the description explains both its resilience, schools reproduce, and its rigidity, curricula resist revision, better than either of its interested literatures allows.

## The dead and their keepers: memory in the villages

The war's longest occupation is of memory, and the villages of the belt keep its archive in forms no ministry administers. The movement built its dead into the landscape deliberately: martyrs' columns at village edges, memorial weeks in the stronghold calendar, the names of fallen commanders given to schools, squads, and children, a liturgy designed to convert loss into continuity and to instruct the living in the price list of allegiance. The state's dead received their own official memorialisation elsewhere, in police lines and capital ceremonies from which the belt's population was largely absent. Between the two liturgies stand the uncommemorated, the villagers killed as informers, as collaborators, as bystanders, as examples, whose deaths neither sovereignty could afford to honour and whose families keep the count in the only register left to them, in genealogy and in silence. Oral historians entering the belt in the decline years found this third archive the most carefully guarded and the most consequential, since it is the one the next generation inherits without mediation: a map of the war drawn by its costs, in which the movement and the state appear not as doctrines but as weathers that passed over particular houses.

The politics of this memory are still working themselves out and belong, by this article's discipline, to the record rather than to prophecy. What can be said is that the belt's reckoning with its half century will not resemble the literatures that fought over it. The population's testimony, as it accumulates, distributes praise and blame with a specificity that embarrasses every general account, honouring the commander who raised the tendu rate and cursing his successor who taxed the harvest, crediting the officer who built the road and remembering the one who burned the granary, and it resists, with the authority of the bereaved, the conversion of its dead into any party's capital. A reference work can do the population no greater service than to preserve that specificity against the simplifications of both interested literatures, which is a further reason this article has declined their arithmetic and their adjectives alike. The corridor's final historian is already alive and was probably born in it. The archive she will judge by is being kept now, in the villages, in the only form that has never yet been successfully falsified in this conflict: the memory of who did what, house by house.

## Generations: the cadres of each decade

Read across its half century, the movement is a relay of generations, each formed by a different India and each handing the next a different organisation. The founders were formed by the 1940s and 1950s: by Telangana and Tebhaga as living memory, by the Chinese victory as contemporary news, by a parliamentary left they experienced as a promise betrayed in their own lifetimes, and they carried into the revolt the era's confidence that history's direction was known and only its tempo disputed. The second generation, the prisoners and rebuilders of the 1970s, was formed by defeat: by the jails, by the Emergency's demonstration that the republic could suspend itself, by the spectacle of the parent parties administering states, and it built accordingly, suspicious of charisma, devoted to structure, patient in a way the founders never were. The third generation, which built the forest organisation and the merger, was formed by the 1980s and 1990s: by the Andhra construction's rise and destruction, by liberalisation's transformation of the economy they had analysed as stagnant, by the arrival of the mining frontier in the forest as the central fact of their constituents' lives, and it produced the movement's most capable military organisation and its most complete adaptation to the geography this article describes. A fourth generation, the forest born cadres of the consolidation and decline, differed from all its predecessors in kind: recruited from the population itself, educated if at all in the movement's own schools and the state's new ones, fighting not for a programme encountered in books but for the valley they were standing in, and correspondingly less legible to the doctrine that nominally commanded them.

The relay explains the movement's strange combination of continuity and transformation. The texts descended intact; the hands changed utterly. A founder of 1969, transported to a stronghold committee of the consolidation period, would have recognised the portraits, the vocabulary, the martyrs' calendar, and almost nothing of the concerns: tendu rates and mining leases where the programme spoke of landlordism, tribal custom where it spoke of the peasantry, a war of position measured in road contracts where it promised the encirclement of cities. The organisation's own historians treat this as the doctrine's application to concrete conditions, as their theory instructs. The structural account offered here suggests the ruder reading: that the organisation was remade by each countryside it survived in, and that the doctrine's continuity is the continuity of a flag under which successive populations fought successive wars. Both readings can be held together, and the strongest version of each requires the other: the flag mattered, since without it the fragments would have dissolved into local revolt and banditry as a hundred forest risings before them had done, and the ground mattered more, since it decided which of the flag's bearers would still exist to argue about its meaning.

## The movement in print and song: the making of a usable past

A movement that lost most of its battles survived in part by winning the archive, and its cultural production deserves record as a mechanism of persistence rather than as decoration. From the first phase onward, the family generated an enormous literature of itself: party journals in a dozen languages, theoretical polemics conducted at a level of textual intensity that exhausted participants and fascinates historians, prison memoirs, martyrologies, and, most durably, song. The cultural fronts of the Andhra and forest movements produced singer poets whose ballads of land, martyrdom, and the coming storm travelled the belt by cassette and memory, performing the movement's history to populations the printed word could not reach, and the martyrs' columns and memorial weeks of the strongholds converted the dead into a calendar. Historians of the family have noted the disproportion: few Indian political movements of comparable military weight have left so dense a written and sung record, and the disproportion is itself explained by the personnel, a movement founded by students and schoolteachers, for whom the pamphlet and the song were native weapons, and staffed in prison by men and women with years to write. The record's existence shaped the afterlife. Every fragment's claim to the inheritance could be documented, every schism argued from chapter and verse, and a young recruit in the forest in the movement's fifth decade could be handed, alongside the rifle, a library in which his squad's presence in that valley had already been narrated as destiny.

The same cultural density armed the movement's critics and the state's. The parliamentary parties' journals preserved the polemic against substitution in the same amber; the security establishment's institutes mined the family's literature for order of battle and intention; and the Indian republic's wider culture absorbed the movement as image, in novels, films, and the figure of the forest rebel that recurs in the country's imagination detached from any programme. Researchers should treat the whole archive with the discipline applied throughout this article: it is the richest source on the subject and the least innocent, written by participants to recruit, to accuse, or to justify, and its silences, on extortion, on punishment, on the population's weariness, are as instructive as its testimonies. The songs, in particular, repay the sceptic's attention, since what a movement sings to the people it claims is evidence of what it believes those people want to hear, and the belt's repertoire, heavy with land, forest, and the dignity of labour, and light with the vocabulary of the founding programme, is the geography of dispossession set to music by its own subjects.

## The strongest objection, stated whole

The brief for this article requires the strongest objection to the movement to be stated at full strength, attributed to the parliamentary communist criticism that has pressed it longest, and it should be given without softening. It runs as follows. A movement that abandoned mass organisation for armed action has, over more than half a century, produced no advance whatever toward its stated objective: no territory permanently held, no state power approached, no transformation of the Indian order begun. What it has produced, in the districts where it operates, is a half century of violence visited on the population it claims to serve: its own killings of landlords, officials, informers real and alleged, and political opponents; the retaliation of the security forces drawn down upon the villages by its presence; the militarisation of administration across the belt, with its camps, its surveillance, and its suspension of ordinary legality; and the conversion of every development question, every school and road and clinic, into a counters in a war neither side can win. The grievances to which the movement responds are real, the objection concedes, and the state's record in the forest is disgraceful; but a method is judged by its fruits, and the fruits of this method, audited across fifty years, are a population exposed to sustained violence from both directions and a politics in which the gun has crowded out every other instrument the oppressed might have used. On the objection's conclusion, the strategic record does not merely disappoint the founding hope. It falsifies it.

## What the presence secured, and what must be conceded

The answer developed in the movement's defence, by its sympathisers among scholars and journalists and in the testimony of the forest population itself, does not dispute the audit's arithmetic so much as its baseline. The comparison, the answer runs, is not between the movement's districts and an imaginary district in which peaceful organisation flourished unhindered; it is between the movement's presence and the actual alternative the record shows, which was unimpeded dispossession administered by forest law, contractor, and mine, in districts where no union, party, or newspaper had ever sustained a presence and where the state's services were a rumour. Against that baseline, the answer enters the movement's concrete credits. In district after district, its arrival raised the price of tendu leaf and the wages of forest work, ended specific exactions by officials and contractors that no petition had shifted, restored to villages land and produce from which they had been excluded, and gave a population accustomed to being administered a weapon with which its consent had, for the first time, to be negotiated. Researchers unsympathetic to the movement's doctrine have recorded these credits in their fieldwork, and the population's own judgements, gathered by journalists across the decades, return repeatedly to the language of dignity: that before the squads came, no one in authority had ever been obliged to fear the villager, and afterward, everyone was. That entry in the ledger is real, and an account of the movement that omits it has omitted the reason the squads were fed.

The concession must then be made as plainly as the brief requires. Bargaining power is not a road to the movement's stated objective, and the credits entered above do not accumulate toward it; they are local, defensive, and reversible, purchased at the price recorded in the objection, and they expire with the organisation that enforces them. The objection's assessment of the strategic record, that five decades of armed struggle have not advanced the project of revolution in India by any measure the movement itself proposed, is, on the evidence available to this article, difficult to dispute, and the attempts to dispute it in the movement's literature invariably change the measure: from victory to survival, from survival to presence, from presence to the keeping of a flame. A flame kept for fifty years in the forest, at the cost the forest paid, is a fact requiring explanation rather than celebration, and the explanation is the one this article has built: the movement endured not because its strategy was working but because the conditions that received it, dispossession organised by law and administration absent by default, continued to be reproduced by the ordinary operation of the state and the market in the mineral belt. It was sustained by its enemy's conduct. The day that conduct changes, in either direction, toward genuine administration or toward overwhelming force, the sustaining conditions alter, and the movement's own doctrine becomes what it was at the founding: a text in search of a countryside.

## Interludes: negotiation and the price of talks

Twice in the movement's later history the war paused at a table, and the pauses illuminate the structure as sharply as the fighting. In Andhra Pradesh in the middle of the 2000s, a state government elected partly on the promise of dialogue held direct talks with the armed organisation's representatives, under a ceasefire whose months saw the squads move openly, hold public meetings, and recruit, while the police held their fire and their intelligence work continued, each side using the interlude to prepare the resumption both expected. The talks foundered on the preconditions each side could not survive accepting, the organisation's demand to keep its arms, the state's demand that arms precede politics, and the war resumed at a higher intensity, with the organisation now better known to its enemy and the population instructed, once again, in the price of visibility. A second interlude, in West Bengal at the end of the following decade, ran through intermediaries appointed by a new government and ended in the death of the organisation's principal spokesman in an encounter whose circumstances remain disputed between the state's account and the movement's, after which the channel closed and has not reopened on comparable terms. This article records the episodes structurally and names no participants, in keeping with its rule for the movement's later decades; the pattern is what concerns the argument.

The pattern is that negotiation, for an organisation of this kind, is not an alternative to its structural situation but an episode within it. A movement sustained by conditions rather than by the balance of forces cannot trade its arms for concessions, because the arms are the only asset its counterparts respect and the conditions that produced it lie outside any agenda a state government can sign: no negotiation in Hyderabad or Kolkata could repeal the forest settlement, staff the belt's clinics, or unclassify a million plots. The talks therefore functioned, for both sides, as operations by other means, reconnaissance, recruitment, legitimation, rest, and their failure was overdetermined in a way the participants' memoirs, blaming bad faith in the particular, consistently understate. The comparative literature on insurgent negotiation adds the melancholy corollary: movements of this type settle, when they settle at all, not by agreement on their programme but by the transformation of their organisation into something that can survive peace, as the electoral stream of this same family had demonstrated a generation earlier by the simple expedient of becoming a party. The forest organisation examined the precedent and declined it, in documents that treat the electoral stream's survival as a fate worse than the war, and the decline that followed is, in part, the price of the refusal.

## The republic's conscience: fact finders in the corridor

A third cast of characters worked the belt across all its phases and left the record on which much of this article silently rests: the civil liberties and democratic rights organisations of the republic itself, whose volunteers, lawyers, teachers, doctors, and journalists from the district towns and the capitals, travelled into the conflict's districts after its worst episodes and published what they found. Their reports, issued over decades by organisations of the open left and centre that agreed with the movement about little else, documented the state's encounters and custodial deaths in the first phase, the senas' massacres and the movement's punishments in the plains war, the militia experiment and the camps in the saturation years, with a consistency of method that made them the closest thing the conflict possessed to a referee: they named the dead of both sovereignties, criticised the movement's courts and the state's encounters in the same paragraph, and were rewarded with the suspicion of both, the movement reading them as the state's soft face, the state periodically prosecuting their members as the movement's. The parliamentary critics quoted their findings against the rebels; the rebels' sympathisers quoted the same findings against the state; and the findings' survival in print, unretracted across fifty years of hostile scrutiny, is the reason a writer can describe this conflict's violence at all without merely ventriloquising a belligerent.

Their presence also marks a difference between the Indian case and the insurgencies with which it is compared, a difference the movement's literature acknowledges with backhanded respect. The republic the movement sought to overthrow contained, inside itself, institutions that investigated the war's conduct in the war's own districts and published the results in the war's own language, at real risk and without permission. The movement's strongholds contained no equivalent: no organisation within them could publish an audit of the people's courts and survive the week. The asymmetry does not settle the argument between the state and its enemies, whose crimes the fact finders recorded without fear of either. It does complicate the movement's claim to embody the people's power against a people's republic that, however imperfectly and however often in the breach, permitted its own conduct to be indicted in its own courts of print. The fact finders' archive, finally, is where the population of the belt speaks with the least mediation available anywhere in the record: in testimony taken down by visitors who had come to listen rather than to recruit or to count, and the future historian of the corridor will build on their boxes of reports before building on anyone's memoirs.

## The law of proscription: bans, lists, and the designation's career

The official designation that runs through this article had a legal career of its own, and tracing it clarifies what the state's classification did besides describe. The armed organisations of the family were banned, in successive incarnations, under the republic's security legislation, the consolidated organisation being proscribed in the period of its formation's aftermath as a terrorist organisation under the amended unlawful activities law, a listing that criminalised membership, support, and association in terms wide enough to reach the courier, the donor, the pamphleteer, and, in the prosecution's periodic ambition, the sympathetic writer. Proscription performed several functions at once for the state that wielded it. It converted a political and administrative problem into a legal status, relieving officials of the burden of characterising the movement afresh in every proceeding. It unlocked the security legislation's procedural arsenal, extended detention, reversed bail presumptions, special courts, in the affected districts. And it drew the line, in law, between the population and the organisation, so that the state's dealings with the belt's inhabitants could proceed under a classification that presumed the distinction it was the war's object to create. The movement treated the ban as a certificate, proof in its own literature that the state feared it as no election could make it feared, and continued its existence in the proscribed form with the martyrology enhanced.

The designation's career in the courts and the press complicates any simple account of it as mere repression or mere description. Judges periodically narrowed the law's reach, holding that membership of a banned organisation, without more, could not sustain a conviction, in rulings the legislature then moved to answer; civil liberties litigators fought the listing's penumbra case by case, defending the lawyer, the doctor, the academic, and the journalist against the charge of association; and the republic's public argument about the ban became, in itself, one of the conflict's central theatres, a proxy war over the question the designation was designed to settle, whether the organisation was a politics to be answered or a crime to be extirpated. This article has maintained its rule throughout: the designation is stated as a fact about designation, its legal consequences described, its justice left to the reader's weighing of the record assembled here. The rule is not evasion. It is the recognition that a reference work's first obligation, on a subject where the classification is itself a weapon in the conflict it classifies, is to hand the reader the record unclassified, and let the verdict form where verdicts belong, in a mind the article has equipped rather than conscripted.

## The state replies: from suppression to saturation

The other protagonist of this history learned as slowly and as expensively as the movement, and the sequence of its methods is the decline's necessary background. The first reply, in 1967 and after, was suppression pure and simple: police action, mass arrest, and the destruction of the organisation in its birthplace, a method adequate to a plains rising of a few hundred and thereafter elevated into doctrine. The second reply, generalised through the 1970s and 1980s, was containment by neglect: the forest districts left to the forest department and the occasional operation, the movement's growth there treated as a law and order problem of the states concerned, and the belt's administration continuing in the extractive pattern that had produced the grievance, a phase in which the state's absence and the movement's construction proceeded as mirror images. The third reply, pioneered in Andhra Pradesh and later generalised, was the specialised campaign: dedicated intelligence, commando forces raised for forest war, rewards and surrender policies aimed at the cadre structure, and political leadership that made the conflict a test of governance. The fourth, which the consolidated period forced into being, was saturation: permanent camps planted inside the strongholds rather than expeditions into them, roads and telecommunications driven behind the camps, the administrative apparatus, schools, health posts, ration distribution, land record camps, following the security frontier in deliberate sequence, and development funds concentrated on the contested districts with a publicity that announced the strategy's premise, that the war would be won by administration or not at all.

### What did holding ground require that clearing it did not?

Permanence, and the budget and patience permanence consumes. A clearing operation visits; a camp, a road, a functioning school and a land record that matches the ground remain, converting the state's presence from an event the population waits out into a condition it calculates with. Holding also required the state's services to outperform its reputation, the harder campaign.

Each phase of the reply carried costs that belong in the same ledger as the movement's, entered with the same restraint. Saturation militarised the civic life of the belt for a generation: villages between two armed sovereignties, young men recruited as informers or special police by one side and targeted as such by the other, and, in one state, a government backed civilian militia raised against the movement, later disbanded after the courts intervened, whose depredations against the tribal population were documented by the republic's own human rights institutions. Deaths in encounters whose circumstances were disputed, custodial deaths, and the displacement of villages caught in the operations form part of the record the state's own commissions and courts have partially acknowledged. This article states the pattern without the arithmetic its evidence discipline forbids, and notes the symmetry the pattern establishes: the state's violence, like the movement's, was most lawless where its presence was least institutional, in the expedition rather than the office, which is the geography thesis read from the other side. Administration, the record suggests, disciplines its possessors as well as its subjects, and its absence corrupts both sovereignties impartially.

## Surrender and rehabilitation: the exit door

Every long insurgency develops an exit door, and the traffic through it is evidence about the organisation that neither its propaganda nor its enemies' can supply. The state's surrender and rehabilitation policies, elaborated across the decades in the affected states, offered the cadre a package: safety from prosecution for most offences, a payment, vocational training or a plot, and a public ceremony in which the surrendered weapon was photographed and the former enemy exhibited as the policy's proof. The movement treated surrender as treason and, in its strong periods, punished or threatened the families of those who took the door, a deterrence that itself testified to the door's importance. The profiles of the surrendered, assembled in journalists' and researchers' accounts, resist every simple reading. Some were exhausted villagers of the militia base for whom the war had become unliveable; some were mid level commanders carrying the intelligence that later undid their districts; some were the disillusioned of the levy economy's corruption and the punishment regime's injustices; and a residue, the state periodically discovered, were villagers surrendered by local arrangement as quotas, innocents exhibited in the ceremony of a war both sides were performing for audiences elsewhere. The exit door's significance for the structural argument is its confirmation from the reverse direction. Surrenders clustered not where doctrine was best refuted but where the configuration broke first: the district where the camp arrived and the road followed, where the organisation's taxes rose as its protections thinned, where the population's audit turned. Men and women rarely surrender an idea. They surrender a situation that has stopped working, and the geography of the surrenders, year by year, is the decline's most honest map, drawn by its participants with their feet.

## Decline: the sustaining conditions weaken

The movement's decline from the levels of its consolidated period is a fact no party to the conflict disputes, though every party explains it to its own advantage, and this article describes it in the general terms the evidence discipline requires: an armed organisation whose territorial presence contracted, whose military capacity diminished, whose recruitment thinned, and whose strongholds were progressively entered by the state's forces and administration. The causes, weighed in the literature across the spectrum, cluster into four. The first is the maturation of the state's response: specialised forces trained for forest war, intelligence penetration fed by surrendered cadres, permanent camps planted inside former strongholds, and the slow extension of roads, communications, and administration behind the security frontier, so that ground once cleared was increasingly held. The second is attrition of leadership: the generation that built the consolidated organisation aged, was killed, captured, or surrendered in a steady drain that clandestine structures replace slowly, since authority in them rests on reputation accumulated over decades. The third is the changing village: roads and mobile networks dissolved the isolation on which parallel power rested, migration carried the young to cities and brick kilns where the organisation could not follow, and a generation grew up for whom the movement was a feature of their parents' world, to be used, evaded, or joined with a calculation their grandparents had not needed to make. The fourth is the movement's own contribution to its decline: levies that hardened into extortion in the eyes of those who paid them, punishments that alienated the families they fell on, obstruction of the schools and works the population wanted, and the recruitment of the very young, each a withdrawal from the account of consent on which the whole structure rested.

### Why did decline follow from the geography that had sustained the movement?

Because the geography was a set of conditions, not a possession. Roads, camps, communications, and administration withdrawn the thinness; intelligence and attrition thinned the organisation that thin country sheltered; and the population's changing calculations withdrew the consent that grievance alone could no longer command. What terrain gave, the alteration of terrain and of the state's presence took back.

Read the four causes together and a pattern emerges that returns the article to its thesis from the far side. Not one of the four is doctrinal. No rival idea defeated the movement in the forest; no mass conversion to another politics emptied its ranks; the parliamentary parties it despised did not out organise it in the belt. The conditions changed. The state, at ruinous expense and over decades, altered the administrative map that had been the movement's first condition, while the movement, by the exactions its own maintenance required, altered the second condition, the population's judgement that its presence was preferable to the alternatives. The geography of dispossession explains the decline with the same variables that explain the persistence, run in reverse, which is the proper test of a structural explanation: it should account for the fall with the machinery that accounted for the endurance, and it does. What the explanation also predicts, and what the record in the plains already showed, is the asymmetry of the ending. An organisation so sustained does not vanish when conditions turn; it contracts toward the pockets where the conditions survive most completely, the deepest forest, the least administered borderlands, and persists there at a level set by the residue of its causes. Reports of its extinction and reports of its resurgence have alternated in the press for decades, each mistaking the residue for the whole or the contraction for the end, and the structural account suggests both will continue to be written until the underlying configuration, forest, minerals, thin administration, contested rights, is itself transformed.

## The informer: intelligence and the corrosion of trust

Beneath the war of squads and camps ran a quieter war that determined much of the louder one's outcome and left the deepest marks on the society that hosted both: the war for information. An organisation embedded in a population is visible to that population in every particular, its routes, its caches, its sympathisers, its commanders' habits, and its survival therefore depends on the population's collective decision, renewed daily, about what to report and to whom. The state's intelligence effort in the belt was, in essence, a decades long campaign to purchase, coerce, or cultivate cracks in that decision: informers recruited from the disaffected, the indebted, the arrested and turned, the surrendered whose knowledge arrived with their persons. The movement's counter effort was a regime of compartmentation and deterrence whose harshest instruments fell on the suspected informer, a category elastic under fear and productive of the movement's most indefensible acts, the killing of villagers on suspicion their accusers could not always substantiate and the organisation's own reviews could not always acquit. Between the two campaigns stood the village, conducting the most sophisticated calculation in the whole conflict: calibrating what each side knew, feeding each the information that kept it safest, and developing the arts of strategic ignorance to a refinement that outside observers, journalists collecting colour, officials collecting reports, routinely mistook for simplicity.

The corrosion this war worked on the social fabric outlasted its battles. Communities remember who was suspected and who informed long after the squads and the camps have gone, and the suspicion, once generalised, dissolves the associational life on which any politics, revolutionary or electoral, must build. Researchers who entered the belt in the decline years record villages reluctant to organise for any purpose, a demobilisation deeper than defeat, and attribute it less to exhaustion than to the learned prudence of people who discovered that organisation attracts information's predators from both directions. The movement's historians and the state's alike prefer their wars kinetic. The record suggests the decisive one was epistemic, and that its victor, in village after village, was neither sovereignty but silence, a outcome neither the doctrine nor the designation had budgeted for and both, in their different idioms, had produced.

## Elections under dual power: the boycott on the ground

One institution reveals the strongholds' real constitution more clearly than any battle report: the election, which the consolidated organisation boycotted in doctrine and policed in practice. In districts of durable presence, the conduct of a poll became a negotiation among three parties, the administration determined to hold it as proof of sovereignty, the candidates of the open parties calculating the price of campaigning, and the organisation deciding, village by village, whether its boycott would be announced, enforced, or quietly suspended. The recorded outcomes across the belt's decades are correspondingly various: polling stations that recorded derisory turnout under an enforced boycott, candidates who campaigned only after understandings with the local committee, officials abducted and released as signals, and, in places, an organisation that taxed the election's logistics while permitting the ritual to proceed. Political scientists who studied voting in the belt drew the conclusion the pattern teaches: sovereignty there was not contested in the manner of two armies but layered, the republic's forms operating on the surface of a deeper jurisdiction, and the villager's political skill consisted in addressing each layer in its own idiom. The boycott, examined at this grain, also exposes the doctrine's limit in the movement's own terms. A strategy of encirclement that cannot prevent the encircled state from conducting its central ritual in the movement's heartland, and that must intermittently license the ritual to preserve its other arrangements, has conceded the population a political life outside the revolution's script, and the population, voting or abstaining for reasons of its own, has never been as unanimous in its assigned role as either literature requires.

## The word itself: how the label travelled

The name outlived every organisation that bore it and acquired a career of its own in the republic's political language, which a complete account should register. In official usage the word became administrative: a category in ministry reporting, a classification that triggered deployments, funds, and special legislation, so that districts acquired an interest in the label's application and others in its removal, and the word's extension at any moment reflected bureaucratic as much as military facts. In journalistic usage it became a compression device, gathering every armed left formation, and occasionally formations with no genealogy in 1967 at all, into a single intelligible threat. In the usage of the parliamentary right and centre it became an epithet, applied to dissent well beyond the family's borders, to striking workers, protesting students, and inconvenient journalists, a career the word shares with the vocabulary of sedition in many democracies. And in the usage of the region's population the word settled into something more precise than any of these: the party, the dada log of the squad's local name, a power with an address and a visiting schedule, judged by conduct rather than by category. The movement's organisations, for their part, fought over the inheritance the word represents, each stream claiming the founding's mantle and denying the others', so that the label now denotes simultaneously a security classification, a journalistic convenience, a political insult, a local sovereign, and a contested will. The discipline this article has practised, specifying the stream, the district, and the decade before permitting any general statement, is the only reliable solvent, and the reader who applies it to the word's public career will find most of its uses dissolving into the interests that minted them.

## Misreadings the record corrects

Gather the errors this article has corrected in passing, since they circulate as the common sense of the subject. The first holds that all Naxalite organisations are armed, when a significant stream of the family renounced armed struggle, built open mass organisations, and has contested elections for decades with members in legislatures. The second holds that the movement is a single organisation taking orders from a single centre, when the record shows a genealogy of fragments, mergers, and autonomous regional commands, whose unity even at its maximum was negotiated rather than commanded. The third holds that its base is the peasantry in the classical sense, sharecroppers and middle peasants of the settled plains, when the durable base of its armed form is Adivasi communities of the forest districts whose grievances centre on forest, land classification, and displacement. The fourth holds that the founding line still governs, when the annihilation doctrine has been repudiated in practice by every major successor, including the armed organisation that displays the founder's portrait, and survives mainly as the accusation each stream levels at the others. The fifth holds that the movement's map corresponds to poverty as such, when India contains vast regions of poverty, including regions of acute agrarian distress, that never sustained it, and forest districts of no exceptional poverty that did; the map corresponds to the specific configuration this article has described, in which poverty is compounded by forest, mineral wealth beneath contested land, thin administration, and a population with a prior tradition of refusal. A reader armed against these five misreadings will find most commentary on the subject, sympathetic and hostile alike, easier to grade: ask which stream is meant, which district, which decade, and which of the interested literatures is speaking, and the confident generalisation usually dissolves into the particular truth it was concealing.

## The scholars' quarrel: how the academy fought over the forest

The belt became, across the decades, one of the most argued over fields in Indian scholarship, and the quarrel among the scholars is itself evidence a researcher should log. Anthropologists who lived in the strongholds, some in declared sympathy and some in declared neutrality that the conditions made heroic, produced accounts of the movement as a moral presence in the villages, a rough sovereignty preferable in its subjects' eyes to the available alternatives, and were answered by colleagues who charged them with romanticising an armed power whose coercion their access depended on not seeing. Historians of the older school treated the whole phenomenon as a footnote to the communist movement's history, a deviation's long decay, and were answered by a younger literature that relocated the subject's centre to the forest and its peoples, for whom the communist genealogy was the footnote and Adivasi history the text. Security studies produced its own shelf, operational in focus, in which the population appears as terrain and the argument of this article appears, when it appears, as the governance variable in a counter insurgency model. Each school accused the others of the sins its own method made invisible, and each was partly right, which is the normal condition of scholarship on a subject where access is rationed by belligerents and every archive is a weapon.

The quarrel's most instructive product is negative: a growing agreement, across schools that agree on little else, about the configuration this article has defended. Sympathetic anthropology and hostile security analysis, party history and tribal studies, converge on the forest, the minerals, the absent administration, and the contested record as the terms in which the phenomenon must be stated, while continuing to war over the evaluations those terms should carry. Convergence of that kind, among observers who would not share a platform, is the nearest thing to verification a subject like this affords, and it is on that convergence, rather than on any single literature's authority, that the geography of dispossession ultimately rests its case.

## A note on the vocabulary: words the conflict made

The conflict generated a vocabulary in which its whole moral argument is encoded, and the reader should be warned about the load the commonest words carry. Encounter, in the official usage of the Indian security establishment, denotes an armed exchange in which the state's forces kill their opponents; in the usage of the movement and the civil liberties record, the same word, often placed in quotation marks, alleges a staged killing of persons already in custody, and entire commissions of inquiry have turned on which meaning a given night's events deserve. Surrender, in the official usage, denotes a cadre's voluntary abandonment of the organisation; the same event appears in the movement's vocabulary as desertion or betrayal, and in the village's, frequently, as a family's negotiated survival, three descriptions of one act among which the facts alone cannot adjudicate. Area, in the movement's usage, denotes the country of a committee's authority, a sovereignty claim in a single syllable; liberated, applied to the same country, asserts the claim's completion; and base, the most modest word in the lexicon, performed in the documents the largest work, converting a refuge into a foundation by naming it one. Even the population's names for the parties carry verdicts: the state appears in the belt's speech as the government, the department, the company, each name a theory of what it is; the organisation appears as the party, the people of the forest, the brothers, or by the local commander's name, each a measurement of its standing in that valley at that season. This article has used the vocabulary of neither belligerent, preferring organisation, movement, and state in their plain senses, and where the interested terms appear they are attributed. The discipline costs fluency. It purchases the only thing a reference work on such a subject has to sell, which is the reader's confidence that the adjectives were not loaded before the evidence was.

## On evidence: how anything is known here at all

A short methodological section is owed to the researcher persona in particular, because this subject's evidence base is unlike that of any other article in the cluster and its peculiarities dictate the conclusions that can honestly be drawn. The sources arrange themselves in four families, each with a governing interest. The movement's documents, programmes, histories, anniversary assessments, and the memoirs of its participants, written to recruit, to justify, and to settle internal accounts. The state's record, police and intelligence reporting, official statements, commission and court proceedings, and the research produced inside the security establishment's own institutes, written to measure a threat and to defend a response. The scholarship, university based history, anthropology, and political science, written for professional purposes and dependent, for access, on the toleration of one armed party or the other. And the journalism, metropolitan and district level, which mediates all the rest to the public and operates under the deadline's own discipline. No family is dispensable and none is innocent, and the working rule of this article has been triangulation under suspicion: a fact is used when hostile families agree on it, a characterisation is attributed to the family that produced it, and a number is declined altogether, since on numbers the families diverge not by error but by design, each enumeration serving an appropriation, a deployment, or a morale. The rule costs the article the appearance of precision. It purchases the reality of it, in a field where the confident figure is the surest signature of an interested source.

Two consequences follow for future work. The archive's centre of gravity is shifting from the participants to the population, as oral history projects and the belt's own writers begin to record the forest's experience in the forest's languages, and the next generation's account of the movement will be answerable to testimony the founding literatures never consulted. And the state's documentary record, encounter reports, surrender files, court depositions, the paperwork of saturation, awaits the historian's equivalent of the forest rights claim: recognition that the record exists, that it belongs in principle to the public whose war it documents, and that its slow opening will revise much of what the interested literatures agreed to leave vague. Until then, the honest scholar's posture on this subject is the one this article has adopted, a confidence about configuration and a humility about everything countable.

## A comparative glance: the world's Maoisms and the Indian exception

The Indian movement belonged to a generation of Maoist insurgencies, and a brief comparison sharpens what is distinctive in its career. In Peru, a party built around a charismatic founder and a doctrine of personality launched a people's war that burned through the highlands and the capital's margins before collapsing with a speed that astonished its enemies once its leadership was captured, a trajectory that vindicates, by contrast, the Indian family's instinct, learned in its own founder's catastrophe, for collective leadership and against the cult: the Indian organisation survived the loss of successive leaderships because it had organised its authority impersonally, in committees and texts. In Nepal, a Maoist movement fighting a monarchy in hill country not unlike the Indian belt fought the state to a stalemate in a decade, then executed the turn the Indian organisation refused, entering a peace process, contesting elections, and taking office at the head of governments, a trajectory the Indian movement's documents studied and rejected as the parliamentary road in its most seductive form. In the Philippines, a Maoist insurgency of comparable vintage persisted across decades at a low simmer, neither winning nor being defeated, in an archipelago whose geography fragmented both the state and the rebellion. The Indian case sits among these as the longest war of the set and the one whose explanation is least available from its doctrine, since the doctrines were near identical across the four and the outcomes were not. What differed was the terrain in the widest sense: the Indian state's peculiar combination of electoral depth and administrative shallowness, a republic too legitimate to collapse and too absent, in its forest marches, to govern, which offered an insurgency neither a palace to storm nor a countryside it could be expelled from, only a permanent margin in which to live. The comparison returns the argument, a final time, to its ground. Movements make their choices, and the Indian movement's choices fill most of this article. But the menu from which history chose among those movements was written by the states and landscapes they faced, and any ranking of their doctrines that ignores the menu will mistake the survivors for the sages.

## The pattern: geography explains composition

The series pattern this article instantiates can now be named in its general form. Whom a movement rests on is settled by where it can survive, fully as much as by whom its programme addresses, and the claim inverts the standard ideological account, in which the base assembles because it has been persuaded. Persuasion happened here, abundantly: the forest population heard the doctrine, judged it, used it, and in large measure made it their own. But persuasion followed presence, and presence followed conditions. The cadres who carried the doctrine into a hundred countrysides survived in the ones where forest, minerals, thin administration, and contested rights combined, and died or dwindled in the ones where they did not, and the doctrine's map at any later date is the fossil of that selection. Intellectuals inside the tradition have sometimes grasped the point in another vocabulary, observing that the Indian revolution's centre of gravity shifted to the most backward regions because the advanced ones were closed to it, and that a movement of the future had come to rest on the population every promise of development reached last. The observation is usually offered as lament. It is better used as method. Read any revolutionary movement's map as a record of the state's unevenness before reading it as a record of the movement's appeal, and the anomalies, the tribal base of a proletarian party, the seminaries of a secular one, the borderlands of every universal faith, begin to resolve into a single intelligible pattern. For the wider field within which this case sits, the series guide to [Marxism in India](/2017/12/01/marxism-in-india-complete-guide/) supplies the frame, and the treatment of Maoism's doctrine in the series comparison of [Maoism and the Soviet tradition](/2018/09/01/maoism-vs-soviet-marxism/) supplies the doctrinal half of the story that this article has deliberately subordinated to the ground.

## For students and exam candidates

Approach this subject as two questions wearing one name. The first is historical: what happened between the rising in the Terai and the consolidation in the forest belt, and the answer is a narrative of split, founding line, repression, fragmentation, and divergent roads, which the streams table compresses into a page. The second is analytical: why is the movement where it is, and the answer is the geography of dispossession, a structural claim about forest, minerals, administrative thinness, and contested rights that can be stated in a paragraph and defended against the rival explanations. Examiners reward the candidate who keeps the two apart, who can narrate the annihilation line without confusing it with the movement's later practice, name the electoral stream without confusing it with the armed one, and grade the security account and the movement's own account as interested literatures rather than as evidence. ## For teachers and seminar leaders

This subject converts unusually well in the classroom, because it takes a topic students arrive believing to be a security file and reopens it as political economy. Set the founding documents against the parliamentary critique and let the seminar fight the split of the late 1960s on the evidence both sides actually had, before revealing the suppression's authorship, which reliably rearranges the room's sympathies and teaches more about the Indian left in an hour than a lecture on its doctrines. Then move the group to the map: give them the configuration of conditions without the movement's name attached, and ask where an armed organisation could live, before showing them where it did. The exercise generalises, and students who have performed it once will perform it unbidden on every insurgency they meet afterward. Close with the strongest objection and the concession it extracts, and require each student to state which of the two, objection or answer, they find survives the evidence, and on what page of the record their judgement rests.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Three underworked veins invite the researcher. The electoral stream's archive, the record of a revolutionary organisation digesting itself into a constitutional party across four decades of Bihar politics, remains disproportionately unexamined beside the literature on the armed streams, though it is the family's most instructive survival. The district level record of parallel power, its courts, levies, and produce interventions, awaits the kind of reconstruction from below that the sources, police files, court records, and the population's memory, still permit and will not permit indefinitely. And the variation across the belt, district against adjoining district under near identical conditions with divergent outcomes, is the natural laboratory in which the structural explanation offered here can be tested, refined, or broken. Journalists covering the subject in the present tense are urged toward the discipline this article has practised: name the stream, name the decade your facts belong to, attribute every account and every figure to the interest that produced it, and treat the population of the forest districts as the story's subject rather than as the scenery of somebody else's war.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the Naxalbari uprising?

A peasant action over land in May 1967 in the country around Naxalbari, in the Terai of northern Bengal, where sharecroppers organised by communist cadres seized disputed plots and harvests, resisted eviction, and set up pockets of parallel authority. A police officer was killed in an ambush, police firing killed villagers at Prasad Jote, and a coalition ministry in Calcutta in which the rebels' own party was the leading partner ordered the suppression that ended the rising within a season. Its importance is disproportionate to its scale: it split the Indian communist movement, named a tendency, and posed the question of the parliamentary road in its sharpest form.

### Q: What is the annihilation line?

The founding doctrine of the party proclaimed in 1969, holding that revolutionary authority and consciousness would be created through the physical killing of individual class enemies, landlords, moneylenders, informers, and officials, by small secret squads, in place of building mass organisations first. Its author treated participation in such acts as the test of a cadre's commitment and the measure of the struggle's advance. Practised across the following years, it exposed the squads to destruction, drew retaliation onto the villages sheltering them, and decayed into elastic target selection. Every major successor organisation has since repudiated it as method, while continuing to claim the founding from which it issued.

### Q: How did the Naxalite movement fragment?

Fragmentation began inside the founding party almost at once, over the annihilation line, the founder's personal authority, relations with foreign parties, and rival readings of the Indian revolution, and it accelerated when repression destroyed the centre in the early 1970s, leaving regional groups to develop in isolation. Released prisoners and surviving committees rebuilt through that decade into a spectrum: mass line reorganisations, orthodox sects guarding the founding texts, regional formations adapted to Bihar's caste war or Telangana's guerrilla tradition, and, in time, an electoral party on one side and consolidating armed streams on the other. The family resemblance survived every split; a single organisation did not.

### Q: Why is the movement based among tribal populations?

The base settled among Adivasi communities because the forest districts offered what the plains denied: cover, distance from the machinery of administration, and grievances, forest rights denied on paper and land taken for mines and dams, that no other political force was organising. A century of forest legislation had turned customary users into encroachers in the record; officials arrived to extract and rarely to provide; and traditions of tribal revolt against outside authority long predated the arrival of any cadre. Doctrine travelled everywhere the movement sent it. It rooted only where that combination waited, among the tribal population of the central and eastern forest belt.

### Q: What explains the movement's persistence?

The reproduction of its conditions. So long as forest districts combined concealment, minerals beneath contested land, administration too thin to govern but present enough to expropriate, and a population whose quarrels with forest administration and project displacement renewed grievance faster than any remedy arrived, an armed organisation could shelter, tax, adjudicate, and recruit. Persistence also drew on craft: patient organisation, the delivery of concrete benefits such as raised produce prices and accessible courts, and the discipline of avoiding battle except at advantage. Where any term of the configuration was withdrawn, by administration arriving, by communications dissolving isolation, or by the organisation's own exactions exhausting consent, persistence failed in proportion.

### Q: Which Naxalite groups contest elections?

The stream that descends from the mass line reorganisations in Bihar. Having demoted the gun below open organising among labourers and poor peasants, it entered the electoral arena by way of a front in the early 1980s and emerged in time as a declared party, returning members to legislatures at state and national level while continuing to claim descent from the rising of 1967 and to describe its aims as revolutionary. The armed streams denounce the turn as renegacy, and the electoral stream returns the compliment by pointing to its survival. Coverage that treats the whole family as armed erases this branch, which is among the movement's most consequential products.

### Q: What is the red corridor?

A label from security commentary for the band of districts across central and eastern India where the armed movement has operated, pictured as a continuous insurgent territory running down the country's spine. The phrase is useful shorthand and poor analysis. Presence within the shaded band ranges from durable parallel administration through episodic transit to a single squad's visiting circuit; regional commands act with wide autonomy; and the estimates behind rival maps diverge enormously, since each measurer counts differently and every measurer is an interested party. Historians of the movement prefer to picture an archipelago of strongholds in a sea of contested countryside, and to ask who produced any given corridor map, and in whose interest.

### Q: What is the Marxist criticism of Naxalism?

Two criticisms, from two directions inside the tradition. The parliamentary parties charge substitution: the armed deed of a clandestine few took the place of mass organisation openly conducted, surrendering the population's real asset, its numbers, for the state's asset, firepower, and furnishing the pretext for emergency powers and militarised administration. Critics inside the revolutionary camp charge inversion: the annihilation line reversed the doctrine it claimed, substituting individual terror for the mass political work that the canon makes prior to arms, a charge pressed most damagingly by founders of the rising itself. Successor organisations absorbed the second criticism almost completely. The first they answer by pointing at the parliamentary parties' own ministries, beginning with the one that suppressed the rising.

### Q: Is it a revolutionary movement or a response to dispossession?

The article's answer is that the question separates what its history joins, and then ranks the parts. As intention and self understanding, the movement is revolutionary: its programmes aim at the overthrow of the Indian state and the order behind it, and its cadres have died in that intention for fifty years. As explanation of where it lives and why it endures, dispossession does the work: the map follows forest, minerals, thin administration, and contested rights, not the distribution of revolutionary conviction, and districts full of conviction without those conditions produced sects, while districts with the conditions and no prior conviction produced the strongholds. Doctrine explains the flag. Geography explains the address.

### Q: What explains the movement's decline?

The weakening of the configuration that sustained it, from both directions at once. The state matured its response across decades: specialised forest war forces, intelligence built on surrendered cadres, permanent camps inside former strongholds, and roads and administration following the security frontier, so that cleared ground was increasingly held. Leadership thinned by death, capture, and surrender faster than clandestine structures can replace authority earned over decades. The villages changed around the movement, as migration, roads, and communications dissolved the isolation parallel power required. And the organisation spent its own capital, through levies felt as extortion, punishments that alienated families, and obstruction of the services the population wanted. No rival doctrine defeated it; its conditions were withdrawn.

### Q: How does the geography of dispossession grade the structural explanation?

By variation, the hardest test a structural claim can sit. The explanation predicts presence where forest cover, mineral endowment, thin administration, and contested rights overlap, and absence where any term is missing, and the record obliges across regions sharing no language or contact: acute poverty without forest produced no durable movement, forest without mining pressure produced less, mining districts with dense administration produced protest movements of other kinds. It also passes the reverse test, accounting for decline with the same variables as the conditions were withdrawn. Where the explanation strains, at district boundaries where near identical neighbours diverged, local leadership and accident resume their rights, and the claim should be graded strong on pattern, modest on the particular case.

### Q: What should a student separate between the movement's doctrine and its social base?

Nearly everything that commentary merges. The doctrine, semi feudal and semi colonial characterisation, people's democratic stage, protracted war, boycott, was fixed at the founding and changed slowly; it explains the movement's vocabulary, its internal quarrels, and its worst strategic choices. The social base changed almost completely, from Bengal sharecroppers through Bihar labourers to Adivasi forest communities, and it explains the movement's durability, its economy, and its limits. Confusing the two produces the standard errors: treating the forest base as proof the doctrine attracted it, or treating the doctrine's texts as descriptions of the forest reality. Keep a column for what the movement believed and a column for who actually sheltered it, and most examination questions on the subject answer themselves.

### Q: Why did the movement take root where it did?

Because rooting is an ecological event before it is an ideological one. An illegal armed organisation needs concealment the plains deny, a population whose grievances the existing parties are not organising, an economy it can tax without destroying, and an antagonist visible enough to organise against. The forest districts supplied all four together: cover and border seams, Adivasi communities abandoned by every other political force, tendu and contractor economies open to intervention, and the forest department and the mine as antagonists delivered by the state itself. Organisations carrying the same doctrine into districts lacking any one of the four left behind pamphlets, martyrs, and police files. The roots followed the soil.

### Q: How does the streams table order the currents?

By the family's real divisions rather than by the initials on its letterheads. Each row takes one major current and reads across five facts: the period of its formation, its settled answer on the armed road, the population it in practice drew upon, its choice about the ballot box it began by boycotting, and how it stands organisationally, stated generally. Read down the columns and the table becomes an argument: bases migrate from plains to forest, methods from annihilation to protracted construction or to the ballot, and the family's history appears as divergent adaptations to selection pressure rather than as a single movement with a single fate. Minor groupings are omitted deliberately, since their inclusion would bury the pattern the table exists to show.

### Q: What separates the parliamentary criticism from the movement's own account?

Standpoint, and the evidence each standpoint can see. The parliamentary criticism is delivered from the arena the movement abandoned: it counts what armed struggle cost in organisations destroyed, liberties suspended, and elections forgone, and it measures the movement against the mass politics it might have joined. The movement's own account is delivered from the forest: it counts what peaceful politics never delivered there, the unrecorded right, the unraised wage, the unpunished exaction, and measures the critics against the villages their ministries policed. Each account is accurate about the other's record and evasive about its own, beginning with the parliamentary parties' authorship of the first suppression. The student's task is not to average them but to hold both against the structural record, which is longer than either memory.
