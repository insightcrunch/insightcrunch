In 1219 a crusader army held the port of Damietta at the mouth of the eastern branch of the Nile, and the sultan of Egypt offered to hand over Jerusalem, the True Cross and a long truce in exchange for its return. The offer was refused. Two years later the same army was trapped between rising water and a Muslim force, and surrendered the city it had spent eighteen months taking, having achieved nothing.

The claim this article defends is the Egypt is the key thesis: that crusader strategists understood Egypt to be the strategic key to the Holy Land, that they acted on that understanding repeatedly, and that the fate of the crusading movement was therefore decided on the Nile as much as in Palestine.

![The crusader invasions of Egypt explained, covering the sieges of Damietta, the defeat at Mansurah and the capture of Louis the Ninth - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-19.webp)

The assumption this corrects is that the crusades were fought in Palestine. Two of the seven numbered expeditions were directed at Egypt from the outset, the earlier attacks of the 1160s were as well, and the greatest single crusader disaster of the whole movement happened in the Delta rather than anywhere near Jerusalem.

The pattern is what makes the subject worth an article. Three major invasions landed at the same port, advanced along the same river, reached roughly the same point and were destroyed in roughly the same way, which is a repetition too exact to be coincidence and which the article's central sections explain.

This article owns the invasions. The wars in Palestine belong to the article on [Saladin and the crusades](/2011/11/01/saladin-and-the-crusades/), the period and the strategic argument to the article on [Ayyubid Egypt](/2011/09/01/ayyubid-egypt-age-of-saladin/), and the regime that emerged from the last of them to the article on [the Mamluk sultanate](/2012/07/01/mamluk-sultanate-of-egypt/).

## Why Egypt

The strategic reasoning behind the invasions deserves setting out first, since it explains everything that follows and is the article's central claim.

### Why did the Crusaders attack Egypt?

Because Egypt was the wealth. The article on Ayyubid Egypt argues that Egyptian revenue funded the Muslim war effort in Syria and Palestine, and crusader strategists reached the same conclusion. Removing Egypt from the equation would have starved their opponents of money, troops and supply, and holding it would have made the crusader states permanent.

The argument is the mirror image of the one the article on Ayyubid Egypt makes. If a rich base funding a contested frontier is what defeats you, then taking the base is the solution.

The wealth was the first element, and the article on Fatimid trade describes Egypt's position as the land bridge between three seas with a renewing agricultural base beneath it, which no other territory in the region matched.

### Why was Egypt a Crusader target?

For its wealth, its position and its vulnerability. It funded the armies the crusader states faced, it lay adjacent to their own territory with a coastline their fleets could reach, and its government had shown itself unable to defend the country during the 1160s. Taking it would have reversed the strategic balance entirely.

The position was the second. Egypt lies adjacent to Palestine, with a coastline European fleets could reach directly, which makes it a far more practical objective than any inland Muslim centre.

The vulnerability was the third and it was specific to the 1160s. The article on how Saladin ended the Fatimids describes a collapsing Fatimid regime importing foreign armies to settle its own factional disputes, which looked like an opening to everyone watching.

The theological attraction existed alongside the strategic one, since some crusading rhetoric identified Egypt with biblical associations, though the strategic argument does not need it.

The alternative approach, of attacking Syrian centres directly, was tried and produced nothing, since inland cities are hard to reach, hard to supply and easy to relieve.

The general observation is that the crusader analysis was correct. Egypt was the key, the strategists who identified it were right, and the reason the strategy failed has nothing to do with the reasoning and everything to do with the ground.

## Why the Route Never Changed

A closing analytical section on the invariance, since it is the most striking feature of the whole subject and deserves a direct explanation.

The observation is that three campaigns separated by decades landed at the same port and followed the same branch, which invites the assumption that later commanders had not studied the earlier attempts.

The assumption is probably wrong. The Fifth Crusade was documented before the Seventh was launched, participants and their descendants were available, and the king who planned the Seventh spent years on preparation.

The explanation is that there was no alternative to study. The article's section on Damietta sets out the constraint: a sea supplied army needs a harbour on a navigable branch, and the eastern branch with Damietta at its mouth was the only combination available.

The alternatives considered were the western approach through Alexandria, rejected as longer and harder, and a direct overland march through Sinai, which requires a base in Palestine and crosses a desert with an army that has to carry its water.

The consequence is that the invariance is a fact about Egypt rather than a failure of imagination, and any commander with the same objective and the same means would reach the same plan.

The corollary is that Egyptian defensive preparation was correspondingly easy, since a defender who knows the route can fortify it, and the article's section on Mansurah describes exactly that being done between the two campaigns.

The general observation is that geography constrains strategy more tightly than most narrative history acknowledges, and when the same operation recurs across generations the explanation is usually that the map permitted nothing else rather than that nobody thought of anything better.

## The Prisoners Nobody Ransomed

The fate of the ordinary soldiers taken in 1250 deserves recording, since the story is usually told about a king.

The scale was large. An army that surrendered as a body produced captives in numbers that no ransom market could absorb, and the article on Saladin and the crusades explains the economics: a prisoner is an asset only if somebody will pay.

The outcome for those with no ransom value was the standard one of the period on both sides, which is enslavement or death, and the accounts record both.

The selection was made on the ordinary criteria. Rank, apparent wealth, the possibility of a family or an order paying, and usefulness as labour determined who survived and in what condition.

The sick were treated worst, and men too ill to be worth transporting were killed, which the accounts record without much comment because it was expected.

The conversion option existed for some, and captives who accepted Islam could improve their position, which is a mechanism the article on conquest and conversion describes in a very different context.

The eventual releases came in stages as ransoms were assembled over years, and some captives remained unaccounted for permanently.

The symmetry with crusader practice is exact, and the article on Saladin and the crusades describes the mass execution of a surrendered garrison by a crusader commander in 1191, which is the same logic applied by the other side.

The general observation is that the ransom conventions that make medieval warfare look restrained applied to a thin layer of the participants, and beneath that layer the treatment of prisoners was determined by what they were worth alive, which for most of them was very little.

## The Eyewitness Account

The memoir written by a companion of the king of France deserves individual treatment, since it is among the finest narrative sources of the medieval period and it shapes everything written about the Seventh Crusade.

The author was a nobleman who accompanied the expedition, was present through the campaign, shared the captivity and wrote his account many decades later in old age.

The value is the immediacy. He records conversations, fear, illness, disputes among the leaders, the appearance of the incendiary weapons and the physical experience of the campaign in a way no chronicle attempts.

The limitations are equally clear. He wrote long afterward, he is devoted to the king he served, he saw only what one man in one part of an army sees, and his memory had decades to reshape what he had witnessed.

The tests set out in the article on Saladin as sultan apply directly, since this is a favourable tradition and the procedure for reading one is the same as for a hostile one: circumstantial detail is reliable, statements against interest carry weight, and characterizing anecdotes carry none.

The statements against interest are the most valuable material, and his accounts of panic, of disagreement among the commanders and of his own fear are the passages a hagiographer would have removed.

The influence on later writing is enormous, and the reason the Seventh Crusade is remembered while the Fifth is not owes a great deal to the existence of a first rate personal account of one and not the other.

The comparison worth drawing is with the Egyptian material, and the article's section on the sources notes that the chronicle tradition on that side records the same events with less intimacy and comparable reliability on the outline.

The general observation is that the survival of one exceptional witness can determine which of two similar events a civilization remembers, and the disproportion between the two Egyptian crusades in modern memory is substantially a fact about a book rather than about the campaigns.

## Egypt's Defensive Record in the Long View

Placing these campaigns in the longer history of invasions of Egypt is worth doing, since the country's record is unusual.

The Arab conquest of the 640s succeeded, and the article on the Arab conquest of Egypt explains why: a Byzantine administration with no local support, a garrison that could not be reinforced and a population indifferent to the outcome.

The Fatimid arrival in 969 succeeded and was negotiated rather than fought, which the article on Fatimid Egypt describes as an occupation with terms agreed in advance.

The crusader attempts across a century failed three times, for the reasons this article has set out.

The Ottoman conquest of 1517 succeeded, coming overland from Syria rather than by sea into the Delta, which is the alternative approach and the one that works.

The pattern that emerges is directional. Egypt has repeatedly fallen to forces arriving overland from Syria and has repeatedly defeated forces arriving by sea into the Delta, which is the article's argument stated as a historical regularity.

The reason is the one the article's sections on the Delta and the flood describe. The northern approach passes through the country's most defensible ground at its most defensible season, while the eastern approach through Sinai arrives at the capital directly.

The exception that tests it is the modern period, when the article on the Citadel of Cairo notes that artillery and steam changed the terms entirely.

The general observation is that a country's defensive geography is directional rather than uniform, and Egypt's record shows a specific and consistent asymmetry between two approaches that any strategist studying it would have identified.

## The Occupation of Damietta

The eighteen months during which a European army governed an Egyptian city deserve a section, since it is the only sustained instance of crusader rule in Egypt.

The city taken in 1219 was largely empty of its original population, since the siege had killed most of them and the survivors were dispersed or enslaved.

The settlement that followed was an attempt at a permanent Latin establishment, with churches consecrated, property distributed among the participating contingents and an ecclesiastical hierarchy installed.

The disputes over that distribution consumed a great deal of the leadership's attention, and the competing claims of the various contingents, the military orders and the Italian cities produced exactly the friction one would expect.

The strategic paralysis of the occupation is the more important point. An army sitting in a captured city for eighteen months while arguing about the next move is losing its advantage continuously, since the enemy is rebuilding and the expedition's participants are going home.

The reinforcement pattern made it worse, since contingents arrived and departed on their own schedules, so the army's strength fluctuated unpredictably and no plan could count on a given force.

The eventual advance was made partly because the position had become untenable politically, since an occupation achieving nothing cannot be sustained indefinitely against the pressure to act.

The end of it came with the surrender of the city as the price of withdrawal in 1221, which means eighteen months of siege and eighteen months of occupation produced precisely nothing.

The general observation is that taking a position and knowing what to do with it are separate problems, and the Fifth Crusade solved the first brilliantly and never addressed the second at all.

## Diplomacy Alongside the Fighting

Negotiation ran continuously through both campaigns and the record is among the most revealing evidence about how the participants understood each other.

The offers began early in both cases. The Egyptian side proposed terms during the first siege of Damietta and repeated them in various forms, and comparable exchanges occurred during the Seventh Crusade before the final surrender negotiations.

The consistency of the Egyptian position is the striking feature. In both campaigns the government offered territory in Palestine, including Jerusalem in the first case, in exchange for the invaders leaving Egypt, which is the same calculation each time: the country is worth more than the city.

The crusader responses differed, and the article's sections on the refused offer and on the two crusades compared describe a divided command in the first case and a king in undisputed authority in the second.

The intermediaries were the usual ones, with envoys, letters and occasionally captured persons carrying messages, and the article on Saladin and the crusades describes the same apparatus operating in Palestine.

The courtesies are recorded on both sides, and the eyewitness memoir of the Seventh Crusade describes exchanges during the captivity that are notably free of the language the campaigns were launched in.

The ransom negotiation of 1250 is the most fully documented instance, and the article's section on it describes a commercial transaction conducted in ceremonial forms.

The 1229 transfer of Jerusalem by treaty, which the article on Ayyubid Egypt records, sits between the two campaigns and demonstrates that the Egyptian preference for a settlement was real rather than a wartime expedient.

The general observation is that the diplomatic record contradicts the framing of unlimited religious war more directly than any argument, and the article on Saladin and the crusades reaches the same conclusion from the Palestinian side of the same conflict.

## The Weapons and the Fighting

A section on how the fighting actually worked in this theatre is worth including, since Delta warfare differs from the Palestinian campaigns the article on Saladin and the crusades describes.

The cavalry charge, which that article identifies as the crusader strength, was largely unavailable here. Ground cut by canals every few hundred metres does not permit a formed charge, and the one occasion when heavy cavalry got loose at Mansurah it destroyed itself in the streets.

The infantry and crossbowmen mattered more correspondingly, since siege work, riverbank defence and the holding of fortified camps are infantry tasks.

The siege engineering was the principal military activity of both campaigns, and the first siege of Damietta involved towers, mining, floating platforms and the assault on the chain tower in midstream.

The riverine fighting is the distinctive element and both sides invested in it, with vessels used for transport, for blockade and for direct engagement on the branches and canals.

The engines are attested on both sides, and stone throwing machines were used against Damietta's walls and against crusader positions in turn.

The incendiary weapons appear repeatedly in the accounts of the Seventh Crusade, and the descriptions of a projected burning compound used against crusader siege works are among the more vivid passages in the eyewitness memoir.

The fortified camp was the standard formation for both armies when static, since a force in this landscape that does not entrench is exposed to the water as much as to the enemy.

The general observation is that a theatre shapes its own tactics, and an army trained for the open ground of Palestine found almost none of its advantages available in the Delta, which is a further reason the campaigns failed that has nothing to do with the quality of the troops.

## The Woman Who Held the Campaign Together

The Egyptian political achievement of 1250 deserves its own section, since concealing a sultan's death during an invasion is a remarkable act and the person responsible is usually mentioned in a clause.

The situation was as bad as it could be. The sultan died while a crusader army held Damietta and was advancing up the river, his designated heir was in a distant province, and the news of a vacant throne would have been worth an army to the enemy.

The concealment was managed by his widow, working with senior officials, and it involved maintaining the appearance of a functioning sultanate, issuing orders in the dead man's name and keeping the knowledge within a very small circle.

The duration was the difficult part, since a deception of this kind is easy for days and hard for months, and the campaign continued through the winter and into the spring.

The military consequence was decisive, since the corps and the commanders fought the battle at Mansurah and conducted the blockade afterward without the disruption a succession crisis would have produced.

The subsequent position she held is the part the article on Ayyubid Egypt records: after the heir was killed she was briefly recognized as ruler, which is a very unusual position in this world and one the article on the Fatimid caliphs notes was not unprecedented in Egypt.

The duration of that position was short and the arrangements that followed are the article on the Mamluk sultanate's subject.

The historiographical treatment is the familiar one, and the article on al-Hakim makes the general point: a woman who exercised power is remembered in a subordinate clause of a story about men, and the competence involved receives a fraction of the attention.

The general observation is that the Egyptian victory of 1250 was as much political as military, and the political element was managed by a person the standard accounts of the Seventh Crusade barely name.

## The Chronology of the Invasions

Setting the sequence out in one place is worth doing, since the article treats themes and a reader needs the order.

In 1163 the first crusader expedition entered Egypt, invited into a Fatimid factional dispute, and a decade of intervention followed in which both crusader and Syrian forces intervened repeatedly and sometimes on opposite sides.

In 1168 a crusader force advanced into the Delta, and the older commercial city south of Cairo was burned by its own defenders to deny it to the enemy.

In 1169 the Syrian force prevailed, a Syrian officer became Fatimid vizier, and the article on how Saladin ended the Fatimids describes what followed.

In 1218 the Fifth Crusade landed near Damietta and besieged it for eighteen months in appalling conditions.

In 1219 the city fell, and the sultan offered Jerusalem, the True Cross and a long truce for its return, which was refused.

Through 1220 the crusader army occupied Damietta while its leaders argued about the next move and reinforcements came and went.

In 1221 the army advanced up the river in summer, was trapped by flooding and Egyptian manoeuvre, and surrendered Damietta in exchange for withdrawal.

In 1229 an Ayyubid sultan transferred Jerusalem to a European emperor by treaty, which the article on Ayyubid Egypt records and which confirms the 1219 offer had been serious.

In June 1249 the Seventh Crusade landed at Damietta and took it in a day.

Through late 1249 the army delayed, then advanced up the eastern branch toward Mansurah.

In February 1250 the battle at Mansurah destroyed the crusader vanguard, and the sultan died during this period with the death concealed.

In April 1250 the crusader army, cut off and starving, attempted a retreat, was overtaken, and the king of France was captured.

In May 1250 the military corps that had won the campaign killed the arriving heir and took power, which is the origin of the Mamluk sultanate.

The shape the sequence reveals is a century of attempts at one objective, all following the same route and all ending at roughly the same point, with the last of them producing the regime that would eventually settle the whole conflict.

## What Would Change This Picture

Naming the evidence that would revise the account is the fairest test of a military narrative built from partisan chronicles.

Muster records or pay accounts from either side would be transformative, since every numerical claim about these campaigns rests on chronicle figures that cannot be checked.

Egyptian administrative records of the defensive preparations would establish whether the doctrine this article describes was articulated as doctrine or reconstructed by historians from consistent outcomes.

Archaeological work on the Mansurah battlefield and on the crusader camps would settle a good deal about positions, and camp archaeology in particular would speak to the disease question.

Medical evidence from burials would allow the illnesses that destroyed both armies to be identified rather than inferred from symptom descriptions in a medieval vocabulary.

The full terms of the 1219 offer, in a documentary rather than a narrative form, would settle how serious it was, which the article treats as probable rather than certain.

Hydrological reconstruction of the Delta's canal system in the thirteenth century would firm up the flooding arguments, which rest on the general pattern rather than on the specific channels involved.

None of these is impossible and several are areas where work continues, so the boundary here is more provisional than in most of this series.

What is secure is the chronology, the landings, the sieges, the shape of Mansurah, the capture and ransom, and the Mamluk consequence. What is reconstruction is the numbers, the specific mechanisms of the flooding, the internal deliberations on both sides and the medical causes.

## The Question Behind the Campaigns

A final analytical section on the strategic problem itself, since it recurs whenever a power tries to attack an enemy's economic base rather than its army.

The reasoning is always the same and always sound in principle. An enemy sustained by a particular territory can be defeated by taking that territory, which removes the need to defeat his forces at all.

The difficulty is always the same too. An economic base is valuable precisely because it is productive and secure, and the properties that make it worth taking generally make it hard to take.

Egypt is an extreme case of both halves. The article on Ayyubid Egypt describes wealth, defensibility and administrative depth in one package, and the same defensibility that made it a reliable base made it an impossible target.

The crusader error was not in the analysis but in assuming the analysis implied a plan, and identifying an enemy's centre of gravity does not by itself mean it can be reached.

The alternative approaches available were unattractive. Attacking Syrian centres had been tried and produced nothing, and defending the coastal strip indefinitely was the strategy that had already failed to produce security.

The absence of a good option is the honest conclusion. The crusader states faced a structural problem with no solution available at their level of resources, and the Egyptian campaigns were the most intelligent attempt at one.

The general observation worth carrying away is that correct strategic analysis and feasible operations are separate things, and a great deal of military history consists of powers that understood their problem perfectly and could not act on the understanding.

## The Military Orders in Egypt

The professional core of the crusader armies deserves attention, since these campaigns cost them heavily and the losses had consequences.

The orders were permanent, disciplined, celibate bodies of fighting men supported by estates across Europe, and the article on Saladin and the crusades describes them as the standing force that distinguished crusader armies from temporary expeditions.

Their role in these campaigns was the same as elsewhere: the core of the cavalry, the most reliable troops, and the units placed where the fighting would be hardest.

The losses at Mansurah were severe and specific. A substantial portion of one order's strength in the region was destroyed in the pursuit into the town, which the article's section on the battle describes as the classic error of heavy cavalry in confined ground.

The consequence for the crusader states was structural, since the orders were their permanent garrison and a generation of trained men cannot be replaced from Europe quickly.

The financial dimension compounded it, since the orders also functioned as the movement's bankers, and the article's section on the ransom notes that the settlement of 1250 drew on their resources.

The recovery took decades and was never complete, and the article's section on the end of the crusader states describes a coastal position progressively reduced across the following forty years.

The comparison with Hattin is exact and worth drawing, since the article on Saladin and the crusades describes the orders' members executed after that battle for the same reason: they were the irreplaceable component.

The general observation is that a force whose replacement takes decades is the wrong force to expend in a single engagement, and the destruction of the crusader states' professional core in a Delta town is among the more consequential losses of the whole movement.

## Why This Theatre Is Neglected

A closing analytical point about the historiography, since the Egyptian campaigns receive a fraction of the attention the Palestinian ones do.

The first reason is the framing. The crusades are narrated as an attempt to hold Jerusalem, so campaigns aimed elsewhere read as digressions rather than as the main strategic effort.

The second is the outcome. Nothing was gained, no territory changed hands permanently, and a campaign that ends where it began produces no map to point at.

The third is the sources' own emphasis, since European accounts written for European audiences dwell on Jerusalem because that is what their readers cared about.

The fourth is that the successful side's records are less accessible to the tradition that shaped the standard narratives, which the article on Saladin and the crusades treats as the general condition of the whole subject.

The fifth is that failures are less studied than successes in military history generally, which is a professional bias rather than a judgment about importance.

The correction this article proposes is straightforward. Two of the seven numbered crusades were directed at Egypt, the movement's greatest single disaster happened there, and the regime that ended the crusader states was created by the last of these campaigns.

The general recommendation is that the theatre a conflict's participants considered decisive is the theatre worth studying, and both sides in this one agreed that Egypt was where it would be settled.

## The Logistics of an Invasion

Describing what an army of this kind actually required makes the failures intelligible in a way a narrative of decisions does not.

The scale of consumption is the starting point. Men, horses and pack animals in the tens of thousands consume food and water daily in quantities that a modern reader consistently underestimates, and horses eat far more than men.

The water requirement in a hot climate is the binding constraint, and the article on Saladin and the crusades describes armoured men suffering heat exhaustion within hours without it.

The supply by sea worked as far as the coast and no further, since the article's section on the naval dimension explains that river supply is a different problem and one the Egyptians could interdict.

The local supply was unavailable, since the countryside was hostile, the population removed what it could and the Egyptian forces destroyed what remained ahead of an advance.

The transport problem inland is severe, since the Delta's canal network makes wheeled transport impractical and the alternative is pack animals, which consume a substantial share of what they carry.

The consequence is a hard radius of operation. An army can advance a certain distance from a secure base and no further, and every kilometre inland shortens the time it can remain.

The Egyptian counter is therefore obvious once the constraint is understood: let the invader advance, cut the river behind him, and wait, which is precisely what happened in 1250.

The general observation is that a campaign's maximum reach is a calculation rather than a matter of will, and the reason no crusader force ever saw Cairo is that Cairo was outside the radius any thirteenth century army could achieve from a Delta port.

## The Crusade in European Memory

How these campaigns were remembered in Europe deserves recording, since the memory differs sharply from the events.

The Fifth Crusade left little impression, and an expedition that spent three years achieving nothing and refused the recovery of Jerusalem does not lend itself to celebration.

The Seventh Crusade is remembered very differently and the reason is the king. A pious monarch who led an expedition personally, was captured, endured captivity with dignity and was later canonized becomes a story regardless of the military outcome.

The eyewitness memoir written by his companion is a substantial part of the reason, since a vivid firsthand account of a disaster keeps it in circulation far longer than a competent chronicle of a success.

The transformation of failure into edification is the mechanism, and a defeat can be read as a trial of faith in a way a victory cannot, which suited the devotional literature of the following centuries.

The strategic lesson was not absorbed, or at least not acted on, since the crusading idea persisted long afterward without any solution to the operational problem these campaigns had demonstrated twice.

The Egyptian memory is correspondingly slight, and the article on the Mamluk sultanate describes a regime whose founding narrative involves the campaign without dwelling on the crusaders themselves.

The general observation is that the losing side usually produces the more durable literature, since defeat requires explanation and explanation produces text, and the best account of these campaigns was written by a man on the side that lost.

## What the Invasions Achieved

Assessing the results is worth doing carefully, since the campaigns are usually written off as total failures and the accounting is more complicated.

The stated objectives were achieved in none of them. Egypt was not taken, Cairo was never reached, Jerusalem was not recovered by force, and the crusader states were not made secure.

The direct costs were enormous. Two large armies were destroyed, an enormous ransom was transferred to Egypt, and the reputational damage of a captured king was severe.

The indirect effects were substantial and none favoured the crusaders. The article's section on the Mamluk consequence describes a regime created by the last invasion that then eliminated the crusader states entirely within four decades.

The one positive result belongs to the aftermath of the Seventh Crusade, when the king spent several years in the crusader states fortifying and reorganizing, which strengthened the coastal position for a generation.

The demonstration effect worked against future attempts, since two catastrophic failures made further expeditions against Egypt harder to finance and harder to justify.

The Egyptian gains were correspondingly large. A defensive doctrine validated twice, an enormous ransom, the removal of the Damietta vulnerability by demolition, and a military establishment with enormous prestige.

The European consequence for the crusading idea was significant, and the difficulty of raising further expeditions after 1250 reflects a movement that had spent its credibility along with its armies.

The general observation is that the accounting on a failed campaign should include what it did for the enemy, and by that measure these invasions were worse than failures: they were transfers of strength from one side to the other.

## The End of the Crusader States

Following the story to its conclusion completes the argument, since the regime the invasions created is the one that finished the crusader presence.

The position after 1250 was that the crusader states held a coastal strip, the article on Saladin and the crusades having described the settlement of 1192 that established it and the subsequent century of coexistence.

The new Egyptian regime was different in kind from its predecessors, and the article on the Mamluk sultanate describes a military establishment ruling in its own name with the succession problem that had destroyed three previous dynasties finally solved.

The Mongol threat arrived first and was stopped in Syria in 1260, which the article on the Mamluk sultanate treats, and which established the new regime's standing across the Muslim world.

The campaign against the crusader states followed and it was systematic. A regime with Egypt's revenue, a professional army and no succession crises could reduce coastal fortresses one at a time over decades in a way the Ayyubids never managed.

The completion came in 1291 with the fall of the last major crusader position, ending nearly two centuries of Latin presence on the Syrian coast.

The irony is the one the article's verdict states. The crusaders invaded Egypt to destroy the power that threatened them, and the invasions produced the power that destroyed them.

The general observation is that the second and third order effects of a military failure frequently exceed the first order ones, and an expedition judged only by whether it achieved its objective will be judged wrongly if it also transformed the enemy.

## The Ransom and What It Reveals

The settlement that freed the king of France deserves examination, since it is unusually well documented and it illuminates how this world actually worked.

The principle is the one the article on Saladin and the crusades sets out through the ransom economy: a prisoner of standing is an asset rather than a liability, since his people will pay, and killing him destroys the value.

The scale here was without precedent. A reigning king of France commanded a sum that no ordinary noble could approach, and the amount agreed represented a very substantial transfer of European wealth to Egypt.

The composition combined cash and the surrender of Damietta, which means the Egyptians recovered the city they had lost and were paid for the inconvenience.

The negotiation was complicated by the revolution occurring on the Egyptian side while it proceeded, which the article's section on the Mamluk consequence describes, so the terms were settled partly with men who had taken power during the discussions.

The conduct on both sides is reported as businesslike. Terms were proposed, amended and agreed in the ordinary manner of the period, and the accounts do not describe the kind of behaviour that would have made a settlement impossible.

The payment was made partly in Egypt and partly afterward, with hostages held against the balance, which is the standard mechanism for a debt too large to settle in one transaction.

The wider release of prisoners accompanied it, though the ordinary soldiers of the army fared far worse than their king, and many were killed or enslaved, which the article on Saladin and the crusades identifies as the general pattern.

The general observation is that a ransom is a commercial transaction dressed in ceremonial language, and the survival of a captured king in a religious war says more about the value of his person than about anybody's mercy.

## Two Crusades Compared

Setting the Fifth and the Seventh beside each other identifies what changed and what did not.

The preparation differed enormously. The Fifth was assembled piecemeal from contingents arriving separately over years; the Seventh was planned, financed and equipped by a single ruler over several years and was among the best organized expeditions of the movement.

The command differed correspondingly. The Fifth had no single authority except a papal legate whose veto overrode military judgment; the Seventh had a king in undisputed command.

The landing differed. The Fifth spent eighteen months taking Damietta; the Seventh took it in a day.

The advance differed in timing rather than in route. The Fifth moved in summer directly into the flood; the Seventh delayed at Damietta until the season had turned against it in a different way.

The outcome was identical. Both armies were trapped upriver, cut off from their base, destroyed by disease and blockade, and forced to surrender Damietta in exchange for withdrawal or release.

The conclusion the comparison forces is the article's central one. Better preparation, better command and a faster capture of the port made no difference whatever to the result, which means the variable that mattered was none of those things.

The variable that mattered was the ground, and the article's sections on the Delta and the flood describe a problem that organization cannot solve.

The general observation is that comparing a badly run failure with a well run one is the most efficient way to isolate the cause, and when both fail identically the cause is not in the running.

## Reading a Failed Campaign

The methodological question this subject raises is how to explain a failure without either blaming the commanders or excusing them, and the procedure is worth setting out.

The first move is to establish the constraints, which the article's sections on the Delta, the flood and the naval dimension do, since a decision can only be judged against what was available.

The second is to identify the decisions that were genuinely open. The refusal of the offer in 1219 was a real choice with a real alternative; the landing at Damietta was not a choice at all.

The third is to separate error from impossibility. Advancing in summer was an error, since the season was known; reaching Cairo from the coast with thirteenth century logistics may have been impossible regardless of season.

The fourth is to check the repetition. When a plan fails identically three times, the explanation is structural, and the article's section on why it always failed sets out the seven constants.

The fifth is to consider the counterfactual honestly. Had the offer of 1219 been accepted the movement would have achieved its objective, which means the failure was not inevitable in the way the operational problem was.

The sixth is to ask what the opponent was doing, since accounts of failure frequently omit the enemy, and the article's section on how Egypt won describes a coherent doctrine applied consistently.

The seventh is to resist hindsight in judging individual decisions, since the papal legate's argument about defensibility was not stupid and the vanguard commander at Mansurah was exploiting a genuine success.

Applied here the procedure yields a clear verdict: correct strategy, insoluble operational problem, and one specific decision in 1219 that could have delivered the objective anyway.

## The Egyptian Political Context

What was happening inside Egypt during each invasion shaped the response, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt supplies the frame.

The Fifth Crusade arrived during the reign of the founder's nephew, who dominated the middle decades of the dynasty, and the family confederation that article describes was functioning reasonably well at the time.

The offer of Jerusalem in 1219 makes sense in that context. A ruler holding Egypt, Syria and other territories through a family arrangement values the revenue base far above a city, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt's base argument explains why: Egypt is what makes everything else possible.

The negotiated transfer of Jerusalem in 1229, which the article on Ayyubid Egypt records, is the same calculation carried out eight years later without a crusader army in the Delta, and it confirms that the 1219 offer was serious rather than desperate.

The Seventh Crusade arrived at a far worse moment. The last effective sultan was ill and died during the campaign, the succession was unsettled, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt describes a family confederation that had fragmented repeatedly.

The concealment of the death is the political achievement of the campaign on the Egyptian side, since a succession crisis announced during an invasion would have been catastrophic.

The military corps was the beneficiary throughout, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt describes a body of purchased and trained soldiers quartered on an island in the river who won the campaign and then took the state.

The general observation is that the Egyptian side won both campaigns while in political difficulty, and won the second while its government was collapsing, which suggests the defensive advantages of the ground were large enough to survive considerable disorder behind them.

## The Cost to Egypt

An article in a series about Egypt should record what the invasions cost the country, since the accounts are written by and about the invaders.

The Delta districts along the line of advance bore the direct cost, with requisition, destruction, displacement and the presence of two armies living off ground that supports a dense agricultural population.

Damietta suffered most and twice. An eighteen month siege in the first invasion reduced its population severely, and the article's section on its fate describes the eventual demolition of the city itself.

The agricultural disruption in a flood dependent system is more serious than it sounds, since the article on taxation describes basins, channels and embankments that require annual maintenance, and a season's neglect in a war zone has consequences beyond that season.

The fiscal cost fell on the whole country, since armies and fortifications are funded from revenue and the article on taxation describes a system that extracts from cultivators regardless of where the money goes.

The demolition of Damietta was a deliberate self inflicted loss, and destroying a major port to deny it to an enemy is a decision that trades commercial capacity for security.

The commercial disruption was real but limited, and the article on Fatimid trade describes networks that operated across every political disturbance of the period, with Italian merchants trading in Egyptian ports while their coreligionists fought the same rulers.

The indirect cost is the largest and it is the one the article's section on the Mamluk consequence describes: the invasions concentrated power in a military corps that then ruled the country for two and a half centuries.

The general observation is that the people who pay for a war are rarely the people who choose it, and Delta cultivators who had no view on Jerusalem lost harvests, homes and lives to a strategic argument conducted in Rome and Paris.

## Disease in the Camps

The largest killer in every one of these campaigns receives almost no attention in popular accounts and deserves a section.

The conditions were the cause. A besieging army camped in one place for months in a hot climate, drinking from a river its own waste enters, with inadequate sanitation and a diet of whatever can be shipped, is an epidemiological catastrophe waiting to happen.

The eighteen month siege of 1218 to 1219 was the worst instance, and the accounts describe an army losing men steadily throughout without any fighting, alongside a besieged population dying in far greater numbers.

The Seventh Crusade's collapse was substantially medical. The accounts of the blockade in 1250 describe symptoms consistent with dysentery and with scurvy from a diet lacking fresh food, and the army that surrendered had been destroyed by illness before the Egyptians did anything.

The retrospective diagnosis is not available, and the article on al-Hakim makes the general point that medieval descriptions written in a humoral vocabulary do not translate into modern categories with confidence.

The asymmetry favoured the defenders, since a local army operating in its own country from established bases with local supply is not sitting in a static camp drinking contaminated water for eighteen months.

The Egyptian doctrine exploited this deliberately, and the article's section on how Egypt won identifies patience as an element: an invading army in the Delta degrades on its own, and a defender who waits is doing something.

The general observation is that pre modern campaigns are decided by disease more often than by battle, and an account that describes the fighting and omits the camps has left out the principal cause of most of the deaths.

## What the Sources Say

The evidence for these campaigns comes from both sides in useful quantity and knowing what kind of material it is matters.

The crusader sources include participant accounts, letters sent to Europe during the campaigns, papal correspondence and chronicles compiled shortly afterward, and the Seventh Crusade in particular produced a famous eyewitness memoir by a companion of the king.

The value of the eyewitness material is very high for the texture of a campaign, since a participant records the mud, the sickness, the arguments in council and the fear, which no chronicle does.

The limitation is the usual one, since a participant sees a small part of an event and reconstructs the rest afterward, and the article on Saladin and the crusades sets out the general problem with battle narratives.

The Egyptian and Syrian sources cover the campaigns from the other side, including the chronicle tradition this series uses throughout and the later Egyptian topographical compilation.

The agreements between the traditions are substantial on the outline of both campaigns, on the Damietta sieges, on the shape of Mansurah and on the terms of the surrenders, which is the corroboration that makes those elements secure.

The disagreements are the usual ones. Numbers are inflated on both sides, motives are attributed favourably, and each tradition explains its own reverses by treachery or divine displeasure.

The documentary material includes the correspondence surrounding the ransom negotiations, which is unusually good evidence for how such settlements actually worked.

The general observation is that these campaigns are among the better documented military operations of the thirteenth century, and the presence of a first rate eyewitness account of the Seventh Crusade makes it possible to describe a medieval campaign's daily experience with unusual confidence.

## The Naval Dimension

Sea power decided the shape of every one of these campaigns and the balance was consistently in the crusaders' favour, which makes their failure more striking.

The crusader advantage was the Italian maritime cities and the fleets of the expeditions themselves, and the article on Saladin and the crusades describes shipping that could supply a besieged position indefinitely.

The Egyptian weakness was structural rather than a matter of neglect, and the article on Fatimid trade identifies the cause: Egypt has almost no usable shipbuilding timber, so every fleet the country ever built depended on imports.

The consequence at the coast is that Damietta could not be held against a fleet supported siege, which is why the Egyptian doctrine conceded it in every campaign.

The consequence inland reverses entirely. River operations are a different problem from sea operations, the branches are narrow and shallow in places, and vessels can be blocked by chains, by sunken hulls or by fortified points.

The chain at Damietta is the first instance, since a tower in midstream held a barrier across the channel and taking it occupied a substantial part of the first siege.

The blockade of 1250 is the decisive instance. Egyptian vessels moved upriver past the crusader position, or were carried overland and launched above it, and cut the supply line between the army at Mansurah and its base at Damietta.

The lesson is that naval superiority at sea does not transfer to a river, and an army that has assumed its supply is secure because it commands the sea discovers otherwise a hundred kilometres inland.

The general observation is that the crusaders held the stronger navy throughout and lost anyway, which is the clearest evidence that the campaigns failed on the ground rather than on the water.

## The Fortress at Mansurah

The position that stopped both major invasions deserves its own treatment, since its establishment is the Ayyubid response to the Fifth Crusade.

The location is on the eastern branch upriver from Damietta, at a point where a canal joins the main channel, which makes it a natural blocking position for anything moving inland.

The foundation followed the Fifth Crusade directly. Having watched an army advance two thirds of the way to Cairo, the Ayyubid government established a fortified town at the point where the advance had been checked, which is a rational and expensive response.

The name itself commemorates the victory of 1221, which is worth noting as an instance of a place named for what happened there and then used to make the same thing happen again.

The function was blocking rather than defending. A fortified position on the route does not need to be impregnable; it needs to force an attacker to deal with it, which costs time and exposes him to the season.

The garrison and the encampment around it made it a base as well as a barrier, and the Egyptian army operating from it in 1250 could strike at a crusader force that had to keep its own position on the far bank.

The performance in 1250 justified the investment completely, and the article's section on the battle describes a crusader vanguard destroyed in the streets of the town itself.

The general observation is that the most effective military architecture is often the least impressive, and a fortified town at the right point on a river achieved what walls and towers elsewhere could not, simply by being where an enemy had to come.

## The Delta as a Battlefield

The physical character of the theatre deserves description before the campaigns, since almost everything that happened follows from it.

The Delta is a fan of land north of Cairo where the Nile divides into branches before reaching the sea, and the article on how the Arab conquest reshaped Egypt describes it as the country's largest area of cultivation and its most productive ground.

The branches are the roads. Two main navigable channels ran to the sea in this period, with smaller ones between them, and an army moving inland follows one of them because the alternative is crossing water repeatedly.

The canal network is dense beyond anything a European army had encountered. The article on taxation describes an irrigation system of basins and channels covering the whole cultivated area, which means the ground between the branches is cut by water at intervals of a few hundred metres.

The consequence for movement is severe. Cavalry cannot manoeuvre across ditches, wagons cannot cross without bridging, and an army advancing off the riverbank is committing to continuous engineering work under harassment.

The consequence for supply is worse. Everything must be carried, since a hostile countryside supplies nothing, and the only practical transport is by boat along the branch the army is following, which means control of the water is control of the campaign.

The seasonal transformation compounds all of it, and the article's section on the flood describes ground that is cultivated in one part of the year and submerged in another.

The settlements are the only firm ground in places, built on mounds above the flood level, which means towns are also the only positions and taking them is unavoidable.

The general observation is that the Delta is the worst terrain in the eastern Mediterranean for an invading army and the best for the defender, and any account of these campaigns that concentrates on decisions rather than on the ground has the emphasis wrong.

## The Attacks of the 1160s

The first phase belongs to the Fatimid period and it is the one that produced the most consequential result, though not for the people who launched it.

### What were the earlier attacks under Amalric of Jerusalem?

Expeditions across the 1160s by the king of Jerusalem, sometimes invited by Fatimid viziers and sometimes fighting them, aimed at controlling Egypt or taking tribute. They competed with Syrian expeditions pursuing the same objective. The outcome was a Syrian army installed in Cairo, which produced exactly what the campaigns were meant to prevent.

The context was Fatimid collapse, and the article on how Saladin ended the Fatimids describes rival viziers seeking outside backing, which drew both crusader and Syrian forces into the country.

The campaigns were repeated across the decade, with tribute extracted at points, alliances made and broken, and both foreign powers intervening on opposite sides of the same Egyptian dispute.

The nearest approach came in 1168, when a crusader force advanced into the Delta and the article on the founding of the garrison city records the older commercial settlement south of Cairo burned by its own defenders to deny it to the enemy.

The failure was decisive in the worst possible way. The Syrian force arrived, the crusader force withdrew, and the article on how Saladin ended the Fatimids describes what followed: a Syrian officer became vizier, abolished the caliphate and united Egypt with Syria.

The irony is complete and worth stating. A decade of crusader effort to prevent Egypt falling under Syrian control produced exactly that outcome, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt describes the consequences for the following century.

The lesson available is that intervening in a collapsing state's internal politics is a mechanism for handing it to whoever intervenes more effectively, which is a pattern with a very long history.

The general observation is that the crusader states' strategic analysis was right and their execution created the situation the analysis was designed to prevent, which is the worst possible combination.

## The Fifth Crusade

The expedition of 1218 to 1221 is the first full scale attempt and it came closer to succeeding than any of them.

### What was the Fifth Crusade in Egypt?

A large European expedition that landed near Damietta in 1218, besieged the city for eighteen months, took it in November 1219, occupied it for a further eighteen months, and then advanced up the river toward Cairo in the summer of 1221. It was trapped between the rising flood and the Egyptian army, and surrendered Damietta in exchange for safe withdrawal.

The landing was at the mouth of the eastern branch of the Nile, which is the point the article's later section explains was the only practical entry.

The siege lasted eighteen months and was appalling for both sides, with disease, hunger and winter conditions killing far more people than the fighting.

The chain across the river was the specific obstacle, since a tower in midstream held a chain barring the passage upriver, and taking it was the necessary first step.

The fall of the city in November 1219 was as much a collapse as a capture, since the garrison and population had been reduced by starvation and disease to a fraction of their original numbers.

The occupation that followed lasted a further eighteen months, during which the army argued about what to do next and reinforcements arrived and departed.

The advance of 1221 was made in the summer, which is the worst possible timing, and the article's section on the flood explains why.

The end was not a battle. The army advanced, found itself in a position where the Egyptians could open canals and flood the ground behind it, and negotiated a withdrawal in exchange for surrendering Damietta.

## The Offer That Was Refused

The negotiation of 1219 deserves its own section, since it is among the more remarkable episodes in crusading history and it is frequently omitted.

### Why did the Crusaders refuse the offer of Jerusalem?

Because the papal legate who held ultimate authority over the expedition insisted on continuing. The military commanders and several of the secular leaders favoured accepting, on the grounds that Jerusalem was the objective and it was being offered. The legate argued that Egypt was the greater prize and that a city without its defensible hinterland could not be held.

The offer itself was extraordinary. The sultan proposed to return Jerusalem, cede substantial territory, restore the True Cross and agree a long truce, in exchange for the crusader withdrawal from Damietta.

The division within the crusader command is well documented and it ran along a predictable line, with the secular leaders and the local barons favouring acceptance and the papal legate refusing.

The legate's argument had real force and this article records it rather than dismissing it. Jerusalem without the surrounding fortresses had proved indefensible before, and a settlement that returned the city would have left the strategic position unchanged.

The counterargument had more force. The offer delivered the movement's stated objective at no cost, and the alternative was a campaign that had already consumed three years.

The repetition of the offer in various forms across the following two years indicates the Egyptian side's consistent preference, which the article's section on the Egyptian response explains: the country was worth more than the city.

The outcome vindicated the acceptors completely, since the campaign ended with the crusaders surrendering Damietta and receiving nothing at all.

The general observation is that a divided command with an ideological faction holding a veto is the worst possible structure for a campaign requiring judgment, and the article on Saladin and the crusades describes the same problem on the other side at Hattin.

### What was the papal legate's role in the Fifth Crusade?

He held ultimate authority over the expedition as the pope's representative, and he used it decisively. He refused the Egyptian offer of Jerusalem in 1219, insisted on the advance up the river in 1221 against military advice, and is generally held responsible by both contemporary and later accounts for the campaign's failure.

## The Seventh Crusade

The expedition of 1248 to 1250 is the most famous of the invasions and it ended in the greatest crusader disaster of the whole movement.

### What was the Seventh Crusade and Louis IX?

An expedition led by the king of France, exceptionally well prepared and financed, which landed at Damietta in June 1249 and took the city immediately when its garrison withdrew. It advanced up the river in early 1250, fought a costly engagement at Mansurah, was then blockaded and starved, and surrendered in April 1250 with the king himself taken prisoner.

The preparation was the campaign's distinguishing feature. The king had spent years assembling resources, building a port of embarkation, stockpiling supplies and financing the expedition, and it was among the best organized crusading efforts ever mounted.

The landing succeeded immediately, unlike the eighteen month siege of the previous campaign, because the Egyptian garrison withdrew rather than defending the city.

The delay that followed is the first error. The army waited at Damietta for months, partly for reinforcements and partly for the flood to recede, which gave the Egyptian side time to prepare.

The advance began in late 1249 and moved slowly up the eastern branch toward the fortified position at Mansurah, which the Ayyubids had established after the previous invasion precisely to block this route.

The sultan died during this period, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt describes the death being concealed by his widow while the campaign continued, which is among the more remarkable political manoeuvres of the period.

The engagement at Mansurah in February 1250 is treated in the article's next section.

The collapse followed rather than accompanied it. The crusader army held its position after Mansurah and was then cut off from Damietta by Egyptian control of the river, starved, weakened by disease, and forced to attempt a retreat that failed.

## Mansurah

The battle of February 1250 deserves careful treatment, since it is the campaign's decisive engagement and its shape is instructive.

### What happened at the Battle of Mansurah?

A crusader vanguard crossed the canal by a ford, attacked the Egyptian camp with initial success, and then pursued into the town of Mansurah itself, where it was cut off in the narrow streets and destroyed. The main army crossed and held its position against counterattacks through the day, so the battle was tactically indecisive and strategically fatal.

The ford is the starting point. The army had been blocked by a canal and could not cross until a local informant reportedly revealed a crossing point, which allowed the vanguard to get over ahead of the main body.

The initial success was real, since the crossing surprised the Egyptian camp and the vanguard broke it.

The pursuit into the town was the error, and it is the classic one. Heavy cavalry in narrow streets loses every advantage it has, and the article on Saladin and the crusades explains that the charge is devastating in the open and useless once formation is lost.

The destruction of the vanguard included a substantial part of the military orders' strength, which the article on Saladin and the crusades identifies as the professional core of crusader armies.

The main army crossed and held, fighting off counterattacks through the day and into the following days, so the field was not lost.

The strategic result was nonetheless decisive, since the crusader army was now on the wrong side of a canal, far from its base, with its best troops dead and an enemy that could cut its communications.

The general observation is that Mansurah demonstrates the difference between a tactical outcome and a strategic one. The crusaders held the field and lost the campaign, because holding a field a hundred kilometres from your supply is not an achievement.

## The Capture of the King

The surrender of April 1250 is the greatest single crusader disaster and the aftermath is unusually well documented.

### How was Louis IX captured and ransomed?

His army, cut off from Damietta and destroyed by disease and starvation, attempted a retreat and was overtaken. The king was captured with the remnant of his force and ransomed for an enormous cash sum together with the surrender of Damietta, negotiated while he was a prisoner. He stayed in the region for years afterward.

The condition of the army by April was desperate. Disease had spread through the camp, supplies had been cut by Egyptian control of the river, and the accounts describe conditions that had destroyed the force before any fighting concluded.

The retreat was attempted too late, and the army was overtaken and destroyed as a fighting body rather than defeated in an engagement.

The capture of a reigning king of France is the element that gave the event its resonance in Europe, since nothing comparable had happened in the crusading movement.

The treatment of the prisoner is reported as correct rather than harsh, and the negotiations proceeded in the ordinary manner of the period, which the article on Saladin and the crusades describes through the ransom economy.

The ransom was enormous, paid partly in cash and completed by the surrender of Damietta, and it represented a very substantial transfer of European wealth to Egypt.

The complication was that the Egyptian side underwent a revolution during the negotiations, which the article's section on the Mamluk consequence describes, and the terms had to be settled with the men who had just taken power.

The aftermath included several years spent in the crusader states fortifying and organizing, which is the constructive part of an otherwise catastrophic expedition.

The general observation is that the expedition's failure was not military incompetence but geography, and the article's central sections argue that the same ground had destroyed the same plan thirty years earlier.

## Why Damietta

The choice of landing point was identical in every campaign and the reasons are worth setting out, since they explain the whole pattern.

### Why was Damietta the landing point every time?

Because it controls the eastern branch of the Nile, which is the only practical route by which an army supplied from the sea can advance toward Cairo. A fleet needs a port, a port needs to be on the branch the army will follow, and the eastern branch was the navigable route inland. There was effectively no alternative.

The geography is decisive. An army arriving by sea must land somewhere with a harbour, must then move inland, and in the Delta that means following a branch of the river because the ground between branches is cut by canals and floods annually.

The eastern branch was the practical route, and Damietta sits at its mouth, which makes the city the gate.

The alternative of Alexandria was considered and rejected in several campaigns, since the western approach leads through a longer and more difficult route to the capital.

The alternative of landing on the coast between them offers no harbour and no river to follow.

The consequence is that the Egyptian defence knew exactly where an invasion would come, which is an enormous advantage and one the Ayyubids exploited by fortifying the approaches upriver.

### What happened to Damietta after the invasions?

After the crusaders surrendered it in 1250 the Egyptians demolished its fortifications and eventually the city itself, on the reasoning that a port which had twice served as a crusader base was more dangerous than useful. A new settlement was later established further from the sea, which is why the medieval city is not where the modern one stands.

The demolition is the clearest possible statement of what the Egyptian side had learned, and destroying one's own port to deny it to an enemy is a drastic measure that indicates how serious the threat was understood to be.

The general observation is that a defender who knows where an attack must come has already won a great deal, and the invariance of the Damietta landing across three campaigns handed the Egyptians that advantage every time.

## The Flood

The physical mechanism that destroyed two of the three invasions deserves its own treatment, since it is the article's explanation for the repetition.

### What role did the Nile flood play in defeating the invasions?

A decisive one in both major campaigns. The annual inundation raises the river and fills the canal network from summer into autumn, converting the Delta into a landscape of water and mud in which an army cannot manoeuvre. Egyptian commanders could also open canals deliberately to flood ground behind an advancing force, which is what ended the Fifth Crusade.

The annual cycle is the underlying fact, and the article on taxation describes the inundation rising through the summer, cresting in late summer or early autumn and receding through the winter.

The effect on the Delta is total. The article on how the Arab conquest reshaped Egypt describes a landscape that is cultivated ground for part of the year and a sheet of water for another part, and an army caught in the wrong phase has nowhere to stand.

The deliberate use is the more interesting element. The canal network can be opened at chosen points, which lets a defender put water where an attacker is or behind him, and this is what ended the Fifth Crusade in 1221 without a battle.

The timing errors are therefore the campaigns' fatal mistakes. The Fifth Crusade advanced in summer, and the Seventh delayed at Damietta until the season had turned against it.

The knowledge available to the crusaders is a real question. Local informants existed, the pattern was not secret, and the article's later section asks whether the commanders understood what they were doing.

The Egyptian awareness was total, since a government whose entire fiscal system runs on the flood measures it obsessively, which the article on taxation describes through the measuring installations that recorded each year's level.

The general observation is that in this theatre the river was a weapon under Egyptian control, and any invasion that did not plan around it was defeated before it started.

## How Egypt Won

The Egyptian conduct of these campaigns is worth setting out as a method rather than a series of events, since it was consistent across all three.

### How did Egypt defeat the Crusader invasions?

By refusing decisive battle, fortifying the approaches upriver, using the canal network and the flood, and cutting the invaders' communications with the coast. In every campaign the crusaders were allowed to take Damietta and advance, and were then trapped in a landscape they could not manoeuvre in, cut off from supply and forced to surrender.

The first element is not defending the coast. Damietta was allowed to fall in every campaign, either after a siege or without one, because holding a coastal port against a fleet supplied enemy is the hardest possible task.

The second is the fortified position upriver, and Mansurah was established and garrisoned precisely to block the route to Cairo after the experience of the Fifth Crusade.

The third is control of the water, both the canals as an instrument and the river as a supply line, which is what cut the Seventh Crusade off from its base.

The fourth is avoidance of decisive engagement, since the article on Saladin and the crusades explains that a formed crusader army is extremely dangerous and the Egyptian side had no need to fight one.

The fifth is patience, since an invading army in the Delta degrades on its own through disease, hunger and the season, and a defender who waits is doing something rather than nothing.

The sixth is the willingness to negotiate, and the offers made in both campaigns show a government that preferred a settlement to a battle and got one twice.

The general observation is that this is a coherent defensive doctrine rather than a series of improvisations, and its consistency across thirty years and two dynasties suggests it was understood as doctrine at the time.

### Did the Crusaders ever capture Cairo?

No. No crusader force ever reached Cairo. The Fifth Crusade advanced perhaps two thirds of the way and was trapped by flooding; the Seventh reached Mansurah, roughly halfway, and was destroyed there and on the retreat. The capital was never seriously threatened by any of the invasions.

## The Mamluk Consequence

The most important result of the invasions was one nobody intended, and it changed Egyptian history for two and a half centuries.

### How did the invasions lead to the Mamluk takeover?

The last effective Ayyubid sultan died during the Seventh Crusade, his widow concealed the death while the campaign continued, and the military slave corps he had built won the fighting at Mansurah and afterward. When his heir arrived and alienated that corps, they killed him and took power themselves, which is the origin of the Mamluk sultanate.

The corps in question had been purchased, trained and quartered by the sultan on an island in the river, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt describes it as the decisive military force of the campaign.

The performance at Mansurah is what gave them the standing, since the destruction of the crusader vanguard in the streets is attributed to them in the accounts.

The concealment of the sultan's death by his widow is the political manoeuvre that held the campaign together and it is remarkable in its own right.

The heir's arrival was the crisis. A man summoned from a distant province, bringing his own people and displacing the commanders who had just won a war, is in an impossible position, and the article on Ayyubid Egypt describes the killing that followed.

The negotiation with the captured king was conducted partly by the new regime, which means the ransom of a king of France was settled with men who had taken power days earlier.

The pattern this completes is the one the article on Ayyubid Egypt traces across three dynasties: an imported military establishment destroying the regime that created it, in the Tulunid case, the Fatimid case and now the Ayyubid.

The step taken here was new, and the article on the Mamluk sultanate describes it: the soldiers dispensed with dynasty entirely and ruled in their own name, which produced the most durable Egyptian regime of the medieval period.

The general observation is that the crusader invasions' greatest effect on Egypt was to concentrate power in a professional military corps at exactly the moment the ruling dynasty lost its head, which is an outcome no participant intended and none could have predicted.

## Why It Always Failed

Assembling the reasons in one place is worth doing, since the repetition is the article's subject.

### Why did every invasion fail at the same place?

Because every invasion faced identical constraints. The only practical landing was Damietta, the only route inland was the eastern branch, the flood and canals made the Delta impassable for much of the year, and the defence knew all of it. They failed at the same point because no other campaign was available.

The first reason is that the objective was too far. Cairo lies well inland from the coast, and an army supplied by sea gets weaker with every kilometre it advances.

The second is that the route was predictable, which the article's section on Damietta explains, and a defender who knows where you must go can prepare the ground.

The third is the water, both as a barrier and as a weapon, which the article's section on the flood describes.

The fourth is the time constraint. A crusading expedition has a limited lifespan, since men come to fulfil a vow and go home, while a defender has unlimited time.

The fifth is the divided command, and the article's section on the refused offer describes an authority structure in which an ideological faction could override military judgment.

The sixth is disease, which killed more crusaders than any Egyptian force did, and the long static periods at Damietta in both campaigns were lethal.

The seventh is that the Egyptian side had a working doctrine, described in the article's section on how Egypt won, and applied it consistently.

The general observation is that the crusader strategic analysis was correct and the operational problem was insoluble with the technology available, which is a distinction worth holding, since being right about the objective is no use if the objective cannot be reached.

## Studying the Invasions

For a student, this topic organizes around one claim and a short list of dates.

Be able to state the Egypt is the key argument, which is that the crusaders attacked Egypt because it funded their opponents. Be able to date the Fifth Crusade to 1218 through 1221 and the Seventh to 1248 through 1250. Be able to explain that Damietta was the landing point every time because it controls the only practical route inland. Be able to describe Mansurah as a tactical stalemate that was strategically fatal. Be able to give the capture of the king of France in 1250 as the greatest crusader disaster. And be able to connect the campaign to the Mamluk takeover.

## Myths Worth Correcting

Six claims about these campaigns circulate and each misleads.

The first is that the crusades were fought in Palestine. Two of the numbered expeditions were directed at Egypt from the outset, the attacks of the 1160s were as well, and the greatest single crusader defeat of the entire movement happened in the Egyptian Delta rather than anywhere near Jerusalem.

The second is that the crusaders never came close to succeeding. The Fifth Crusade took Damietta, held it for eighteen months and advanced roughly two thirds of the way toward Cairo before the water stopped it, and the Seventh took the same city in a single day.

The third is that Jerusalem was never on offer during these campaigns. It was offered in 1219, together with the True Cross and a long truce, and the offer was refused by the papal legate over the objections of the military commanders.

The fourth is that Mansurah was a crusader defeat in the field. The vanguard was destroyed and the main army held its position, and the campaign was lost afterward through blockade and starvation.

The fifth is that the king of France was killed or humiliated. He was captured, correctly treated, ransomed for an enormous sum and remained in the region for years afterward.

The sixth is that the invasions achieved nothing at all. They achieved nothing their planners wanted, and they produced the Mamluk sultanate, which the article on that regime describes as the power that stopped the Mongol advance and then eliminated the crusader states entirely within four decades.

A seventh belief is a framing error rather than a myth, which is treating the failures as incompetence on the part of the commanders. The strategic reasoning behind the invasions was correct and the operational problem they faced was insoluble with thirteenth century logistics, and confusing those two things obscures what the campaigns actually demonstrate about the relationship between analysis and feasibility.

## The Verdict

The argument this article has defended is that the crusaders were entirely right about Egypt and entirely wrong about what could be done with the insight, and that keeping those two judgments apart is the whole of understanding these campaigns.

The analysis was sound and it deserves saying clearly, because these campaigns are usually written up as folly. The article on Ayyubid Egypt argues that Egyptian wealth funded the Muslim war effort and that a rich secure base beats a contested frontier over time. Crusader strategists reached exactly the same conclusion independently, identified the correct centre of gravity, and committed their largest expeditions to acting on it.

The execution was impossible with the means available, which is a different criticism entirely. An army supplied by sea, attacking a capital far inland, across a landscape cut by canals and flooded for months of every year, against a defender who knew the only available route and controlled the water on it, was attempting something the logistics of the thirteenth century could not deliver regardless of how well the attempt was organized.

The repetition is the proof and it is the article's strongest evidence. Three campaigns, separated by thirty years in the case of the two great expeditions, landing at the same port, following the same branch and failing at roughly the same point, is not a sequence of coincidences or a record of individual incompetence. It is a demonstration that the constraint was structural and that the campaigns were shaped by the map rather than by the men.

The Egyptian doctrine was the other half of it, and the article's section on how Egypt won sets out a method applied consistently across three campaigns and two dynasties: concede the coast, since it cannot be held against a fleet; fortify the river upstream at the point where an advance must pass; control the canals and the water; refuse a decisive battle that is not needed; cut the invader's communications with his base; and then wait, because an army in the Delta destroys itself.

The consequence nobody intended was the Mamluk sultanate. The article on the Mamluk sultanate describes a regime born directly out of the last invasion, created when the corps that won the campaign killed the arriving heir and ruled in its own name, and that regime then stopped the Mongol advance in Syria within a decade and removed the crusader states from the region entirely within four.

The irony that closes the subject is therefore complete and it operates twice. The attacks of the 1160s, launched to prevent Egypt falling under Syrian control, produced exactly the union of Egypt and Syria they were meant to prevent. The invasions of the thirteenth century, launched to break the power that threatened the crusader states, produced the power that finished them.

The qualifications are those stated throughout and several are substantial. Force numbers on both sides are unusable, since no muster records survive and medieval chroniclers used figures rhetorically. Command decisions are attributed retrospectively by writers who knew how the campaigns ended. The sequence of events at Mansurah is reported differently by each side, particularly the initial success and its collapse. And the ransom figure for the king is given variously across the accounts.

What remains is the observation this article opened with. In 1219 a crusader army holding a port in the Delta was offered Jerusalem, the True Cross and a long truce in exchange for giving it back, and it refused because it wanted Egypt instead. Two years later it surrendered the port and received nothing. Thirty one years after the offer, a king of France sat as a prisoner a few kilometres upriver from the same port, negotiating his release with men who had seized power in Cairo the previous week, and Jerusalem was still not theirs and never would be.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What were the Crusader invasions of Egypt?

A series of European military expeditions directed at Egypt rather than at Palestine, spanning roughly a century. The earliest were the repeated attacks of the 1160s under the king of Jerusalem, sometimes allied with Fatimid factions. The Fifth Crusade landed at Damietta in 1218, took the city after an eighteen month siege and advanced toward Cairo before being trapped by flooding in 1221. The Seventh Crusade landed at the same port in 1249, took it immediately, and was destroyed at Mansurah and on the retreat in 1250, with the king of France captured.

### Q: Why did the Crusaders attack Egypt?

Because Egypt was where the money was. Egyptian revenue funded the armies the crusader states faced in Syria and Palestine, so removing Egypt from the equation would have starved their opponents of troops, supply and pay, and holding it would have made the crusader states permanent. The strategic analysis was entirely correct and it mirrors the argument this series makes from the other direction: a rich, defensible base attached to a contested frontier is what wins a protracted war, and the way to defeat such an arrangement is to take the base.

### Q: What was the siege of Damietta?

There were two, and the first is the more remarkable. In 1218 the Fifth Crusade landed near the port at the mouth of the eastern Nile branch and besieged it for eighteen months, including taking a tower in midstream that held a chain barring the river. The city finally fell in November 1219, less by assault than by collapse, since starvation and disease had reduced the garrison and population to a fraction of their numbers. In 1249 the Seventh Crusade took the same city in a single day, because its garrison withdrew rather than defending it.

### Q: Did the Crusaders ever capture Cairo?

No. No crusader force ever reached the capital. The Fifth Crusade advanced perhaps two thirds of the distance from Damietta before being trapped by the rising flood and Egyptian control of the canals in 1221. The Seventh Crusade reached Mansurah, roughly halfway, where its vanguard was destroyed in the streets and the main army was subsequently blockaded and starved. The attacks of the 1160s reached the Delta and the area south of Cairo but never took the city. In a century of invasions the capital was never seriously threatened.

### Q: How did Egypt defeat the Crusader invasions?

By a consistent doctrine rather than by a series of improvisations. The coast was conceded in every campaign, since holding a port against a fleet supplied enemy is nearly impossible. A fortified position was established upriver at Mansurah to block the route to Cairo. The canal network was used both as a barrier and as a weapon, since canals can be opened to put water behind an advancing army. Decisive battle was avoided, communications with the coast were cut, and the invaders were allowed to degrade through disease, hunger and the season.

### Q: What was the Fifth Crusade in Egypt?

A large European expedition that landed near Damietta in 1218 and besieged it for eighteen months in appalling conditions before taking it in November 1219. It then occupied the city for a further eighteen months while its leaders argued about what to do next, and finally advanced up the river toward Cairo in the summer of 1221, which was the worst possible timing. The Egyptians opened canals to flood the ground behind it, and the army surrendered Damietta in exchange for a safe withdrawal, having achieved nothing in three years.

### Q: Why was Egypt a Crusader target?

For three reasons that reinforced each other. Its wealth funded the Muslim war effort, which made it the strategic centre of gravity of the whole conflict. Its position adjacent to Palestine with a Mediterranean coastline made it reachable by European fleets, unlike inland Syrian centres. And during the 1160s its government had visibly collapsed, importing foreign armies to settle its own factional disputes, which looked to every observer like an opening. The analysis was right; what defeated it was the ground rather than the reasoning.

### Q: What was the Seventh Crusade and Louis IX?

An exceptionally well prepared expedition led by the king of France, who spent years assembling resources, building a port of embarkation and financing the campaign. It landed at Damietta in June 1249 and took the city at once. After delaying for months it advanced up the eastern branch, fought a costly and inconclusive engagement at Mansurah in February 1250, was then blockaded and cut off from its base, and surrendered in April with the king himself taken prisoner. He was ransomed for an enormous sum plus the return of Damietta.

### Q: Why did every invasion fail at the same place?

Because every invasion faced identical constraints and had no alternative plan available. The only practical landing was Damietta, since a sea supplied army needs a harbour on the branch it will follow inland. The only practical route was the eastern branch, since the ground between branches floods and is cut by canals. The flood made the Delta impassable for much of the year and could be directed deliberately by opening canals. And the Egyptian defence knew all of this, which meant the ground upriver could be fortified in advance.

### Q: What happened at the Battle of Mansurah?

A crusader vanguard crossed a canal by a ford, surprised and broke the Egyptian camp, and then pursued into the town itself, where heavy cavalry in narrow streets loses every advantage it has. It was cut off and destroyed, including a substantial part of the military orders' strength. The main army then crossed and held its position against counterattacks through the day. The battle was therefore tactically indecisive, since the crusaders held the field, and strategically fatal, since they were now stranded far from their base with their best troops dead.

### Q: How was Louis IX captured and ransomed?

His army, cut off from Damietta by Egyptian control of the river and destroyed by disease and starvation, attempted a retreat in April 1250 and was overtaken before reaching safety. The king was taken with the remnant of his force. He was treated correctly, in the ordinary manner of the period's ransom conventions, and released for an enormous cash sum together with the surrender of Damietta. The negotiations were complicated by a revolution occurring on the Egyptian side while they proceeded. He remained in the crusader states for several years afterward.

### Q: What role did the Nile flood play in defeating the invasions?

A decisive one in both major campaigns. The annual inundation raises the river and fills the canal network from summer into autumn, turning the Delta into a landscape of water and mud where an army cannot manoeuvre or supply itself. Beyond the natural cycle, canals could be opened deliberately at chosen points to put water behind an advancing force, which is precisely how the Fifth Crusade was ended in 1221 without a battle being fought. A government whose entire fiscal system runs on measuring the flood understood this instrument perfectly.

### Q: Why did the Crusaders refuse the offer of Jerusalem?

Because the papal legate holding ultimate authority over the Fifth Crusade insisted on continuing, over the objections of the secular commanders and the local barons, who favoured acceptance. His argument had genuine force: Jerusalem without its defensible hinterland had proved impossible to hold before, and Egypt was the greater strategic prize. The counterargument had more force, since the offer delivered the movement's stated objective at no cost. The outcome vindicated the acceptors entirely, since the campaign ended two years later with nothing gained.

### Q: What was the papal legate's role in the Fifth Crusade?

He held ultimate authority as the pope's representative, which gave him a veto over decisions the military commanders would have made differently. He refused the Egyptian offer of Jerusalem, the True Cross and a long truce in 1219. He insisted on the advance up the river in the summer of 1221 against military advice about the season. Both contemporary and later accounts generally hold him responsible for the campaign's failure. The episode illustrates the danger of a divided command in which an ideological faction can override operational judgment.

### Q: How did the invasions lead to the Mamluk takeover?

The last effective Ayyubid sultan died during the Seventh Crusade, and his widow concealed the death while the campaign continued. The military slave corps he had purchased, trained and quartered on an island in the river won the fighting at Mansurah and afterward, which gave them enormous standing. When his heir arrived from a distant province and alienated the men who had just won the war, they killed him and took power in their own name. That is the origin of the sultanate that then ruled Egypt for two and a half centuries.

### Q: What were the earlier attacks under Amalric of Jerusalem?

A series of expeditions across the 1160s aimed at controlling Egypt or extracting tribute from it, launched into the collapse of the Fatimid regime and competing directly with Syrian expeditions pursuing the same objective. Alliances shifted constantly, with crusader and Syrian forces sometimes backing opposite sides of the same Egyptian factional dispute. The nearest approach came in 1168. The outcome was precisely what the campaigns had been intended to prevent: a Syrian army installed in Cairo, which produced the united Egypt and Syria of the following century.

### Q: Why was Damietta the landing point every time?

Because it sits at the mouth of the eastern branch of the Nile, which was the only navigable route by which an army supplied from the sea could advance toward Cairo. A fleet needs a harbour, the harbour needs to be on the branch the army will follow, and the ground between branches is cut by canals and floods annually. Alexandria was considered in several campaigns and rejected, since the western approach involves a longer and more difficult route. There was effectively no alternative, which handed the defence an enormous advantage.

### Q: What happened to Damietta after the invasions?

The Egyptians demolished its fortifications and eventually the city itself after the crusaders surrendered it in 1250, on the reasoning that a port which had twice served as a crusader base was more dangerous to hold than to destroy. A new settlement was established later at a position further from the sea, which is why the medieval city does not stand where the modern one does. Destroying one's own major port is a drastic measure and it indicates exactly how seriously the recurring threat was understood.

### Q: Did the Crusaders understand Egyptian geography?

Partly, and the gap between what they knew and what they acted on is the interesting question. Local informants were available, the flood cycle was no secret, and the Fifth Crusade's experience was documented before the Seventh was launched. Yet the Fifth advanced in summer and the Seventh delayed at Damietta until the season had turned. The likeliest explanation is not ignorance but the structure of a crusading expedition, in which men serve for a limited period and the pressure to act outweighs the argument for waiting.

### Q: How did the invasions affect ordinary Egyptians?

Severely in the affected districts and hardly at all elsewhere. The Delta towns along the line of advance experienced siege, occupation, requisition and the destruction that accompanies a campaign, and Damietta's population endured an eighteen month siege in the first invasion and displacement in the second. Beyond the immediate theatre, the working year continued as the article on taxation describes it, set by the flood and the harvest rather than by the war. The demolition of Damietta afterward displaced a substantial urban population permanently.
