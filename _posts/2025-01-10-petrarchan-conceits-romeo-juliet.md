## Petrarchan Conceits in Romeo and Juliet: The Verdict in Brief

Petrarchan conceits are the load-bearing cliches of this play, and the play knows it. The verdict argued across the pages that follow is blunt: Romeo and Juliet does not merely decorate its first act with the frozen-and-burning machinery of the sonnet tradition and then forget the machinery once the real love begins. It runs the machinery at full power, lets the audience hear every gear, hands the mockery of the machinery to the wittiest man in Verona, and then shows a fourteen-year-old girl rebuilding the same devices so that they carry weight they never carried in the borrowed versions. The Petrarchan conceit in this play is a system under audit. Romeo deploys it as a complete technology of desire for a woman who never speaks. Mercutio exposes it as a technology, naming Petrarch himself in open court and demoting Laura to a kitchen wench. Juliet accepts the system's raw materials, the sun, the stars, the sea, the saint, and spends them on a man who answers back, which the system was never designed to survive. What dies in the play is not the conceit. What dies is the conceit's alibi, the pretence that the figures were feelings.

![Petrarchan conceits in Romeo and Juliet, the borrowed machinery of the sighing lover examined - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Three claims support the verdict, and each is testable against the text. First, the early conceits form a system rather than a scatter of pretty lines: siege, blazon, paradox, and elemental physics interlock in Romeo's first speeches, and the system has rules an audience of the 1590s could recite from the sonnet sequences on their own shelves. Second, the mockery is internal and early, not a modern critic's imposition. Mercutio laughs at the manner in Act 1 Scene 4, anatomizes it in the conjuring of Act 2 Scene 1, and delivers its obituary in Act 2 Scene 4, all before the lovers have exchanged a single private word beyond the feast. Third, the play outbids what it mocks. Juliet's speeches take the hyperbolic scale of the tradition, its suns and seas and star-fields, and attach the scale to propositions that can be checked: a contract, a refusal of false oaths, a wedding night awaited in plain impatience. The figures survive the audit because a speaker finally means them at the size she states them.

The evidence is arranged as a before-and-after. The ledger is the argument in miniature: nothing in the second column is plainer because it is smaller. It is plainer because it is answerable.

A note on the cliche the article is written against. The received account of the play treats its love-talk as a single sincere substance, the poetry of young love at full flood, and on that account the Rosaline speeches and the orchard speeches differ only in their object, as if a change of girl explained a change of language. Restoring the conceit system dissolves that account. The two bodies of speech differ in kind, in ownership, and in their relation to fact, and the play marks every difference itself, in dialogue, with named mechanisms: translation, diagnosis, correction, repricing. A reader who cannot tell a recitation from a reply will misread both, praising the recital for a passion it is designed to simulate and underrating the replies for a plainness they never aim at. The sections below therefore proceed as an audit rather than an appreciation: system first, performance second, mockery third, rebuilding fourth, residue last, with the critics called as witnesses once the evidence is in. Quotations follow René Weis's Arden Third Series text and are cited by act, scene, and line, kept short, because the argument turns on mechanisms that a few lines at a time expose better than anthologies do.

One complication governs the whole reading and must be faced rather than waved past. Romeo never wholly stops figuring his feeling through inherited images. In the tomb, hours of stage time after the Friar has diagnosed his early love as reading by rote, Romeo still reaches for a conceit, the merriment of dying men as a lightning before death, and still casts Death as a rival paramour keeping Juliet in the dark. The residue is real. The argument here is that the residue convicts the system rather than the man: by Act 5 the inherited figure is made to describe a fact the audience can see, a corpse that looks alive, a keeper who is not a lover. The machinery remains; the lying has gone out of it. That distinction, between a device and the use a speaker makes of it, is the play's own, and the sections below earn it scene by scene.

## What Is a Petrarchan Conceit? The System Before the Play

A conceit, in the usage the period inherited, is not a compliment and not a metaphor in the loose modern sense. It is a comparison argued like a case. The Petrarchan conceit takes a resemblance, often a strained one, and then works it: extends it, subdivides it, draws consequences from it, and holds the beloved and the lover inside its logic for the length of a sonnet or a speech. When Romeo reports that Rosaline has armed herself against Cupid's bow and will not stay the siege of loving terms, the figure is not decoration on a refusal. The figure is the refusal, translated into a military system in which her chastity is a garrison, his suit an assault, and time itself a besieger's ally. A conceit is a small machine for converting feeling into structure. Petrarchism is what happens when the machines are standardized, shared, and handed down ready-made.

The standardization begins with Petrarch's Rime sparse, the sequence of sonnets and canzoni addressed across a lifetime to Laura, a woman seen, desired, unattainable, and finally dead. The habits the English sonneteers inherited from that sequence are specific and enumerable. The beloved is praised by inventory, the blazon that lists brow, eyes, lips, hand, and foot as if the lady were an estate being surveyed. Her refusal is constitutional, not personal: she is ice because the genre requires a cold pole against which the lover's fire can define itself. The lover's state is reported as a set of coupled symptoms, burning and freezing, hoping and despairing, living and dying, each term requiring its opposite to be intelligible. Time is the enemy and the only editor: the sequence stretches one unchanging situation across hundreds of poems, so that the conceits, not events, supply all the motion. English writers took the kit over wholesale. Wyatt carried Petrarch into the Henrician court and roughened the lines; Surrey regularized the English sonnet form that Shakespeare would later use; and in the early 1590s, after the posthumous printing of Sidney's Astrophil and Stella in 1591, the sequence became a fashion with a market. Daniel's Delia, Constable's Diana, Lodge's Phillis, and Drayton's Idea all rehearsed the same machinery within a few years of the play. The first audiences of Romeo and Juliet did not need the system explained. Half of them had been writing it.

The kit has a second inventory, less often named, and the play's first act is unintelligible without it: the lover's own body, itemized as faithfully as the lady's. Where the blazon lists her brow, eyes, and lips, the symptom catalogue lists his sighs, tears, pallor, wakefulness, and loss of appetite, the standard issue of the suffering sonneteer. The two inventories are gears in one machine. Her perfection is proved by the completeness of her list; his sincerity is proved by the completeness of his. Neither list can be checked against a person, which is why the machine runs so smoothly for so long: the lady's features are never seen at the distance the blazon pretends, and the lover's symptoms are performed in company, for witnesses, as a young man's credentials. A sigh, in this economy, is a publication. Romeo's friends know his symptoms before they know his lady's name, and they discuss the symptoms as connoisseurs, which is to say the machine has already socialized them. When Benvolio prescribes the standard cure, a new object for the eyes, he is not stepping outside the system. He is operating its maintenance schedule, since the kit always assumed the lady was replaceable and the suffering was the constant.

The sequence form adds a third component, a career. A sonnet sequence is not a diary but a profession conducted in public: the lover's unchanging situation, renewed poem by poem, advertises his constancy, his craft, and his availability for patronage in a single gesture. Petrarch made a life's work orbit one woman's refusals, and the English imitators understood the arrangement's convenience. A mistress who yielded would end the sequence and the career together. This is why the kit's ladies are so uniformly armed, distant, and silent, and why their cruelty is always described as a constitutional feature rather than a decision: the genre's economics require a beloved who functions as a fixed star, and a fixed star's only duty is not to move. The conceits are the star's recorded light, impressive exactly in proportion to the distance they advertise. Shakespeare's play collapses the distance to the width of a dance floor, then to the height of a window, and the recorded light has to be re-measured at every step. The re-measuring is the plot of Romeo's language.

Nor was the kit ever quite as solemn as its worst practitioners. The tradition carried a counter-current from the start: complaints that the lady's tyranny is a fiction the lover maintains for copy, admissions that the suffering is a chosen costume, parodies of the inventory in which the beloved is praised for what she lacks. Petrarch himself ironizes his posture often enough that his English heirs inherited the irony with the liturgy. This matters for the verdict, because it means the play's mockery cannot be credited to Shakespeare's modernity breaking a medieval mould. The mould had cracks by design. What Shakespeare adds is a theatre: bodies, timing, an answering voice, and a friend laughing in the street, all the machinery a lyric sequence lacks, turned against the posture the sequence made possible.

That competence matters, because Shakespeare builds the play's first act for connoisseurs of the kit. The blazon, to take the system's most mechanical part, is a liturgy of the beloved's features in a fixed descending order, and its function is quietly possessive: to itemize a woman is to claim knowledge of her, and to claim knowledge is to claim a kind of title. The frozen-and-burning symptom list performs the complementary move on the lover's side, itemizing his interior as the blazon itemizes her exterior. Between the two inventories stands the central transaction of Petrarchism: a woman who does not speak is converted into a text that a man composes, and the man's suffering in composing it becomes the proof of his depth. Every element of Romeo's Rosaline suit, as the next section shows, is drawn from this liturgy, and every element will be tested by the arrival of a beloved who composes back.

The symptom side of the system had a medical warrant that modern readers miss, and recovering it sharpens the play's joke. In the Galenic physiology every educated Elizabethan absorbed, the lover's coupled symptoms were a climate of the body: the heart's fire and the liver's cold, humours in revolt, sighs as the body's venting of overheated spirits, tears as its condensation. To burn and freeze simultaneously was not a poet's paradox but a diagnosis with a literature behind it, the melancholy of love as a recognized distemper with stages and prognoses. The conceit system borrowed medicine's authority the way it borrowed war's and theology's, and a sonneteer's symptom list functioned as a case history he had written for himself. This is why the play's plain speakers keep reaching for clinical language against the kit. The Friar gathers herbs and talks dosage; Benvolio prescribes displacement therapy in the physics of counter-fire; even Montague reports his son's condition by timetable and symptom like a physician taking a history. The drama sets two diagnostic systems against each other, the lover's self-written case history, in which the disease is incurable because incurability is the credential, and the practitioners' case history, in which the disease is a habit of the eyes with a known cure. Act 1 belongs to the first system. The rest of the play is the second system's long consultation.

The system's elegance should not be undersold, because the play does not undersell it. A standardized kit lets a poet signal a whole emotional situation in four words and lets an audience measure a speaker's deviation from the standard with precision. When Juliet later says that her bounty is as boundless as the sea, the line lands with its full force only on ears that have priced a hundred sonnet seas and know the going rate of the comparison. Shakespeare's critique of Petrarchism is an insider's critique, mounted with the tradition's own tools, and its target is narrow: not hyperbole, not inventory, not paradox, but the posture that uses all three to keep the beloved silent and the lover interesting. The machinery is acquitted. The posture is convicted.

## Act 1 Scene 1: Romeo Performs the Whole System at Once

Romeo's first entrance is a recital. Benvolio has spent the scene's middle movement describing a young man who seals himself in artificial night, avoids company, and weeps at dawn, and when the young man finally walks onstage he confirms the diagnosis by performing it. His opening exchanges with Benvolio run through the kit in catalogue order. Love is first an element system: "Love is a smoke raised with the fume of sighs; / Being purged, a fire sparkling in lovers' eyes; / Being vexed, a sea nourished with lovers' tears" (1.1.190-192). The conceit is worked exactly as the manuals prescribe. One vehicle, smoke, is subdivided into states; the states are mapped onto the lover's symptoms; the mapping closes with a definition offered as a question, "What is it else? A madness most discreet, / A choking gall, and a preserving sweet" (1.1.193-194). Nothing in the speech is felt wrongly. Everything in it is felt from stock.

The recital has been framed, before Romeo speaks a word of it, by a father's clinical report that reads like a symptom catalogue compiled by someone else. Montague describes a son who flees the light, makes himself an artificial night, and weeps the morning dew fuller; Lady Montague and Benvolio add the sycamore grove westward of the city and the hour, an hour after dawn, at which the condition performs itself (1.1.124-140). The family speaks of the love as physicians speak of a humour, by its timetable and its hiding places, and Montague's summary could stand as the system's motto from the receiving end: "Could we but learn from whence his sorrows grow, / We would as willingly give cure as know" (1.1.149-150). Nobody in the scene, notably, asks after the lady. The sorrows are the subject; their object is a rumour. When Romeo finally enters and justifies the report, his first account of his state reaches for the feud's vocabulary before the lady's: "Here's much to do with hate, but more with love. / Why then, O brawling love, O loving hate" (1.1.166-167). The conceit machine and the hate machine share a grammar of yoked opposites, and the play's opening movement wants the audience to hear the kinship. A city that talks in antitheses about its enemies will raise sons who talk in antitheses about their mistresses, and neither talk requires its object to be present, known, or even correctly named.

The symptom catalogue follows, and it is here that the play tips its hand earliest. Romeo reels off the coupled contradictions of the Petrarchan condition: "O brawling love! O loving hate! / O anything, of nothing first create! / O heavy lightness, serious vanity, / Misshapen chaos of well-seeming forms, / Feather of lead, bright smoke, cold fire, sick health" (1.1.176-180). The compressed figure of oxymoron is one device inside the larger conceit system, and its full career in the play, from this chain to Juliet's very different chain in Act 3, belongs to the companion study of [Oxymoron and Antithesis: 'Loving Hate'](/2025/01/03/oxymoron-antithesis-romeo-juliet/). What matters for the system is the chain's behaviour as a chain. The terms accelerate, thin out, and begin to comment on themselves: a chaos of well-seeming forms is not a description of Rosaline, about whom the speech says nothing at all, but a description of the speech, forms seeming well while signifying in a heap. Shakespeare lets the catalogue run one link too long, and the extra link is the sound of a device idling. The audience is being taught to hear the difference between a figure under load and a figure coasting, and the lesson is delivered before Mercutio says a word.

The recital's close confesses the arrangement in so many words. Benvolio, probing for the person inside the performance, asks whether this love of Romeo's is not misread, and Romeo answers with the system's doctrine of the dissolved self: "I have lost myself; I am not here; / This is not Romeo, he's some other where" (1.1.195-196). Absence is the Petrarchan lover's final credential. The lady's absence from the scene is matched by the lover's absence from his own body, and the two absences guarantee each other, since a self that has checked out cannot be asked to verify anything. The play remembers the formula and answers it exactly, two acts later, in the mouth of the girl the system could not process. Juliet, discovered at her window, does not lose herself in anybody's conceit. She specifies, conditions, corrects, and schedules, the most present speaker in the play, and her presence is the standard against which Romeo's Act 1 absence stands measured. The transformation article owns the full itinerary of that education; the system-level entry in the ledger is simple. The kit taught a lover to be interesting by being absent. The play teaches him, at ruinous speed, to be present.

Notice what the recital never contains: a fact about Rosaline. She has a name, eventually, and a vow, reported later, and that is the whole of her. The conceits have no need of her particulars because the system is designed to run on an absent object; indeed, particulars would jam it, since a real woman's actual preferences cannot be sieged, inventoried, and symptom-mapped with the same fluency. Benvolio, the scene's resident plain speaker, answers the recital with the pragmatist's shrug and a prescription, examine other beauties, which treats the system as a habit of the eyes rather than a condition of the heart. The Friar will make the same diagnosis in politer Latin two acts later. The play's first act is often described as establishing Romeo's love. It is more exact to say that it establishes Romeo's fluency, and fluency, in a borrowed language, is the problem the rest of the play is built to solve.

### Which single speech in Act 1 Scene 1 contains the whole Petrarchan system in miniature?

The Rosaline report at 1.1.199-219 contains the whole system in miniature. Within twenty lines Romeo deploys the siege, the armed goddess, the wound-proof chastity, the economics of wasted beauty, and the lover's vow to keep besieging, every major device of the sonnet kit assembled around a woman who is given no line, no entrance, and no reply.

The speech deserves slow attention because it is the baseline against which every later revision is measured, and because its craftsmanship is genuine. Shakespeare does not give Romeo bad conceits for Rosaline. He gives him good ones, correctly assembled, and lets their correctness be the indictment.

## The Siege of Rosaline: Conceits Aimed at a Woman Who Never Answers

The report begins in the military register that courtship-by-conceit always keeps in reserve: "She will not stay the siege of loving terms, / Nor bide th'encounter of assailing eyes, / Nor ope her lap to saint-seducing gold" (1.1.210-212). Every term does double duty. The siege casts the lover as an army and the lady as a fortified town, which flatters his persistence and excuses its failure in a single move, since towns are not blamed for holding out and armies are not blamed for trying. The assailing eyes convert the blazon's inventory into ordnance: to look at her, in this system, is already to attack her, and her beauty's defence is to be looked at without effect. Saint-seducing gold smuggles in a third system, the economic one, in which access can be purchased and refusal is a pricing decision. Three conceit families, war, vision, and money, are stacked inside three lines, and Rosaline, who has done nothing but decline, is represented as a fortress, a target, and a market simultaneously. The system's productivity is the point. Given any refusal, it can manufacture significance at industrial rates.

The Diana figure raises the report from tactics to theology: "She hath Dian's wit, / And, in strong proof of chastity well armed, / From Love's weak childhood bow she lives unharmed" (1.1.215-217). Diana, the virgin huntress, is the blazon's presiding deity, the form the unyielding mistress takes when the lover wants her refusal to be sacred rather than stubborn. Armed chastity is the Petrarchan lady's standard equipment, and the line's compliment is double-edged in a way Romeo cannot hear: to praise a woman as Diana is to praise her for a vow that excludes the praiser, so that the conceit congratulates the very condition it laments. The system requires this incoherence and calls it refinement. A beloved who could be persuaded would collapse the genre; a beloved figured as a goddess cannot be persuaded by definition, and the lover's endless suit is thereby promoted from failure to devotion.

Buried in the siege report is a four-word exhibit that fuses the system's two master dialects, money and devotion, and the fusion is worth isolating because the feast scene will spend its whole length prising the two apart. Rosaline will not "ope her lap to saint-seducing gold" (1.1.212). Gold that seduces saints: the phrase imagines a currency so potent it could corrupt holiness itself, and imagines the lady's refusal as a treasury declining a bribe at the shrine's door. Courtship, worship, and purchase are one transaction in the figure, which is why the system's lovers are always at once pilgrims and besiegers and bidders, and why its ladies are always at once saints and garrisons and vaults. Romeo utters the fusion as a commonplace, unaware that he has stated the whole tradition's working model in a single line. At the feast, faced with an actual shrine that answers, he will have to separate the dialects in real time: the pilgrim figure survives Juliet's audit because she re-founds it on touch and consent, while the bidder's figure, the gold, is left behind at the orchard wall and does not reappear in his courtship until Mantua, where it buys poison. The system's fused currency is the Rosaline campaign in miniature, and the play's disentangling of it, devotion kept, purchase indicted, is the neatest single summary of the argument this article has been making at scale.

Then the report turns economist, and produces its most revealing conceit: "O, she is rich in beauty, only poor / That, when she dies, with beauty dies her store" (1.1.213-214), expanded a breath later into the posterity argument: "For beauty, starved with her severity, / Cuts beauty off from all posterity" (1.1.218-219). Beauty is capital; chastity is hoarding; children are the interest the capital refuses to earn. The argument was a commonplace of the procreation sonnets that open Shakespeare's own sequence, where a fair youth is urged to breed before his beauty's lease expires, and it sits oddly in a lover's mouth, since it prices the beloved as an asset depreciating in a vault. Romeo delivers it as tenderness. That tonal blindness is the system's signature: inside the kit, any proposition about the beloved, however acquisitive, counts as praise provided the figure is handsome. The woman at the centre of the transaction has by now been a town, a goddess, and an estate. She has not been a person, and the play will spend four acts itemizing the cost of the omission. Readers who want the unseen lady's own dossier, her appearances by report and the strange career of a character built entirely from other people's sentences, will find it in [Romeo's Rosaline Problem: Love Before Juliet](/2022/05/20/romeo-rosaline-infatuation-before-juliet/).

One refinement of the report shows the system at its most intellectually dishonest, and its most accomplished. Romeo concludes the lady's portrait in a paradox of manufacture: she is "too fair, too wise, wisely too fair, / To merit bliss by making me despair" (1.1.223-224). Her perfection, compounded of beauty and wisdom in exactly the kit's proportions, produces his damnation as a by-product, and the by-product is then entered as evidence of the perfection. The logic is a closed loop, and closed loops are the system's native shape: the lady's refusal proves her excellence, her excellence justifies the suit, the suit's failure proves the refusal, and the lover's despair certifies the whole circuit as love. No observation from outside the loop is admissible, which is why Rosaline's actual wishes, supposing the character to have any, are structurally irrelevant. A woman inside this machinery cannot testify. She can only be cited.

The report's closing move completes the system's portrait. Asked by Benvolio whether the suit might prosper, Romeo answers with the system's doctrine of its own permanence: she will not be hit with Cupid's arrow, and he, in consequence, will live her chastity's mourner indefinitely. The vow to persist is the kit's final article of faith, because a Petrarchan lover's identity depends on the suit never ending. Success would retire him; refusal keeps him in office. When the play later gives Romeo a beloved who accepts him inside two days, the speed that moralists deplore is, from the system's point of view, a catastrophe of genre: the machinery was built for a siege of years, and Juliet opens the gates before the engines are warm. The scene that follows the report administers the system's standard cure and, in doing so, routes the whole tragedy through the machinery's last service. Benvolio's prescription is the kit's own maintenance physics: "One fire burns out another's burning, / One pain is lessened by another's anguish" (1.2.45-46), a new object for the eyes to displace the old. Romeo accepts the therapy on the system's own terms, agreeing to attend the Capulet feast to gaze at Rosaline among Verona's beauties and to rejoice in splendour of his own (1.2.100-103). The vow is a conceit's vow, splendour being the feast's expected currency, and it contains the machine's fatal design flaw in miniature. A system that treats the beloved as an optical dosage can always be outbid by a stronger dose. The feast will administer one, in a room where the new object is not an image in a catalogue but a dancer who takes the patient's hand and begins, within fourteen lines, to correct his figures. The siege engines are wheeled to the one wall in Verona behind which the garrison answers back.

The haste debate belongs to another article. The structural point belongs here. The conceit system does not merely describe unrequited love. It requires unrequited love, and it discards, as unusable, any woman who answers.

## The First Laugh: Mercutio Answers the System in Act 1 Scene 4

The mockery begins earlier than the mockery is usually dated. Criticism tends to start the anti-Petrarchan campaign at the conjuring scene, but the campaign opens on the way to the feast, in the street, with Romeo still sighing in full kit. He announces that he cannot dance because he carries a soul of lead, and he describes his condition in the system's purest diction: "I am too sore enpierced with his shaft / To soar with his light feathers" (1.4.19-20). Cupid's arrow, Cupid's feathers, the wound that grounds the bird: three stock properties in two lines, produced on demand for a situation, a walk to a party, that has nothing to do with Rosaline at all. The conceits are now functioning as small talk. That is the moment Mercutio has been waiting for, and his reply is the play's first act of literary criticism: "If love be rough with you, be rough with love; / Prick love for pricking, and you beat love down" (1.4.27-28).

The criticism works by translation. Mercutio takes the system's elevated physics, in which love is a god whose shafts no armour stops, and re-states the identical situation in the idiom of the street brawl and the bedroom, where love is a thing that can be pricked back. Nothing about Romeo's feeling is denied. Its costume is denied. Romeo's rejoinder shows how completely the costume has become the feeling: "Is love a tender thing? It is too rough, / Too rude, too boisterous, and it pricks like thorn" (1.4.25-26). Offered a chance to drop the machinery, he instead feeds Mercutio's verb back into it, pricking converted on the spot into a thorn, the thorn into a new symptom for the catalogue. The exchange is a perfect miniature of the play's method. The mocker supplies the plain word; the system absorbs the plain word and ornaments it; and the audience, laughing, learns to watch for the absorption as the tell. A conceit in Romeo's early mouth is not a lie exactly. It is a reflex, and reflexes are what education has to reach.

The blind Cupid deserves a sentence of his own, because the figure is the system's built-in excuse and Mercutio's joke exposes it. In the iconography every Elizabethan knew from emblem books and tapestries, Cupid is blindfolded: love strikes at random, and the lover is therefore not responsible for his condition, his choice of object, or his conduct under it. Romeo leans on the excuse throughout the Rosaline campaign. His suffering is weather, sent by a blind archer, and his only duty is to endure it picturesquely. Mercutio's Queen Mab speech, delivered minutes later in stage time, replaces the blind god with a tiny charioteer whose visions are manufactured out of the dreamer's own appetites, courtiers dreaming of curtsies, lawyers of fees, soldiers of throats cut. Desire, in Mercutio's counter-system, is not inflicted. It is secreted. The speech's full anatomy belongs to its own article, but its position in the argument is fixed here: before the feast, before Juliet, the play has already staged the debate between the conceit's account of love and the bawdy account, and awarded the wit, if not yet the verdict, to the bawdy.

Queen Mab's catalogue deserves a closer look from the conceit side, because its form is the joke's engine. Mercutio's fairy is described in a miniature inventory of her equipage, atomies for horses, grasshopper wings for a cover, a spider's web for traces, and then the speech inventories her clientele as the blazon inventories a lady, item by item in fixed order: courtiers dreaming of curtsies, lawyers of fees, ladies of kisses, soldiers of cutting throats (1.4.55-95). It is a blazon of other people's appetites, executed with the liturgy's own completeness, and its descent from gallantry to butchery follows the blazon's logic to the blazon's destination. The speech is Mercutio's credentials as a critic. He can run the machine as well as Romeo can, better, since he can run it on the machine's operator, and the fluency licenses the verdict that follows in later scenes. Shakespeare is careful on this point throughout the play. The mockery of the conceits is never assigned to a speaker who could not produce them. It is assigned to the play's most inventive talker, to a friar who builds a rival system from his garden, and to a girl who outbids the originals. Incompetence never gets a vote.

### Where does the play first laugh at the conceits, and whose laugh is it?

Mercutio laughs first, in Act 1 Scene 4, on the way to the feast. His instrument is translation: every elevated term Romeo produces, the shaft, the feathers, the tender thing, is handed back in the vocabulary of pricking and beating down, so the audience hears the machinery change register and recognizes the costume as a costume.

The scene also marks the limit of the laughter, and the limit is part of the play's fairness to its target. At the threshold of the feast Romeo stops the procession with a premonition, his mind misgiving some consequence yet hanging in the stars that will bitterly begin its fearful date that night and expire the term of a despised life in his breast by some vile forfeit of untimely death (1.4.106-113). The speech is not a conceit recital; it is the system's opposite, a presentiment without a figure to hold it, and Mercutio has no translation for it. The wit who answered every sigh with a prick answers the foreboding with logistics, strike drum and march, because bawdy criticism has jurisdiction over postures, not over omens. Shakespeare ends the quartet of street scenes on that boundary deliberately. The machinery of Petrarchism will be laughed at, diagnosed, and rebuilt across the play, but the thing approaching through the feast is not machinery, and the laughter's silence at the threshold is the play conceding, early, the difference between a manner of talking about love and the event that is about to test every manner Verona owns.

The laugh matters because of its timing in the design. Shakespeare does not protect the system through Act 1 and expose it in Act 2. He exposes it in the same hour that establishes it, so that the audience watches the feast scene with double hearing: Romeo's new conceits for Juliet will be judged, in real time, against Mercutio's translation test. Few plays train their audiences so deliberately, or so early, in how to listen to their own love poetry.

## The Feast: Conceits Answered Instead of Recited

The feast scene administers the test. Romeo's first sight of Juliet produces a speech that is still, formally, a cluster of conceits, and the article's argument depends on saying so plainly. "O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright! / It seems she hangs upon the cheek of night / As a rich jewel in an Ethiope's ear: / Beauty too rich for use, for earth too dear" (1.5.44-47). Torches, jewel, the snowy dove among crows that follows at 1.5.48-49: these are the tradition's counters, light and rarity and the dark ground that sets them off. Yet two structural changes have already occurred inside the borrowed forms. The first is grammatical. The Rosaline conceits ran on conditionals and sieges, on what she would not do; the feast conceits run on similes anchored to a present object, "it seems she hangs", and a simile, however extravagant, points at something in the room. The second change is that the object talks back, and what she talks back in is the system's own highest form.

The shared sonnet of the feast is the conceit system's repair attempted from inside. Romeo opens with the pilgrim figure, his lips as two blushing pilgrims at her shrine, his hand's touch a sin to be smoothed. In a sequence sonnet the figure would run its fourteen lines unopposed and close on the lover's solitary point. Here the supposed shrine answers in the same form, corrects the figure's theology, extends its logic, and improves it: "Good pilgrim, you do wrong your hand too much, / Which mannerly devotion shows in this; / For saints have hands that pilgrims' hands do touch, / And palm to palm is holy palmers' kiss" (1.5.97-100). The conceit survives the exchange only by becoming a negotiation. Its terms are now jointly owned, and its conclusion, the kiss, is reached by argument rather than asserted by posture. Juliet's closing verdict on the whole performance, "You kiss by th' book" (1.5.111), is the play's most compact piece of criticism: the book is named, the technique is graded, and the grade is affectionate. The sonnet form itself, its quatrains and its couplet, is the subject of the companion article on the play's sonnet architecture; the point owned here is narrower. A conceit answered is a conceit changed in kind, from recitation to contract, and the feast is where the play first proves it.

### How much of the Rosaline manner survives the feast scene itself?

Almost none of its machinery, and all of its scale. The siege, the symptom catalogue, and the hoarded-beauty economics vanish within the scene and never return in Romeo's mouth. What survives is the hyperbolic reach, torches outshone and beauty priced above use, now attached to similes about a woman standing in the room who is about to correct his figures.

The exchange's sequel administers a quieter test, and the system fails it in an instructive way. After the first kiss the lovers begin again in the same measure, Romeo's sin purged and demanded back, Juliet's answers keeping the rhyme alive, and a second sonnet visibly assembles itself, quatrain by quatrain, until the Nurse's summons breaks it off at four lines and the form is left unfinished on the floor of the feast (1.5.112-115). The fragment matters to the audit. In the sequence world a sonnet is a sealed unit, completable at leisure because nothing in that world interrupts; in Verona the form must survive a household, a watchful nurse, a kinsman memorizing a face for later vengeance, and it cannot. What the play puts in the completed sonnet's place is not prose but process: the lovers will spend the rest of the action exchanging shorter and shorter units, couplets, single lines, shared lines, an aubade argued in exchanges of two and three, as if the fourteen-line room kept being rebuilt at the size the moment allows. The conceit system's preferred architecture, the long solo structure raised over years, is precisely what the play's world forbids, and the broken second sonnet is the moment the prohibition is first enforced, gently, by a servant calling a girl to her mother.

The sonnet's opening crux shows the system's theology being set at the editor's desk, a point the critics' section will need. Romeo's first line prices his hand's offence: the unworthiest hand has profaned a holy shrine, and a penalty is due. The second quarto reads the penalty as "the gentle fine is this", a payment, which keeps the conceit inside the system's economics, sin as a debt discharged by a kiss. The first quarto reads "the gentle sin is this", which makes the kiss itself the transgression and the conceit's theology considerably more dangerous. Editors divide on the reading, and the division is not pedantry, because the whole feast exchange turns on whether the lovers' figures are a currency or a crime. Weis prints the second quarto's fine, and this article follows the Arden text, but the variant is worth recording in an audit of the system: the tradition itself could not decide whether its central transaction was commerce or sacrilege, and the indecision is preserved in the play's earliest witnesses like a crack in the kit's casing.

The feast also quietly retires the blazon. Romeo never inventories Juliet's features in the descending liturgy, at the feast or afterward; his figures for her are of light and value, states rather than parts. The inventory will be performed once more in the play, in full, by Mercutio, in the dark, at a wall, about Rosaline, as a joke. The placement is the judgment. The blazon belongs to the absent beloved and the mocking friend; the present beloved gets metaphors she can answer.

The feast speech's last figure quietly registers the change of regime before any of this is argued. After the torches and the jewel, Romeo's eye settles on a living thing in motion: "So shows a snowy dove trooping with crows / As yonder lady o'er her fellows shows" (1.5.48-49). A dove trooping is a creature among creatures, outshining its company by moving as they move, and the simile's modesty is its news. The system's icons were static by design, a jewel in an ear, a saint in a shrine, a star at its distance, because stillness is what lets an icon be worshipped without inconvenience. The dove is the first of Romeo's figures that could, in principle, turn its head and look back. Within fifty lines it does, and the play's argument about the conceits is under way in earnest.

## The Conjuring: Mercutio Lowers the Blazon to the Body

Act 2 Scene 1 is the system's public execution, staged as a magic rite. Romeo has vaulted the Capulet orchard wall and cannot be found, so Mercutio undertakes to raise him by the only liturgy Romeo is known to worship: "I conjure thee by Rosaline's bright eyes, / By her high forehead and her scarlet lip, / By her fine foot, straight leg, and quivering thigh, / And the demesnes that there adjacent lie, / That in thy likeness thou appear to us" (2.1.17-21). The speech is a blazon performed as a spell, and its descent down the body is the criticism. The liturgy begins where the sonnets begin, eyes and forehead, passes the scarlet lip in correct order, and then keeps descending past the point where the sonnet tradition stops, into leg, thigh, and the adjacent demesnes, where the inventory's true destination stands confessed. Mercutio's argument is that the blazon was always this journey with the last stops suppressed. By completing the route at full speed, in prose rhythms wearing verse's clothes, for an audience of laughing friends, he converts the system's most reverent device into its most obscene one without altering a single item on the list. The full bawdy register of the scene, its pears and medlars and open-arse jokes, is anatomized in [Mercutio's Bawdy: Sex, Wit, and Wordplay](/2022/09/16/mercutio-bawdy-wit-wordplay/); the conjuring's place in the conceit argument is the formal one. An inventory that can be finished as a dirty joke was never a description of a woman. It was a route map of a man's attention, and the joke's accuracy is proved by the map's coherence.

The pupil's first judgment on the lesson arrives at the scene's threshold, and it is easy to walk past. Romeo, hidden in the orchard, hears the conjuring end and opens the balcony movement by rating the rater: "He jests at scars that never felt a wound" (2.2.1). The sentence is small and its placement is exact. In Act 1 Romeo received Mercutio's translations without defence, feeding the plain words back into the machine. In Act 2 he assesses the mocker's standing to mock, distinguishing between wit about wounds and knowledge of them, and the distinction is the first piece of literary judgment Romeo ever utters in his own voice. The education the Friar will shortly name has begun before the Friar speaks: the reciter is becoming a critic, and his first critical act is to grade the play's best critic and find the grade incomplete. The scars line also fixes the limits of Mercutio's jurisdiction for the whole article's argument. Laughter can expose a posture, but it cannot replace one, and the man who jests at scars will be dead of one before the play's argument about language is half finished, still jesting. What replaces the posture has to come from a speaker with standing in the subject, which is why the play reserves the rebuilding for the beloved and the diagnosis for the friar, and gives the wit only the demolition he is qualified for.

The scene's cruel elegance is its aim. Mercutio conjures a Romeo who no longer exists. The man inside the wall has spent the interval falling out of the system; the friend outside the wall is exorcising a posture already vacated, with perfect technique, to no effect: "He heareth not, he stirreth not, he moveth not; / The ape is dead, and I must conjure him" (2.1.15-16). Shakespeare underlines the obsolescence by giving Benvolio the scene's most quietly devastating observation, that Romeo has retired to a tree and wishes his mistress were the fruit the tree drops. Even the well-wishers now speak of the Rosaline suit in the past's grammar. The mockery in this play is not a debate the system loses on points. It is a requiem the system does not know is being sung over it, and the dramatic irony is structural: the audience, alone with Romeo in the orchard minutes later, will hear what replaced the machinery while Verona's wits are still performing at its grave.

## Act 2 Scene 4: Petrarch Named, Laura Demoted, the Verdict Delivered

The morning after the orchard, Mercutio delivers the obituary in explicit terms, and the play permits itself the luxury of naming its target: "Now is he for the numbers that Petrarch flowed in. Laura to his lady was but a kitchen wench" (2.4.38-40). Every element of the sentence prosecutes. The numbers are Petrarch's measures, the fluent verse of the sequence, and Mercutio's past tense demotes the whole tradition to a superseded style, something a man was for. Laura herself, the silent sun around which three hundred sonnets orbit, is reclassified as a kitchen wench beside Juliet, which is not an insult to Juliet but a measurement of the inflation the system required: its ladies had to be goddesses because nothing less could justify the machinery, and a living girl has just exposed the exchange rate. The sentence continues through a roll call of poetry's famous ladies, Dido a dowdy, Cleopatra a gypsy, Helen and Hero hildings and harlots, Thisbe with a grey eye or so, all of them devalued in a breath. Literature's beloveds are repriced like last season's stock. It is the funniest literary criticism in Shakespeare and the most exact: the conceit system is a currency, and Mercutio is announcing its collapse.

The same scene supplies the counter-evidence that the collapse is real, in the famous exchange of prose wit that follows. Romeo, returned from the Friar's cell, trades puns with Mercutio at Mercutio's own speed, goose for goose, lie for lie, until the mocker surrenders with delight: "Now art thou Romeo. Now art thou what thou art, by art as well as by nature" (2.4.86-87). The compliment's precision is easy to miss. Mercutio does not say that Romeo has stopped performing. He says that art and nature have come into alignment, that the performance is now the man's own. Set against the Act 1 recital, the diagnosis is complete. The Rosaline speeches were art without nature, a borrowed part recited correctly; the wit combat is art with nature, invention under pressure, in prose, about nothing, for the joy of the exchange. The system's antithesis was never artifice as such. It was artifice in place of a respondent. Where Rosaline gave the machinery silence to echo in, Mercutio gives it opposition, and opposition is the one whetstone on which Romeo's language sharpens.

Mercutio's target in the scene is wider than one lovestruck friend, and the wider target explains the obituary's confidence. Warming to his theme, he curses the whole fashion industry of affected speech: "the Pox of such antic, lisping, affecting fantasticoes; these new tuners of accents!" (2.4.28-29). The conceit system, in Mercutio's analysis, is one product line in a general market of borrowed voices, new tuners of accents selling pronunciation, posture, and sighing by the season. That is why the Laura sentence can be delivered as market news rather than as an argument. A fashion does not need refuting once its season turns; it needs only a reporter willing to say the season has turned, in hearing of the customers. Romeo's return in prose, trading inventions at speed, is the new season arriving in person, and Mercutio, who hates the old fashion and loves the friend, greets the arrival with the relief of a man whose market call has come true in front of him. The scene is the play's happiest, and its happiness is critical before it is anything else: two speakers delighting in a language neither of them borrowed.

One more exhibit from the scene belongs in the record, the fishified lament: "O flesh, flesh, how art thou fishified!" (2.4.38). Mercutio greets the returned Romeo by mourning what the sighing diet has done to him, flesh gone cold-blooded and limp on a diet of sonnets. The joke compresses the whole critique into a single metamorphosis. The Petrarchan regime, for all its fires, leaves a man cold; its burning is a figure, and figures do not warm. Against the cold fish stands the scene's actual Romeo, warm with a secret, quick with a pun, hungry for breakfast and a wedding. The play's sympathies are not in doubt. It loves the machinery the way a satirist loves a target he has mastered, and it loves the man in proportion as he climbs out of it.

## The Orchard: Juliet Rebuilds the Machinery from Inside

Act 2 Scene 2 is where the outbidding happens, and it begins with a conceit so famous that its membership in the system has become invisible. "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2-3). The line is usually quoted as the dawn of plain sincerity after the Rosaline postures, and the ledger's second column depends on refusing that simplification. Juliet is the sun is not plain speech. It is the boldest figure Romeo has yet attempted, a metaphor with no simile's hedge, and its opening word is a command to the cosmos: "Arise, fair sun, and kill the envious moon" (2.2.4). The moon is Diana's, the very goddess under whose arms Rosaline's chastity held its siege. In three lines the system's presiding deity is ordered off the sky by the system's own central device, the celestial comparison, now aimed at a woman who is present, awake, and about to speak for herself. The machinery has not been discarded. It has been repossessed.

Before the lovers exchange a word, Juliet has already performed the system's most elegant demolition, and she performs it with the blazon's own tools. Her soliloquy on the name runs the inventory backwards: "What's Montague? It is nor hand, nor foot, / Nor arm, nor face, nor any other part / Belonging to a man" (2.2.40-42). Hand, foot, arm, face: these are the blazon's stations, the parts a sonneteer would praise in descending order to compose his lady. Juliet recites the same list to dissolve a composition, arguing that no accumulation of parts amounts to the person, and that the name, the feud's label pasted over the parts, is the least substantial item of all. The manoeuvre deserves its place at the centre of the conceit argument. The play's heroine does not reject figurative language; she takes the tradition's signature figure, the catalogue of the beloved's parts, and runs it in reverse gear to prove that identity is not an inventory. Where the blazon composed a woman out of features so a man could own the composition, the anti-blazon decomposes a name into features so a woman can free the man. Same machinery, reversed polarity, and the difference is entirely in whose purpose the machine is serving.

What Juliet does with the repossessed machinery is the article's central exhibit. Overhearing nothing at first, she dismantles the name system in soliloquy and then, discovering her listener, sets the terms on which figures will be permitted between them. Romeo's glove wish, "O that I were a glove upon that hand, / That I might touch that cheek" (2.2.24-25), is the old machinery at its most wistful, a conceit for converting distance into imagined contact. Juliet's answering practice replaces imagined contact with negotiated fact. She refuses the oath by the moon on grounds that are, at bottom, editorial: the moon is inconstant, its figure unstable, and she will not have her love's evidence sworn on a vehicle that changes monthly (2.2.109-111). No character inside a sonnet sequence ever copy-edits the lover's figures. Juliet copy-edits as a condition of proceeding, and then supplies the replacement figure herself: "My bounty is as boundless as the sea, / My love as deep; the more I give to thee, / The more I have, for both are infinite" (2.2.133-135). The sea was Romeo's own vehicle in Act 1, a sea nourished with lovers' tears. In Juliet's mouth the same element is redefined from an emblem of weeping to a mathematics of increase, a figure that argues a proposition, that giving enlarges rather than spends, which the scene then enacts as she gives the contract, the hour, and the terms. The conceit has become an instrument of thought.

### What separates a conceit Romeo borrows from a comparison Juliet invents?

Direction of fit. Romeo's borrowed figures are chosen because the tradition stocks them, and Rosaline is then fitted inside their logic as garrison, goddess, or estate. Juliet's comparisons are chosen because a proposition needs stating, boundlessness, depth, increase by giving, and the figure is built to the proposition's measure and dismissed when the proposition is proved.

The full itinerary of Romeo's idiom across the play, its stations in Mantua and at the tomb as well as in the orchard, is traced in [From Petrarch to Passion: Romeo's Changing Voice](/2022/05/27/romeo-petrarchan-language-transformation/), and the present article deliberately confines itself to the system level: what the kit contained, who mocked it, and what its devices became under new management. At system level the orchard's verdict is crisp. The Petrarchan machine required a silent object, a single speaker, and a timescale of years. The orchard supplies a speaking object, two speakers correcting each other, and a timescale of one night. Under those conditions the machine's products change character as reliably as the Friar's flowers change effect by dose. Which brings the argument to the garden, and to the play's own theorist of dosage.

One structural measure of the rebuilding deserves its own sentence before the garden, because it is the measure the sequence form itself supplies. A sonnet sequence runs on dilated time: years of identical mornings, the lady's refusal renewed like a lease, the lover's situation deliberately frozen so the poems can accumulate. The orchard scene runs the same materials at the opposite pressure. Within a single night the figures are proposed, corrected, replaced, and converted into instruments with deadlines attached, a messenger to be sent at nine, a word to be returned by morning, a marriage to be solemnized the next day. Juliet's contract language is the anti-sequence's signature. Where the kit's temporality is a siege, endless by design, hers is a schedule, finite by agreement, and every figure she builds is built to be cashed by a stated hour. The conceit system did not survive contact with a timetable. It was replaced, in that orchard, by something the tradition had no form for: figures with appointments.

## The Friar's Garden: A Rival System of Comparisons

Friar Laurence enters the play carrying a basket and a counter-poetics. His dawn soliloquy in Act 2 Scene 3 builds an entire system of comparison out of the garden: the earth as mother and tomb, the weed and the flower sharing one womb, virtue and vice as the same plant differently applied, the flower that cheers in the smelling and kills in the tasting (2.3.1-30). The speech is sometimes treated as a thematic overture and left at that. Within the conceit argument it has a sharper function. It demonstrates that Shakespeare had a second machine for figuring the world, a natural one, in which comparisons are drawn from things the speaker handles daily and can be falsified by handling them. A herb either heals or harms at a stated dose. The Petrarchan kit, by contrast, cannot be falsified at all: no evidence about Rosaline could have refuted a single one of Romeo's figures for her, because the figures were never about her. The Friar's garden is the play's standing reminder that a conceit can be answerable to the world, and his diagnosis of Romeo, delivered within the hour, applies the garden's standard to the lover's art.

The diagnosis is the most quoted sentence in the whole debate: "Thy love did read by rote, that could not spell" (2.3.88). Its metaphors are pedagogical. Romeo's Rosaline love was a schoolboy's recitation, sounds produced from a memorized page by a reader who cannot yet construct the words himself. The Friar does not say the love was feigned; rote reading is real reading, of a kind, and the boy at his lesson is sincere in his way. He says it was uncomprehended fluency, exactly the quality the audience heard in Act 1 when the oxymoron chain ran one link too long. The Friar then does the thing no voice inside the system ever does: he cross-examines the new love for its credentials, asks where and when the lovers met, marvels at the speed, and consents for reasons of state rather than of poetry, that the alliance may turn the households' rancour to pure love. Even his consent is phrased as a hoped-for consequence in the world, not as a figure. Between the garden speech and the rote verdict, Act 2 Scene 3 installs the standard by which the whole conceit question is judged in this play: a figure earns its place by spelling, by construing the world it claims to describe, and the Friar's standard will still be in force at the tomb, where Romeo's last conceits are finally, terribly, spelled correctly.

The Friar's method with the patient deserves recording, because it is the opposite of Mercutio's and the two methods together triangulate the system's weakness. Mercutio translates the conceits into a lower register and laughs. Laurence cross-examines the lover like a confessor taking an inventory: where was the new love met, when, at whose house, with what knowledge of the old one's claims (2.3.51-90). The questions are a confessor's and a clinician's at once, and every one of them demands a fact, the species of statement the conceit system is designed never to produce. Romeo's answers, notably, are factual and brief; in the Friar's cell the recital stops, because the room runs on evidence. Even the consent is audited. The Friar agrees to the marriage not because the new love's figures are finer but because a consequence in the world can be projected from it, the turning of two households' rancour to pure love, and he attaches the audit's condition in the scene that follows hard upon it: love moderately, since violent delights have violent ends. The play's adults, in short, do not hate the conceits. They refuse to accept them as documentation. Between the street's laughter and the cell's cross-examination, the kit is denied both of its shelters, taste and authority, and the rebuilding that follows happens under supervision, which is why its products can be trusted further than the recital's ever were.

### What does the play gain by giving the mockery to Mercutio before Juliet corrects the manner from inside?

Separation of the two critiques. Mercutio's laughter proves the manner is ridiculous from outside, a judgment of taste any wit in Verona could reach. Juliet's corrections prove the stronger point from inside the love itself: the manner fails even on its own terms, because its figures cannot survive an answering voice, an editor, or a fact.

## Juliet Outbids the System: The Wedding-Night Speech and the Aubade

If the orchard shows the machinery repossessed, Act 3 Scene 2 shows it outbid. Juliet's epithalamium opens with a conceit of cosmic transport, "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds, / Towards Phoebus' lodging" (3.2.1-2), and proceeds to the most extravagant figure in the play: "Give me my Romeo; and, when I shall die, / Take him and cut him out in little stars, / And he will make the face of heaven so fine / That all the world will be in love with night / And pay no worship to the garish sun" (3.2.21-25). Measure this against the system's standard products. The sonnet lady's eyes were stars, conventionally, in poem after poem; Juliet inverts the assignment, makes the man the star-field, and lodges the hyperbole in a future she knows is a bargain with death. The figure is larger than anything in Romeo's Rosaline recital and it is doing opposite work: his figures inflated a refusal, hers prices a possession. She is not performing a condition for an audience of sonneteers. She is alone, impatient, and spending the tradition's whole treasury on one night's delay. The prosody of the speech, its gallop and its checks, belongs to the close reading of the speech's verse music; the conceit-level fact stands here. In Juliet's hands hyperbole stops being a posture and becomes a unit of account, the only denomination large enough for what she means.

The aubade of Act 3 Scene 5 completes the education. The lovers wake to a disputed bird, and their exchange is, formally, a contest of conceits: the nightingale on the pomegranate tree against the lark, the herald of the morn (3.5.1-7). In the old system a figure was asserted and admired. Here figures are evidence, advanced, challenged, and withdrawn under pressure of fact. Juliet argues the nightingale because the nightingale's song, if true, buys time; Romeo, who has more to lose by the error, concedes the lark and offers the desperate gallantry of accepting her figure at the price of his life, until she herself revokes it: "It is, it is. Hie hence, be gone, away" (3.5.26). The scene is the conceit system's graduation ceremony. Two speakers who began, one as a reciter and one as a corrector of recitations, now handle figures the way the Friar handles herbs, as instruments whose truth is tested against the world and whose dose can kill. No sonnet sequence contains such a scene, because no sonnet sequence lets the lady rule on the evidence. The epithalamium holds one more exhibit, small enough to miss and exact enough to convict. Juliet describes her own position in the system's economic dialect, the dialect Romeo had used to price Rosaline's hoarded store: "O, I have bought the mansion of a love, / But not possessed it, and though I am sold, / Not yet enjoyed" (3.2.26-28). Bought, sold, possessed, enjoyed: the market conceit entire, but with its polarity corrected at every terminal. Romeo's economics had made the woman the asset and the man the auditor of her waste. Juliet makes herself both parties, buyer and goods, and reports the transaction's incompleteness as a timetable problem rather than a tragedy of refusal. The figure has not been abandoned; it has been audited, and it balances. Even her falconry runs the same correction. Where a sonneteer would figure the lady as the falcon and himself as the lure, Juliet figures her own blood as the unmanned falcon that must be hooded against bating in her cheeks (3.2.14-15), assigning the wildness to her own body and the discipline to her own hand. System after system passes through her speeches and comes out re-owned.

The play's deepest anti-Petrarchan stroke is not Mercutio's laughter. It is Juliet's jurisdiction.

## The Residue: What Romeo Never Quite Unlearns

The brief for this article demands the complication, and the complication deserves its full weight. Romeo in the tomb is a man whose idiom has been rebuilt at every station the play offers, by Mercutio's laughter, Juliet's editing, the Friar's diagnosis, exile, and loss. His final speech is nevertheless dense with conceits, and two of them are pure Petrarchan stock. The first is the merriment figure: "How oft when men are at the point of death / Have they been merry! which their keepers call / A lightning before death" (5.3.88-90). The second is the paramour figure, in which Death himself becomes the system's last cold beloved, inverted: "Shall I believe / That unsubstantial Death is amorous, / And that the lean abhorred monster keeps / Thee here in dark to be his paramour?" (5.3.102-105). The mistress who would not yield has become a keeper who will not release; the siege has been reversed and lost. A reading that claims Romeo simply escapes Petrarchism cannot survive these lines, and this article does not make that claim.

Mantua supplies the residue's sharpest exhibit, sharper than the tomb's, because it shows the old error running in reverse. Romeo greets Balthasar with the report of a dream: "I dreamt my lady came and found me dead, / Strange dream that gives a dead man leave to think, / And breathed such life with kisses in my lips / That I revived and was an emperor" (5.1.6-9). The dream is a conceit, the kiss as resurrection, the beloved's breath as the animating spirit, pure kit material of the sweetest grade. In Act 1 Romeo's error was to dissolve facts into figures, a girl into a garrison. In Act 5 his error is to promote a figure into a fact, waking "joyful" on the strength of it and carrying that manufactured confidence into the interview that kills him. The play is pitilessly symmetrical. The machinery that once manufactured a mistress out of nothing now manufactures news out of a dream, and the news is believed in the same unexamined way, because the habit of treating figures as evidence was never the recital's alone to unlearn. Balthasar's report, the apparent fact that corrects the dream, is itself a misreading of a staged appearance, so that Romeo rides to Verona suspended between a figure taken for news and news taken from a figure, and the tomb is where the two currencies are finally exchanged at their true rate.

What has changed is the figures' relation to fact, and the change is the verdict's refinement rather than its defeat. The lightning conceit describes something the audience can verify: Romeo is, observably, in the strange calm the figure names, and he offers the figure as a hypothesis about himself, flagged with its source, "which their keepers call", a citation rather than an assertion. The paramour conceit is likewise a reading of evidence, the evidence of a face that will not behave like a corpse's: "Death, that hath sucked the honey of thy breath, / Hath had no power yet upon thy beauty" (5.3.91-92). In Act 1 the figures floated free of any checkable world and manufactured a woman. In Act 5 the same machinery is set to work on a scene, and its failure to account for the scene, beauty unexplained by death, is precisely the truth the machinery cannot reach, that the woman is alive. Romeo dies inside a conceit that is almost right, which is the play's final refinement of its theme. The system is not a lie detector's target. It is a language, and like any language it can be spoken by rote at dawn and spelled, letter by letter, in the dark, at the price of everything.

The apothecary transaction, an hour earlier in the same movement, shows the rebuilt idiom working at full strength, and it works in the system's own economic dialect. Romeo prices the poison against the gold with an argued inversion: "There is thy gold, worse poison to men's souls, / Doing more murder in this loathsome world / Than these poor compounds that thou mayst not sell" (5.1.80-82). Store, waste, and value, the whole vocabulary of the Rosaline economics, is here turned on the world that taught it, and the figure is spelled in the Friar's sense: it describes a verifiable arrangement, a starving man, a statute, a price, and it persuades, which no Rosaline figure ever had to do. The conceit has become testimony. Set the two economic speeches side by side, the posterity argument of Act 1 and the gold argument of Act 5, and the education stands complete in a single pair of exhibits: same machinery, same speaker, one figure floating free of any checkable world and manufacturing a woman, the other nailed to a transaction and manufacturing a purchase. The residue, properly audited, is not that Romeo still uses the kit. It is that the kit, in disciplined hands, was an instrument all along, and the tragedy includes the years he spent letting it play him.

## The Critics' Quarrel: Was the Mockery in the Tradition, or in the Play?

Three named positions divide the question, and the article's verdict depends on adjudicating between them rather than merely collecting them. M. M. Mahood, in Shakespeare's Wordplay, reads Romeo's early manner from the character outward: the Act 1 conceits and wordplay are the authentic sound of a young man performing a part he has learned from books, and the play's later language, Mercutio's wit, Juliet's corrections, Romeo's own sharpening, dramatizes an education in which the performed part is gradually replaced by speech that answers to occasion. On Mahood's account the mockery is Shakespeare's, aimed at a phase of the hero: the tradition supplies the costume, the play supplies the growing boy who bursts it. Heather Dubrow, in Captive Victors, her study of Petrarchism in the narrative poems and sonnets, complicates the direction of the irony: the Petrarchan tradition as Shakespeare received it was never a single sincere posture but a contested field, stocked from the start with counter-currents, complaints against the mistress's tyranny, admissions of the lover's self-deception, and parodic possibilities. On Dubrow's account, a play that mocks the kit is not breaking with the tradition. It is activating one of the tradition's own circuits, and Romeo's folly is not that he uses Petrarchan devices but that he uses them as if the ironizing circuit did not exist. Jill Levenson's Oxford edition stands between the two as the editor's witness: her introduction and commentary track the play's saturation in the sonnet vogue of the early 1590s and note how relentlessly the text itself, not its critics, juxtaposes the sighing manner with voices that puncture it, Mercutio's Petrarch sentence among them.

The disagreement is genuine and worth stating exactly. If Mahood is right in full, the conceits are a symptom and the play is a cure narrative, and Juliet's own magnificent conceits in Act 3 become an embarrassment to the reading, survivals of the disease in the healthiest speaker. If Dubrow is right in full, Romeo's education shrinks to a change of fashion, one circuit of the tradition exchanged for another, and the Friar's rote verdict loses its moral weight. The text adjudicates. Two exhibits decide between the accounts, and they split the honours. Exhibit one favours Dubrow: the oxymoron chain of Act 1 collapses into self-description inside Romeo's own mouth, "misshapen chaos of well-seeming forms", before any mocker has spoken, which means the strain was audible in the material itself, as a tradition-aware audience was equipped to hear. Shakespeare did not inject irony into a sound device; he composed for listeners who could already hear the device creak. Exhibit two favours Mahood: the play insists on the difference between the recital and the rebuilt speech at the level of the man's soul, not his fashion, giving the Friar the rote-and-spelling metaphor, giving Mercutio the art-and-nature compliment, and giving Juliet jurisdiction over figures as a condition of love. A mere change of circuit would not require an editor inside the fiction. The adjudicated verdict is therefore double, and the doubleness is the play's: the tradition arrived pre-ironized, and the hero arrived unable to hear the ironies, and the tragedy of his language, in miniature, is the distance between what his kit already knew and what he had yet to learn. Each account also fails somewhere the other succeeds, and the failures mark the verdict's borders. Mahood's education plot cannot comfortably house Juliet, whose conceits are the play's most extravagant and whose education runs the other way, from a girlhood of fourteen lines in her first scene to the star-field of the epithalamium: if the manner were simply a disease of immaturity, its finest products would not belong to the play's fastest maturer. Dubrow's contested field cannot comfortably house the Friar, whose rote verdict is not one more turn in the tradition's game but a moral and pedagogical judgment delivered from outside the game entirely, by a man whose own comparison system is grown in a garden and tested on his tongue. The play, characteristically, declines to choose between the diachronic and the synchronic account. It stages the tradition as a field of forces and the hero as a boy moving through it, and it lets the audience watch both truths operate in the same speeches: the kit creaking under its own irony, and a speaker learning, line by corrected line, to hear the creak.

René Weis's Arden edition, the text quoted throughout this article, keeps the evidence for both exhibits legible, printing the fuller second-quarto readings against which the first quarto's compressions can be measured; where the quartos divide on the feast sonnet's crux, the gentle fine against the gentle sin, the editor's choice between a theology of payment and a theology of transgression quietly tunes how borrowed the pilgrim conceit sounds, a reminder that the system's degree of irony is partly set, for every generation, at the editor's desk.

What a system cannot survive is the loss of its standard of value and its signature device in the same act, at the hands of the same laughing witness, while its former operator stands by trading prose. ## The Final Reckoning: A System Acquitted, a Posture Convicted

The play's double attitude toward its inheritance, veneration and mockery in the same breath, resolves once the defendant is correctly named. The defendant was never the conceit. A conceit is an instrument for thinking in comparisons, and the play's greatest speeches, the orchard contract, the wedding-night epithalamium, the tomb's last readings of a face, are conceits built to bear weight and bearing it. The defendant is the posture the kit made available to a young man of the 1590s: the posture of the solo sufferer, whose beloved's silence is the condition of his eloquence, whose figures answer to a tradition rather than to a woman, and whose fluency is indistinguishable, even to himself, from feeling. Against that posture the play marshals its full company: a friend who translates it into the street, a friar who diagnoses it as rote, a beloved who refuses to be its object and insists on editing its products, and a plot that gives its hero twenty-four hours of answered speech in which to learn the difference. The cliche reading of the play, the one that takes all the love-talk as sincere because all of it is beautiful, survives only by declining to listen to the play's own listeners. Verona hears the difference between recitation and speech. The ending's waste is measured by how late the hero's education completes itself: he learns to spell his figures in a tomb, over a woman the figures cannot wake, in the last minutes the feud allows him. The machinery was never the tragedy. The schooling was almost too slow, and that almost is the play.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Petrarchan ledger, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Petrarchan ledger is the before-and-after table set out in this article, pairing each conceit Romeo borrows for Rosaline with the answering speech in which he or Juliet rebuilds the same device. Its pattern is transfer of ownership. In the left column every figure belongs to the tradition and describes a woman who never speaks; in the right column the same figures, the sea, the siege, the shrine, the stars, belong to the lovers and describe arrangements they actually make. The ledger exposes that the play never discards a device. It repossesses devices, one by one, from a system that required silence into a dialogue that enforces answers.

### Q: What is a conceit, and what makes one Petrarchan rather than merely elaborate?

A conceit is a comparison argued like a case: a resemblance is proposed, then extended, subdivided, and made to yield consequences, so that the figure organizes a whole speech rather than decorating a line. A conceit is Petrarchan when its parts come from the standardized kit that descends from Petrarch's sequence to Laura: the beloved inventoried by the blazon, her refusal figured as siege and armed chastity, the lover's condition reported as coupled symptoms of fire and ice, hope and despair. Elaborate comparisons exist in every tradition. The Petrarchan conceit is recognizable by its personnel, a silent lady and a solo sufferer, and by its economics, in which her withholding is the engine of his eloquence.

### Q: Who was Petrarch, and which of his habits did the English sonneteers inherit?

Petrarch was the fourteenth-century Italian poet whose Rime sparse, a sequence of sonnets and longer poems addressed to Laura, fixed the template of European love lyric for two hundred years. Laura is seen, desired, unattainable, and eventually dead, and the sequence stretches that single unchanging situation across a lifetime of poems. The English sonneteers inherited the situation whole: the sequence as the form of a love affair, the lady praised by inventory and figured as saint, goddess, and fortress, the lover's interior reported as a parliament of opposites, and time itself cast as the only agent of change. Wyatt and Surrey naturalized the kit at the Henrician court, Sidney's Astrophil and Stella revived it, and the printed vogue of the early 1590s made it the common property of every literate playgoer.

### Q: What is a blazon, and why does it praise the beloved piece by piece?

A blazon is the beloved's body rendered as an inventory, brow, eyes, lips, hand, and foot praised in a fixed descending order, as a herald blazons a coat of arms or a steward lists an estate. The piece-by-piece method is the point. To itemize a woman is to claim knowledge of her, and to publish the inventory is to convert that claim into a kind of title, all without requiring her to speak, consent, or even appear. The blazon is therefore the conceit system's most revealing device: perfect courtesy in form, pure possession in function. Romeo never performs one for Juliet. Mercutio performs the last one in the play, for Rosaline, at speed, descending past the sonnet's stopping point into open bawdy, and the liturgy's destination stands confessed.

### Q: Why does Romeo say Rosaline's beauty will be cut off from all posterity?

Because in the economics of the system, beauty is capital and chastity is hoarding. Romeo argues that Rosaline, rich in beauty, is poor in the only sense that matters to his conceit: when she dies, her store dies with her, since she has refused to spend it on children who would carry it forward. The argument was a commonplace of the procreation sonnets that open Shakespeare's own sequence, where a fair youth is urged to breed before his beauty's lease expires. In a lover's mouth it is a strange tenderness, pricing the beloved as a depreciating asset in a vault, and Romeo delivers it without hearing the pricing. The tonal blindness is diagnostic. Inside the kit, any proposition about the lady counts as praise provided the figure is handsome.

### Q: How do the smoke, fire, and sea images of Act 1 Scene 1 build one system of feeling?

The three images are states of one physics, set out in Romeo's first definition of love at 1.1.190-192. Love is a smoke raised with the fume of sighs; purged, it becomes a fire sparkling in lovers' eyes; vexed, it turns to a sea nourished with lovers' tears. Smoke, fire, and sea are not three ornaments but three phases of a single substance, mapped in order onto the lover's symptoms: sighing, burning, weeping. The coherence is the criticism's starting point. Romeo's early figures are often dismissed as random fireworks, when in fact they interlock like a textbook, which is precisely the problem. A feeling that arrives as a complete system, with its phases pre-mapped, has been learned rather than suffered, and the play will spend its length teaching the speaker to build figures from occasions instead of occasions from figures.

### Q: Why is Mercutio's mockery in Act 2 already out of date when he delivers it?

Because its target has vacated the posture being mocked. At the orchard wall in Act 2 Scene 1, Mercutio conjures Romeo by Rosaline's features in the full blazon liturgy, performing an exorcism of the sighing lover. The man he is conjuring has, within the hour, met Juliet, completed a sonnet with her, and arranged a marriage. "He heareth not, he stirreth not, he moveth not; the ape is dead," Mercutio complains, and the joke cuts deeper than he knows: the ape, the performing animal of the Rosaline act, is indeed dead, though not by any exorcism of Mercutio's. Shakespeare builds the obsolescence into the timing so that the audience hears the mockery as a requiem sung over an empty position. The system's most brilliant enemy is fighting its ghost, and the new Romeo is already elsewhere, being educated by a stricter critic.

### Q: Why does Mercutio name Petrarch and Laura outright in Act 2 Scene 4?

The naming is the play's one explicit citation of its source tradition, and Mercutio spends it like a death certificate. "Now is he for the numbers that Petrarch flowed in. Laura to his lady was but a kitchen wench" (2.4.38-40). The past tense demotes the whole sequence tradition to a superseded fashion, and the kitchen wench performs the repricing: Laura, the silent sun of three hundred sonnets, is valued below a living girl in Verona, which exposes the inflation the system always required. Its ladies had to be goddesses because nothing less could justify the machinery spent on them. By naming Petrarch rather than merely mocking sighing, Shakespeare has the verdict entered against the tradition itself, in open court, by the play's licensed wit, at the exact moment Romeo returns fluent in a new and unborrowed art.

### Q: How does Juliet's 'Gallop apace' speech outbid the conceit manner instead of abandoning it?

Juliet keeps the manner's scale and reverses its direction. The sonnet tradition made the lady's eyes into stars by routine; Juliet, alone on her wedding night, makes the man into the star-field: "Take him and cut him out in little stars, / And he will make the face of heaven so fine / That all the world will be in love with night" (3.2.22-24). The hyperbole is larger than anything in Romeo's Rosaline recital, and it does opposite work. His figures inflated a refusal into a career; hers price a possession she is impatient to collect, and the figure is lodged in a bargain with death, "when I shall die", that she states as a condition rather than a sigh. Outbidding, in the play's economy, means the same treasury spent at full value: figures sized to the feeling and mortgaged to facts, instead of figures borrowed at the going rate to fill a posture.

### Q: How did Wyatt and Surrey alter the conceits they carried from Petrarch into English?

Wyatt, carrying Petrarch into the Henrician court a generation before the play, roughened what he borrowed. His most famous adaptation turns Petrarch's hind sonnet into a courtier's warning, "Whoso list to hunt, I know where is an hind", where the beloved's collar reads as a king's property mark and the conceit becomes an instrument of court survival rather than devotion. Surrey smoothed the line, regularized the English sonnet's shape of three quatrains and a couplet, and made the kit portable. Between them the conceits lost their Italian liturgical finish and gained an English double life, available for irony, policy, and eventually for drama. Shakespeare inherits the kit from their hands already loosened, already capable of being quoted at an angle, which is why his mockery can be an insider's: the tradition arrived in England with its tongue partly in its cheek.

### Q: Why did the sonnet vogue of the early 1590s make the play's first audiences expert in this machinery?

Because the machinery was on every bookstall. Sidney's Astrophil and Stella reached print in 1591 and set off a rush of sequences, Daniel's Delia, Constable's Diana, Lodge's Phillis, Drayton's Idea among them, all rehearsing the same blazons, sieges, and symptom catalogues within a few years of the play's first performances. A playgoer of 1595 had priced a hundred sonnet seas and could recognize a stock figure the way a modern listener recognizes a borrowed chord. Shakespeare composes for that expertise. The Act 1 recital is funny and damning only to ears that know the going rate of every figure in it, and the feast scene's corrections land as corrections only against the standard the audience carries in memory. The play's critique of Petrarchism is a collaboration with its first audience's fluency.

### Q: How does the play's handling of the blazon compare with the anti-blazon of Shakespeare's Sonnet 130?

Sonnet 130 performs the blazon backwards: "My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun; / Coral is far more red than her lips' red", item by item the inventory is read out and refused, until the close swears that the love is rare in proportion as the comparisons were false. The play dramatizes the same manoeuvre at full scale. Inside Romeo and Juliet the inventory is never refused line by line; instead it is retired by reassignment. The only complete blazon in the play is Mercutio's conjuring, spoken as a joke over an absent woman, while the present beloved is figured whole, as sun, sea, and saint, in comparisons she answers and edits. Poem and play agree on the verdict from opposite directions: the inventory was never a description of a woman, and love is proved by what survives the inventory's collapse.

### Q: Why does the Petrarchan system need the beloved to refuse, and what breaks when Juliet consents?

Refusal is the system's engine. A sequence requires an unchanging situation stretched across hundreds of poems, and only a lady who never yields can supply one; her coldness licenses the lover's fire, her silence licenses his inventory, her permanence in refusal licenses his career. The moment the beloved consents, every term of the machine reverses: the timescale collapses from years to hours, the solo voice becomes a dialogue, and the figures must suddenly describe arrangements, meetings, contracts, a wedding at a stated hour. Juliet's consent breaks the genre so completely that the play has to change kind around her, from sequence to drama, from siege to marriage plot. What the moralists call the lovers' haste is, structurally, the sound of a lyric machine being forced to run at dramatic speed.

### Q: Why do Romeo's Rosaline conceits need heavier editorial glossing than his speeches for Juliet?

Because they are written in a dead fashion's dialect. Dian's wit, saint-seducing gold, the siege of loving terms, the childhood bow of Love: each phrase assumes the reader's fluency in a kit that was already ageing when the ink dried, and editors of the Arden and Oxford texts must unpack the allusions one by one before the lines will construe. The Juliet speeches gloss themselves against the scene. A sun that kills an envious moon explains its own astronomy to anyone watching a girl at a window; bounty boundless as the sea states its mathematics in the hearing. The gloss burden is evidence in the article's case. Language that needs a commentary to be felt was composed for a coterie's game; language that survives its fashion was composed for a person.

### Q: How does the Friar's herb speech set a rival system of comparisons against Romeo's borrowed ones?

The Friar's dawn soliloquy builds its figures from his own basket: the earth as mother and grave, the flower that cheers in the smelling and kills in the tasting, virtue and vice as one plant differently applied. Every comparison in the garden system is falsifiable by handling. A dose either heals or harms, and the figure can be checked against the herb. No evidence about Rosaline could have refuted a single one of Romeo's figures for her, because they were never about her; the garden's standard, applied an hour later in the same scene, produces the play's diagnosis of the borrowed love as reading by rote that could not spell. The rival system does not banish figures. It demands that figures answer to the world they claim to describe, and it is still the standard in force at the tomb.

### Q: What work does the blindfolded Cupid of Romeo's early speeches do for his excuses?

The blindfold outsources responsibility. In the emblem tradition every Elizabethan knew, Cupid shoots blindfolded, so the lover's condition is weather: an arrow arrived from a god who could not see its target, and neither the choice of object nor the conduct of the suit can be laid at the sufferer's door. Romeo leans on the arrangement throughout the Rosaline campaign, sore enpierced by the shaft, unable to soar, a patient rather than an agent. Mercutio's Queen Mab speech, delivered in the same scene as the blind god's last appearance, replaces the archer with a tiny charioteer whose dreams are secreted by the dreamers' own appetites, lawyers of fees, soldiers of throats. The counter-system is brutal and liberating at once: desire is manufactured at home, and what is manufactured at home can be revised at home, as the feast is about to prove.

### Q: How should an essay argue that the play mocks the conceits without mocking the love?

By separating the device from the posture and marching the evidence in performance order. Open with the Act 1 recital and note what it omits, any fact about Rosaline, then take Mercutio's three prosecutions in sequence: the street translation of Act 1 Scene 4, the lowered blazon of the conjuring, the Petrarch obituary of Act 2 Scene 4. Set against them the rebuildings: the answered pilgrim of the feast, the edited sea of the orchard, the outbidding stars of the wedding-night speech. The essay's verdict follows from the pattern rather than from taste: the play grades every figure by its answerability, acquitting hyperbole that states a proposition and convicting fluency that fills a silence. Love is never the accused. The accused is a manner of talking that needed the beloved to stay silent, and the prosecution witnesses are the play's own best speakers.

### Q: How do the golden statues complete the play's argument about turning a person into a precious image?

The fathers end the play doing to the lovers what the blazon did to Laura. Montague will raise Juliet's statue in pure gold; Capulet answers with Romeo's, jointure for jointure, until the lovers stand as Verona's matched pair of precious objects, inventoried in superlatives by men who never heard either of them speak a true sentence. The statues are the conceit system's final victory and its final exposure. Everything the play taught its audience to suspect, the conversion of a person into a valuable image that can be admired without being answered, is performed in state, in gold, over the bodies, and the Prince's closing couplet duly converts the pair into a story, the last inventory of all. The lovers escaped the system by answering each other. Verona repossesses them by casting them, and the play's coldest judgment is that the city prefers them that way.

### Q: How should an actor play the Act 1 conceits so they sound read rather than felt?

By fluency. The recital should move at the even, finished tempo of a piece known by heart, with a young man's visible pleasure in the figures' finish, the catalogue's links clicked shut one after another without a search for the next. Nothing should be fumbled, because Romeo is not failing at feeling; he is succeeding at recitation, and the success is the symptom. The performance then has somewhere to go: the feast's broken starts before an actual girl, the orchard's figures interrupted and corrected, the wit combat's speed earned in company. Audiences should be able to hear the book behind the boy in Act 1 precisely so that they can hear it close, scene by scene, as occasions replace it. An actor who plays the Rosaline speeches as sincere agony flattens the play's whole argument about language into a mood.

### Q: What does Romeo's 'lightning before death' conceit in the tomb show about the habit he never quite loses?

It shows the habit surviving its education. Dying men, Romeo observes, are often merry, "which their keepers call / A lightning before death" (5.3.88-90): the inherited figure is still his instrument for reading his own state, five acts after the Friar diagnosed the rote. What has changed is the figure's discipline. He cites its source instead of asserting it, offers it as a hypothesis about a calm the audience can observe, and sets beside it the paramour conceit, Death keeping Juliet in the dark, which reads the tomb's evidence and fails against it instructively, since her beauty will not behave as a corpse's should. The habit of figuring feeling through stock images was never the disease. Rote was. In the tomb the stock image is spelled against a visible scene, and its failure to account for her living face is the last true thing it tells him.
