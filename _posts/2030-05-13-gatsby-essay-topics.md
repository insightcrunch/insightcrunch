## Great Gatsby Essay Topics That Score Well: The Central Claim

The search for great Gatsby essay topics that score well usually begins in the wrong place. The writer hunts for a clever subject, something original enough to impress and safe enough to defend, and treats the choice as decoration, a garnish added before the real work of writing starts. The claim of this article is that the choice is not decoration. It is the first and most consequential argumentative decision the writer makes, because a topic either supplies the essay with something to argue or condemns it to description. The topics that reward an argument, on the American Dream, on class, on the symbols, on the narration, are productive for the same reason every time: they are arguable and rich in evidence. A strong topic is one that invites a defensible claim rather than a description, and choosing well sets up the whole essay before a single paragraph is drafted.

![Student choosing Great Gatsby essay topics from a study guide - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Consider what actually happens when an examiner reads the first page of a Gatsby essay. Within a few sentences, often before the introduction has ended, the reader forms a judgment that rarely reverses: this writer has something to say, or this writer is filling space. That judgment does not come from vocabulary or sentence length. It comes from whether the essay is organized around a claim or around a subject. An essay organized around a claim has a direction; every paragraph either advances the argument or answers an objection to it. An essay organized around a subject has only a territory; every paragraph wanders a little further into the territory, reporting what it finds, until the conclusion arrives and restates the territory as though restatement were an insight. The difference between these two essays is made at the moment of choosing the topic, and no amount of later polishing can convert the second kind into the first. This is the practical meaning of the claim that topics reward an argument: the reward is not mystical. A good topic hands the writer a claim-shaped starting point, and everything downstream, the thesis, the paragraph plan, the selection of evidence, becomes easier.

The four territories named in the claim deserve their prominence because each one passes the two-part test that this article will apply throughout. The American Dream is productive because the novel itself stages a dispute about what the dream promises and what it costs, so a writer can take a position and find passages on both sides. Class is productive because the book draws its social boundaries with unusual sharpness, old money against new, East Egg against West Egg, the Buchanans against the Wilsons, and every boundary is crossed or policed in scenes a writer can point to. The symbols are productive because they recur with variation, the green light changing its charge from the first chapter to the last, the valley of ashes accumulating meanings, the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg inviting rival readings, and variation is the raw material of argument. The narration is productive because Nick Carraway is both the teller and a participant with interests, so every claim about what happens must pass through the question of who is telling it. A topic drawn from any of these territories can be arguable and evidence-rich. A topic drawn from outside them can also work, but the writer should understand that these four are the deep wells, and a topic that cannot connect to at least one of them will usually be thin.

There is a temptation, especially for strong students, to treat topic choice as the one part of essay writing that cannot be taught, a matter of instinct or taste that some writers have and others do not. This article rejects that temptation. Choosing well is a learnable move with visible mechanics. It can be practiced, it can be tested against the text before drafting begins, and its failures have recognizable patterns, the topic too broad to argue, the topic too narrow to sustain, the topic that is really a thesis wearing a disguise, the topic that is really a summary waiting to happen. The sections that follow name those patterns, show the productive territories in detail, and demonstrate the conversion of topics into theses with worked transformations, so that the reader finishes with a method rather than a mood. The method has a single standard at its center, and it is worth stating plainly before anything else: a topic is strong when it is arguable, meaning a thoughtful reader could disagree with the claim it invites, and evidence-rich, meaning the novel supplies the passages, images, and scenes needed to defend that claim. Everything else in this article is elaboration of that standard.

## What Makes a Topic Productive: Arguable and Evidence-Rich

Productivity is a property of the topic, not of the writer. A productive topic does work for whoever picks it up, because its structure pulls the essay toward argument, while an unproductive topic does the opposite, pulling even a capable writer toward summary. The two qualities that make a topic productive deserve separate treatment, because they fail in different ways and are tested by different questions.

Arguability is the first quality because it is the one that creates the essay. A topic is arguable when the claim it invites is one that a thoughtful reader of the novel could reasonably dispute. The test is not whether disagreement is possible in principle; any sentence can be denied by someone determined to deny it. The test is whether the disagreement would be interesting, the kind of disagreement that makes both sides reach for the text. Take the topic area of class and social standing. The topic "the difference between old money and new money in the novel" is not yet arguable; it names a subject about which true things can be said, but the true things, that the Buchanans inherited their wealth and Gatsby did not, are settled by the text and cannot be disputed. The arguable version asks what work that difference does: does the novel treat old money as morally superior to new money, or does it treat both as corrupt in different ways? Now there is a dispute worth having. One reader can argue that Tom Buchanan's brutality and Daisy's carelessness show old money as the deeper rot, the casual cruelty of people who have never needed to examine themselves. Another reader can argue that Gatsby's criminal associations, his bootlegging and his bond with Meyer Wolfsheim, show new money as equally compromised, the novel refusing to romanticize either side. Both readers have evidence. Both can point to scenes. The topic became productive the moment it admitted that dispute, and the essay it produces will have something to do.

Evidence-richness is the second quality, and it is the one that sustains the essay past the opening. A topic is evidence-rich when the novel supplies enough concrete material, passages, images, scenes, patterns of language, to support a full argument without the writer having to stretch or repeat. The green light is the textbook case. It appears at the end of the first chapter, with Gatsby reaching toward it across the water, and it returns in the closing pages of the novel, where Nick meditates on its meaning. Between those appearances, the color green recurs in the novel's imagery, and the light itself accrues associations with distance, desire, and the future. A writer who chooses the green light as a topic area has material for an introduction, several body paragraphs, and a conclusion, all of it checkable against the text. Contrast a topic area with thin evidence, such as the significance of a single minor figure who appears once, or the meaning of a detail mentioned in passing. The writer may have a genuine insight, but one insight does not make an essay. After the first paragraph the material is exhausted, and the remaining pages are filled with repetition, speculation, or drift into neighboring subjects. The examiners who complain that an essay "runs out of steam" are usually describing a topic that was never evidence-rich enough to sustain the length required of it.

The conjunction of the two qualities is what matters, and each quality guards against a characteristic failure of the other. Arguability without evidence-richness produces the bold claim that collapses, the essay that opens with a striking position and then cannot support it, filling pages with assertion because the text will not supply proof. Evidence-richness without arguability produces the well-stocked summary, the essay that marches competently through the material, quoting accurately and observing correctly, but never saying anything a reader could disagree with, so that the competence has no point. The strong topics named in this article's claim pass both tests together. The American Dream is arguable because the novel can be read as endorsing the dream's promise and as exposing it, and it is evidence-rich because the dream is staged in Gatsby's pursuit, in Nick's judgments, in the valley of ashes, and in the closing pages. Class is arguable because the moral ledger between old and new money is genuinely disputed, and evidence-rich because the social world is drawn in scene after scene, the Buchanan house, the Wilson garage, the Plaza Hotel confrontation. The symbols are arguable because their meanings shift and invite rival readings, and evidence-rich because they recur. The narration is arguable because Nick's reliability is a live question, and evidence-rich because every page is narrated by him. When a writer tests a candidate topic, these are the two questions to ask, and in this order: could a smart reader disagree with the claim this topic invites, and does the novel give me enough to work with? A yes to both is the whole method of choosing well, and the rest of this article shows the method applied.

One more distinction clarifies what a topic is, by separating it from its neighbors. A subject is the broadest unit: "the American Dream," "social class," the whole territory. A topic is a specified portion of a subject: "Gatsby's dream of Daisy," "the novel's judgment on old money," a narrowed ground with a boundary. A question is what the prompt asks about the topic: "does the novel treat Gatsby's dream as noble or corrupt?" And a thesis is the writer's answer: the arguable sentence the essay will defend. Choosing well means moving deliberately down this ladder, subject to narrowed ground to question to thesis, and the characteristic failures are all skipped rungs. The too-broad topic never descends past the subject. The too-narrow topic descends past the topic to a detail with no question in it. The topic mistaken for a thesis jumps from subject to answer without ever finding the question. The ladder gives the writer a way to locate any choosing problem: ask which rung is missing, and supply it.

The test is easier to trust after watching it grade three real candidates. Take "the green light" as a first candidate. Arguable? As usually framed, no: the settled reading, hope and the unreachable future, is not disputed by any serious reader, so the essay can only restate it. Evidence-rich? Yes, abundantly, but the abundance serves description, not argument. Verdict: unproductive as framed, productive once specified to the light's changing function. Take "whether Tom Buchanan's violence is presented as personal cruelty or as the expression of his class" as a second candidate. Arguable? Yes: the personal-cruelty reading points to Tom's individual brutality, the broken nose in the apartment, the casual racism, the bullying; the class-expression reading points to the pattern, the way his violence always lands on those below him, Myrtle Wilson, George Wilson, and never on his equals. Evidence-rich? Yes: the apartment scene, the Plaza confrontation, the aftermath in which Tom and Daisy retreat. Verdict: productive as stated. Take "the significance of the number of Gatsby's party guests" as a third candidate. Arguable? Barely: a writer could claim the numbers measure the hollowness of his social world, but no reader is waiting to dispute it. Evidence-rich? No: the novel gives guest lists and crowds but no sustained material on counting. Verdict: unproductive, a curiosity rather than a topic. Notice how the test sorts without taste or instinct. It asks two questions, takes the answers, and the verdict follows.

Arguability needs one calibration, because not every disagreement is a productive one. The test requires that the disagreement be interesting, and interesting has a concrete meaning here: both sides must be able to reach for the text, and the outcome must change how the reader understands the novel. "Gatsby's parties were held on Saturdays" can be denied only by misreading; that is not a disagreement but an error, and topics built on errors are not arguable but merely wrong. "Daisy loved Gatsby more than Tom" admits textual support on both sides, the reunion's passion against the Plaza's retreat, but the dispute, pursued honestly, changes little about the novel's larger design; it is arguable but thin, and essays built on it tend to exhaust themselves in weighing scenes. "Whether the novel treats Gatsby's dream as noble or corrupt" changes the reader's understanding of the whole book depending on the answer; that is the interesting kind. The calibration rule: prefer the dispute whose resolution would alter the novel's meaning over the dispute whose resolution would merely settle a fact. Examiners recognize the difference instinctively, because the interesting dispute is the one that makes them reconsider their own reading, and reconsideration is what top marks reward.

Evidence-richness needs its calibration too. Abundance alone is not richness; what matters is variety across the essay's needs. A topic with ten passages that all make the same point is less evidence-rich than a topic with five passages that make five different points, because the essay needs material for its opening, its development, its counter-reading, and its close. When building the evidence list, the writer should check not just the count but the coverage: is there material for the claim, material for the strongest objection, and material for the reply? The class topic above passes this coverage test, since Tom's violence supports the claim, Gatsby's criminality supports the objection, and the distribution of consequences supports the reply. A topic that supplies only the claim's evidence will produce an essay that asserts without answering, and examiners notice the missing objection the way a reader notices a missing stair.

## The Strongest Topic Areas: Dream, Class, Symbols, Narration

The four territories named in the central claim are not equal in the demands they make, and a writer who understands their differences chooses more intelligently. Each is deep, but each is deep in a different way, and the kind of thesis each one supports follows from the kind of depth it offers. This section walks through the four, with attention to where each one tempts the writer into error.

The American Dream is the most chosen topic area and the most mishandled. Its productivity is obvious: the novel is saturated with the dream's language and imagery, from Gatsby's self-invention to the green light to the closing meditation on the future, and the question of what the book finally says about the dream admits genuine disagreement. Its danger is equally obvious: because the dream is everywhere in the novel, a topic framed as "the American Dream in The Great Gatsby" is really a topic about the whole book, and a topic about the whole book cannot be argued in the space of an essay. The productive move is to narrow the dream to a mechanism, a character, or a scene, and to take a position on that narrowed ground. "Whether Gatsby's dream ennobles or corrupts him" is arguable and evidence-rich: the ennobling reading points to his devotion, his capacity for hope, the famous passage about his wonder when he first saw Daisy; the corrupting reading points to the bootlegging, the parties as performance, the criminal ties. "How the novel uses the valley of ashes to price the dream" is narrower still and equally productive: the ashes are the industrial waste the dream leaves behind, and the writer can argue about whether the novel treats that waste as the dream's necessary cost or its accidental byproduct. The dream rewards writers who specify which version of the dream they mean, Gatsby's personal dream of Daisy, the national myth of self-making, the economic machinery that funds the parties, because each version has its own evidence and its own disputable claim. Writers who refuse to specify get the territory instead of the argument, and the territory is too large to defend.

The three versions of the dream deserve to be distinguished carefully, because each supports different theses and draws on different evidence. The personal dream is Gatsby's dream of Daisy: the green light, the shirts thrown in the air, the reunion, the past he wants to repeat. Theses on this version argue about the dream's object, whether Daisy was ever worthy of the devotion or whether the devotion needed an unworthy object to sustain itself, and the evidence is the love story's scenes. The national myth is the dream of self-making: James Gatz becoming Jay Gatsby, the schedules of the young Gatz, the invented past, the parties as the performance of arrival. Theses on this version argue about whether the novel treats self-invention as genuinely possible or as a performance that always requires an audience and a fortune, and the evidence is the biography the novel reconstructs. The economic dream is the machinery: the bootlegging, Meyer Wolfsheim, the underworld connections that fund the parties and the pursuit. Theses on this version argue about whether the novel treats Gatsby's criminality as incidental to his dream or as its necessary condition, the price the dream exacts, and the evidence is the business the novel keeps half-hidden. A writer who names which dream is meant has already done half the choosing; a writer who mixes them without noticing has chosen none of them.

Class and social standing form the second deep well, and their productivity comes from the novel's unusually sharp social geography. East Egg and West Egg face each other across the water, old money and new money, the Buchanans' inherited ease against Gatsby's performed arrival. The Wilsons in the valley of ashes anchor the bottom of the hierarchy, and the novel never lets the reader forget the distance between the garage and the mansion. A class topic becomes arguable the moment it asks what the novel thinks about these divisions rather than merely describing them. Consider the difference between "social class in The Great Gatsby" and "whether the novel reserves its harshest judgment for old money or distributes it evenly." The first invites a tour of the social world, accurate and dull. The second invites a dispute with evidence on both sides: the harshest-judgment-for-old-money reading cites Tom's violence, Daisy's carelessness, the famous passage about the Buchanans retreating into their money; the even-distribution reading cites Gatsby's criminality, the vulgarity of the parties, the corruption of the self-made myth. Both readings have passages. Neither is settled. That is what makes the topic productive. Class topics also reward attention to the novel's smaller social markers, the way characters speak, the way they handle objects, the parties' guest lists, the distinction between those who are invited and those who simply come, because Fitzgerald renders class in behavior rather than in declarations, and behavior gives the writer quotable evidence.

Those markers deserve enumeration in prose, since they are the concrete material a class topic lives on. Speech is the first: Tom's blunt assertions, Daisy's musical evasions, Gatsby's careful "old sport," each a class performance the writer can quote and argue about. Objects are the second: the Buchanan house described in its solidity, Gatsby's mansion in its theatricality, the yellow car as wealth made mobile and lethal, each carrying the social meaning the essay can dispute. Hospitality is the third: who is invited, who merely arrives, who is welcomed, who is watched, the parties' crowds against the funeral's emptiness, a whole social system rendered in guest lists. And money's movement is the fourth: who pays, who is paid, who can afford consequences and who cannot, the economic facts underneath the manners. A class topic that works through these markers has evidence for every paragraph and a dispute running through all of it, whether the novel treats these performances as the substance of class or as its costume. The writer who chooses class should choose it through the markers, not above them, because the markers are where the arguable claims live.

### Which topic areas punish the careless writer most?

The American Dream and the symbol system punish carelessness most, because both look inexhaustible and both collapse under vague handling, while class and narration reward even modest precision. The dream tempts sloganizing without specification, and the symbols tempt single-meaning decoding, so both demand deliberate narrowing where the more concrete territories protect the writer.

The dream punishes the writer who treats it as a slogan. Because the phrase "the American Dream" is so familiar, it is easy to write about it without ever saying which dream is meant, and an essay that never specifies its object can only repeat generalities. The writer who specifies, Gatsby's dream of Daisy as distinct from the national myth, or the economic dream as distinct from the romantic one, finds the topic opening up instead of closing down. The symbols punish the writer who decodes them once and stops. Because a symbol like the green light is so tempting to translate, the careless essay renders it as a single meaning, hope, and then has nothing left to say. The careful writer tracks the symbol's movement, its first appearance, its returns, its changed charge in the closing pages, and finds that the movement itself is arguable. Class and narration are more forgiving. A class topic that names specific characters and scenes, Tom's apartment violence, the Plaza confrontation, the garage, almost cannot go wrong, because the evidence is so concrete. A narration topic that stays close to Nick's actual sentences, his judgments, his self-exculpations, his retrospective framing, has the same protection. The pattern is worth internalizing: the more abstract the topic area, the more precision it demands; the more concrete the material, the more it protects the writer.

### When does a symbol topic beat a theme topic?

A symbol topic beats a theme topic when the writer wants to argue about how meaning is made rather than what is meant, because symbols give the essay traceable evidence while themes invite summary. When a theme draft drifts into listing illustrations, switch to the symbol; the trackable image restores the argument.

A theme-first approach, "illusion versus reality in the novel," asks the writer to survey a large idea across many scenes, and the survey form pulls toward summary: here is an illusion, here is a reality, here is another. A symbol topic, "the changing charge of the green light," asks the writer to track one thing through its appearances, and the tracking form pulls toward argument: the light means this here and that there, and the change itself is the claim. This is not a rule against theme-based essays; some of the strongest Gatsby essays are theme essays. It is a diagnostic for writers who find their theme drafts drifting into description. When the drift starts, the cure is usually to anchor the theme in a symbol, a scene, or an image, and let the concrete thing carry the abstract claim. "How the novel's parties stage the difference between appearance and reality" is a theme made arguable by its anchor; the parties are describable, quotable, and finite, and the writer can argue about what the staging shows. The general principle: the more the topic names something the reader can picture, the more the essay can argue about it, and symbols are the most pictureable things in the novel.

The symbol system's productivity deserves a fuller account, because it is the territory where the difference between a dead topic and a live one is most visible. A dead treatment of a symbol asks what a symbol means, as though the novel had planted a riddle with a single answer. "What does the green light symbolize" is the most famous dead topic in Gatsby studies, not because the green light is uninteresting but because the question has a settled answer, hope and the future, and a settled answer cannot be argued. A live treatment asks how a symbol works, when it appears, what changes between its appearances, and what those changes do to the reader. "Why the green light matters more at the novel's end than at its beginning" is live: the writer can argue that the light's meaning deepens, or that it empties out, or that Nick's closing meditation reframes everything the light meant earlier, and each position has textual support. The same transformation applies across the system. The valley of ashes is dead as "what does it represent" and live as "how the ashes accumulate meaning across the chapters in which they appear." The eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg are dead as a decoding exercise and live as a question about perspective: who sees them, when, and what the characters project onto them. The cars are dead as status symbols and live as instruments, what the yellow car actually does in the plot, who drives it, who dies by it. In every case the move is the same: replace the question of meaning with the question of function, and the topic becomes arguable and evidence-rich at once.

The narration is the fourth territory, and it is the one most writers underuse. Nick Carraway narrates the entire novel, which means every topic area, the dream, class, the symbols, is mediated by his telling. A narration topic makes that mediation the subject. The productive questions are the ones that treat Nick as a writer with interests rather than as a window onto events. "How Nick's retrospective framing shapes the reader's judgment of Gatsby" is arguable: one reading holds that Nick's admiration ennobles Gatsby beyond what the facts support, another holds that Nick's moral judgments, his famous distinction between Gatsby's hope and the foul dust that floated in its wake, are the novel's real moral center. Both readings work from Nick's sentences. "What Nick gains by telling the story after the fact" is equally productive: the retrospective structure lets him arrange, omit, and comment, and the writer can argue about whether the arrangement clarifies or distorts. The danger of narration topics is the opposite of the dream's danger. Where the dream tempts vagueness, narration tempts the writer into treating Nick as a real person to be psychoanalyzed, and the essay becomes speculation about motives the text never shows. The discipline is to stay with the sentences, what Nick says, when he says it, what he withholds, because the narration topic is productive exactly insofar as it is textual.

The American identity, the fifth thread named in the brief, runs through all four territories and is best handled as a connective rather than a separate subject. Gatsby's self-invention as an American story, the frontier myth rewritten as Long Island parties, the national past invoked in the closing pages, all of these give a writer ways to connect a dream topic to a narration topic or a class topic to a symbol topic. The [themes overview](/2022/01/31/great-gatsby-themes-overview/) maps these connections at the whole-novel level, and a writer choosing a topic benefits from seeing where the territories touch before committing to one.

It is worth pausing on why the connective matters for choosing, since connectives are where good essays find their second paragraph. A topic that lives in one territory has one dispute; a topic that connects two has the dispute between them. "How Gatsby's self-invention as an American success story depends on Nick's telling of it" joins the dream to the narration, and the essay can argue about whether the telling creates the myth or merely reports it. "How the novel's symbols price the class system" joins symbols to class, and the essay can argue about whether the imagery condemns or merely decorates the hierarchy. These joined topics are harder to choose well, because the writer must control two territories, but they are also harder to write badly, because the join itself supplies the argumentative friction. Writers who have mastered single-territory choosing should practice the join as the next step, using the same two-part test on the combined ground.

## How the Prompt Signals Its Topic Area

In examinations the writer does not always choose freely; the prompt constrains the choice, and reading the prompt's signals is part of choosing well. Prompts signal their territory through their verbs and their nouns, and a writer who reads those signals chooses a topic the prompt can reward rather than one the prompt will punish.

The nouns point to territory. A prompt naming "the American Dream" points to the dream territory; one naming "social class," "wealth," or "society" points to class; one naming "imagery," "symbols," or a specific image points to the symbol system; one naming "the narrator," "perspective," or "telling" points to narration. The verbs point to the kind of claim. "Discuss," "evaluate," and "to what extent" invite the arguable thesis directly; they are permissions to take a position. "Explore" and "consider" look softer but reward the same move; an exploration with a position scores above an exploration without one. "How does Fitzgerald present" asks about method, which suits symbol and narration topics, where the how is traceable. The writer's job is to match a prepared topic to these signals and then re-narrow: the prepared class topic meets the wealth prompt by specifying which aspect of wealth this prompt asks about, and the re-narrowing is what turns preparation into an answer rather than a reproduction.

The dangerous prompts are the open ones, "write about an aspect of the novel you find interesting," which signal nothing and therefore tempt the writer to choose nothing, drifting into the broadest available territory. The defense is to impose the narrowing the prompt omits: pick the prepared topic with the sharpest dispute, specify it to the aspect genuinely found interesting, and proceed as though the prompt had asked for it. Open prompts reward the writer who brings their own discipline, because the examiner, deprived of a question to check the answer against, checks instead for the presence of an argument. The topic that supplies one wins; the topic that merely names an interest loses. In every case the principle is the same: the prompt constrains, the writer chooses within the constraint, and the choosing is still governed by the two-part test.

Prompt misreading is the quiet saboteur of good selection, and it takes two characteristic forms. The first is answering the prepared choice instead of the prompt: the writer, pleased with a prepared topic, reproduces it without re-narrowing, so the essay argues well about something the question did not ask. Examiners penalize this specifically, because relevance is a separate criterion from argument quality; a brilliant essay on the wrong question is still the wrong answer. The defense is the re-narrowing sentence, written during planning, that states the dispute as this prompt frames it. The second form is the opposite: the writer, intimidated by a precisely worded prompt, abandons the prepared choice for something improvised that matches the wording more closely but was never tested. Close matching feels safe and is dangerous, because the improvised topic has no evidence list and no confirmed dispute. The correct response to a precise prompt is to fit the prepared topic to it, not to invent anew. Both misreadings share a root, which is treating the prompt as either irrelevant or all-determining; the truth is between, the prompt sets the question, the prepared topic supplies the tested ground, and the writer's job is the fitting.

## The Complication: Too Broad, Too Narrow, and the Topic That Is Not a Thesis

The standard this article teaches, arguable and evidence-rich, sounds simple, and simple standards are the ones most often misapplied. The misapplications have names, and naming them is the honest complication this section addresses, because choosing well requires recognizing the characteristic ways choosing goes wrong. There are three, and each has a distinct texture.

The too-broad choice is the most common failure, and it is the failure of ambition. The writer chooses a subject worthy of a book, "the American Dream in The Great Gatsby," "wealth and morality," "the role of women," and discovers, usually three paragraphs into the draft, that the subject cannot be argued within the essay's limits. What happens next is predictable. The essay becomes a survey, touching each relevant scene briefly, quoting a line here and a line there, never staying anywhere long enough to make a point. The writer senses the thinness and compensates with generality, sentences that are true of the novel and of a dozen other novels, "wealth corrupts those who possess it," "dreams can be destructive." The examiner's margin note, when it comes, says "descriptive" or "lacks focus," and both notes describe the same underlying condition: the topic admitted no dispute because it attempted everything. The diagnostic question for broadness is concrete: can you state, in one sentence, what your essay will argue that a thoughtful reader could disagree with? If the answer requires a paragraph, or if the answer is really a list of things the essay will cover, the topic is too broad. The cure is not to abandon the territory but to narrow it by one of three cuts. Cut by character: not "the American Dream" but "Gatsby's dream of Daisy as the novel tests it." Cut by scene: not "wealth and morality" but "the Plaza Hotel confrontation as the novel's judgment on its wealthy." Cut by mechanism: not "the role of women" but "how the novel uses Daisy's voice, the famous passages about its charm, to make carelessness look like innocence." Each cut preserves the territory's depth while giving the essay a boundary, and a boundary is what makes argument possible.

The too-narrow choice is the rarer failure and the more quietly fatal one, because it often disguises itself as precision. The writer chooses a subject so small that it cannot sustain an essay, "the significance of Gatsby's yellow car in chapter seven," "the meaning of the owl-eyed man's spectacles," "why Nick mentions a particular book on Gatsby's shelf," and the draft runs out of material before it has begun. The writer then does one of two things. Some pad, repeating the single insight in different words until the paragraph count is met, and the padding is visible because the same sentence keeps returning in new clothes. Others drift, quietly expanding the topic until it becomes a different, broader topic, and the essay's opening no longer matches its ending. Both outcomes fail, and both are avoidable. The diagnostic question for narrowness is the mirror of the broadness test: can you name, before drafting, four or five distinct pieces of textual evidence the essay will use? Not four ways of saying the same thing, but four genuinely different passages, images, or scenes. If the list cannot be made, the topic is too narrow for the length required. The cure is to widen by connection: the yellow car connects to the novel's larger treatment of wealth as an instrument of harm, the owl-eyed man connects to the theme of spectatorship and judgment, the bookshelf connects to Gatsby's performed identity. Widening is not the same as abandoning precision; it is finding the larger pattern of which the precise detail is an instance, and arguing about the pattern through the detail.

The third failure is the most instructive because it looks like success. The writer chooses what appears to be a subject and what is really a thesis, or rather, the writer mistakes a thesis-shaped sentence for a topic and then never does the work the thesis requires. "That Gatsby's parties reveal the emptiness of Jazz Age wealth" is not a topic; it is a claim, and a defensible one. But the writer who treats it as a topic, as a subject to be covered rather than a position to be defended, will write an essay that lists the parties' features, the guest lists, the orchestras, the food, without ever arguing the emptiness, because arguing requires engaging the counter-reading, and the counter-reading, that the parties are genuinely joyful, that their emptiness is Nick's projection, is never addressed. The essay reads as competent and earns a middling mark, and the writer cannot understand why, because the opening sentence looked like an argument. The lesson is that a topic is not a thesis, and the conversion between them is not automatic. A topic names the territory. A thesis stakes the claim. The writer who confuses them skips the most important step, which is deciding what position the essay will defend and what evidence will defend it. The worked transformations in the next section show that conversion in detail, because it is the step where topic choice pays off or is wasted.

There is a further complication worth stating plainly, because it affects how the standard should be applied under examination conditions. The arguable-and-evidence-rich test assumes the writer has time to test and revise the choice, and timed essays do not always allow it. Under time pressure, the risk calculus shifts. A slightly broad topic that the writer knows deeply is safer than a perfectly calibrated topic chosen cold, because depth of knowledge compensates for imprecision of boundary, while a precise boundary around unfamiliar material collapses under pressure. The practical advice follows: prepare topics, not theses, before the examination, two or three from different territories, each tested against the text in advance, so that the exam itself requires only the final narrowing to fit the prompt. Preparation of this kind is the subject of the study section later in this article, and it is the single highest-value use of revision time for the essay paper.

A final word on the failures, because they share a root. The too-broad choice, the too-narrow choice, and the topic mistaken for a thesis all come from the same error, which is treating the topic as a label for the essay rather than as the essay's first argumentative decision. A label describes what the essay is about. A decision commits the writer to a direction. The writers who choose well are not the ones with the most original subjects; they are the ones who understand that the choice is a commitment, and who make it as one, narrowing until the topic can be disputed and checking until it can be defended. Originality, when it comes, comes later, in the handling, not in the choosing.

Three brief autopsies make the failures concrete, because abstract warnings are easy to nod at and hard to apply. First autopsy: the essay titled "The American Dream in The Great Gatsby" that earned a middle mark despite fluent writing. The topic was the whole territory. The introduction announced that the novel explores the dream, the body visited Gatsby's parties, the green light, and the ending, and the conclusion restated that the dream is complex. Every sentence was true and none was arguable; the examiner's note said the essay "covers the topic" rather than arguing it, which is the precise language of the too-broad failure. The writer could have saved it with one cut, "Gatsby's dream of Daisy as the novel tests it," and one dispute, ennobling or corrupting, and the same material would have produced an argument. Second autopsy: the essay on "the owl-eyed man's function as a moral witness" that collapsed after two pages. The insight was genuine, the owl-eyed man does watch, and does judge, but the novel gives him two scenes, and two scenes cannot carry an essay. The writer padded with repetition and then drifted into the parties generally, and the introduction's promise was quietly abandoned. The cure was widening by connection, the owl-eyed man as an instance of the novel's spectatorship, which would have supplied the parties, Nick's watching, and the funeral as evidence. Third autopsy: the essay that opened "Gatsby's parties reveal the emptiness of Jazz Age wealth" and then listed the parties' features for six pages. The opening was a thesis, but the writer treated it as a topic, never engaging the counter-reading that the parties are joyful, never arguing the emptiness, only inventorying it. The mark reflected an essay that announced an argument and delivered a catalogue. In each autopsy the failure was diagnosable before drafting with the two questions, and in each the cure was available: narrow, widen, or convert.

The choosing routine can be written as a short checklist, not to be followed mechanically forever but to be internalized until it becomes habit. First, frame the candidate topic in a sentence and ask whether a thoughtful reader could dispute the claim it invites; if the answer is no, the topic is dead as framed, and the writer must specify until a dispute appears or discard it. Second, list four or five distinct pieces of textual evidence, checking coverage for the claim, the objection, and the reply; if the list cannot be made, the topic is too narrow, and the writer must widen by connection. Third, check the breadth: can the dispute be stated in one sentence, or does stating it require a paragraph? If a paragraph, the topic is too broad, and the writer must cut by character, scene, or mechanism. Fourth, confirm the topic is not a thesis in disguise by asking what work remains for the essay to do; if the sentence already contains the whole argument, the essay will have nothing to prove, and the writer should step back to the topic and convert deliberately. Fifth, match the topic to the format: sturdy and memorizable for timed exams, ambitious and carefully evidenced for coursework, joined at a named point of contact for comparative work. Five checks, each with a concrete question and a concrete repair. Writers who run candidates through them stop choosing by feel and start choosing by test, and the essays that follow show the difference on the first page.

## Adapting a Topic into a Thesis: Worked Transformations

A topic is a starting point, and the essay begins when the starting point is converted into a claim. This conversion is the most teachable part of the whole process, because it follows visible moves, and this section shows those moves applied to one topic from each of the four territories. The pattern in every case is the same: the topic names the ground, the thesis takes a position on it, and the position is stated with the specificity that lets the essay be checked against the text.

Take the dream territory first. The topic, as a writer might initially frame it: "the American Dream in The Great Gatsby." The conversion begins by specifying which dream, because the novel contains several, and then by finding the dispute. One productive specification: Gatsby's personal dream, the green light, Daisy, the future he reaches toward. The dispute: does the novel treat that dream as noble in itself and corrupted only by its object, or does it treat the dream as corrupt from the start, a fantasy that requires self-deception? The thesis: "The Great Gatsby treats Gatsby's dream as noble in its capacity for hope and corrupt in its chosen object, so that the novel's tragedy is not the failure of the dream but its success, Gatsby gets close enough to Daisy to discover that what he wanted was the wanting." Notice what the conversion added: a position a reader could dispute, the names of the mechanism, hope versus object, and a paradox, success as tragedy, that gives the essay its energy. The evidence is ready to hand: the early passages on Gatsby's wonder, the reunion scenes, the closing meditation. The topic invited description; the thesis demands argument.

Take the class territory. The topic: "old money and new money in the novel." The specification: the moral comparison between the two orders, as staged in the scenes where they collide. The dispute: whether the novel's harshest judgment falls on old money, embodied in Tom Buchanan's casual violence and Daisy Buchanan's carelessness, or whether it distributes blame, refusing to romanticize Gatsby's criminal ascent. The thesis: "The Great Gatsby reserves its contempt for old money's carelessness rather than new money's vulgarity, because Tom and Daisy can afford the consequences of their actions while Gatsby and the Wilsons pay for them, and the novel measures morality by who bears the cost." The conversion added the mechanism, the distribution of consequences, and the test, who pays. The evidence: the New York apartment scene where Tom breaks Myrtle Wilson's nose, the Plaza confrontation, the Buchanans' retreat at the novel's end, Gatsby's death in the pool while Daisy and Tom leave town. A reader could argue the other side, that Gatsby's bootlegging and his association with Meyer Wolfsheim implicate new money just as deeply, and that counter-reading is exactly what makes the thesis arguable.

Take the symbols. The topic: "the green light." The specification: the light's function across its appearances, from the first chapter to the closing pages. The dispute: whether the light's meaning deepens or empties as the novel proceeds. The thesis: "The green light loses its particular meaning as Gatsby approaches it and gains a universal one, so that the symbol's arc from Daisy's dock to the novel's final pages mirrors the essay's own movement from desire to disillusionment, and Nick's closing meditation is not a restatement of the light's meaning but its transformation." The conversion added the arc, the change between appearances, as the thing to be argued about. The evidence: the end of chapter one, the reunion, the final pages. The counter-reading, that the light means the same thing throughout and Nick merely elaborates it, is available and defensible, which is what makes the claim worth defending.

Take the narration. The topic: "Nick Carraway as narrator." The specification: the retrospective structure, Nick telling the story after the events, with the benefit and the distortion of hindsight. The dispute: whether Nick's hindsight clarifies his judgment or corrupts it, whether the older Nick telling the story sees more clearly or merely arranges the past to excuse himself. The thesis: "Nick Carraway's retrospective narration is reliable about what happened and unreliable about why he tells it, and the novel's real subject is the gap between those two reliabilities, the story Nick reports and the story his telling serves." The conversion added the split, event versus motive, and named the gap as the subject. The evidence: Nick's opening disclaimers, his judgments of Gatsby, his treatment of his own role in arranging the reunion, his closing sentences. The counter-reading, that Nick is straightforwardly honest and the novel gives no reason to doubt his self-account, has support too, and the essay earns its marks by working through the dispute rather than ignoring it.

Four conversions, one method. In each case the writer specified the topic, located the dispute, and stated a position with a mechanism. The method is mechanical enough to be practiced and flexible enough to fit any territory, and it is the bridge between this article and [the article on thesis statements](/2030/04/08/gatsby-thesis-statement/), which takes the converted claim and develops it into the full architecture of the essay. The topic article owns the choosing; the thesis article owns the building. Together they cover the distance from a blank page to an arguable sentence.

A second round of transformations, on less obvious candidates, shows the method handling harder material. Take the valley of ashes. The topic as first framed, "the valley of ashes as a symbol," is the dead kind: the settled reading, industrial waste as the moral cost of wealth, invites restatement. The specification: the ashes' placement between West Egg and New York, the ground everyone must cross, and the question of whose cost the ashes represent. The dispute: does the novel treat the ashes as the necessary price of the Jazz Age economy, waste the system cannot avoid producing, or as its indictment, waste the system chooses to look away from? The thesis: "The valley of ashes is not the novel's symbol of poverty but its ledger of cost, and Fitzgerald places it on the road between wealth and the city so that every journey in the novel must pass through the bill, which makes the ashes an accusation rather than a backdrop." The conversion found the dispute in the placement, not in the meaning, and the essay now has something to argue: accusation or backdrop, with the road's geography as evidence.

Take Daisy's voice. The topic, "the significance of Daisy's voice," risks the same deadness, since the famous description of its charm is widely quoted and rarely argued about. The specification: the voice's function in the scenes where decisions are made, the reunion, the Plaza confrontation, the final withdrawal. The dispute: does the voice's charm humanize Daisy, making her carelessness tragic, or does it operate as her alibi, making carelessness sound like innocence? The thesis: "Daisy Buchanan's voice is the novel's most effective instrument of evasion, because its charm persuades Nick, Gatsby, and the reader to experience her withdrawals as sorrows rather than choices, and the essay that tracks the voice through the decisive scenes finds carelessness wearing the sound of feeling." The counter-reading, that the charm is genuine and Daisy's tragedy is real, has the novel's own lyricism behind it, which is what makes the claim worth defending. In both transformations the method did the same work: it refused the settled meaning, specified the ground, and located the live disagreement.

It helps to watch a conversion fail and then be repaired, because the failure mode is instructive. A writer takes the topic "the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg" and produces what looks like a thesis: "The eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg represent God watching and judging the characters." The sentence has the shape of a claim, but the test exposes it. Arguable? Not productively: the God-reading is the standard one, and disputing it requires more ingenuity than the text repays. Specific? It names the image but no mechanism, no scene, no change. The sentence is a decoding wearing a thesis's clothes, and an essay built on it will restate the decoding for pages. The repair starts by abandoning meaning for function. Who looks at the eyes, and when? George Wilson looks at them in his grief and reads them as God; Nick notes them as an advertisement; the characters project onto them what they need. The dispute appears: do the eyes judge, or do they merely receive the characters' projections, judging nothing? The revised thesis: "The eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg do not watch the novel's world so much as expose the watchers, because each character who looks at them sees a different moral order, and the billboard's meaning is made by the looking rather than the paint." Now the essay has appearances to track, Wilson's grief, Nick's observation, the ash-gray setting, and a claim a reader could dispute. The failed version asked what the eyes mean; the repaired version asks what they do. That single shift, from meaning to function, is the most reliable repair in the whole method, and writers should reach for it whenever a conversion stalls.

### What does choosing well look like on the first page of the essay?

Choosing well shows on the first page as a thesis that names its ground and its dispute, so the reader knows within seconds what will be argued and what evidence will decide it. The introduction names the territory, states the claim, and signals the evidence, and the conversion from topic to thesis is visible.

The opening paragraph of a well-chosen essay does three things quickly. It names the territory, the dream, class, a symbol, the narration, in the novel's own terms rather than in generalities. It states the claim the essay will defend, a sentence a thoughtful reader could disagree with. And it signals the evidence, the scenes or images or passages where the claim will be tested, so the reader can see the essay's shape before it unfolds. An introduction that does these three things has converted its topic into a thesis, and the conversion is visible. An introduction that names only the territory, however elegantly, has not chosen well yet, whatever its polish. Examiners learn to read introductions for this signal, and the signal is reliable: essays that announce their dispute in the first page go on to argue, and essays that do not go on to describe.

The discipline extends beyond the introduction. A well-chosen topic keeps paying off in the body paragraphs, because each paragraph can be checked against the claim: does this passage advance the argument or answer an objection? Paragraphs in a well-chosen essay have that checkable quality; the writer knows what each paragraph is for. Paragraphs in a poorly chosen essay drift, because the topic supplied no principle of selection, and the writer reaches for whatever material is at hand. The difference is visible to the examiner by the second page, and it compounds. This is why the article's claim insists that choosing well sets up the whole essay: the setup is not a metaphor. It is the literal mechanism by which the topic determines the thesis, the thesis determines the paragraph plan, and the plan determines the essay.

### Why do examiners reward topic discipline?

Examiners reward topic discipline because a disciplined topic makes the essay's thinking visible, and visible thinking is what the mark scheme measures, not coverage and not polish. A narrowed, evidence-checked topic forces the writer to judge between readings, and that judgment is the thinking the top bands describe and reward.

Consider the examiner's position. Dozens of essays on the same novel arrive together, most of them competent, many of them accurate, and the examiner's task is to distinguish the thinking from the reporting. The distinction cannot be made on accuracy alone, because accurate summary is common. It is made on the presence of an argument, and an argument is present exactly when the essay takes a position a reader could dispute and defends it with evidence. Topic discipline, the narrowing to arguable ground, the checking for evidence, the conversion into a thesis, is what produces that presence. The examiner rewards it because it is the thing being examined. This is also why originality of topic is overrated as a strategy. An unusual topic argued badly scores less than a familiar topic argued well, because the mark scheme measures the quality of the thinking, not the novelty of the subject. The dream, class, the symbols, the narration, these are the most written-about territories in Gatsby studies, and they remain the highest-scoring, because they are the most arguable and the most evidence-rich. Familiarity is not a penalty. Vagueness is.

The mark scheme's language confirms this from the other side. The descriptors for the top bands speak of argument, judgment, and the handling of alternative readings; the descriptors for the middle bands speak of knowledge, understanding, and range. Knowledge and range are what the undisciplined topic supplies: the survey knows a lot and covers a lot. Argument and judgment are what the disciplined topic makes possible: the narrowed topic forces the writer to judge between readings, and the evidence check forces the handling of alternatives. A writer who understands this stops asking "what can I write about" and starts asking "what can I argue," and the change of question changes the essay. The topic is where that change happens, which is why the examiner, reading the first page, is really reading the topic: the introduction reveals whether the writer asked the first question or the second, and the mark follows.

## Choosing for Different Essay Formats: Coursework, Timed Exams, Comparative Work

The standard does not change between formats, but the choosing strategy does, because each format changes what the topic must survive. A topic that is ideal for a two-week coursework essay can be dangerous in a forty-five-minute exam, and the writer who understands why chooses differently for each.

Coursework rewards the ambitious topic, because time is the resource the format supplies. With days to test, revise, and gather evidence, the writer can afford a topic whose dispute is subtle and whose evidence must be assembled carefully: a narration topic that tracks Nick's judgments across all nine chapters, a symbol topic that follows the color imagery through its variations, a combination topic that joins narration and class. The coursework danger is the opposite of the exam danger. Where the timed writer chooses too hastily, the coursework writer chooses too grandly, and the essay sprawls. The discipline for coursework is the same two-part test applied with more patience: test the topic against the text, revise the narrowing, and confirm the evidence list before the drafting begins, because coursework gives you the time to do the checking that the exam denies.

Timed exams reward the sturdy topic, the one whose dispute can be stated in a sentence and whose evidence can be listed from memory. Class topics and tracked-symbol topics are the natural choices, for the reasons given earlier: concrete scenes, quotable images, clean disputes. The timed-exam choosing routine should be rehearsed, not improvised. In preparation, the writer selects two or three topics from different territories, narrows each, identifies each dispute, and memorizes the evidence list, so the exam requires only matching the prompt to the nearest prepared topic and re-narrowing to fit. The most common timed-exam error is choosing in the exam hall from scratch, seduced by a topic that looked interesting during revision but was never tested. A tested sturdy topic beats an untested brilliant one under time pressure, because the test is what makes the topic reliable, and reliability is what the clock demands.

Comparative work, pairing Gatsby with another text, changes the choosing logic most sharply, because the topic must now be arguable about two works at once. The productive comparative topics are the ones where the comparison creates the dispute: does the second novel treat the self-made myth the way Gatsby does, or does it invert it? The unproductive ones merely staple two single-text topics together, "the American Dream in Gatsby and in the other novel," which produces two half-essays rather than one argument. The choosing rule for comparative essays: the topic must name the point of contact, the shared question both texts answer differently, and the thesis must take a position on the difference. "How the two novels price the self-made myth" is a topic; "the self-made myth in two novels" is a stapled pair. The evidence burden doubles, so the comparative writer should be especially strict about the evidence check, confirming material from both texts before committing.

Across all three formats, one principle holds: prepare topics, not essays. The writer who memorizes a finished essay can only reproduce it, and reproduction fails the moment the prompt differs. The writer who prepares tested topics, each with its dispute identified and its evidence listed, can adapt to any prompt in the territory, because adaptation is what the preparation trained. The study section that follows is built around this principle.

## When the Prompt Fights Your Prepared Topic

Sometimes the examination prompt does not match any prepared topic, and the writer must choose under fire. The method for this situation is a compressed version of the full routine, designed to run in the planning minutes rather than over days.

First, read the prompt for its territory signals, the nouns and verbs described earlier, and identify the nearest prepared topic, even if the fit is imperfect. Proximity matters more than perfection under time pressure; a prepared class topic bent toward a prompt about wealth will outperform an unprepared topic chosen cold, because the prepared topic carries its evidence list with it. Second, re-narrow explicitly: write one sentence stating the dispute as this prompt frames it, and check that the sentence is arguable. If the sentence collapses into description, narrow further, by character, by scene, or by mechanism, until the dispute reappears. Third, run the fastest version of the evidence check: name three passages you can actually quote or closely paraphrase from memory. Three is the timed-exam minimum, and if three cannot be named, the topic is not ready and a nearer prepared topic should be tried instead.

What the writer must not do in this situation is abandon preparation for inspiration, choosing a topic that feels responsive to the prompt but was never tested. The untested topic is where the characteristic exam failures come from: the broad topic that becomes a survey, the clever topic that runs out of evidence, the thesis-shaped topic that never engages its counter-reading. A prepared topic, even imperfectly fitted, has survived the two-part test; the untested one has survived nothing. The planning minutes are for fitting, not for inventing, and writers who internalize that distinction choose better under pressure than writers who trust the moment.

## Study Section: Test the Topic Against the Evidence, Then Practice

Choosing well is a skill, and skills are built by repetition with feedback, not by reading about the skill once. This section sets out the study routine this article recommends, in two stages that match the two qualities of a productive topic.

The first stage tests the topic against the evidence before any drafting begins. The writer takes the candidate topic, narrows it by one of the three cuts described earlier, by character, by scene, or by mechanism, and then checks whether the novel supplies the material to sustain it. The practical form of the check is a short evidence list: four or five passages, images, or scenes, each tied to a location in the novel, that the essay could use. If the list can be made, the topic is evidence-rich enough. If it cannot, the topic needs widening by connection or narrowing by specification, and the writer knows this before the draft, when revision is cheap, rather than mid-draft, when it is expensive. The annotated text at [VaultBook's Great Gatsby study page](https://vaultbook.net/tools/great-gatsby-annotated-text.html) is the place to run this check, because the annotations let the writer test a candidate topic against the novel's actual passages, tracing a symbol's appearances or a character's scenes, before committing the essay to it.

The second stage practices the conversion of the topic into a thesis under conditions that resemble the examination. The writer takes the tested topic, states the dispute it admits, and drafts the arguable sentence, then revises it against the standard, arguable, specific, defensible, until the sentence could survive a skeptical reader. Doing this three or four times, across the four territories, builds the choosing habit faster than any amount of reading about choosing. The essay-practice environment at [ReportMedic's Great Gatsby practice page](https://reportmedic.org/tools/great-gatsby-essay-practice.html) is where to do this work, because it is built for developing a topic into an argument and getting feedback on the result, which is exactly the move this article teaches.

The drill itself can be run in fifteen minutes and is worth repeating weekly. Take one broad topic, "the American Dream," and produce three different narrowings, one by character, one by scene, one by mechanism, each with its dispute stated in a single sentence. Then take one narrow topic, "the owl-eyed man's spectacles," and widen it by connection to two different larger patterns, each with its evidence list. Then take one dead topic, "what the green light symbolizes," and convert it twice, once to a functional claim about its changing charge and once to a narration claim about how Nick's telling frames it. Six conversions, each checked against the two-part test, each producing a thesis-shaped sentence. The drill trains exactly the moves the examination demands: narrowing under the prompt's constraint, widening when the material runs thin, and converting the topic into the claim. Writers who run it regularly report that the choosing stage, which once consumed their planning time in anxious circling, becomes the fastest part of the process, because the moves are rehearsed and the test is trusted.

The routine closes the loop with the rest of the series. The full essay process, from prompt to polished draft, is taught in [the hub guide on writing a Great Gatsby essay](/2030/04/01/how-to-write-gatsby-essay/), which places topic choice among the other stages. A companion resource on essay questions is maintained separately at [/2030/05/20/gatsby-essay-questions/](/2030/05/20/gatsby-essay-questions/). The topic article you are reading owns the choosing; the hub owns the process; the practice tools own the repetition. A writer who uses all three, choosing with the standard, placing the choice in the process, and rehearsing it with feedback, arrives at the examination with topics prepared and the choosing habit trained, which is the whole of what preparation can do.

A worked evidence-list check shows what the first stage looks like in practice. Take the candidate topic "how the novel distributes moral judgment between old and new money," narrowed by scene to the Plaza Hotel confrontation and its aftermath. The evidence list: first, the confrontation itself, where Tom exposes Gatsby and Daisy retreats, giving the essay its central scene; second, the New York apartment episode earlier, where Tom breaks Myrtle Wilson's nose, establishing the pattern of old-money violence; third, Gatsby's criminal associations, the bootlegging and Meyer Wolfsheim, giving the counter-reading its material; fourth, the Buchanans' departure at the novel's end, retreating into their money, giving the essay its closing evidence; fifth, Nick's final judgment on the Buchanans as careless people, giving the essay a quotable verdict to argue with or against. Five distinct items, each locatable, each serving a different paragraph. The dispute check: does the novel condemn old money more harshly, or both orders evenly? Both sides have passages. The topic passes, and the writer knows it before drafting, which is the entire point of the exercise.

A further practice sharpens the habit between study sessions: the choosing journal. The writer keeps a running document, one entry per candidate topic, each entry containing the topic as first framed, the narrowed version, the dispute stated in one sentence, the evidence list, and the verdict with its reason. Failed candidates are kept, not deleted, because the record of why a topic failed, too broad, too narrow, no dispute, thin evidence, is what trains the eye to recognize the patterns quickly. Over weeks the journal becomes a personal catalogue of tested topics, and the catalogue is what the writer carries into the examination: not memorized essays but proven ground. The journal also cures the most stubborn choosing weakness, which is the reluctance to discard. Writers fall in love with their topics, especially clever ones, and defend them past the point where the test has failed them. A journal entry that records the failure in the writer's own words makes the discard easier, because the decision was already made during testing, when it was cheap.

## The Verdict: Choose the Dispute, Not the Subject

The position this article takes is simple enough to state in one sentence, and the whole article has been an elaboration of it: choose the dispute, not the subject. The writers who score well on Gatsby essays are not the ones who find the cleverest topic. They are the ones who find, inside an ordinary topic, the argument it can sustain, and who commit to that argument early enough for it to shape the essay.

That commitment has a temporal dimension worth naming. The writer who chooses the dispute early, during planning, gives the essay its shape before the sentences exist, and the drafting becomes the work of filling a shape already made. The writer who postpones the choice, hoping the argument will emerge from the writing, discovers that writing without a dispute produces description, and that description, once written, resists conversion into argument. The pages exist, the effort was spent, and the essay cannot be argued into shape afterward without being rewritten. Early choosing is therefore not just better choosing; it is the only choosing that fully works, because the topic's power over the essay is exercised before the draft, in the planning, and a topic chosen late governs nothing.

The standard itself is worth one last statement, since everything in this article reduces to it. A topic is strong when a thoughtful reader could disagree with the claim it invites and the novel supplies the evidence to defend that claim. Arguable and evidence-rich: two words that contain the whole method. The writer who tests every candidate against them, who narrows the broad, widens the narrow, and converts the topic into the thesis, will choose well on any prompt, in any format, on this novel or any other. The choosing is learnable, the test is mechanical, and the reward is an essay that argues. Choose the dispute.

This verdict has a consequence that some readers will resist. It implies that topic choice matters more than most writing guides admit and less than the cleverness market suggests. It matters more because it determines, before drafting, whether the essay will argue or describe, and no later stage can reverse that determination. It matters less because the choosing itself is mechanical, a standard applied with discipline, not a flash of inspiration available only to the gifted. The dream, class, the symbols, the narration: these territories are open to every writer, and the standard, arguable and evidence-rich, can be learned by anyone willing to test a candidate topic against the text and revise it until it passes.

The cleverness market deserves a direct answer, because it is the main rival to the verdict. The market says: distinguish yourself with an unusual topic, something no other candidate will write about, and the examiner will reward the originality. The verdict says: the examiner rewards the argument, and the unusual topic is usually unusual because it is thin. The market's advice fails in the predictable way. The writer chooses the exotic topic, discovers mid-draft that the evidence will not sustain it, and either pads or drifts, while the writer who chose the familiar class topic with a sharp dispute writes an essay that argues on every page. Originality is not worthless; an unusual topic that passes the two-part test is a genuine advantage, because the examiner reads it with fresh attention. But the test comes first and the novelty second, and any choosing strategy that reverses that order is gambling the essay on a property the mark scheme does not measure.

The honest complication remains, and the verdict does not dissolve it. Topics will still tempt toward broadness and narrowness, and the topic will still disguise itself as a thesis. The defense against those temptations is not a better list of topics but a better habit of choosing, the habit of asking, of every candidate, whether a thoughtful reader could disagree with the claim it invites and whether the novel supplies the evidence to defend it. A writer with that habit can take the most familiar subject in Gatsby studies and produce an essay that argues. A writer without it can take the most original subject and produce an essay that describes. The topic is the essay's first decision, and like all first decisions, it rewards the writer who makes it deliberately.

There is a final reason to take choosing seriously, and it concerns the writer rather than the essay. The habit of choosing well, specifying the ground, locating the dispute, testing the evidence, is not specific to Gatsby. It is the habit of argument itself, and it transfers to every essay the writer will ever write, on any text, in any subject. The Gatsby essay is simply an unusually good place to learn it, because the novel's disputed territories make the disputes visible and its concrete scenes make the evidence checkable. Learn the habit here, on the dream and the class and the symbols and the telling, and it stays learned. That is the deeper reward the topic offers, beyond the mark on any single essay: a way of choosing that works everywhere choosing matters.

## The Topic During Drafting and Revision

Choosing well does not end when drafting begins; the topic keeps governing the essay, and the writer who consults it during drafting writes a tighter essay than the writer who files it away. The mechanism is the paragraph check described earlier: every paragraph is measured against the claim, and the measurement has three possible outcomes.

The first outcome is advance: the paragraph supports the claim with new evidence, and it stays. The second is object: the paragraph raises the counter-reading fairly and answers it, and it stays, because answering objections is part of arguing. The third is drift: the paragraph reports material that is interesting and true but does not serve the claim, and it goes, however painful the cut. Most weak essays are not weak in their sentences; they are weak in their third-outcome paragraphs, the interesting digressions the writer could not bear to delete. The topic, consulted honestly, identifies those paragraphs, because the topic is the standard the paragraphs were supposed to serve. Revision, seen this way, is not polishing but re-choosing: confirming that every paragraph still belongs to the topic that was chosen, and cutting what does not.

The topic also governs the conclusion. A conclusion that restates the topic, "this essay has explored the American Dream in The Great Gatsby," confesses that the essay never left the territory. A conclusion that restates the thesis and its defense, "the dream ennobles the hoping and corrupts the wanted, and the evidence of the pursuit, the reunion, and the closing pages bears this out," shows an essay that argued. Writers who chose well find the second kind of conclusion easy to write, because the thesis was arguable from the start and the paragraphs defended it; writers who chose poorly find it impossible, because there is no thesis to restate, only the territory. The conclusion is therefore a final diagnostic of the topic: if it cannot be written as the defense of a claim, the choosing failed, and the failure is visible on the last page as surely as on the first.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What Great Gatsby essay topics score well?

The topics that score well are the ones that let the writer argue rather than describe. In practice that means topics drawn from the novel's most disputed and best-evidenced territories: the American Dream, class and social standing, the symbol system, and the narration. A topic on whether the novel treats Gatsby's dream as noble or corrupt scores well because both readings have textual support and the writer must adjudicate between them. A topic on how the novel distributes moral judgment between old money and new money scores well for the same reason. What these topics share is not their subject matter but their structure: each admits a genuine dispute and supplies the passages to conduct it. Examiners reward the thinking the dispute forces, so the scoring follows the topic's productivity, not its novelty.

### Q: Which topic areas are strongest?

The four strongest areas are the American Dream, class, the symbols, and the narration, and each is strong for a different reason. The dream is strong because the novel stages an unresolved dispute about what the dream promises and costs, giving the writer a live argument with evidence on both sides. Class is strong because the social geography, East Egg against West Egg, the Buchanans against the Wilsons, is drawn in concrete scenes the writer can quote. The symbols are strong because they recur with variation, and variation is arguable. The narration is strong because Nick Carraway mediates everything, so claims about the telling are always available. The American identity threads through all four as a connective. A topic that touches at least one of these areas starts with depth; a topic that touches none of them starts with a handicap.

### Q: What makes a topic productive?

A productive topic is arguable and evidence-rich, and both qualities must be present together. Arguable means the claim the topic invites is one a thoughtful reader could dispute with textual support; if no reasonable reader could disagree, the essay has nothing to prove. Evidence-rich means the novel supplies enough concrete material, passages, images, scenes, to sustain a full argument without padding or repetition. The conjunction matters because each quality guards against the other's characteristic failure. Arguability without evidence produces the bold claim that collapses for lack of support. Evidence without arguability produces the accurate summary that never says anything disputable. Before committing to a topic, the writer should test both: state the dispute it admits, and list four or five pieces of textual evidence it can use. A yes to both is the whole test.

### Q: How do you adapt a topic into a thesis?

The adaptation follows three moves: specify, locate the dispute, and state a position with a mechanism. Start with the topic in its broad form, for example the green light, and specify it to something arguable, the light's changing function from the first chapter to the closing pages. Then locate the dispute the specified topic admits: does the light's meaning deepen or empty out as Gatsby approaches it? Then state the position as a sentence a reader could disagree with, naming the mechanism: the light loses its particular meaning and gains a universal one, and Nick's closing meditation transforms rather than restates it. The converted sentence is now a thesis because it is arguable, specific, and defensible. The method works on any territory, and it is mechanical enough to practice: specify the ground, find the disagreement, commit to the claim.

### Q: What topics are too broad or too narrow?

A topic is too broad when it attempts more than the essay can argue, and too narrow when it supplies less than the essay needs. "The American Dream in The Great Gatsby" is too broad: it names the whole novel's territory, admits no single dispute, and the essay becomes a survey. "Wealth and morality" is too broad for the same reason. At the other end, "the significance of one minor detail mentioned once" is too narrow: the insight may be genuine, but one insight does not fill an essay, and the draft will pad or drift. The tests are practical. For broadness: can you state in one sentence what the essay will argue that a reader could dispute? For narrowness: can you name four or five distinct pieces of textual evidence before drafting? The cures are narrowing by character, scene, or mechanism, and widening by connection to the larger pattern the detail instances.

### Q: How does choosing a topic set up the essay?

Choosing sets up the essay because the topic determines the thesis, the thesis determines the paragraph plan, and the plan determines the essay, in a chain no later stage can reverse. A topic that admits a dispute converts naturally into an arguable thesis, and an arguable thesis gives every paragraph a job: advance the claim or answer an objection. A topic that is really a subject, however, converts into no thesis at all, and the paragraphs, having no principle of selection, can only wander through the territory reporting what they find. This is why examiners form their judgment so early: the first page reveals whether the writer chose a dispute or a subject, and the rest of the essay follows from that choice with near-mechanical reliability. Choosing well is therefore the highest-leverage decision in the process, made before drafting, and worth more revision time than any later polish.

### Q: Which topics let a first-time Gatsby writer argue rather than summarize?

First-time writers argue best with topics anchored in concrete, quotable material, because concreteness protects against the drift into summary that inexperience invites. Class topics are the safest starting ground: Tom Buchanan's violence, the Plaza Hotel confrontation, the contrast between the Buchanan house and the Wilson garage, all supply scenes the writer can point to and dispute about. Symbol topics tracked across appearances are nearly as safe: the green light's first and last appearances give the essay a built-in structure of comparison. Narration topics suit writers comfortable staying close to sentences, working from what Nick actually says. The dream territory, though the richest, is the riskiest for beginners, because its abstraction tempts vague handling; a first-timer who chooses the dream should narrow it to a single mechanism, such as how the valley of ashes prices the dream, before drafting.

### Q: How do you narrow a broad topic without making it trivial?

Narrow by character, by scene, or by mechanism, and check that the narrowed version still admits a dispute. "The American Dream" narrowed by character becomes "Gatsby's dream of Daisy as the novel tests it," which is arguable because the novel can be read as ennobling or corrupting that dream. Narrowed by scene it becomes "the Plaza Hotel confrontation as the novel's judgment on its wealthy," arguable because the scene supports rival readings of who is condemned. Narrowed by mechanism it becomes "how Daisy's voice is used to make carelessness look like innocence," arguable because the charm can be read as genuine or as alibi. The triviality danger appears when the narrowing removes the dispute, leaving only a fact: "the green light appears twice" is narrow but dead. The rule: every cut must preserve something a thoughtful reader could disagree about, or the precision was wasted.

### Q: How do you widen a topic that is too narrow?

Widen by finding the larger pattern of which the small detail is an instance, and argue about the pattern through the detail. A topic on the yellow car alone runs out of material, but the car instances the novel's treatment of wealth as an instrument of harm, and that treatment is arguable and evidence-rich: who drives, who is harmed, who pays. A topic on the owl-eyed man alone is thin, but he instances the novel's theme of spectatorship, who watches, who judges, who merely observes, and that theme runs through the parties, the narration, and the funeral. The widening must keep the original detail as the essay's anchor, because the detail is what keeps the widened topic concrete; the pattern supplies the argumentative room, and the detail supplies the evidence. What widening must not do is abandon the detail for the abstraction, because then the essay loses the very precision that made the narrow topic attractive.

### Q: Can one topic serve several different essay prompts?

Yes, and the best-prepared topics do, because a productive topic is flexible by construction. A topic on how the novel distributes moral judgment between old and new money can answer prompts about class, about character, about the American Dream, and about the ending, since each prompt is really asking the writer to take a position the topic already contains. The flexibility comes from the topic's dispute-structure: any prompt that touches the territory can be met by restating the dispute in the prompt's terms. The limit is that the adaptation must be genuine. A writer who forces an unrelated prompt onto a prepared topic, answering the prepared essay instead of the asked question, fails the prompt no matter how good the topic. The discipline is to re-narrow the topic for each prompt, specifying which version of the dispute this question asks about, so the preparation serves the answer rather than replacing it.

### Q: How many topics should you prepare before an exam?

Two or three, drawn from different territories, each tested against the text in advance. One topic is fragile: if the exam prompt does not touch its territory, the writer improvises under pressure, which is when the worst choosing happens. Two or three from different areas, for example one on class, one on the symbols, one on the narration, cover the prompt space well, because most Gatsby prompts fall into or near one of the four productive territories. More than three spreads preparation thin; the writer knows a little about many topics and cannot argue any of them deeply, and depth is what the mark scheme rewards. Each prepared topic should be carried not as a thesis but as a narrowed, evidence-checked topic with its dispute identified, so the exam itself requires only the final narrowing to fit the prompt. Preparation of topics, not of finished essays, is the efficient use of revision time.

### Q: Which topics hold up best under timed exam conditions?

The topics that hold up under time pressure are the ones the writer knows most deeply, because depth compensates for the imprecision that haste introduces. Structurally, class topics and symbol topics are the sturdiest: their evidence is concrete and locatable, scenes and images the writer can summon without the text open, and their disputes are clean enough to state in a sentence. Narration topics hold up for writers who have internalized Nick's key passages, but they punish the writer who must reconstruct the argument from scratch. Dream topics are the least sturdy under pressure unless narrowed in advance, because the abstraction invites the vague handling that time pressure encourages. The general rule for timed conditions: prefer the topic whose evidence you can list from memory and whose dispute you can state in one sentence, and distrust the topic that looked brilliant during unhurried preparation but cannot be summarized quickly.

### Q: What makes a class topic arguable rather than a summary of rich and poor?

A class topic becomes arguable when it asks what the novel thinks about its social divisions rather than merely describing them. "The difference between old money and new money" invites summary: the Buchanans inherited wealth, Gatsby did not, and the essay reports these facts accurately and pointlessly. "Whether the novel reserves its harshest judgment for old money or distributes it evenly" invites argument: one side cites Tom's brutality and Daisy's carelessness as evidence that inherited wealth is the deeper rot, the other cites Gatsby's bootlegging and criminal associations as evidence that the novel condemns both orders. The mechanism that makes the topic arguable is the moral question underneath the social description, who the novel blames and why. Class topics stay arguable as long as they keep asking that question; they slide into summary the moment they start cataloguing wealth instead of judging it.

### Q: How do you choose between two topics that both seem good?

Apply the two-part test comparatively and choose the topic with the clearer dispute and the more checkable evidence. State each candidate's dispute in one sentence: the topic whose dispute is sharper, more interesting, and more genuinely two-sided wins on arguability. Then list each candidate's evidence: the topic with four or five distinct, locatable passages wins on evidence-richness. If one topic wins both comparisons, the choice is made. If they split, prefer the one whose evidence you know better, because defensibility under examination conditions depends on command of the material. A further tiebreaker: prefer the topic that connects to more than one territory, since a class topic that touches the symbols, or a narration topic that touches the dream, gives the essay more room to develop. What you should not do is choose by novelty or by affection; the mark scheme rewards the quality of the argument, and the test measures that quality directly.

### Q: When should you combine two topic areas, and when should you not?

Combine when the combination creates a sharper dispute than either area alone, and not when it merely doubles the territory. "How Nick's narration shapes the novel's treatment of class" is a productive combination: the narration question, does Nick's telling clarify or distort, meets the class question, who does the novel judge, and the intersection is arguable in ways neither question is alone. "The American Dream and the symbols" is usually an unproductive combination: it joins two large territories without specifying the point of contact, and the essay becomes two half-essays stapled together. The test for a combination is whether you can state its dispute in one sentence that genuinely needs both areas; if the sentence can be stated with one area alone, the second is decoration. Combinations also cost evidence: the essay must now supply material from both territories, so only combine when both sides of the pairing are well-evidenced.

### Q: How do you test a topic against the text before committing to it?

Run the evidence-list check: narrow the topic by character, scene, or mechanism, then list four or five distinct passages, images, or scenes the essay could use, each tied to a location in the novel. If the list can be made with genuinely different items, not one insight repeated, the topic is evidence-rich enough to sustain the essay. Then state the dispute in one sentence and check that a thoughtful reader could take the other side with textual support; if so, the topic is arguable. If either check fails, revise before drafting: narrow a failing-broad topic, widen a failing-narrow one by connection, or reframe until the dispute appears. The check takes minutes and saves the hours a bad topic costs mid-draft. An annotated text is the ideal instrument for it, since tracing a symbol's appearances or a character's scenes across the novel is exactly what the check requires.

### Q: Which topics let you write about narration without retelling the plot?

Narration topics stay analytical, rather than collapsing into plot summary, when they focus on how Nick tells rather than what happens. "How Nick's retrospective framing shapes the reader's judgment of Gatsby" works from Nick's sentences, his opening disclaimers, his famous judgments, his arrangement of scenes, and never needs to recount events for their own sake. "What Nick gains and loses by telling the story after the fact" works from the structure, the omissions, the hindsight, the self-exculpations. The topics that fail are the ones that treat Nick as a character in the story rather than as its teller: "Nick's relationship with Jordan" retells the plot because it follows events, while "how Nick presents his relationship with Jordan" analyzes the telling because it follows sentences. The discipline is to keep every paragraph anchored in what Nick says and how he says it, quoting his language, so the essay argues about narration instead of narrating.

### Q: Why is "easy" a bad reason to choose a topic?

Because ease and productivity are different properties, and the easy topic is usually easy for the wrong reason. A topic feels easy when it asks for no dispute, "the green light symbolizes hope," and the absence of dispute is exactly what makes it unproductive: the essay will describe accurately, argue nothing, and earn the middling mark that accurate description earns. The topics that score well feel harder at the choosing stage, because they require the writer to find the dispute and take a position, but they are easier at the drafting stage, because the dispute supplies the essay's structure, its paragraphs, and its energy. Choosing by ease optimizes the wrong stage. It buys comfort in the first ten minutes and pays for it in every paragraph after. The writers who choose well accept a harder choosing stage, specifying, testing, converting, in exchange for a draft that almost writes itself, because the argument was built before the writing began.

### Q: What do you do when your chosen topic runs out of evidence mid-draft?

Stop and widen by connection rather than padding or drifting. First diagnose: list the evidence used so far and confirm the shortage is real, not just a failure to look, since writers under pressure often forget material they know. If the shortage is real, find the larger pattern the topic instances and argue the pattern through the original detail: the narrow symbol topic becomes a claim about the symbol system, the narrow scene topic becomes a claim about the novel's method in that kind of scene. Keep the original detail as the essay's anchor so the widening does not become a new, unprepared topic. What you must not do is pad, repeating the insight in new words, because examiners recognize padding instantly, or drift silently into a different topic, because then the introduction no longer matches the essay. A mid-draft widening, done deliberately and signaled in the prose, reads as development; padding reads as emptiness.

### Q: Is it better to pick a familiar topic or an unusual one?

Pick the familiar topic argued well over the unusual topic argued thinly, because the mark scheme measures the quality of the thinking, not the novelty of the subject. The most familiar territories, the dream, class, the symbols, the narration, are familiar for a reason: they are the most arguable and the most evidence-rich ground in the novel, which is why generations of strong essays have been written on them. An unusual topic can distinguish an essay, but only when it passes the same two-part test, and unusual topics fail the evidence test more often, since the novel's deep wells are deep precisely because they have been worked. The real originality in a Gatsby essay comes from handling, the precision of the dispute, the aptness of the evidence, the fairness to the counter-reading, not from the exoticism of the choice. Choose the topic you can argue best, familiar or not, and let the argument be the distinction.
