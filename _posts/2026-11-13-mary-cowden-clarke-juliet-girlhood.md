In 1851 Mary Cowden Clarke published a story that Romeo and Juliet never asked for and has never quite escaped: a hundred-page childhood for Juliet Capulet, written two and a half centuries after the play. The tale, "Juliet; The White Dove of Verona," forms the tenth of the fifteen narratives in The Girlhood of Shakespeare's Heroines, and it does something no editor, actor, or critic of the play had attempted at that scale. It takes a heroine whom Shakespeare introduces at the edge of fourteen, already inside a marriage negotiation, and it walks backwards into the nursery, the schoolroom years, and the slow cooling of the Capulet household, inventing the formation that the tragedy withholds. The result is the most sustained piece of creative criticism the play received in the nineteenth century, and the most revealing failure of sympathy and scholarship to stay in their separate lanes.

The book's strangeness deserves a moment's stare, because familiarity has dulled it. A canonical tragedy, complete in itself, famous for three centuries, is supplied with a prose childhood for its heroine by a private scholar with no university, no chair, and no precedent for the undertaking beyond a preface of her own composition. The undertaking succeeds commercially for two generations, is shelved beside the concordance that made its author's name, is condensed by her sister, reprinted on two continents, exhibited by the Folger Shakespeare Library as an instrument of Victorian girlhood, and is then, by the middle of the twentieth century, mislaid by the very profession that inherited its questions. Every stage of that career is a datum. The success says something about the nineteenth century's hunger for Shakespeare's women as persons. The mislaying says something about the twentieth century's embarrassment at the forms female scholarship had been obliged to take. The tale's recovery by Gross, Marshall, Brown, and the Folger's curators says something about the questions criticism asks when it goes looking for its own excluded ancestors. This article keeps all three datums in play, because the Girlhood is never only about Juliet. It is about who gets to explain a heroine, in what genre, and at what price in rigour and in reach.

The verdict of this article is that the invention deserves to be read as criticism, with strict limits attached. Cowden Clarke was not a daydreaming amateur. She was the compiler of the first complete concordance to Shakespeare, a woman who had spent sixteen years indexing every word of the plays before she wrote a line of the Girlhood, and her Juliet tale is anchored in the play's own evidence about the Capulet household with a precision that later, respectable criticism of the play has repeatedly confirmed. Where the tale fails, it fails in one locatable place: it imports a Victorian ideal of girlhood innocence into a play whose heroine is defined by the speed and candour of her desire, and it protects the child from the very knowledge that makes the woman. Read with those limits marked, the tale remains the sharpest nineteenth-century account of how Juliet was made, and a permanent lesson in where backstory ends and evidence begins.

## Who Was Mary Cowden Clarke?

Mary Victoria Novello was born in London on 22 June 1809, the eldest daughter of the music publisher and composer Vincent Novello, and she grew up inside one of the most literary households in England. Charles and Mary Lamb were intimates of the family; Mary Lamb gave the girl lessons in Latin and in the reading of poetry, and Leigh Hunt and John Keats moved through the same rooms. The Lambs' Tales from Shakespeare, the prose retellings of 1807, formed part of her childhood furniture, and the line from those retellings to her life's work is direct: the child who was given Shakespeare as story became the woman who rebuilt Shakespeare as an index, an edition, and finally a set of invented biographies. In July 1828, at nineteen, she married Charles Cowden Clarke, her brother Alfred's business partner, a lecturer on literature more than twenty years her senior, and the marriage became one of the great working partnerships of Victorian Shakespeare studies.

The partnership's first fruit was hers alone, and it was enormous. In 1829, the year after her marriage, she began compiling a complete verbal index to the dramatic works, a concordance that would register every passage in which every word occurs. Twelve years went to the compiling and four more to the press. Issued in eighteen monthly parts across 1844 and 1845 and published in volume form in 1845 as The Complete Concordance to Shakespeare, the work displaced the partial indexes of Samuel Ayscough and Francis Twiss and gave the century its first full map of the text. She went on to publish Shakespeare Proverbs in 1848, to edit the plays in an edition of 1860, to produce with her husband the illustrated Cassell edition completed in 1868 and The Shakespeare Key in 1879, and to write the memoir My Long Life near the end of a life that closed at Genoa on 12 January 1898. The Girlhood sits in the middle of this record, and its position matters: the tales were written not before the scholarship but after it, by the one reader in England who could have found, from memory, every line Shakespeare ever wrote about childhood.

### What did the Complete Concordance do that no earlier Shakespeare index had done?

Earlier indexes, Ayscough's of 1790 and Twiss's of 1805 to 1807, were selective, gathering striking passages under chosen words. Cowden Clarke's concordance was exhaustive in principle: it registered every occurrence of every word across the whole dramatic canon, so that any claim about Shakespeare's language could be tested against the complete evidence.

The older books were anthologies wearing the dress of reference. Hers was a census.

The consequence for the Girlhood is easy to miss and hard to overstate. A critic who invents a childhood for Juliet after sixteen years of indexing stands in a different relation to her invention from a novelist who invents one from affection. Cowden Clarke knew exactly how little the play says about its heroine's first thirteen years, because she had handled, in alphabetical captivity, every word of what it does say. She knew the Nurse's single anecdote in Act 1 Scene 3 was the whole of the play's memory, and she knew the weight of every "child," "girl," "maid," and "lamb" in the canon. The tales are therefore best understood as the overflow of a total knowledge of the text into the one territory the text refuses to enter. Her authority as inventor is parasitic on her authority as indexer, and Victorian readers understood the bargain: the title pages of the Girlhood advertise her as the author of the Complete Concordance, selling the fiction on the credit of the reference book.

The labour behind that credit is worth one paragraph of respect, because it disciplines everything the tales attempt. The concordance was compiled by hand, word by word and play by play, across twelve years that began when its author was twenty, and it was then seen through the press across four more, appearing in eighteen monthly parts before the bound volume of 1845 closed the undertaking. There was no staff, no grant, no institution: a young married woman at a desk, building the instrument that scholars at the universities would use for the rest of the century. The monthly-part format matters to the Girlhood's prehistory as well, for Cowden Clarke learned from the concordance's serialization how a large Shakespeare project could be sold to households in slices, and the Girlhood's own appearance in one-shilling parts repeats the lesson with a scholar's deliberateness. She knew her market because she had already supplied it, at the highest level of rigour the market contained. When the tales are patronized as a lady's pastime, the chronology is the rebuttal: the pastime was issued, part by part, by the author of the century's standard verbal index, in the same commercial form, to overlapping subscribers.

That credit also explains a peculiarity of the attribution, which the record keeps slightly untidy and which deserves untangling. The Girlhood is Mary Cowden Clarke's work alone, conceived and written by her, and published under the married name that the concordance had made famous. The joint productions with Charles Cowden Clarke are separate items on the shelf: the Cassell edition of the plays, The Shakespeare Key, the Recollections of Writers. Because husband and wife shared a surname, a profession, and eventually an edition, later cataloguers have sometimes smeared the credit for her solo works across the partnership, and the Girlhood has occasionally been treated as a household product rather than a single woman's book. It was not. The preface speaks in her voice, the design is hers, and the sixteen years of verbal indexing that underwrite the tales were hers. When the work was reissued in a condensed edition by Bickers and Son in 1890, the editor was her sister Sabilla Novello, which kept the book, fittingly, inside the family's female line of Shakespeare labour.

The life around the books deserves its paragraph, because the Girlhood's confidence came from a formation as literary as any she invented. The Novello salon of her childhood put the girl at the table with the Lambs, Hunt, and Keats; her governess months and her early magazine pieces under the initials M. H. taught her the periodical trade from inside; and in 1847 and 1848 she acted in the amateur theatricals that Charles Dickens organized to endow the Shakespeare birthplace curatorship, playing Dame Quickly at the Haymarket in May 1848 and touring with the company to Liverpool, Birmingham, Edinburgh, and Glasgow. The woman who would write Juliet's childhood had played Shakespeare's women, in costume, before paying audiences, in a company run by the century's greatest novelist, and the experience is written into the tales' theatrical instinct for the scene that forms a character. Dickens appears again in the dedications of the Girlhood, alongside Macready, the tragedian who defined the era's stage Shakespeare, which places the book precisely: not a schoolroom spin-off but a production of the innermost Shakespeare circle of the day, by its most industrious scholar. The Clarkes left England for Italy in 1856 and settled eventually at Genoa, in the house they named Villa Novello, and the distance changed nothing in the output. From Italy came her edition of the plays in 1860, the first complete edition of Shakespeare prepared by a woman, then the Cassell edition with her husband, The Shakespeare Key, the Recollections of Writers with its letters from Keats, Hunt, Dickens, and the Lambs, and at eighty-seven the autobiography My Long Life. The Girlhood, read against this shelf, is not an interlude between serious works. It is the centre of the career, the place where the indexer, the editor, the actress, and the novelist in her all worked at once.

## What Is The Girlhood of Shakespeare's Heroines?

The Girlhood of Shakespeare's Heroines appeared between 1850 and 1852 from W. H. Smith and Son in London, in three volumes collecting fifteen tales, each with its own title page and its own dedication. The tales were also sold periodically in one-shilling parts, each part containing a complete story, which put them within reach of the schoolroom and the railway stall as well as the subscription library. An American edition from G. P. Putnam in New York in 1852 dressed the second series in cloth stamped as a Shakespeare gift book, and the physical history of the work is part of its meaning: this was Shakespeare packaged for presentation, a book designed to be given to a girl by someone who wished her reading well. The dedications tell the same story of literary belonging, running to figures such as William Charles Macready, Charles Dickens, Douglas Jerrold, Leigh Hunt, and John Payne Collier. Reprints followed across the century, from Bickers and Son and Putnam in the late 1870s to the condensed Novello edition of 1890 and a Dent edition of 1907, and Cambridge University Press reissued the three volumes in 2009.

The fifteen heroines are arranged as a gallery of formations. The first series carries Portia, the heiress of Belmont; the Thane's daughter, Lady Macbeth; Helena, the physician's orphan; Desdemona, the Magnifico's child; and Meg and Alice, the merry maids of Windsor. The second series, the volume that concerns this article, carries Isabella the votaress; Katharina and Bianca, the shrew and the demure; Ophelia, the rose of Elsinore; Rosalind and Celia, the friends; and Juliet, the white dove of Verona. The third series completes the gallery with Beatrice and Hero, Olivia, Hermione, Viola, and Imogen. Each title does quiet critical work. A heroine is named by the condition of her girlhood rather than by the catastrophe of her maturity: not the suicide of Verona but the white dove, not the madwoman of Elsinore but the rose. The naming announces the method, which is to read the woman backwards into the girl and to treat the play's ending as the last term of a development rather than as a fate dropped from the sky.

The preface states the design in terms that later critics have quoted whenever the book is discussed, and they bear paraphrasing closely. The aim, Cowden Clarke declares, is to trace the probable antecedents in the histories of Shakespeare's women: to imagine the circumstances, scenes, events, and companions of their infant lives that might have fostered the germs of the character visible in their maturity. The tales invent such adventures as might be supposed to colour the heroines' future lives, placing each girl in situations that lead naturally up to, and account for, the known conclusion of her story. Two words in that programme deserve emphasis, because the whole argument of this article turns on them. The first is "probable": the tales claim not fancy but likelihood, the standing of a hypothesis. The second is "germs": character, in this theory, is botanical. It grows from seeds planted in childhood, and the critic's task resembles the gardener's, tracing the flower back to the soil. When the method works, the tale reads like an explanation. When it fails, it fails as gardening fails, by forcing a plant to prove the soil it was potted in.


The gallery principle repays a closer look, because it governs the Juliet tale's choices from outside. A series of fifteen formations is not fifteen separate commissions; it is one argument repeated under fifteen sets of conditions, and the argument is that Shakespeare's women are made, not born, in circumstances a reader can reconstruct. The titles enforce the comparison. Portia is tried against wealth, an heiress raised among caskets and suitors in a dead father's house. Lady Macbeth, the Thane's daughter, is tried against ambition absorbed at a father's court. Ophelia, the rose of Elsinore, is tried against a court that polices her reading and her company. Juliet is tried against the feud and the formal nursery, and her tale's distinction within the series is that her conditions are the most purely domestic of the fifteen: no court intrigue, no supernatural visitation, no disguise plot, nothing between the girl and her formation except parents, a servant, a priest, and a city's hatred. That purity is why the tale became the volume's test case. If the method can explain Juliet from a nursery, it can explain anyone; if it cannot, the failure will be visible without distractions. The preface frames the whole gallery with a borrowed image of green immaturity, the heroines shown in their sallet days, and the borrowing is itself a piece of method: the woman who indexed the canon reached for its own language to name the state before character sets. The Girlhood, that is, presents itself from its first pages as a work inside Shakespeare's words, not outside them, and every later judge of the book, friendly or hostile, has had to reckon with that self-placement.

## What Girlhood Does Cowden Clarke Invent for Juliet?

The Juliet tale is the longest sustained act of invention in the volume, running to roughly a hundred pages in modern reprint, longer, as Julia Reinhard Lupton's Arden Critical Reader observes, than the play it precedes. Its title is taken from the play's own first sight of her. When Romeo sees Juliet across the Capulet hall in Act 1 Scene 5, he reaches for the image of a snowy dove trooping with crows, beauty too rich for use and for earth too dear, and Cowden Clarke lifts the dove out of the ballroom and installs it over the whole childhood. The choice is a thesis in miniature. The tale will present a creature defined by whiteness, gentleness, and apartness, a bird among darker birds, and it will ask the reader to watch the Verona of the feud close around a nature that the feud's categories cannot describe. Everything the tale invents is arranged to make that one image biographical.

The narrative begins where the play cannot: in a Capulet nursery where the heir is loved. The tale's boldest structural decision, and the one later scholarship has fastened on, is its handling of the parents. In the opening movement of the tale the bond between the child and her father and mother is warm and present; the girl is petted, watched, and prized in a great house that has buried its other children and keeps its hopes in her. Then, across the years of the story, the warmth is withdrawn by degrees into ceremony. Affection formalizes. The parents retreat behind the etiquette of a Veronese palace, and the grown girl's contact with them dwindles, in the tale's own summary of the arrangement, to the wonted periodical salutes exchanged between Italian parent and child: the kiss at set hours, the blessing at set seasons, the interview by appointment. The tale thus invents for Juliet an emotional biography in two acts, intimacy lost to protocol, and it lodges the explanation of the play's most startling fact inside that loss. A daughter who has been ceremonially starved of her parents will not take her sorrows to them. She will take them where the tale sends her: downstairs, to the servant who raised her.

That servant is the tale's true second protagonist. The Nurse of the play, bawdy, garrulous, and treacherous at the crisis, is rebuilt by Cowden Clarke as the child's lifelong companion and effective mother, the warm presence against which the parents' formality is measured. As the periodical salutes replace parental intimacy, the girl spends her days in the Nurse's orbit, and the tale treats the substitution as the central fact of the formation: Juliet is mothered by a hireling because her mother has become a ceremony. Lady Capulet, in turn, is given the shaping role that Irene Montori's reading of the tale identifies as primary in the damaged upbringing: a mother whose love curdles into form, whose daughter learns early that maternal attention arrives dressed as etiquette and departs on schedule. The tale does not make Lady Capulet a monster. It makes her something colder and more useful to the argument, a woman performing parenthood in the style of her class while the actual work of love is done by a servant with a dead daughter of her own. Readers of the play will recognize the target. In Act 1 Scene 3 Lady Capulet must ask the Nurse how old her own daughter is, and she delivers the marriage proposal like an ambassador reading credentials. The tale simply extends that chill backwards across thirteen years and asks what it does to a child.

The remaining architecture is the feud, present in the tale not as brawls in the street but as the climate of the house. The child grows up inside the great enmity the way a child grows up inside a climate of weather: it governs where she may walk, whom she may greet, which names may be spoken at table, and it teaches her before any lover does that Verona's deepest law is a prohibition. Cowden Clarke is careful with the proportions here. The tale is not a chronicle of street fighting witnessed by a girl; it is the record of a household in which hatred is part of the furniture, older than the heir, tended by her father, spoken by her cousin Tybalt with a young man's pride in it. By the time the tale approaches its end, the girl it hands over to the play is fully assembled: devout, solitary, starved of parental confidence, practised in concealment by the mere structure of her home, mothered by a servant, and carrying a capacity for absolute attachment that has never yet been offered an object worthy of it. The Friar completes the household map. In the tale he stands as he will stand in the play, her friend and counsellor, the one adult male whose ear is safe, and the tale's placement of him in her childhood explains, as the play alone does not, why a fourteen-year-old with a catastrophe in her hands walks straight to his cell.

### How does the tale end, and where does it hand its Juliet back to the play?

The tale ends at the threshold of the tragedy, closing the childhood just as the marriage market opens. Its final movement gathers the formation into its consequences: formal parents, a confiding Nurse, a safe Friar, the house of the feud, and a girl taught that love, when it comes, must be hidden to be kept.

The handover is the tale's neatest piece of criticism, because nothing in it needs to be altered for the play to begin. That ending exposes the design's elegance and its danger in a single stroke. A backstory that dovetails perfectly with its text flatters the reader's sense of inevitability, and inevitability is the one thing the play's Juliet keeps refusing. The girl of the tale is a mechanism wound to produce the woman; the woman of the play is a person who surprises every other character in Verona, her parents, her Nurse, her Friar, and her husband, in that order. The tale can explain why she hides her marriage. It cannot explain why she is quicker than everyone around her, and the distance between those two explanations is the distance this article will keep measuring.

A word is owed to the tale's handling of its heroine's inner furnishing, since the summary table can only gesture at it. The play gives its Juliet two possessions that no other character in Verona shares: a private language of reckoning, in which she audits her own feelings in soliloquy with an accountant's precision, and a habit of prayer that is practical rather than decorative, a technology for getting through the next hour. Cowden Clarke's formation has to manufacture both, and she manufactures them from the nursery's two poles. The reckoning comes from solitude: a girl whose parents greet her by appointment learns to conduct her real conversations inwardly, and the tale lingers over the habit of a child who thinks in private because the house offers no one to think aloud with except a servant. The prayer comes from the house's religion, the one sanctioned channel through which a Capulet daughter might address a power higher than her father, and the tale treats her devotion not as ornament but as training: the girl who has practised speaking to heaven in secret will not be silenced by a hall full of angry men. Both furnishings are inventions, and both are charged to the text. The soliloquies exist; the orisons exist; what the tale adds is only the connective childhood, the years in which such habits could have been laid down. This is the method at its most nearly respectable, and it is worth distinguishing from the dove. The dove is an essence imposed on the girl. The habits are causes proposed for documented effects, and a reader can refuse them without refusing the effects, which is exactly the freedom a piece of criticism ought to leave its audience.

The tale's handling of time is its quietest and most consequential invention, and it deserves separate notice. Shakespeare's tragedy is the most compressed of his major works: nine months of source narrative folded into five days, a heroine who meets, marries, beds, and buries her husband inside a single week of stage time. Cowden Clarke's answer to that compression is expansion at the same ratio in the opposite direction. Where the play gives the woman five days, the tale gives the girl thirteen years, narrated at the pace of seasons rather than hours, and the contrast is itself a reading of the play. The tale proposes that the tragedy's famous haste is legible only against a slowness the audience never sees: that a girl formed across thirteen unhurried years, in a house where nothing was ever urgent except ceremony, would experience the play's five days as a detonation precisely because her whole prior life had been a held breath. Formation criticism rarely earns so elegant a structural point. The play's tempo, on this reading, is not a dramatist's convenience but the measure of a collision between two time-schemes, the nursery's long patience and the marriage market's sudden hurry, and the heroine is the place where the schemes strike. A reader who returns to Act 1 Scene 2 with the tale in mind hears Capulet's request for two more summers differently: the father is asking for the nursery's time, and the tragedy begins when Verona refuses to grant it.

## The Juliet Tale Among Its Sister Tales

A reader who knows only the Juliet tale knows the method in its purest but not its fullest form, and the gallery repays a survey at the level of frame and condition, which is where its comparisons are honest. The fifteen tales divide by the kind of world that does the forming. At one pole stand the court tales. Ophelia, the rose of Elsinore, is formed inside a palace that treats a daughter as an instrument of policy, watched by a father and a king; Rosalind and Celia grow up paired inside a usurper's court, their friendship itself a formation, the sane household improvised by two girls inside a corrupt one. At the other pole stand the household tales, of which Juliet's is the type: no court, no politics, only kinship, service, and money. Between the poles the paired tales develop the method's most elegant device. Katharina and Bianca are written as sisters formed by the same house into opposite women, the shrew and the prize, so that the formation argument can be run twice on identical conditions with contrary results; Viola and Olivia, Beatrice and Hero, repeat the pairing by blood or by household, character read as contrast. The device matters for Juliet because it exposes what her tale cannot do. She has no pair. Susan, the one girl who might have stood beside her in the nursery, is dead before the play begins and absent from any formation but the Nurse's memory, and the tale's Juliet is therefore the gallery's only heroine formed in true solitude, without sister, cousin, friend, or rival of her own sex and age. The solitude is Shakespeare's gift to the method, and the tale is honest enough to build on it: the dove is alone among the crows because the play left her alone.

The remaining conditions complete the map of what the method believes. Portia is formed by wealth and a dead father's posthumous government, the heiress of Belmont raised inside a will. Lady Macbeth is formed by ambition witnessed at close quarters, a thane's daughter reared in a household where advancement is the family religion, and her tale is the gallery's dark twin to Juliet's: both girls absorb a household creed in childhood, hatred in the one case and reaching ambition in the other, and both enact the creed in marriage at a speed that kills. Isabella is formed against the world rather than by it, the votaress whose girlhood bends steadily toward the convent the play finds her entering. Set the fifteen side by side and the book's anthropology comes into view. Character, for Cowden Clarke, is what a household, a court, a creed, or a fortune does to a girl before she is old enough to refuse it, and the plays are the records of the refusal or the failure to refuse. Juliet's distinction in that company is the narrowness of her world and the completeness of her refusal. No other heroine is given so little formation material, a feud, a ceremony, a servant, a priest, and none spends it with such completeness. The tale's hundred pages are the measure of how hard the method must work when the world it is given is a nursery and the outcome it must explain is a tomb.

The pattern the summary exposes is double. On its credited side, every invention answers a genuine silence or a genuine oddity in the play: the mother's distance, the Nurse's maternity, the Friar's unearned trust, the speed of the attachment. On its debited side, every invention converts one of the play's effects into a cause located in childhood, which is the characteristic move of the biographical imagination and the characteristic temptation of the prequel form. The table cannot settle whether the conversion is criticism or decoration. It can only make the conversion visible, row by row, which is the first thing a reader needs.

Two rows deserve prose emphasis because they carry the article's whole argument between them. The title-image row shows the method at its most exposed: a metaphor spoken by a lover at first sight, in the middle of a ballroom, under the pressure of desire, is promoted into the organizing fact of a thirteen-year biography. Nothing in the play licenses the promotion except the image's beauty, and the tale's wager is that beauty in Shakespeare is diagnostic, that the figures his characters reach for under pressure tell the truth about what they see. Romeo's dove does tell a truth about Juliet as Verona receives her, set apart, watched, gleaming in a crowd of darker birds, and the tale is at its most defensible when it reads the image as the city's truth rather than the girl's essence. The marriage-market row shows the opposite exposure. There the tale argues that emotional starvation explains the speed of the attachment, and the argument is not drawn from a metaphor but from the play's structure: a heroine processed toward a bridegroom she has never chosen, by parents who consult her feelings as a formality, in a house where her confidante is a servant. That row needs no imported psychology, only attention, and it is the row a modern reader is most likely to carry back into the play. The distance between the two rows, one resting on an image, the other on a structure, is the distance between the tale's decoration and its criticism, and the summary's purpose is to let a reader feel that distance without a guide's insistence.

The assumption column is the summary's conscience, and it is the column a hostile reader should attack first. An assumption, in this table's usage, is the premise an invention needs in order to work: that a lover's metaphor can be a biography, that an only child is first cherished and then managed, that hatred absorbed early becomes secrecy later. Some of the assumptions are the common property of all formation thinking and can be granted cheaply. Others are the tale's private wagers, and they are priced accordingly in the sections that follow. The discipline the table imposes is simply that no invention gets to travel without its premise displayed, which is more than the tale's first century of readers, friendly or hostile, consistently demanded of it.

## The Childhood the Play Itself Remembers

Before the invention can be judged, the evidence must be measured, and the measurement is startling. Shakespeare's play, which counts its heroine's age aloud three times in its first act and builds its tragedy on her youth, remembers almost nothing of her childhood. The whole of Juliet's first thirteen years survives in a single speech: the Nurse's great reminiscent ramble in Act 1 Scene 3, delivered while Lady Capulet waits to open the marriage negotiation. From that speech, and that speech nearly alone, posterity learns that Juliet was weaned at three when the Nurse laid wormwood on her breast and the child, tasting the bitterness, fell out with the dug; that an earthquake shook the dove-house eleven years before; that the toddler, running, fell forward and broke her brow, acquiring a bump the Nurse compares, laughing, to a young cockerel's stone; that the Nurse's husband, a merry man dead by the time of the telling, picked the child up and made the bawdy joke about falling backward in maturity that the Nurse has been repeating, delighted, ever since; and that her own daughter Susan, born at the same season, died in infancy. That is the archive. A weaning, a fall, a joke, a dead foster-sister, an earthquake. Everything else is the play's present tense.

### What does the play itself remember of Juliet's childhood?

Almost nothing, and what it remembers arrives as comedy in a servant's mouth. The Nurse's Act 1 Scene 3 anecdote is the complete record: a weaning embittered with wormwood, a toddler's fall that raised a bump on her brow, a husband's joke about her future, a foster-sister who died in infancy.

The poverty of that archive is the tale's charter, and Cowden Clarke, of all readers, knew its exact dimensions. Consider what the play does with the little it keeps. The childhood exists in the tragedy only as an anecdote told by a servant to entertain a mother who does not know her daughter's age, in the antechamber of a marriage market, minutes before the daughter enters to be told she must marry. Lady Capulet's great line of the scene, delivered in paraphrase here because its force lies in its content, is the confession that she was a mother at the age her daughter has reached: the woman's own girlhood is invoked not as memory but as precedent, a warrant for hurrying the child. Between the Nurse's fond, trivial, physical recollections and the mother's single actuarial fact, the play sketches an entire sociology of this girl's upbringing without narrating a day of it. The formation is present in the tragedy as pressure, never as story. Cowden Clarke's invention is, at its root, the conversion of that pressure into story, and its legitimacy depends on whether the pressure was really there. It was. A mother who needs her daughter's age from the servant, a childhood whose public record is a weaning joke, a father who in Act 1 Scene 2 calls his child a stranger in the world who has not seen the change of fourteen years and asks for two more summers before she is ripe to be a bride: these are not gaps in the play's knowledge. They are the play's knowledge, stated in its habitual shorthand. The tale's defence begins from the observation that Shakespeare distributes the evidence of the formation across a dozen lines and trusts the audience to feel its weight. Cowden Clarke merely refuses the shorthand.

Two further pieces of the play's evidence deserve naming, because the tale leans on both and both are load-bearing. The first is Capulet's disclosure in Act 1 Scene 2 that the earth has swallowed all his hopes but Juliet: the other children are dead, and the heir's singularity is a fact of the house before it is a sentiment. A girl who is the sole surviving vessel of a great family's hopes grows up under a particular weather, prized and watched in childhood, priced and hurried in adolescence, and the tale's two-act structure of cherishing followed by management is an extrapolation from exactly this line. The second is the conduct of Act 3 Scene 5, where the formation shows its results. When the marriage to Paris is forced, Juliet appeals in sequence to her mother, who answers that she is done with her, and to the Nurse, who advises bigamy. The play's catastrophe of counsel, the moment the girl is abandoned by both her mothers at once, lands with its full weight only if the audience understands that these two women between them constitute the whole of her upbringing. Shakespeare shows the abandonment. Cowden Clarke's tale is the long attempt to show what was abandoned.

The archive's smallness has one more feature that the tale exploits with real finesse: its chronology. The Nurse's anecdote dates the childhood by an earthquake, eleven years before the action, which places the weaning in a Verona shaken in body as the feud shakes it in spirit, and the play lets the detail pass as a servant's colour. The tale takes the earthquake's measure differently, as the founding atmosphere of a girlhood spent in a city where the ground itself is unreliable and the houses are armed. A concordance-maker notices such things for a living, and the tale's sensitivity to the play's buried datings is a signature of its author's training: the formation is anchored not only in the anecdote's events but in its calendar. Set beside this the play's other buried datum, the Lammas-tide birthday that makes the heroine's age computable to the week, and the shape of the evidence is complete. Shakespeare gives his Juliet a precisely dated body and an almost entirely unrecorded mind. The play knows when she was born, when she was weaned, when she fell, and nothing of what she thought about any of it. A critic of Cowden Clarke's formation, whose life's work was the proposition that a text is a system of findable relations, could hardly have left that asymmetry alone. The tale is her answer to it: the mind, supplied at the same standard of probability as the dates, by the only scholar who had handled every word of both.

One more item in the archive belongs here, because the tale leans on it harder than on any other: the father's grief. When Capulet finds his daughter apparently dead on her wedding morning, his lament reaches for the language of dispossessed fatherhood: death is his son-in-law, death is his heir, his daughter death hath wedded (4.5). The lines are routinely played as bombast, and the tale invites a colder reading. This is the grief of a man whose relation to his child has been entirely proprietary, formed in the grammar of heirs and settlements, and it arrives too late to be anything else: the father can mourn the marriage that death has stolen because marriage was the only transaction through which he had ever approached her. Read backwards through the tale's thirteen years, the lament is the periodical salutes with the formality stripped away by catastrophe, the ceremonial parent discovering, over a body, the daughter the ceremonies kept at a distance. The play does not state that reading; it licenses it, in exactly the tale's sense of licence, by placing the vocabulary of inheritance in the father's mouth at the moment of loss. A concordance author is trained to hear such placements. The Girlhood's coldest pages, the formal nursery and the appointment-book affection, are best understood as the prehistory this lament requires: prose written to make a father's most conventional speech sound, on reflection, like a confession. Where the tale confines itself to that work, supplying the prehistory that the play's own words demand, it is doing the thing its preface promised, probable antecedents for a known conclusion, and doing it better than the professional criticism of its century generally managed.

## Biography Written Backwards: The Prequel Method

The Girlhood belongs to a form that had no settled name in 1851 and has acquired several since: the prequel, the backstory, the expansion. Its procedure is the reverse of the sequel's. A sequel inherits characters and spends them forward into new events; a prequel inherits an ending and is forbidden to change it, so its whole inventive energy is driven backwards into causation. Every incident in the Juliet tale is chosen under the shadow of a known conclusion, and the preface's botanical language of germs and fostering is simply the honest name for that discipline: the end is the flower, and the tale is an essay on the soil. Sarah Annes Brown, in the Shakespeare Survey essay that gives the form its best modern account, calls the prequel a palinode, a poem that takes back what an earlier poem said, and argues that the successful prequel is an essentially subversive form, since it cannot merely repeat its original and must offer a new way into the old text. The classification is exact for the Girlhood. A palinode does not contradict its predecessor's facts; it contradicts its predecessor's emphases, and Cowden Clarke's tale contradicts Shakespeare's emphasis at the deepest level available to it. The play insists that Juliet's story begins at the marriage market and that her past is a joke in a servant's mouth. The tale insists, for a hundred pages, that the past is the story.

The method has a further, quieter dimension that the concordance throws into relief. Writing a childhood for a character requires deciding which of the adult's traits are seeds and which are accidents, and that decision is an act of interpretation indistinguishable from editing or staging. When Cowden Clarke decides that the germ to be fostered is Juliet's capacity for hidden, absolute attachment, and that the fostering conditions are parental coldness and a servant's warmth, she is making the same kind of claim that a critic makes who argues, in an essay, that the tragedy is driven by the failures of the Capulet household. The difference is one of costume. The essay argues in propositions and citations; the tale argues in scenes and years. Brown's palinode framing helps here as well, because a palinode is still a poem: it answers art with art, and its evidence is the answering work's coherence with the original. By that standard the tale's method is neither naive nor lawless. It has a rule, stated in its own preface: invent only what leads naturally up to the known conclusion. The rule is also the method's built-in corruption, since a childhood engineered to account for an ending will always find the ending's causes, whether or not the character, let alone the play, ever contained them.

There is a biographical symmetry too neat to omit. The woman who spent sixteen years building an index that runs forward from every word to all its occurrences then spent three years building tales that run backward from every heroine to all her origins. Index and Girlhood are the same scholarly gesture in opposite directions: both refuse to let the text be a sequence of scenes, and both rebuild it as a system of relations in which any point can be reached from any other. The concordance is the Girlhood's credential and its control. When the tale asserts that the Nurse is the emotional centre of Juliet's formation, the assertion carries the weight of a reader who had transcribed every line the Nurse speaks and every line spoken to her. Modern readers who meet the Girlhood as a curiosity of Victoriana have the sequence backwards. In its author's career, and in its first readers' eyes, the tales were the dividend paid by the most laborious work of Shakespeare reference the century produced.

The formation novel supplies the method's literary context, and it sharpens both the defence and the prosecution. Cowden Clarke wrote the Girlhood in the years when the Bildungsroman, the novel of formation, was becoming the century's dominant account of how a self is made: Jane Eyre had appeared in 1847, David Copperfield was unfolding in monthly parts as the first Girlhood tales were printed, and the culture's imagination of character was being retrained to expect a childhood behind every adult crisis. The tales are the formation novel's critical cousin. They borrow its causality, its conviction that the woman is the girl's consequence, and apply that causality to persons who arrive with their endings already canonical. The borrowing explains the book's hold on its first readers, who were being taught by the novel to ask of every character the question the tales ask of Juliet: what made her so? It also dates the method's weakness precisely. The formation novel's causality is a novelist's instrument, powerful for persons invented to be explained and treacherous for persons invented to be enacted. A dramatic character is not a consequence but a function of scenes, and the scenes of Romeo and Juliet are engineered for speed, surprise, and collision, not for the slow legibility of growth. When the tale's causality is laid over the play's engineering, the two systems grind, and the grinding is audible wherever the tale is most confident. The adjudication this article eventually reaches can be previewed in those terms: the Girlhood succeeds wherever the play itself thinks in formations, in the household, the nursery, the confessional, and fails wherever the play thinks in collisions, in the ballroom, the street, and the tomb.

## What Victorian Girlhood Is Doing Inside Verona

No honest account of the tale can stop at its method, because the Girlhood was also, and deliberately, a book for girls. It was sold in shilling parts, bound as a gift book, dedicated across the literary world, and aimed at the reader its author would address again late in life, in an 1887 contribution to the Girl's Own Paper titled "Shakespeare as the Girl's Friend." George C. Gross, in the Victorian Studies essay of 1972 that founded the modern study of the book, described its author as a popular writer and a teacher of her vast audience on the subject of Victorian womanhood, and argued that the tales performed an oblique education: within the safe conduct of Shakespeare's prestige, they treated the passages of female life, courtship, marriage, desire, disappointment, that the official culture of girlhood preferred to leave unspoken. The Folger Shakespeare Library's exhibition Shakespeare's Unruly Women made the same point from the objects themselves, noting that the Girlhood deals with subjects, among them sexual assault and the melancholy that follows childbirth in other heroines' tales, that mothers of the period did not readily discuss. Shakespeare, in this economy, is a licence. What could not be said to a Victorian girl in a conduct book could be shown to her in Verona, at a safe distance of costume and centuries, wearing the unimpeachable authority of the national poet.

### Why did Victorian readers want the heroines to have been girls first?

Because the culture of girlhood had become an argument about formation. Mid-Victorian England believed, in its schools, sermons, and novels, that the woman is made in the girl, that character is fostered early by scenes, companions, and examples. A heroine who sprang into existence at her catastrophe offended that belief.

Giving her a childhood made her legible to the age's deepest theory of persons. The one passage this article owes to a sibling study concerns the portrait tradition the Girlhood inherited. Cowden Clarke did not begin the female-authored criticism of Shakespeare's women; she radicalized it. The tradition had been opened two decades earlier by [Anna Jameson and the Victorian Juliet](/2026/11/06/anna-jameson-victorian-juliet/), whose Characteristics of Women of 1832 had treated the heroines as portraits to be contemplated, moral and psychological studies of finished women. Jameson's Juliet is a completed being, love itself in female form, held up for admiration. Cowden Clarke's decisive move was to take Jameson's portrait off the wall and give it a past: to convert the icon into a case history. Where the portrait asks what the heroine is, the girlhood asks what made her, and the second question is the more dangerous one, because its answers can be checked against the play and can accuse the play's adults. The Girlhood's Verona is therefore not Jameson's gallery transported. It is a nursery with the door left open, and the draught that comes through it is the cold of the Capulet marriage market arriving early.

The permission behind the portrait had a longer pedigree, and tracing it clarifies what kind of act the Girlhood was. The line of women's Shakespeare mediation that later critics draw runs from the Lambs' Tales of 1807, in which Mary Lamb retold the comedies as prose stories intended for young readers, through Jameson's portraits, to Cowden Clarke's childhoods. Each step enlarges the woman's office. The Lambs abridge, and are permitted to, because abridgement serves the young. Jameson interprets, and is permitted to, because interpretation is framed as sympathy. Cowden Clarke invents, and the permission runs out at exactly the point where sympathy becomes authorship, which is why her book, and not the Lambs' or Jameson's, attracted the charge of presumption that still clings to it. The line also runs through her own family: it was Mary Lamb who had taught the young Mary Novello Latin and poetry in the salon years, so the Girlhood's author had been formed, in the most literal biographical sense, by the first woman in the sequence she completed. The tales know their ancestry even when their reviewers forgot it. A girlhood written by Mary Lamb's pupil, in the wake of Jameson's portraits, with a concordance behind it, is not a hobbyist's excursion. It is the third generation of a female critical tradition that the institutions had excluded from every other genre, arriving at last at the boldest genre of all, and finding the door of judgement guarded precisely there.

Innocence is the load-bearing Victorian import, and it must be named plainly. The white dove of the title is not only Romeo's metaphor; it is the age's requirement for its heroine, a purity that precedes experience and explains it. In the tale's economy, the girl's innocence is not ignorance exactly, but a protected inwardness: she is kept from knowledge by the household's formality, and her inner life grows rich in the shelter of being overlooked. Gross's education thesis cuts both ways here. The tale teaches its girl readers about desire by tracing a heroine whose desire will be total, yet it teaches them inside a frame that insists the desiring woman was an innocent child and that the innocence is the explanation of the desire's purity. That is a Victorian syllogism, not a Shakespearean one. The play's Juliet is not rendered innocent by her playwright; she is rendered young, which is a different and harder thing, and the difference between those two renderings is where the case against the tale begins.

The gift-book evidence lets the point be grounded in an object rather than an atmosphere. A surviving copy of the Putnam second series, the volume containing the Juliet tale, carries a presentation inscription dated January 1855, one friend giving the book to another, and the volume itself advertises its purpose on its covers as a Shakespeare gift book, stamped in gold. Consider what that transaction means. A woman of the 1850s, choosing a present for a friend, chose a hundred-page invented childhood for Juliet Capulet, bound as an improving treasure, over every competing gift the market offered. The tale's readership was not primarily the study or the schoolroom. It was the drawing room and the birthday table, and its criticism travelled under the passport of affection. That passport explains both the book's reach and the suspicion it attracts. Criticism that arrives as a gift is under no obligation to argue; it is under an obligation to be loved, and a childhood written to be loved will keep the sharp edges of its subject wrapped. The Juliet of the tale is a presentable Juliet, one a giver could hand to a girl without a qualm, and the presentability is purchased with the heroine's own most unpresentable quality, the frankness of her desire. The case for the tale will have to answer not only Fleming's charge of naivety but this quieter charge of upholstery: that the gift book's manners, not the author's mind, set the limit on what the invention was allowed to see.

## The Case Against the Invented Girlhood

The prosecution's classic statement is Juliet Fleming's. In "The Shakespeare Ladies'," her study of the female Shakespeare tradition, Fleming takes the Girlhood as the type of a criticism that proceeds by love and filiation: a criticism that, in her account, holds Shakespeare as an object of devotion, seeks first to restore him and then to be erased by his prior truth, and has consequently been discounted as naive, blind to the historical and textual integrity of the works it adores. The charge deserves its full weight. On Fleming's reading, the tale's perfect dovetailing with the play is not its triumph but its symptom. A childhood designed to account for a known conclusion can never surprise its maker, and a criticism that cannot be surprised has stopped reading and started illustrating. Julia Reinhard Lupton's Arden Critical Reader delivers the same verdict in a colder idiom, finding no critical assessment in what it calls the fanciful compendium of imagined backstories, and remarking of the Juliet narrative that it is longer than the play itself and connected to it by little more than devotion. Length, on this view, is the tell. The play achieves its Juliet in five days and a servant's anecdote; the tale needs a hundred pages, and the surplus is sentiment.

The sharper count in the indictment is not sentimentality but falsification, and it can be proved from the play's text. Cowden Clarke's Juliet is formed by protected innocence, a girl kept from knowledge until love arrives as revelation. Shakespeare's Juliet announces herself, at not yet fourteen, as the most erotically literate person in her play. The girl who waits for her wedding night in Act 3 Scene 2 does not speak like a protected innocent; she speaks in imperatives to the night, reckoning her desire with a frankness that still startles readers, and when Romeo is banished she processes the catastrophe in the great oxymoron speech of the same act, calling him a beautiful tyrant, a fiend angelical, a dove-feathered raven, a wolvish-ravening lamb. The lamb of this play, it is worth pausing to note, lives here: not as a child's pet in an invented nursery, but as one pole of a paradox in a wife's mouth, innocence and appetite fused in a single image for the husband she has already married and bedded. Irene Dash's account of Juliet's soliloquies traces the same trajectory from within, an inwardness moving steadily toward erotic self-consciousness and command. Sophie Duncan, calling Juliet the perceptive centre of the play, makes the complementary point about the tradition: the heroine has repeatedly been softened into an emblem by the cultures that admire her. The tale's white dove is one such softening, executed with genius. It takes a heroine whose distinction is the speed at which innocence converts itself into knowledge, and it pensioners her inside the innocence, explaining the woman by the one phase of her the woman had already outgrown.

A second falsification follows from the first, and it concerns the tale's treatment of the play's central datum, her age. The play is brutally exact: she has not seen the change of fourteen years, her father asks two more summers, Paris answers that younger than she are happy mothers made, and the whole machine of the tragedy is geared to the scandal of a child processed as a bride, a subject this series treats in [Juliet at Thirteen: Shakespeare's Daring Heroine](/2022/07/01/juliet-thirteen-years-old-heroine/) and in [How Old Are Romeo and Juliet? The Age Question](/2022/02/18/how-old-are-romeo-and-juliet-age-question/). The tale, by giving that child a long, sheltered, carefully narrated girlhood, commits a subtle theft against the datum. It makes the thirteen years feel full, furnished, and safe, when the play's point is that they were none of those things: that a girl can arrive at the marriage market with her entire remembered past amounting to a weaning joke because the household that owned her never thought her interior life worth recording. The tale grieves over the empty nursery by filling it. Filling it is the kindest possible response and the least accurate one. Verona's crime against Juliet, as the play presents it, is not that her childhood was cold. It is that her childhood was nobody's subject. A hundred pages of devoted attention, however intelligent, repeal the crime in the act of describing it.

The condescension that greets the book in later writing is itself evidence for the prosecution's climate, if not for its verdict. A distinguished modern essay on Shakespeare and Freud, pausing over the character-criticism tradition in a magazine for general readers, introduces the Girlhood as a publication of a less serious kind that enjoyed an enormous success throughout the Victorian period, and describes its contents as totally fabricated backstories, the parenthesis doing the hanging. The gesture is worth naming because it is typical: the book is waved past on the way to weightier matters, its fabrication treated as self-evidently disqualifying, its author's sixteen years of indexing unmentioned. Leonard Barkan's phrasing there is the drawing-room version of Fleming's scholarly charge, and the two together mark the poles of the dismissal, the donnish and the donnishly amused. What neither pole engages is the tale's actual content. The dismissal proceeds from the form, invention, to the verdict, unserious, without an intervening reading, and a criticism judged without being read has been convicted on its costume. The defence is entitled to that procedural point, and it is a strong one. It does not acquit the tale of importing its innocence or of filling the empty nursery. It acquits its author of the charge that has actually stuck to her name, the charge of not knowing what she was doing. She knew. The preface's talk of probability and germs is the talk of a scholar stating a method and its risks in advance, and the prosecution's failure to quote the method while condemning the results is the weakest joint in its case.

The age count can be pressed one stage further, into the tale's deepest structural choice. Verona, as the play builds it, is a city that does not recognize girlhood as a protected season at all. Lady Capulet was a mother at the age her daughter has reached; Paris pleads that younger than Juliet are happy mothers made; Capulet's scruple is not that his child is a child but that she is unripe, a horticultural judgement about timing rather than a moral one about kind. The play's world, in other words, has no nursery in the Victorian sense, no consecrated interval of innocence between infancy and the marriage market, and its heroine's tragedy is partly the tragedy of a girl processed by a system that never granted her the status the tale writes back into her past. Cowden Clarke's thirteen sheltered years are therefore not merely an addition to the record. They are the importation of an institution, Victorian girlhood itself, into a dramatic world constituted by its absence. This is the strongest form of the falsification charge, and it should be stated at full strength: the tale does not recover Juliet's girlhood, because the play's Verona had no such thing to recover. It invents the institution and then the girl to fit it. The defence's answer, when it comes, will have to be that the invention is a measuring instrument rather than a claim: that a reader sees the play's missing nursery most clearly when a great reader builds one and sets it beside the text. Whether that answer suffices is the question the adjudication must settle.

## The Case for the Invented Girlhood

The defence begins by granting the prosecution its facts and disputing its standard. If the tale is measured as biography, it is invention, and invention about a fictional person is a category error. But the tale was never offered as biography except in costume. It was offered, by the most textually saturated reader of the age, as an argument about where the play's effects come from, and arguments are not refuted by being narrated. Gail Marshall, in Shakespeare and Victorian Women, restores the scale of the author the prosecution patronizes, calling Cowden Clarke the pre-eminent female scholar of the century, and the description matters because it relocates the Girlhood inside a scholarly career rather than a gift-book sideline. Gross's Victorian Studies essay supplies the complementary relocation: the tales addressed a vast female readership as serious moral and emotional education, which means the tale's sentiment was not a failure of rigour but a chosen instrument, the form in which a woman scholar of 1851 could publish criticism of Shakespeare's women to the audience that most needed it. Brown's palinode completes the defence's theory. A prequel that merely flatters its original is decoration, but a prequel that revises the original's emphases is criticism by other means, and the Juliet tale revises the play's emphasis at its most vulnerable point: it transfers the tragedy's centre of gravity from the lovers to the household, and from fate to upbringing.

### What in the tale did later criticism of the play itself come to confirm?

Its household reading. The tale's central claims, that the Capulet parents' love had formalized into ceremony, that the Nurse was the girl's mother in fact, and that the tragedy grows from that substitution, are claims the play's own text supports and later criticism of the tragedy repeats in its own idiom.

The idiom is easy to sample: the mother who asks a servant for her daughter's age, the father whose tenderness turns to tyranny in a single scene, the servant begged at the crisis to go on being a mother, who refuses. The confirmation can be made concrete. Take the tale's prize exhibit, the periodical salutes: the grown girl's contact with her parents restricted to ritual greetings exchanged at set times. No such scene exists in the play, yet the invention merely dramatizes the play's documented etiquette. In the tragedy, Juliet addresses her mother with a formal "Madam" and delivers her obedience in set speeches; Lady Capulet, receiving her daughter's death, grieves in the cadences of public lamentation; Capulet, who in Act 1 Scene 2 speaks of his child with real tenderness, by Act 3 Scene 5 is offering her a choice between the bridegroom and the street. The arc the tale invents, cherishing curdling into management, is the arc the play performs at high speed in its own five days, and the tale's slowness is precisely its critical contribution: it shows the five days as the last phase of a thirteen-year process. The same vindication holds for the Nurse. The play's Juliet, abandoned in Act 3 Scene 5, pronounces the substitution herself when she turns from the Nurse's counsel to call her a most wicked fiend and resolves that the Friar, or her own hand, must end the matter: the servant who was her mother has resigned the office. A reader who meets that scene after the tale understands its desolation in a way a reader of the bare play must work harder to earn. This series' study of [The Women of Verona: Juliet, Lady Capulet, Nurse](/2023/05/05/women-of-verona-romeo-and-juliet/) maps the same triangle from within the play; the tale is the triangle's prehistory, and the fit between them is not the fit of a key cut to flatter a lock, but of two readings converging on one structure.

## Gross Against Fleming: An Adjudication

The disagreement can be stated cleanly, because both sides have been heard at strength. Fleming's prosecution holds that the Girlhood is the type of a filial, devotional criticism, naive about history and text, whose inventions merely restore and then vanish into Shakespeare's truth. Gross's defence, seconded by Marshall and Brown, holds that the tales are a major woman scholar's chosen instrument, an education for female readers and a palinode that revises the play's emphases, transferring the tragedy's cause from fortune to formation. Each side is right about the thing it is looking at, and the adjudication turns on separating the tale's two objects. As an account of a fictional girl's actual past, the tale is worthless, and Fleming wins without a contest: there is no past, there is a play, and the play's past is a weaning anecdote and an earthquake. As an account of the play, of what its silences and pressures mean, the tale is a serious and partly ratified reading, and Gross wins on points: its household thesis survives contact with the text, its pedagogy was real, and its method, the argued backstory, has become one of the standard instruments by which later ages interrogate the canon.

The rule that emerges, and that this article adopts as its verdict, is a rule about answerability. An invented backstory is criticism exactly where it remains answerable to the text: where each invention can be charged to a line, a silence, or a structural pressure in the work it precedes, and where the inventor can show the charge. It decays into decoration where it merely imports its own period's furniture and calls the import an explanation. Measured by that rule, the Juliet tale passes on its architecture and fails on its atmosphere. The cooling parents, the maternal Nurse, the safe Friar, the climate of the feud: all chargeable, all illuminating, all earned from the text by the one reader alive who had indexed it entire. The white dove's protected innocence: imported, decorative in the precise sense that it explains the heroine by the importer's values rather than by the play's, and contradicted by the desiring, reckoning Juliet of Acts 3 and 4. Cowden Clarke's tale is therefore neither the naive devotional exercise of the prosecution nor the flawless palinode of the defence. It is the strongest Victorian reading of how Juliet was made, damaged at its centre by the Victorian need for her to have been innocent, and it is most valuable to a modern reader when both halves of that sentence are kept in view at once.

The rule deserves one more testing, against the hardest case the tale offers, because a standard that only acquits easy instances is worth little. The hardest case is the periodical salutes themselves, the tale's signature claim, the invention a hostile reader would strike at first. Are they answerable, or are they the author's own family romance projected onto Verona? The audit runs as follows. The play supplies a mother who summons her daughter by attendant rather than entering her room, a father who discusses the girl's disposal with a suitor before he discusses it with her, a heroine who confides in a servant and a priest and in no parent at any point of crisis, and a household whose single scene of united family feeling is the lament over a corpse. Those are lines, silences, and pressures of the most concrete kind. The salutes are the tale's name for their sum: not an episode imported from a memoir of Italian manners, though the costume is Italian and period, but a compression of documented dramatic facts into one repeatable gesture. That is what criticism does when it is working. It takes dispersed evidence and gives it a handle. The handle can be quoted, disputed, and improved, and the dispute is only possible because the handle was offered in good faith, with its evidence in view. Fleming's standard, that no past exists beyond the play, would forbid the handle on principle; the play's own dramaturgy, which asks its audience to feel the weight of thirteen unnarrated years pressing on five narrated days, invites it. On the hardest case, the tale is acquitted, and the acquittal is the stronger for having been earned against the prosecution's best count rather than its weakest.

One caution belongs inside the verdict, because the tale's afterlife demonstrates it. Answerable invention has a way of hardening into received fact. The periodical salutes, the tale's finest stroke, are an invention, yet they are quoted in later writing about the play with a confidence that drifts toward the textual, as if Shakespeare had staged the Capulets' formality instead of implying it through a mother's ignorance of her daughter's age. The drift is the prequel's occupational disease, and it afflicts the form's masterpieces as reliably as its hackwork: a backstory vivid enough to be believed will be believed, and then the original stands accused of the backstory's inventions when a colder reader compares them. The Girlhood cannot be blamed for its readers' carelessness, but the phenomenon belongs in any honest adjudication, because it marks the point at which criticism by invention becomes a risk to the text it serves. The safeguard is the one this article has practised throughout: keep the columns separate. What Clarke invents, what the play supplies, and what the invention assumes are three different kinds of thing, and the tale rewards a reader exactly in proportion as the reader refuses to merge them.

## The Girlhood's Afterlife: Editions, Girl Readers, and the Backstory Habit

The book's material afterlife is the easiest of its claims to verify, because it is written in reprints. A work that failed would not have been reissued by Bickers and Putnam in the late 1870s, condensed by the author's sister for Bickers in 1890, taken up by Dent in 1907, and reprinted entire by Cambridge University Press in 2009. Between those poles stands the author's own late return to her first audience: the 1887 Girl's Own Paper essay "Shakespeare as the Girl's Friend," in which the woman of seventy-eight addressed the granddaughters of her first readers and restated the Girlhood's founding wager, that Shakespeare, properly introduced, is a girl's ally rather than her examination. The wager had a lineage by then. The Lambs' Tales had addressed young ladies in 1807; Jameson had given them portraits in 1832; Cowden Clarke gave them biographies in 1851; and the girls' Shakespeare industry of the later century, the school editions, the heroine galleries, the gift books with engraved portraits of Juliet in white, descends in a straight line from the shelf she stocked. The Folger's Shakespeare's Unruly Women exhibition placed the Girlhood among the instruments by which the nineteenth century taught its daughters what a woman was, and the placement is a judgement in itself: the book had become an exhibit in the history of female reading, which is what its author always intended it to be.

### Which later backstories descend from Clarke's experiment?

The durable descendants are the works that treat a Shakespearean silence as an invitation to argued invention. Lois Leveen's novel Juliet's Nurse of 2014 is the clearest heir in this play's line, writing the same household and the same substitution of servant for mother from the Nurse's side of the nursery door.

It carries with it the conviction that the tragedy is explained by the unrecorded years. The form Cowden Clarke legitimated, the scholarly fan's prequel, is a standard shelf of the play's afterlife. The habit outgrew the nursery. The twentieth and twenty-first centuries made the backstory a major literary industry, from Jean Rhys's Wide Sargasso Sea answering Jane Eyre to the shelves of Shakespearean prequels and servant's-eye retellings that booksellers stock as a category, and Sarah Annes Brown's account of the prequel as palinode was written to explain exactly this lineage. Cowden Clarke stands near its head, not because she invented the idea of supplying a past, but because she was the first to supply one at this scale, under scholarly colours, for the female characters of the national poet, and to state the method in a preface as a theory of character. Her direct descendants in method are the writers, very often women, very often learned, who treat the canon's margins as under-evidenced rather than empty. Her indirect descendants are less welcome to her shade: the content industries that generate backstory as franchise maintenance, where the past is supplied not to interrogate the work but to extend its shelf life. The distinction matters, and the Girlhood is the cleanest early instrument for drawing it, because its inventions can still be audited against its preface's rule. Where the tale's Verona answers the play, it is the ancestor of Rhys and Leveen. Where it merely furnishes the nursery, it is the ancestor of the merchandise.

Her other books for the same constituency fill out the picture of a deliberate career. Shakespeare Proverbs of 1848 had already packaged the canon as domestic wisdom; World-noted Women of 1858 extended the gallery method from Shakespeare's heroines to the famous women of all lands and ages, types of womanly attributes arranged for the same improving shelf; and the Girlhood sits between them as the centrepiece of a one-woman curriculum, in which the national poet's women are first indexed, then proverbialized, then given childhoods, then set beside the heroines of history as models and warnings. No male scholar of the century built anything like it, because no male scholar needed to: the universities, the editions, and the reviews were open to men by right, and a man with Cowden Clarke's knowledge would have been absorbed into the apparatus of editing and lecturing that employed her husband. The curriculum was the form her exclusion took, and its very existence is the strongest contextual evidence for Gross's reading of the tales. A writer content with decoration does not build a system. The Girlhood's inventions belong to a lifelong, structured attempt to put Shakespeare's women at the centre of female education, and they should be judged as the working parts of that attempt, with its ambitions and its blind spots intact, rather than as the embroidery of a lady amateur. The blind spots were the system's as much as the woman's. A curriculum for girls in 1851 could not have carried the play's Juliet entire, and the tale's innocence is partly the price of admission to the schoolroom shelf its author was determined to stock.

The tale's independent life in the modern book trade supplies a final, concrete measure of its standing. The Juliet narrative has been issued on its own, detached from the other fourteen tales, in a modern reprint that gives it a hundred pages and a volume's dignity, the only one of the girlhoods to be so singled out in that series. Detachability is a critical verdict by other means. It records the judgement of publishers, and behind them of readers, that this tale, alone among the fifteen, carries a weight its companions do not: that the white dove's childhood has become a small classic of the play's afterlife, read by people who will never open Portia's or Imogen's. The singling out also completes the tale's strange symmetry with its subject. Juliet is the heroine whom the play itself detaches from every context, family, city, genre, until she stands alone in the tomb; her girlhood has undergone the same detachment in the book trade, separated from its gallery to stand alone on the shelf. Cambridge University Press's decision to reissue the complete three volumes in 2009, in a scholarly paperback series of recovered Victorian texts, ratifies the other half of the standing: the whole Girlhood, not merely its famous tenth tale, is a document the profession intends to keep. Between the standalone dove and the reissued gallery, the book's afterlife has settled into the double form this article has argued for throughout, popular survival and scholarly custody at once.

That double form is also the answer to the question with which the afterlife began, the question of influence. The Girlhood's influence on how Juliet's youth was imagined does not need to be hunted through allusions in later novels, though they exist; it stands in the plain bibliographic fact that for a century and a half, any reader who wanted Juliet to have had a childhood reached, knowingly or not, for the shape Cowden Clarke gave it: the formal parents, the fostering servant, the watching city, the girl alone with her inner life. Later backstories vary the furniture. The floor plan is hers.

## Reading Juliet Through Girlhood: Value and Limits

The value of the Girlhood, gathered from the evidence heard in this article, can be stated in three sentences. It is the first reading of the play to locate the tragedy in the heroine's formation rather than in fortune, feud, or haste alone, and its location has been ratified by the text and by later criticism often enough to count as a discovery. It is a document of female scholarship at its most resourceful, a concordance-maker smuggling criticism into the only genre her century offered her for reaching the readers she cared about, and succeeding for a hundred years. And it is a controlled experiment in method: because its author knew the text entire and stated her rule in advance, its successes and failures can be audited line by line, which makes it the best teaching instrument the prequel form has produced. A student who learns to test the tale against Act 1 Scene 3 has learned the whole discipline of distinguishing interpretation from decoration.

The limits are equally statable. The tale cannot be used as evidence about Shakespeare's Juliet, only as evidence about the Victorian Juliet and about the pressures the play exerts on a certain kind of devoted, learned reader. Its innocence is an import and must be quarantined: any account of the heroine that begins from the white dove must be corrected by the Juliet who reckons her desire in Act 3 and her danger in Act 4 with a clarity no one around her matches. And its determinism must be resisted at the root. The play's Juliet is not the sum of her nursery. She is the character who exceeds every explanation Verona offers for her, including, in the end, the explanation her most loving critic built for her out of thirteen invented years. The Girlhood's final lesson is the one its author, to her credit, built into the form and perhaps did not intend: that a childhood, however probable, is not a person, and that the space Shakespeare left around his heroine was not an oversight to be repaired but a liberty to be respected. Cowden Clarke filled the space more intelligently than anyone else has done. The space is still there, and the play is still larger than her filling of it, which is why her book survives as criticism rather than as a replacement: it sends its reader back to the five days, the weaning joke, the cold mother and the warm servant, with better questions than the reader brought to them.

For the reader who comes to the tale fresh, a protocol is worth stating, because the book's pleasures and its dangers arrive in the same chapters. Read the play's first act first, and fix the archive in mind: the weaning, the fall, the joke, the dead foster-sister, the mother asking the servant for the child's age. Then read the tale, and mark, in the margin if necessary, every point at which an invention charges itself to that archive or to the play's later scenes, and every point at which the charge fails and the furniture is simply Victorian. The exercise takes an evening and teaches more about the difference between criticism and decoration than a shelf of theory, because the tale is honest enough to make its seams findable and good enough to make the finding pleasurable. Read in that spirit, as its author's preface invites and her prosecutors decline to do, the white dove of Verona stops being a curiosity of Victoriana. It becomes what it was built to be: the record of the best-informed reader of her century asking the play the one question the play refuses to answer, and answering it herself, at length, in the open, where the answer can be checked. The checking is the tribute. A book that can still sustain it, a hundred and seventy years of condescension notwithstanding, has earned its place in the play's critical history, and its Juliet, corrected by the play's own, can stand.

For teaching, the tale's usefulness is of the same double kind. Set beside the play in a classroom, it performs two services no commentary performs as well. It makes the conventions of character criticism visible as conventions, because its inventions wear their joins openly, and a student who has watched Cowden Clarke build parental coldness out of a mother's two jokes has learned to watch every critic, including the respectable ones, for the same carpentry. And it restores to the heroine the dimension that performance most easily flattens, duration: an actress's Juliet blazes for an evening, while the tale's Juliet takes thirteen years to become, and the friction between those two Juliets is the most instructive friction the play's afterlife offers. The limits hold in the classroom as in the study. The tale must be taught as evidence about 1851 before it is used as evidence about the play, its innocence read as a document of the giver's shelf rather than the heroine's nature. Taught in that order, prosecution first and defence second, it does what the best criticism in any genre does: it leaves the play larger than it found it, and the reader warier, in the best sense, of every future filling of its silences, including the fillings the reader will be tempted to write alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch girlhood summary, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch girlhood summary is the table in this article that sets each element of Cowden Clarke's invented childhood for Juliet beside the evidence the play supplies and the assumption the invention carries. Its pattern is double: every invention answers a real silence or oddity in the play, the mother's distance, the Nurse's maternity, the Friar's unearned trust, while every invention also converts one of the play's effects into a childhood cause. The summary exists to make that conversion visible row by row, so a reader can credit the tale where it argues from the text and discount it where it merely decorates.

### Q: Who was Mary Cowden Clarke, and why did her scholarship give her inventions authority?

Mary Cowden Clarke (1809-1898) was a London-born Shakespeare scholar, the eldest daughter of the music publisher Vincent Novello, educated in a household where Charles and Mary Lamb were intimates and Mary Lamb taught her Latin and poetry. She married Charles Cowden Clarke in 1828 and spent the following sixteen years compiling the first complete concordance to Shakespeare, published in 1845. Her inventions carried authority because they issued from total textual knowledge: the woman imagining Juliet's childhood had indexed every word of the plays, and her title pages sold the tales on the concordance's credit.

### Q: What is The Girlhood of Shakespeare's Heroines, and how was it published?

The Girlhood of Shakespeare's Heroines is a collection of fifteen tales by Mary Cowden Clarke, each inventing the childhood of one of Shakespeare's women and tracing the germs of her mature character. W. H. Smith and Son published it in London between 1850 and 1852 in three volumes, and the tales were also sold separately in one-shilling parts, each part complete in itself. An American edition from G. P. Putnam followed in 1852, bound and stamped as a Shakespeare gift book, and reprints continued across the century, with a Cambridge University Press reissue in 2009.

### Q: What is the Juliet tale called, and where does its dove come from?

The Juliet tale is called "Juliet; The White Dove of Verona," the tenth of the fifteen tales. Its dove comes from the play's first sight of the heroine: in Act 1 Scene 5 Romeo, watching her cross the Capulet hall, sees a snowy dove trooping with crows, and calls her beauty too rich for use and for earth too dear. Cowden Clarke lifts the lover's metaphor out of the ballroom and builds a childhood under it, making whiteness, gentleness, and apartness the principles of the girl's formation. The title is therefore a thesis: the tale reads the woman backwards from the image her lover first found for her.

### Q: How long is the Juliet tale beside the play it precedes?

The Juliet tale runs to roughly a hundred pages in modern reprint, which makes it longer than the play itself, as the critical backstory chapter of Julia Reinhard Lupton's Arden Critical Reader remarks with some dryness. The disproportion is the point at issue between the tale's prosecutors and defenders. To the prosecution, the surplus length is sentiment, a hundred pages where five days and one anecdote sufficed. To the defence, the length is the argument's necessary form: a formation cannot be shown at the play's speed, and a backstory that matched the tragedy's brevity would merely repeat its silences instead of examining them.

### Q: What happens to Juliet's bond with her parents across the tale?

Across the tale the bond cools by degrees from warmth into ceremony. The child begins as the loved and petted heir of a great house that has buried its other children, but as she grows, affection formalizes, and her contact with her parents dwindles to the wonted periodical salutes exchanged between Italian parent and child: greetings at set hours, interviews by appointment. The invention is the tale's boldest, because it converts the play's most startling household fact, a mother who must ask the Nurse her daughter's age, from a condition into a process with a history and a cost.

### Q: Why does the Nurse become the emotional centre of the tale's Juliet?

The Nurse becomes the centre because the tale's parents vacate the position. As parental affection retreats into ceremony, the girl's days gather around the servant who suckled her, and the Nurse is rebuilt as her lifelong companion and mother in fact, the warm presence against which the palace's formality is measured. Cowden Clarke is extending the play's own evidence: the Nurse keeps Juliet's infancy, carries her secrets, and is the adult she runs to, while her mother proposes marriage by embassy. When the play's Nurse resigns the office in Act 3 Scene 5 by counselling bigamy, the desolation lands because the tale has shown what the office was.

### Q: What design did Cowden Clarke declare for the Girlhood in her preface?

Her preface declares a design of probable antecedents. The tales aim to trace the earlier histories of Shakespeare's women by imagining the circumstances, scenes, events, and companions of their infant lives that might have fostered the germs of the character visible in their maturity, inventing adventures that colour their future lives and placing each girl in situations that lead naturally up to the known conclusion of her story. Two commitments govern the whole book: the inventions claim likelihood rather than fancy, and character is treated as growth, a flower to be traced back to its soil.

### Q: How did sixteen years on the concordance shape the tales that followed it?

The concordance gave the tales their method and their limits. Twelve years of compiling and four of printing taught Cowden Clarke the exact dimensions of every silence in the plays: she knew that Juliet's childhood survives in a single anecdote, and she knew the weight of every reference to childhood in the canon because she had filed them all. The tales are the overflow of that knowledge into the territory the text refuses to enter. They also inherit the indexer's discipline: the inventions fasten on documented oddities, the mother's distance, the servant's maternity, rather than roaming free, because their author had spent sixteen years learning where the text ends.

### Q: Who actually wrote the Girlhood, and why is the credit sometimes blurred?

Mary Cowden Clarke wrote the Girlhood alone. The blurring comes from the family firm: she shared a surname, a profession, and later an edition of the plays with her husband Charles Cowden Clarke, and the couple's genuinely joint works, the Cassell edition completed in 1868 and The Shakespeare Key of 1879, sit beside her solo works on the shelf. Cataloguers have sometimes spread her credit across the partnership. The record is plain, however: the preface speaks in her voice, the design is hers, and the concordance that underwrites the tales was her sixteen years of labour, not the household's.

### Q: What did George Gross argue the tales taught their girl readers?

George C. Gross, in his 1972 Victorian Studies essay on the Girlhood, argued that the tales performed an education in womanhood under Shakespeare's safe conduct. He described their author as a popular writer and a teacher of her vast audience on the subject of Victorian womanhood, and he showed the tales treating the passages of female life, courtship, desire, marriage, and disappointment, that official girlhood culture left unspoken. The Folger Shakespeare Library's exhibition Shakespeare's Unruly Women later made the complementary point from the books themselves: the Girlhood deals with subjects, including sexual assault, that mothers of the period did not readily discuss with daughters.

### Q: Why does Juliet Fleming treat the Girlhood as the type of a naive criticism?

Juliet Fleming, in "The Shakespeare Ladies'," treats the Girlhood as the type of a filial criticism: a criticism that loves its object, holds Shakespeare as an object of devotion, and seeks first to restore him and then to be erased by his prior truth. On her account such criticism has been discounted as naive because it is blind to the historical and textual integrity of the works, and the tale's perfect fit with the play is the symptom rather than the triumph, since a childhood engineered to account for a known ending can never be surprised by its subject. The charge is the prosecution's classic statement and the one any defence must answer.

### Q: What does Sarah Annes Brown mean by calling the prequel a palinode?

Sarah Annes Brown, in her Shakespeare Survey essay "The Prequel as Palinode," means that a prequel sings against its original. A palinode is a poem that recants an earlier poem, and Brown argues that the successful prequel is an essentially subversive form: forbidden to change the known ending, it must offer a new way into the old text, contradicting the original's emphases while respecting its facts. Applied to the Girlhood, the term dignifies the method. Cowden Clarke does not dispute a single event of the tragedy; she disputes its centre of gravity, moving the cause of the catastrophe from fortune and haste to upbringing and household.

### Q: Where does the tale's Juliet part company with the play's Juliet?

The parting comes over innocence and desire. The tale's Juliet is formed by protected innocence, a girl kept from knowledge inside a formal household until love arrives as revelation. The play's Juliet, at not yet fourteen, is the most erotically literate person in her tragedy, reckoning her desire in Act 3 Scene 2 with a frankness that still startles, and processing betrayal in the same act through oxymorons that fuse innocence with appetite, the dove-feathered raven and the wolvish-ravening lamb. Critics such as Irene Dash, tracing her soliloquies, find an inwardness moving toward erotic self-consciousness and command. The tale pensioners her inside the innocence she had outgrown.

### Q: How were the tales sold, and as what kind of present were they given?

The tales were sold in two forms at once: gathered in three volumes from W. H. Smith and Son between 1850 and 1852, and issued periodically in one-shilling parts, each part containing one complete tale, which put them within reach of schoolrooms and railway stalls. In America, G. P. Putnam's 1852 edition bound the second series in cloth stamped in gold as a Shakespeare gift book, and surviving copies carry presentation inscriptions from friend to friend. The format is evidence about the readership: this was improving literature designed to be given to a girl by someone who wished her reading, and her formation, well.

### Q: Who edited the condensed edition of the Girlhood late in the century?

Sabilla Novello, the author's younger sister, edited the condensed edition published by Bickers and Son in 1890. The appointment kept the book inside the family's female line of Shakespeare labour: Mary Cowden Clarke was born a Novello, her father Vincent Novello's publishing house stood behind much of her career, and her sister's editorship abridged the fifteen tales for a later generation of the same girl readership. The condensed edition is one station in a long material afterlife that runs from the shilling parts of 1850 through Bickers and Putnam reprints of the 1870s and a Dent edition of 1907 to the Cambridge University Press reissue of 2009.

### Q: What did Cowden Clarke write for girl readers about Shakespeare in her late seventies?

In 1887, at seventy-eight, she published "Shakespeare as the Girl's Friend" in the Girl's Own Paper, returning to the audience of the Girlhood nearly four decades on. The essay restates the founding wager of the tales: that Shakespeare, properly introduced, is a girl's ally rather than her examination, a friend whose heroines model the passages of her own life. The piece matters because it closes the circuit of her career. The child taught poetry by Mary Lamb became the indexer of the canon, then the inventor of its heroines' childhoods, and finally the elderly advocate addressing the granddaughters of her first readers in their own magazine.

### Q: Which four tales stand beside Juliet's in the second series?

The second series, the volume of 1851, gathers five tales: "Isabella; The Votaress," "Katharina and Bianca; The Shrew and the Demure," "Ophelia; The Rose of Elsinore," "Rosalind and Celia; The Friends," and "Juliet; The White Dove of Verona." The company is instructive. Juliet's girlhood was written as one panel in a gallery of formations that spans a novice under vows, two sisters divided by temperament, a prince's discarded love, and two cousins in exile, each title naming the condition of the girlhood rather than the catastrophe of the maturity. The series design insists that Juliet's case is not unique: every heroine, the volume argues, was a girl before she was a fate.

### Q: How does the tale place Friar Laurence in Juliet's early life?

The tale makes the Friar her established friend and counsellor long before the crisis, a fixture of the household's spiritual life whose ear is safe. The placement answers a genuine oddity in the play: Shakespeare's Juliet, with a catastrophe in her hands, runs to Friar Laurence's cell in Act 4 Scene 1 and unburdens herself with a confidence the text never pauses to explain, staking her life on his counsel and his chemistry. A reader arriving from the tale understands the trust as a habit of years. The invention is characteristic of the method at its best, charging a real structural pressure in the play to a plausible history and charging nothing else.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch girlhood summary to judge an invented backstory?

An essay should use the summary's three columns as a test. For any invented backstory, the essay asks what the invention asserts, what in the original text supplies or permits it, and what assumption the invention smuggles in from its own period. Where the middle column is full and the third is honest, the backstory is criticism; where the middle column is empty and the third is crowded, it is decoration. Applied to Cowden Clarke, the test acquits the architecture, the cooling parents, the maternal Nurse, the safe Friar, and convicts the atmosphere, the protected innocence that the desiring Juliet of the play refutes.
