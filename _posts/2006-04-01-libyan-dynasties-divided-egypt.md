For more than two centuries, the men who ruled Egypt carried a title no Egyptian king had ever used. Alongside the traditional royal names and the cartouches and the epithets borrowed from the great pharaohs of the past, they styled themselves Great Chief of the Ma, chief of a Libyan tribal grouping their ancestors had belonged to. They built at Karnak, campaigned into the Levant, buried their dead in Egyptian style, and wrote in hieroglyphs. And they never gave up the chiefly title, because that was where their armed men came from and armed men were what kept them on the throne.

That combination, pharaoh and tribal chief simultaneously, is the key to the Libyan period and to why it ended as it did. Egypt had spent the New Kingdom fighting Libyan incursions, defeating them repeatedly, and then settling the defeated in military colonies in the Delta and employing their sons as soldiers. Within a few generations the descendants of those settlers held the senior military commands. Within a few more, one of them took the throne, and his dynasty ruled Egypt for roughly two hundred years. Then the same structure that had raised them broke the country apart, because a political system built on chiefs who commanded personal followings produces more chiefs, and each of them wants a share.

This article follows the military story: where the Libyans came from, how Egypt fought them and then absorbed them, how a Meshwesh family at Bubastis took the crown, what Shoshenq I actually achieved with his Levantine campaign, how the armed structure of Libyan rule worked, and why an Egypt that began the period with one king ended it with half a dozen simultaneously. The organizing claim is the warrior-chief-to-pharaoh thesis: the Libyan dynasties turned settled foreign warlords into legitimate pharaohs, and their tribal military structure, which was the source of their strength, was also what ultimately fractured Egypt into rival chiefdoms.

## Who the Libyans Were

The people Egypt called Libyans were not a single nation, and getting the terminology right matters for everything that follows.

### The names in the Egyptian record

Egyptian texts distinguish several groups to the west. Tjehenu and Tjemehu are the older designations, appearing from the Old Kingdom onward and referring to populations of the western desert and the coastal region west of the Delta. Libu, the term from which the modern word Libya derives, appears later, in the Ramesside period. Meshwesh, frequently abbreviated in later texts to Ma, appears alongside the Libu and becomes the group most consequential for Egyptian history, since the dynasties that eventually took the throne traced themselves to it.

These are Egyptian labels for peoples the Egyptians encountered, not necessarily the names those peoples used for themselves, and the relationship between the categories shifted across two thousand years of usage. What the labels consistently indicate is a set of pastoral and semi-pastoral populations occupying the desert margins and coastal strip west of the Nile, organized around chiefs and kin groups rather than around a centralized state.

### How they lived

The archaeological record for the Libyan peoples before their settlement in Egypt is thin, which is itself informative: mobile pastoralists leave less than settled agriculturalists. What Egyptian sources describe, and what the material evidence supports, is a cattle-keeping economy supplemented by cultivation where water allowed, in a landscape that was drying progressively across the later Bronze Age.

Egyptian art depicts Libyans with a consistent set of markers: long cloaks, sometimes open at the front, side-locks of hair, feathers worn in the hair, tattooed or painted body decoration, and beards on some figures. These are conventions, applied to a type rather than drawn from individuals, but the specific elements recur consistently enough to suggest they were built on observed differences.

### The homeland west of the Delta

They came from the region immediately west of Egypt, the desert margins and Mediterranean coastal strip of what is now Libya and the western Egyptian desert. They were not distant invaders arriving from overseas but neighbors, pushed eastward toward the Nile by progressive aridification and by pressure from populations behind them.

That proximity is the essential fact. Egypt's relationship with the Libyans was a frontier relationship with an adjacent people, continuous across the whole of Egyptian history, rather than an encounter with a remote power. The Delta's western edge was permeable, the desert routes were known, and movement across the boundary was constant at a low level even in periods when no campaign was recorded.

### Why they pushed east

The long-term driver appears to have been environmental. The Sahara had been drying since the mid-Holocene, and the pastures that supported cattle-keeping populations in the eastern Sahara contracted steadily. A cattle economy under that pressure has limited options, and the Nile Valley, with permanent water and rich pasture in the Delta, was the obvious destination.

Pressure from further west may have contributed. The great migrations associated with the end of the Bronze Age across the eastern Mediterranean, discussed in the account of [the Sea Peoples and the Bronze Age collapse](/2005/07/01/sea-peoples-bronze-age-collapse/), involved population movements that touched the Libyan coast, and some Egyptian records of Libyan attacks describe them in coalition with groups identified among the Sea Peoples. The two phenomena overlap in time and to some extent in personnel.

## Egypt Fights the Libyans

Before Libyans ruled Egypt they fought it, repeatedly and at scale, and the New Kingdom wars are the essential background to everything that followed.

### The Ramesside invasions

Seti I and Ramesses II both campaigned against Libyans and built fortresses along the western approaches to the Delta, a chain of installations extending westward along the coast. Those forts indicate that the threat was taken seriously enough to justify permanent military infrastructure well before any major invasion.

The invasions came under Merneptah and again under Ramesses III. Merneptah's records describe a large Libyan movement into the western Delta, led by a chief of the Libu, in coalition with groups from the sea, and present it as a defeated invasion with very large numbers of enemy dead and captives. The figures in Egyptian victory inscriptions should be treated cautiously, since inflation of enemy casualties was standard practice, but the scale of the response indicates a genuine emergency.

Ramesses III faced further Libyan attacks, recorded on the walls of his mortuary temple at Medinet Habu, and again claimed decisive victories. The Ramesside record as a whole is examined in the guide to [Ramesside Egypt](/2005/01/01/ramesside-egypt-complete-guide/).

### What the invasions actually were

The Egyptian sources present these events as invasions to be repelled. The evidence suggests something closer to attempted migration. Egyptian accounts mention women, children, cattle, and household goods accompanying the fighting men, which is not the profile of a raiding party. These were populations trying to move into the Delta and settle there.

That distinction matters for what happened next. A defeated raiding force is dispersed and sent home. A defeated migrating population, with nowhere to go back to, has to be dealt with some other way, and Egypt's chosen method created the Libyan period.

### Why did Egypt hire the men it had fought?

Because Egypt needed soldiers and the defeated Libyans needed land. Rather than exterminating or expelling them, the Ramesside state settled captured and surrendered Libyans in military colonies, chiefly in the Delta, granting land in exchange for military service, a standard imperial solution used by many states before and since.

The arrangement made sense on its own terms. Egypt's own manpower was increasingly committed to agriculture and to the temple estates, mercenary recruitment was already normal practice, and Libyan fighting men were available in quantity and had proven their capability. Settling them in organized communities under their own leaders reduced the administrative burden and produced a reserve of trained soldiers on the frontier where they were most needed.

### The unintended consequence

What the policy also produced was a large, cohesive, armed population inside Egypt, retaining its own leadership structures, holding land by military tenure, and concentrated in exactly the region that would become Egypt's political center after the New Kingdom collapsed. Egyptian sources record Libyan settlements and Libyan military units across the Delta and into Middle Egypt.

Those communities did not dissolve into the Egyptian population. They adopted Egyptian religion, Egyptian names alongside Libyan ones, and Egyptian burial practice, while keeping their chiefly titles, their kin-group organization, and their military role. Two or three centuries of that produced a Delta in which the men with the weapons were Libyan-descended and organized under their own chiefs, and the men with the crown were not.

## From Chief to Pharaoh

The transition from settled military colonists to kings of Egypt took roughly two centuries and happened without any recorded moment of conquest.

### The Twenty-first Dynasty background

The New Kingdom ended in fragmentation, with kings at Tanis in the Delta ruling alongside the High Priests of Amun at Thebes, an arrangement examined in the account of [how Egypt split after the New Kingdom](/2005/12/01/how-egypt-split-after-new-kingdom/). Through that period, Libyan-descended military families continued to hold positions in the Delta, and some of them appear in the record with both Egyptian titles and the chiefly designation.

The family that eventually took the throne was based at Bubastis in the eastern Delta and held the position of Great Chief of the Ma across several generations. Genealogical texts trace the line back through a sequence of chiefs, and the ancestor from whom the family reckoned descent bears a name that is Libyan rather than Egyptian in form.

### How Libyan chiefs became pharaohs

By accumulation rather than by conquest. Libyan military families held land, commanded troops, and intermarried with Egyptian elite and royal lines across generations, so that by the time Shoshenq I took the throne around 945 BCE his family already controlled the most powerful military bloc in the Delta and had ties to the ruling house.

The transition appears to have been legitimate in Egyptian terms. Shoshenq I was connected by marriage to the Twenty-first Dynasty, and his accession follows the death of the last king of that line without any record of a struggle. This was not a foreign conquest. It was the assumption of the throne by the most powerful family in the country, which happened to be of Libyan descent, through the ordinary mechanisms of Egyptian dynastic politics.

### The double title

What makes the Libyan kings distinctive is that they did not abandon the chiefly designation. Great Chief of the Ma continues in use alongside pharaonic titulary, held by kings and by their relatives who governed regions and commanded troops.

That retention is the single most revealing fact about the period. An Egyptian king's authority derived in theory from the gods and from the office; a Libyan chief's derived from his kin group and his followers. The Libyan kings held both, and they needed both, because the pharaonic title gave them legitimacy while the chiefly title gave them soldiers. Keeping the second meant keeping the structure that produced it, and that structure was tribal, segmentary, and prone to fission.

### Names, marriage, and assimilation

The Libyan elite used Egyptian names extensively, and royal names in the period draw heavily on the great names of the past. They also retained a set of names that are Libyan in origin, including Shoshenq, Osorkon, Takelot, and Nimlot, which recur across the dynasties and function as family markers.

Marriage connected them to Egyptian elite lineages, including priestly families at Thebes and Memphis, and those connections ran in both directions across several generations before the dynasty took the throne. Royal women of Libyan descent held the office of God's Wife of Amun at Thebes, and the mechanics of how that office governed the south are examined in the account of [the God's Wife of Amun and temple power](/2006/03/01/gods-wife-of-amun-temple-power/).

### Did the Libyan kings keep their own customs?

They kept some and dropped others. They retained Libyan personal names, the chiefly titles, kin-group organization, and a feather ornament in some depictions. They adopted Egyptian religion, Egyptian burial, hieroglyphic writing, Egyptian royal titulary, and Egyptian administrative forms wholesale.

The pattern is selective retention of the things that underwrote their power base and wholesale adoption of the things that legitimated their rule. Nobody in Egypt seems to have found this contradictory, which tells us something about how Egyptian identity actually worked.

## Shoshenq I and the Great Reign

The founder of the Twenty-second Dynasty was the most effective king the Libyan period produced, and his reign set the pattern the later dynasty could not sustain.

### Who was Shoshenq I

Shoshenq I took the throne around 945 BCE, founding the dynasty that ruled from Bubastis and Tanis. He inherited the position of Great Chief of the Ma and appears to have been connected by marriage to the outgoing Twenty-first Dynasty, which gave his accession a legitimate footing.

His reign is marked by three things: an attempt to reunify the country under a single royal authority, a substantial building program including work at Karnak, and a military campaign into the Levant that is the best-attested Egyptian intervention in Asia since the New Kingdom.

### The reunification strategy

Shoshenq I's central political problem was the same one every king of the period faced: how to control Thebes and the south from a base in the Delta. His solution was appointment. He installed his son as High Priest of Amun at Thebes, and he placed family members in other senior positions, both religious and military, across the country.

This was a reasonable strategy and it worked in his lifetime. Its weakness became apparent within two generations, for reasons discussed below: appointing sons to hereditary offices creates hereditary rivals, and a king with many powerful relatives has many potential competitors.

### Did Shoshenq I invade the Levant

Yes, and it is the best-documented Egyptian campaign in Asia for centuries. A relief and accompanying topographical list at Karnak, on the Bubastite Portal, records the campaign and names a long series of places in the southern Levant that the Egyptian force reached or claimed. A fragment of a stela bearing his name was also found at Megiddo, which is independent physical confirmation of Egyptian presence at a major site.

The campaign is generally connected with the biblical account of an invasion by a king named Shishak during the reign of Rehoboam, in which the treasures of the temple and palace at Jerusalem were taken. The identification of Shishak with Shoshenq I is widely accepted, though not universally, and it is one of the principal synchronisms linking Egyptian and biblical chronology. Jerusalem itself does not appear securely in the Karnak place list, and how the two accounts relate in detail is debated.

### What the campaign achieved

Militarily it appears to have been successful in the immediate sense. The place list is extensive, covering routes through the Negev, the coastal plain, and northward into the Jezreel Valley region, and the Megiddo fragment confirms that the force reached at least that far.

Strategically it achieved much less than the New Kingdom campaigns it echoed. No permanent Egyptian administration was established in the Levant, no garrison network is attested, and no successor repeated the achievement. The campaign looks like a large raid for plunder and prestige rather than the reestablishment of an empire, and the plunder may well have funded the building program at Karnak.

Shoshenq I died before the Karnak decoration commemorating the campaign was completed, which is why parts of it remain unfinished. That detail is a small but telling indication of how much the dynasty's fortunes depended on the individual king.

The dynastic picture is genuinely confusing because the lines overlap, so setting them out with their bases and their role in the fragmentation is the most useful single reference.

The table's central lesson is in the last three rows. The two royal dynasties get the numbers and appear in the king lists, but the political reality of the later period was a landscape of chiefs and local rulers, several of whom used royal titles, none of whom could dominate the others.

### How many kings ruled Egypt at the same time

At the height of fragmentation, several. Piye's Victory Stela, recording the Kushite campaign, names multiple rulers using royal titles simultaneously across the Delta and Middle Egypt, alongside a larger number of Great Chiefs and local rulers who did not claim kingship. Four men with royal titulary at once is a reasonable reading of that document.

## How Libyan Rule Worked

Understanding the fragmentation requires understanding what kind of political system the Libyans actually operated, because it was not simply a weakened version of Egyptian kingship.

### Kingship on Egyptian terms

In its formal apparatus, Libyan rule was entirely Egyptian. The kings took the five-part royal titulary, wrote their names in cartouches, built and restored temples, endowed cults, appointed priests, and had themselves depicted in the traditional royal scenes. Their monuments at Karnak, Tanis, Bubastis, and elsewhere are Egyptian monuments in every respect.

They were buried in Egyptian style. The royal tombs at Tanis, discovered largely intact, contained silver coffins, gold masks, and jewelry of high quality, buried within the temple precinct. The Tanis finds are among the richest royal burials to survive from Egypt, and they demonstrate that the material culture of Libyan kingship was thoroughly Egyptian and thoroughly well funded.

### Authority on chiefly terms

Underneath that apparatus operated a different logic. A Libyan chief's power rested on his kin group and on the armed followers who owed him personal allegiance. That authority was not territorial in the Egyptian sense; it attached to a man and his people rather than to an office and a province.

The consequence was that Libyan rulers governed through relatives. Sons, brothers, and cousins were installed as chiefs of cities, commanders of troops, and holders of priestly offices, and each of them commanded a following of his own. A king's authority over such a relative was personal and negotiated, not administrative.

### What made Libyan kingship unstable?

Every senior appointment created a potential rival. Because Libyan authority derived from kinship and personal followings rather than from delegated royal office, a king's son placed in command of a city with troops became an independent power in his own right, and his sons inherited that position rather than holding it at the crown's pleasure.

This is the mechanism that fractured Egypt, and it is worth stating precisely. In a functioning Egyptian administration, a provincial governor held office from the king and could be removed. In the Libyan system, a Great Chief held his position from his lineage and his followers, and the king could remove him only by defeating him. Every generation produced more such figures, each with a legitimate claim to his father's position, and the number of independent power centers therefore increased monotonically.

### The comparison with Egyptian administration

The classic Egyptian state controlled provinces through appointed officials, moved them between posts, and audited them. Nomarchs who turned their offices into hereditary local power bases were the recognized symptom of state decay, and the Old Kingdom's collapse is conventionally explained partly in those terms.

The Libyan system began where the Egyptian system ended. Hereditary local power was not a symptom of breakdown but the normal structure, present from the start. That the Libyan dynasties held Egypt together for as long as they did is therefore more surprising than that they eventually failed to.

### The contrast with classic Egyptian administration

In its foundation rather than its forms. Libyan kings used the full pharaonic apparatus, but their actual authority rested on kinship, personal followings, and armed retainers rather than on an appointed bureaucracy. They governed through relatives who held power in their own right instead of officials who held it from the crown, which made the system militarily robust and administratively fissile.

## The Libyan Army

Since the dynasties rose on military power, the composition and methods of their forces deserve direct treatment.

### The military settlements

The core of Libyan military strength was the settled communities established by the Ramesside state and expanded afterward. These held land by military tenure, meaning that the obligation to serve came with the land, and they were organized under their own chiefs rather than under Egyptian officers.

That structure produced a standing reserve of trained fighting men distributed across the Delta and into Middle Egypt, available to whichever chief they followed. It also meant that Egyptian military capacity was, in practice, the aggregate of many separate followings rather than a single national army answerable to the crown.

### What weapons and tactics did Libyan armies use

Egyptian depictions of Libyans in combat show them with bows, javelins or throwing spears, swords, and shields, wearing cloaks rather than armor. Once settled in Egypt and serving as Egyptian troops, they would have used standard Egyptian military equipment, including the composite bow, bronze weapons, and the khopesh, alongside whatever they retained of their own traditions.

The chariot, which had been the decisive arm of the New Kingdom army and whose introduction is examined in the account of [the chariot and Bronze Age Egypt](/2002/09/01/chariot-and-bronze-age-egypt/), continued in use in this period. Piye's Victory Stela mentions chariotry among the forces of the northern rulers, and horses feature prominently in that text.

What the sources do not indicate is any distinctive Libyan military technology or tactical innovation. Their advantage was organizational and demographic rather than technical: cohesive kin-based units with strong internal loyalty, available in quantity, in a country whose native military establishment had declined.

### Did the Libyans fight to control Egypt

They fought each other constantly and fought outsiders rarely. There is no record of a Libyan war of conquest against Egypt; the dynasties came to power through accumulation and dynastic succession. Once in power, however, Libyan rulers fought repeatedly among themselves over territory, precedence, and the great offices, and those internal conflicts are the period's characteristic form of warfare.

The most fully documented episode is a prolonged conflict at Thebes recorded in inscriptions of a prince Osorkon, generally called the Chronicle of Prince Osorkon, carved on the Bubastite Portal at Karnak. The texts describe repeated intervention in Theban affairs, revolt, suppression, and renewed disorder across many years. Whatever the details, the picture is of a royal house unable to secure the south permanently and returning to force again and again.

### Warfare between the chiefdoms

By the later period, when the Delta held multiple simultaneous rulers, conflict among them was routine. Tefnakht of Sais assembled a coalition and expanded eastward and southward, absorbing or subordinating rival centers, and it was this expansion that triggered the Kushite response.

Piye's stela is the best source for what this warfare looked like, describing sieges of walled towns, river operations, the submission of individual rulers, and shifting alliances among the northern powers. The picture is of small-scale, city by city campaigning between roughly comparable forces, with fortified towns as the principal objectives.

### Why the military system failed against outsiders

A collection of chiefly followings can fight each other indefinitely without producing a force capable of resisting a unified invader. When Piye came north with a single army under a single command, the northern coalition dissolved into a series of individual surrenders, exactly as coalitions of independent rulers with divergent interests tend to do.

The same weakness reappeared under Assyrian invasion a generation later, when Delta rulers switched allegiance repeatedly. Egypt in this period had no national army because it had no national state, and no amount of individual military competence compensated for that.

## The Fragmentation

The break-up of Libyan Egypt is the period's defining process, and it can be traced with reasonable precision.

### The first split

The Twenty-third Dynasty appears in the record as a second royal line, generally understood as a branch of the same Libyan family, ruling simultaneously with the Twenty-second. Its emergence marks the point at which the fiction of a single kingship became impossible to maintain.

The chronology and even the location of this dynasty are contested. Different reconstructions place its principal seat at Leontopolis in the Delta or associate it more closely with Thebes, and the sequence and identity of its kings are debated. What is not in doubt is that from the later ninth or eighth century BCE onward, more than one man was using royal titulary in Egypt at the same time.

### Why did Libyan rule become so fragmented

Because the system produced new independent power centers with every generation. Kings appointed sons and brothers as chiefs of cities, commanders, and high priests, and those positions became hereditary within each branch. Since authority rested on lineage and personal followings rather than on revocable royal office, the crown could not recall what it had granted, and the number of autonomous rulers grew steadily.

### The proliferation of chiefdoms

By the middle of the eighth century BCE the Delta contained a substantial number of independent or semi-independent centers. Great Chiefs of the Ma held Mendes, Sebennytos, Busiris, and other cities. Local rulers held Herakleopolis and Hermopolis in Middle Egypt, controlling stretches of the Nile corridor. Thebes was governed through the Amun establishment.

Piye's stela, listing the rulers who submitted to him, is effectively a census of this fragmentation, and it names a considerable number of distinct authorities across the north. Several used royal names; more used chiefly titles; all were independent enough that submission had to be obtained from each separately.

### Tefnakht and the attempted reunification

Tefnakht of Sais, in the western Delta, was the one figure who came close to reversing the process. He built a coalition, extended his authority across much of the Delta, and pushed south into Middle Egypt, besieging Herakleopolis and threatening the approaches to Upper Egypt.

His expansion was the immediate trigger for Kushite intervention. Piye campaigned north to protect Kushite interests and clients, defeated the coalition, and received Tefnakht's submission. Tefnakht's son Bakenranef is counted as the Twenty-fourth Dynasty and was subsequently defeated by the Kushite king Shabaka, which ended the Saite attempt.

The irony is worth naming. The one Libyan-descended ruler who tried to rebuild a unified Egyptian kingdom was defeated by the Kushites, whose own successful reunification followed within a generation. Tefnakht had the right strategy and insufficient time.

### What ended Libyan rule in Egypt

The Kushite conquest ended Libyan supremacy, though Libyan-descended rulers persisted as local powers for decades afterward. Piye's campaign around 727 BCE subordinated the northern rulers, and Shabaka's defeat of Bakenranef around 716 BCE ended the last independent royal line. The full sequence is set out in the account of [the Nubian pharaohs who ruled Egypt](/2006/01/01/nubian-pharaohs-who-ruled-egypt/).

Libyan-descended dynasts were not eliminated. They continued as vassals under Kushite overlordship and later under Assyrian patronage, and the Saite dynasty that eventually reunified Egypt descended from the same western Delta line as Tefnakht. In that sense Libyan rule did not end so much as change its relationship to the crown, and then, under the Saites, reassert itself successfully.

## Were the Libyans Foreign?

The question of how Egyptians regarded their Libyan rulers is more interesting than it first appears, and the answer complicates the standard categories.

### The case for foreignness

Libyan rulers retained a title, Great Chief of the Ma, that explicitly named a non-Egyptian people. They used personal names that were Libyan in origin and recognizably so. Some depictions preserve the feather ornament associated with Libyans in Egyptian art. Their political structure, resting on kin groups and personal followings, was not an Egyptian form.

Later Egyptian tradition also treated the period as distinct. The dynastic scheme separates the Libyan dynasties as their own lines, and Egyptian memory of the era, insofar as it can be recovered, does not present it as a golden age.

### The case against

By the time Shoshenq I took the throne, his family had been in Egypt for centuries. They worshipped Egyptian gods, built Egyptian temples, wrote in Egyptian, held Egyptian priestly offices, married Egyptian elite families, and were buried in Egyptian style with Egyptian funerary equipment. Their accession followed Egyptian dynastic convention rather than conquest.

No Egyptian revolt against Libyan rule as foreign rule is recorded. The conflicts of the period are between Libyan-descended rulers, not between Egyptians and Libyans. The Theban troubles documented in the Chronicle of Prince Osorkon concern political control and succession, not ethnic resistance.

### The Kushite accusation

The sharpest evidence comes from the Kushites, who attacked the legitimacy of the Libyan dynasts in explicitly religious terms. Piye's Victory Stela stages the northern rulers as ritually deficient and the Kushite king as scrupulously correct, and it is on that basis rather than on ancestry that the Kushite claim to superiority rests.

That is revealing on both sides. A newly arrived Nubian dynasty accused a Delta elite settled for centuries of insufficient Egyptian authenticity, and the accusation was framed as a matter of religious practice rather than descent. Legitimacy in Egypt was performative: it depended on doing the right things correctly, not on where a family had come from.

### Were the Libyan pharaohs considered foreign

They were recognized as being of Libyan descent, since they said so themselves in their titles, but they were not treated as foreign occupiers. No revolt against them as foreigners is recorded, they held every Egyptian office including the priesthoods, and Egyptian identity in this period rested on religious and cultural practice rather than on ancestry.

### Comparison with other foreign rulers

Set against the Hyksos or the Kushites, the Libyan case is distinctive because there was no conquest and no moment of arrival to remember. The Hyksos rule of the north ended in a war of expulsion that later Egyptian tradition remembered as liberation, a story examined in the account of [the Hyksos and the Second Intermediate Age](/2002/07/01/hyksos-second-intermediate-period/). The Kushites arrived with an army in living memory.

The Libyans simply accumulated. There was no invasion to resent, no expulsion to celebrate, and no clear point at which Egypt could be said to have fallen to them. The broader pattern of how Egypt absorbed and redefined foreign rulers is treated in the study of [foreign rule and Egyptian identity](/2002/12/01/foreign-rule-and-egyptian-identity/).

## What the Libyan Period Built

The military and political story can obscure the fact that these were substantial reigns that left substantial monuments.

### Tanis and the royal tombs

Tanis in the northeastern Delta served as a principal royal residence and burial place. The royal tombs there, built within the temple precinct rather than in a separate necropolis, were found containing burials of Twenty-first and Twenty-second Dynasty kings that had escaped the comprehensive plundering suffered by the Theban royal tombs.

The contents included silver coffins, gold funerary masks, jewelry, and vessels of high quality. The silver coffin of Shoshenq II, with a falcon head, is among the more remarkable objects to survive from Egypt. These burials establish that the Libyan monarchy commanded genuine wealth and employed craftsmen of the first rank, which is not what a period conventionally described as decline would predict.

Much of the stone used at Tanis was moved from earlier sites, and the city contains a great deal of reused Ramesside material. That practice has been read as evidence of poverty, but it can equally be read as the ordinary Egyptian habit of quarrying older monuments, which every period practiced.

### Building at Bubastis and Karnak

Bubastis, the dynasty's ancestral seat, received a major temple of the goddess Bastet with a festival hall built by Osorkon II, decorated with scenes of a jubilee celebration. The reliefs are among the more informative surviving depictions of royal festival ritual.

At Karnak, Libyan kings added to the great precinct, and the Bubastite Portal carries both Shoshenq I's campaign relief and the Chronicle of Prince Osorkon. Building at the principal temple of Amun was the standard way an Egyptian king demonstrated legitimacy, and the Libyan dynasts did it consistently. The complex they added to is described in the account of [the temple of Karnak at Thebes](/2003/09/01/temple-of-karnak-at-thebes/).

### Bronze work and craft

The period produced metalwork of exceptional quality, including inlaid bronze statuary of which the figure of the God's Wife Karomama is the outstanding surviving example. The technical accomplishment of these pieces is high by any standard and represents a genuine specialty of the era.

The wider artistic character of the fragmented centuries, including the archaizing revival and the fine bronzes, is examined in the account of [art and revival in a fragmented Egypt](/2006/05/01/art-revival-fragmented-egypt/).

### The Apis burials and institutional continuity

The burial of the Apis bulls at Saqqara continued through the period and generated dated records that are among the more useful chronological sources for the era. The maintenance of that cult across political fragmentation illustrates a general pattern: religious institutions carried on regardless of how many kings claimed the throne, and the temple establishments were the element of continuity that held Egyptian civilization together while the political structure came apart.

## Chronology and Its Problems

Dating the Libyan period is unusually difficult, and a reader should understand why reputable sources give different numbers.

### The overlap problem

Egyptian dynastic numbering, derived from the scheme of Manetho, presents dynasties as consecutive. For this period they were not. The Twenty-second, Twenty-third, and Twenty-fourth Dynasties overlap substantially, and all three overlap the Twenty-fifth. Adding their lengths in sequence produces a chronology hundreds of years too long.

Reconstructing the real sequence requires establishing which kings were contemporary, which is done through genealogies of priestly families, dated documents, the Apis burial records, and synchronisms with dated events outside Egypt. The results are workable but not tight, and different scholars produce different arrangements.

### The Shoshenq problem

Multiple kings named Shoshenq reigned during the period, and assigning particular monuments and dated documents to the correct one is a persistent difficulty. The same applies to the Osorkons and the Takelots. Numbering conventions differ between reconstructions, so a Shoshenq III in one account may not be the same king as a Shoshenq III in another.

This is not a trivial cataloguing issue. It affects the reconstruction of the dynastic sequence, the dating of the fragmentation, and the interpretation of the Theban conflicts, and it is the reason chronologies of the period continue to be revised.

### The anchors that do exist

Several fixed points constrain the reconstruction. The Shishak synchronism, if the identification with Shoshenq I holds, ties his campaign to the reign of Rehoboam and provides a link to the chronology of the Levantine kingdoms. Assyrian records supply firm dates for the later part of the period, particularly for the Kushite and Assyrian conflicts. The Apis burial records give regnal dates for a series of reigns.

Genealogical documents, notably a long priestly genealogy from Memphis, allow generations to be counted back and provide independent constraint on the sequence.

### How to state Libyan dates responsibly

The reasonable formulation is that the Twenty-second Dynasty was founded around the middle of the tenth century BCE, that a rival royal line emerged in the later ninth or eighth century, that fragmentation intensified through the eighth century, and that Kushite supremacy displaced Libyan royal authority in the later eighth century. Precise regnal dates for individual kings should be given with the acknowledgment that they rest on a contested reconstruction.

The king-list evidence itself has limitations that apply across Egyptian history, discussed in the account of [what the Turin King List tells us](/2003/02/01/turin-king-list-explained/), and the Third Intermediate Period is where those limitations bite hardest.

## Sources for the Libyan Period

The evidence base is distinctive and its shape explains why the period is understood as it is.

### Royal monuments

Building inscriptions, temple reliefs, and stelae from Karnak, Bubastis, Tanis, and elsewhere supply the framework of reigns, campaigns, and royal activity. The Bubastite Portal at Karnak is the single most important monument, carrying both the Shoshenq I campaign relief with its topographical list and the Chronicle of Prince Osorkon.

These are royal texts with the usual limitations. Campaign records claim success, building texts claim piety, and neither reports difficulties. The Chronicle of Prince Osorkon is unusual in that it records repeated setbacks, which makes it more informative than most royal inscriptions and also raises questions about why a text of that character was carved at all.

### Burial evidence

The intact royal tombs at Tanis are the outstanding archaeological source, providing direct evidence of royal wealth, craft standards, and burial practice. The Apis burials at Saqqara supply dated records. Private burials across the Delta and at Thebes fill out the picture of elite society.

The Delta's archaeology is generally poorer than Upper Egypt's because of the water table, later occupation, and the destruction of mud-brick settlement, which means the region where the Libyan dynasties were based is systematically less well documented than the region they struggled to control.

### Genealogies and priestly records

Priestly families recorded their descent, sometimes across many generations, and these documents are among the most valuable sources for reconstructing the period's chronology and its social structure. They also show how thoroughly Libyan-descended families had integrated into the Egyptian religious establishment.

### External sources

Assyrian royal inscriptions provide contemporary external evidence for the later part of the period. Biblical texts supply the Shishak account, which is the earliest external narrative touching an Egyptian king of the era, though its relationship to the Egyptian record is debated. Piye's Victory Stela, a Kushite document, is the single best description of the political landscape at the end of the period, and it survives precisely because a hostile outsider found the fragmentation worth cataloguing.

### What is missing

There is no administrative archive. Settlement archaeology in the Delta is limited. Nothing survives from the Libyan population's own perspective before its Egyptianization, and no source describes the settlement process from the settlers' side. The internal politics of the chiefdoms are visible only through the eyes of kings and conquerors, and ordinary experience during the period is nearly invisible.

## Misconceptions About the Libyan Period

Several standard characterizations do not survive contact with the evidence.

### That the Libyans conquered Egypt

There was no Libyan conquest. The New Kingdom defeated Libyan invasions decisively and settled the defeated as military colonists. Two centuries later a family descended from those settlers took the throne through dynastic succession, with a marriage connection to the outgoing line and no recorded fighting. The Libyan dynasties came to power from inside Egypt, not from outside it.

### That Libyan rule was a foreign occupation

The Libyan kings held every Egyptian office, built and endowed Egyptian temples, married Egyptian elite families, and were buried in Egyptian style. No Egyptian revolt against them as foreigners is recorded, and the conflicts of the period were among Libyan-descended rulers rather than between Egyptians and Libyans.

### That the period was uniformly weak and impoverished

The Tanis royal burials contain silver coffins, gold masks, and jewelry of exceptional quality. Shoshenq I mounted the most substantial Egyptian campaign into the Levant in centuries. Building continued at Karnak, Bubastis, and elsewhere. Bronze statuary of the period is technically outstanding. The political fragmentation was real, but treating the era as materially impoverished misreads the evidence.

### That fragmentation was caused by weak kings

Individual competence varied, but the fragmentation was structural. A political system in which authority rested on kinship and personal followings, and in which senior appointments became hereditary within branches of the ruling family, produces new independent power centers automatically. Stronger kings slowed the process; none of them could reverse it, because doing so would have required dismantling the basis of their own authority.

### That the Libyans were culturally destructive

The opposite is closer to the truth. Temple institutions continued, the Apis cult was maintained, craft traditions produced work of the first rank, and the archaizing artistic movement that would flourish under the Kushites and Saites had begun. The dynasties were consistent patrons of Egyptian religion, which is precisely what their legitimacy required.

### That Libyan rule ended abruptly with the Kushites

Kushite supremacy ended Libyan royal authority, but Libyan-descended rulers continued as local powers under Kushite and then Assyrian overlordship. The Saite dynasty that eventually reunified Egypt and presided over its Late Period revival descended from the same western Delta line as Tefnakht. In the long view, Libyan-descended families lost the crown in the eighth century BCE and recovered it in the seventh.

## The Segmentary State: A Closer Analysis

The single most useful way to understand the Libyan period is as a state built on a different organizational principle from the classic Egyptian model, and the difference repays a careful look.

### Two models of authority

Egyptian kingship at its functioning height worked through delegated office. The king appointed a vizier, nomarchs, treasury officials, and army commanders; those men held their positions from him; they could be transferred, promoted, or dismissed; and their authority derived from the office rather than from themselves. Records were kept, accounts were audited, and the apparatus outlasted individuals.

Libyan authority worked through personal allegiance within kin groups. A chief commanded men who followed him because of who he was and what he had given them. His authority was portable in the sense that it went where he went, and it was inheritable in the sense that his son had a strong claim on the same following. It could not be revoked from above without a fight.

### Why the two combined at all

The Libyan kings needed both. Pharaonic legitimacy gave them the right to rule Egypt: the temples, the priesthoods, the administrative traditions, and the population's acceptance all depended on the king being a proper Egyptian king. Chiefly authority gave them the armed force that made rule possible in a country whose native military establishment had decayed.

Neither alone would have sufficed. A Libyan chief without pharaonic legitimacy could hold a Delta town but not Egypt. An Egyptian king without a Libyan following had no army. The Twenty-second Dynasty's founder held both, and the arrangement worked for as long as one man could plausibly claim both.

### The mathematics of fission

The failure mode is predictable once the structure is described. Suppose a king has three sons and places them in charge of three cities with troops. Each is now a chief with a following. Each of their sons inherits a claim. Within three generations there are potentially nine such figures, each with a legitimate position, each commanding men, and each with grounds to consider himself as entitled as the man on the throne.

Egyptian administrative kingship handled this by making offices non-heritable and rotating officeholders. Libyan kingship could not, because the whole basis of the family's power was the inheritance of chiefly authority. Removing heritability would have destroyed the mechanism that had put them on the throne in the first place.

### Why nobody fixed it

It is worth asking why no Libyan king simply reformed the system. The answer is that the reform would have required disarming his own relatives, and his relatives were his army. A king who dismantled the chiefly structure would have had no force with which to enforce the dismantling.

There is also no sign that anyone at the time analyzed the problem in these terms. Contemporaries saw specific conflicts with specific rivals, not a structural tendency. The pattern is visible in retrospect because the whole sequence can be seen at once.

### The comparison with later Egypt

The Saites, descended from the same Libyan stock, eventually reunified Egypt and governed it effectively. What they did differently was build a professional army, including foreign mercenaries who had no local kin base and depended entirely on royal pay, and rebuild a centralized administration. In other words, they abandoned the segmentary structure and returned to the Egyptian model, funded by trade and by the wealth of a reunified country.

That contrast is the clearest demonstration that the problem was structural rather than ethnic. The same families, organized differently, produced a functioning centralized state. The organization was the variable.

## What the Libyan Centuries Changed

The period altered several things that persisted long after Libyan royal authority ended.

### It normalized foreign descent in the Egyptian monarchy

Before the Libyan dynasties, foreign rule in Egypt meant the Hyksos, remembered as an occupation ended by a war of liberation. After two centuries of Libyan-descended pharaohs who built temples, endowed cults, and were buried as Egyptian kings, the proposition that a king of non-Egyptian ancestry could be a legitimate pharaoh was established in practice.

The Kushites benefited directly from that precedent. Their claim rested on religious correctness rather than descent, and it was accepted, in a country that had spent two hundred years accepting kings who called themselves Great Chief of the Ma.

### It shifted Egypt's center north permanently

Thebes had been the religious and often the political heart of the New Kingdom. The Libyan dynasties governed from Bubastis, Tanis, and other Delta centers, and every subsequent Egyptian regime governed from the north: the Saites from Sais, the Persians through Memphis, the Ptolemies from Alexandria. Thebes remained religiously significant and politically peripheral.

### It made the temple establishment the element of continuity

Through two centuries of political fission, the temples continued. Cults were maintained, priesthoods filled, festivals celebrated, and the Apis bulls buried on schedule. Egyptian civilization persisted through the fragmentation because its institutional carrier was religious rather than political, a pattern examined in the account of [temples as power centers in late Egypt](/2006/07/01/temples-as-power-centers-egypt/).

### It set up the Kushite intervention

Tefnakht's expansion, itself a product of the fragmentation, drew Piye north. Without a divided Delta and a Saite ruler trying to unify it by force, there would have been no occasion for a Kushite campaign. The Libyan period created the conditions for its own ending.

### It produced the dynasty that would restore Egypt

The Saite line that reunified Egypt, sponsored the Late Period revival, and gave the country its last long era of native rule descended from the western Delta Libyan elite. The eventual beneficiary of the Libyan settlement was the Libyan-descended Twenty-sixth Dynasty, arriving three centuries after the first Libyan colonists were settled by Ramesside kings.

## The Honest Verdict

Three conclusions hold against the evidence.

The Libyan dynasties were legitimate Egyptian kings, not occupiers. They came to power from inside the country through dynastic succession, held every Egyptian office, patronized the temples consistently, produced monuments and craft of high quality, and faced no recorded resistance framed as opposition to foreign rule. Any account that treats the period as an alien interruption misdescribes what happened.

Their military structure was genuinely effective and genuinely destabilizing, and both halves of that matter. Libyan military settlements gave Egypt a fighting force when its own had decayed, and gave the Twenty-second Dynasty the strength to take and hold the throne. The same structure, resting on hereditary chiefly authority and personal followings, multiplied independent power centers with every generation until Egypt contained several simultaneous kings and a larger number of chiefs. The strength and the weakness were the same feature seen from different angles.

The fragmentation was structural, not a failure of individual kings. Shoshenq I campaigned successfully in the Levant and installed his relatives across the country; his arrangements produced the rivals who split it. No Libyan king could have solved the problem without dismantling the basis of his own power. The Saites eventually solved it by building a professional army and a centralized administration, which is to say by ceasing to govern as Libyan chiefs.

The warrior-chief-to-pharaoh thesis captures the whole arc. Defeated invaders were settled as soldiers, their descendants became the military class, one family took the crown, and for two centuries Egypt was ruled by men who were simultaneously pharaohs and tribal chiefs. That combination gave them the throne and then took the country apart, because a kingdom built on chiefs produces more chiefs, and a country with many chiefs has no king.

## Studying the Libyan Dynasties

For a student, this period rewards structure over detail, because the detail is genuinely tangled.

The essential sequence runs from the Ramesside wars and the settlement of defeated Libyans in Delta military colonies, through the accession of Shoshenq I around 945 BCE and his Levantine campaign, to the emergence of the rival Twenty-third Dynasty, the proliferation of Delta chiefdoms, Tefnakht's attempted reunification, and the Kushite intervention that ended Libyan supremacy.

The essential argument is the warrior-chief-to-pharaoh thesis, and the mechanism to be able to explain is why hereditary chiefly authority multiplies power centers where delegated royal office does not. The essential evidence to name is the retention of the title Great Chief of the Ma alongside pharaonic titulary, the Bubastite Portal at Karnak for the campaign and for the Chronicle of Prince Osorkon, the Tanis royal burials for the wealth of the monarchy, and Piye's Victory Stela for the political landscape at the end.

The essential caution is chronological: the dynasties overlap, the Shoshenq and Osorkon numbering differs between reconstructions, and any set of precise regnal dates for this period rests on a contested arrangement. A candidate who can explain the structural cause of the fragmentation and name those four pieces of evidence is well equipped for any question the topic is likely to produce. ## The Delta in the Libyan Age

Because the period's political center was the Delta, and because the Delta is poorly served by the standard narrative of Egyptian history, the region deserves its own treatment.

### A landscape of cities

The Nile Delta is not a valley but a fan of branches spreading across a wide alluvial plain, historically with several major distributaries rather than the two that survive. That geography produces a different political pattern from the Nile Valley proper. In the valley, a single river corridor means that whoever controls the river controls movement, and power tends to concentrate. In the Delta, multiple branches, marshland, and a broad cultivable plain support many centers, none of which naturally dominates the others.

The cities that mattered in this period were distributed across that fan: Tanis and Bubastis in the east, Mendes and Sebennytos in the center, Busiris on one of the central branches, and Sais toward the west. Each sat on a branch with its own hinterland, its own temple, and its own agricultural base.

### Why the Delta favored fragmentation

Geography reinforced the segmentary political structure. A chief holding a Delta city with its branch of the river and its surrounding farmland had a viable independent economic base and no compelling need to submit to a neighbor. Controlling him required a campaign through marshy country against a fortified town, which was expensive and slow.

The valley south of Memphis has no comparable feature. There, a ruler controlling the river at any point controls traffic through the whole corridor, and the towns lie strung along a single line rather than dispersed across a plain. That is why Upper Egypt in this period was governed as a unit through Thebes while the Delta was not governed as a unit at all.

### The economy of the Delta

The Delta was agriculturally rich, with better rainfall than Upper Egypt, extensive pasture, and access to the Mediterranean. Its cattle herds were substantial, its vineyards notable, and its fisheries productive. It also sat astride the routes to the Levant, which made its eastern cities points of contact with Asiatic trade.

That wealth is part of why the Libyan military settlements prospered and why the chiefdoms they produced were viable. A chief in the Delta had resources; a chief in a marginal region would have had to accept a patron.

### The archaeological difficulty

Understanding this period suffers from a systematic evidentiary problem: Delta archaeology is much harder than valley archaeology. The water table is high, mud-brick settlement decays or is dug away for fertilizer, alluvial deposition buries early levels under meters of silt, and modern population density limits excavation.

The consequence is that the political heartland of the Libyan dynasties is the part of Egypt we know least about, while Thebes, which they struggled to control from a distance, is the best-documented site in the country. That imbalance shapes every account of the period, including this one, and it means the Theban perspective is overrepresented in the sources relative to its actual political weight at the time.

## Life Under Libyan Rule

The period's ordinary experience is difficult to recover, but several things can be said with reasonable confidence.

### Continuity in the temples

Temple institutions ran continuously. Priests were appointed, offerings made, festivals celebrated, and endowments maintained across the whole period regardless of how many kings claimed the throne. For most people, whose contact with the state was mediated through the local temple rather than through a royal administration, this continuity would have been the dominant experience.

The Apis burials at Saqqara continued on schedule. Theban festivals continued. The rhythm of religious life that structured the Egyptian year did not break, and that is a substantial part of why Egyptian civilization survived a period of political disintegration with its culture intact.

### The military class

The Libyan-descended military settlements formed a distinct social stratum, holding land by service obligation and organized under their own leaders. For the men in those communities, the period offered opportunity: military service was a route to land, status, and in exceptional cases to very high office indeed.

Their households appear to have been integrated into Egyptian life in most respects while retaining a distinct identity marked by naming practices and titles. What that felt like from inside is not recorded, but the persistence of Libyan names across many generations suggests a group that maintained a sense of itself.

### The costs of fragmentation

Political division had real costs. Warfare between chiefdoms meant sieges, disrupted harvests, and interrupted trade along the river. Piye's stela describes towns under siege and populations in distress, and while it is a hostile source, the general picture of a country in which armed men fought over cities is unlikely to be invented.

Long-distance trade and large state projects both suffer when no single authority spans the country. Egyptian activity abroad contracted sharply from New Kingdom levels, and nothing on the scale of the great Ramesside building programs was attempted.

### What did not collapse

Against those costs should be set what continued. Craft production remained excellent, as the bronzes and the Tanis burials show. Literacy and scribal training persisted. Temple estates continued to function as economic institutions. Agricultural production, which depended on the Nile rather than on the state, went on as it always had, a relationship examined in the account of [how the Nile built the Egyptian state](/2000/08/01/how-the-nile-built-egyptian-state/).

The Third Intermediate Period is often described as a dark age, and whether that label is deserved is a question with a genuine debate behind it, taken up in the comparison of [whether the Third Intermediate Period was a dark age](/2006/08/01/third-intermediate-a-dark-age/). What the Libyan evidence contributes to that debate is a picture of political disintegration accompanied by cultural and institutional continuity, which is not the same thing as collapse.

## The Thebes Problem

Every Libyan king faced the same difficulty in the south, and the sequence of attempted solutions is instructive.

### Why Thebes could not be ignored

Thebes held the temple of Amun, the god whose endorsement legitimated Egyptian kingship, together with the largest concentrated religious wealth in the country and a priestly establishment with its own institutional continuity. A king who did not control Thebes was a king whose legitimacy could be questioned by whoever did.

The city was also six hundred kilometers upstream of the Delta capitals, in a region the Libyan military settlements did not cover. Force could be projected there, but not maintained cheaply, and the local establishment had every incentive to preserve its autonomy.

### The appointment strategy

Shoshenq I's answer was to install his son as High Priest of Amun, and successive kings followed the same approach. It worked in the short term and failed structurally for exactly the reason discussed above: a royal son with the Amun establishment behind him became a power in his own right, and his descendants inherited the position.

The Chronicle of Prince Osorkon documents what that failure looked like in practice. The texts describe repeated intervention at Thebes, the suppression of opposition, renewed disorder, and further intervention, across a long span of years. A royal house that must reconquer its own southern capital periodically has not solved its problem.

### The God's Wife solution

The eventual answer, developed across the Libyan and later periods, was to shift authority at Thebes to the God's Wife of Amun, a celibate royal daughter who could hold the establishment without founding a rival line. Libyan royal women held the office, and Shepenwepet I, daughter of Osorkon III, is the holder under whom it visibly acquires quasi-royal status.

This was the one durable solution anyone found to the Theban problem, and it is worth noting that it worked by removing heritability, which is precisely the feature the Libyan system could not remove from its own structure. Kings could impose non-heritability on an office they created; they could not impose it on the chiefly authority that underpinned their own throne.

### What Thebes did in the meantime

The Theban establishment itself was not passive. Its priestly families held office across generations, intermarried, and accumulated wealth. When Kushite influence arrived, Thebes accommodated it; when Assyrian armies came, the city endured a sack and continued; when the Saites took over, the same officials remained in place.

That resilience made Thebes both indispensable and ungovernable from a distance. The city did not need a king in order to function, which is exactly why kings found it so difficult to control.

## The Period in the Longer Arc

Placing the Libyan dynasties in the sequence of Egyptian history clarifies what was and was not unusual about them.

### Against earlier intermediate periods

Egypt had fragmented before. The First Intermediate Period followed the Old Kingdom's collapse and produced competing centers at Herakleopolis and Thebes before reunification. The Second Intermediate Period saw the Hyksos in the north and a Theban kingdom in the south.

The Third Intermediate Period differs from both in an important respect: it lasted far longer and produced far more simultaneous authorities. The earlier fragmentations resolved into two-way contests that one side eventually won. This one dissolved into a multiplicity that no internal party could reassemble, and reunification came from outside, first from Nubia and then from a Delta line backed by Assyria.

### Against the New Kingdom

The contrast with the preceding period is stark in political terms and much less so in cultural ones. The New Kingdom was a centralized empire with an army projecting force into the Levant and Nubia, funded by tribute and gold, a system described in the account of [gold, tribute, and the Egyptian empire](/2003/12/01/gold-tribute-egyptian-empire/). The Libyan period had none of that.

But temples were still built, texts still copied, craftsmen still trained, and gods still served. The apparatus of Egyptian civilization survived the loss of the empire, which suggests the civilization was never as dependent on imperial success as its royal monuments implied.

### Against what followed

The Kushite and then Saite periods restored unity and produced a genuine cultural revival, with the archaizing movement flowering into the distinctive art of the Late Period. Both regimes built on foundations the Libyan period had laid: the precedent of foreign-descended legitimate kingship, the God's Wife mechanism for controlling Thebes, the northward shift of the political center, and the archaizing artistic direction.

The Libyan centuries look, from that angle, less like an interruption than like a transitional phase in which the political forms of the New Kingdom were abandoned and the forms that would define the Late Period were assembled. That is a more useful way to read them than as decline between two peaks.

## The Ramesside Wars in Detail

The military background deserves fuller treatment, because the shape of those wars determined the shape of the settlement that followed.

### The western fortresses

Ramesses II built a chain of installations along the coast west of the Delta, extending a considerable distance toward the Libyan coast. These were not simply watchtowers; excavated examples show enclosed forts with storage, wells, and accommodation, positioned along a route rather than clustered at a single frontier point.

The infrastructure tells us something the inscriptions do not. Building and supplying a chain of forts across waterless country is expensive, and no state does it for a nuisance. Ramesside kings expected sustained pressure from the west and invested accordingly, well before the great invasions of the following reigns.

### Merneptah's war

The invasion recorded under Merneptah involved a Libyan chief leading a large movement into the western Delta in coalition with groups from the sea. Egyptian accounts describe a battle, claim enormous enemy casualties, and record the capture of very large numbers of prisoners along with livestock and equipment.

Two details in the Egyptian record matter more than the casualty claims. First, the invaders brought families and herds, which marks this as attempted settlement rather than raiding. Second, the coalition structure indicates that Libyan chiefs could combine with other groups for a common objective, which is a level of political coordination that a purely fragmented tribal landscape would not produce.

### Ramesses III and the second wave

Ramesses III recorded further Libyan conflicts on the walls of his mortuary temple at Medinet Habu, in reliefs and texts that constitute one of the largest surviving bodies of Egyptian military documentation. He claims decisive victories and the capture of large numbers.

The temple reliefs show captives being branded and assigned, and the texts describe settling prisoners in strongholds under the king's name. That is the settlement policy stated explicitly, in a royal monument, as a form of triumph. Egypt was not reluctantly accommodating a defeated enemy; it was announcing the incorporation of that enemy's manpower into its own military system as a demonstration of royal power.

### What the numbers can and cannot tell us

Egyptian casualty and prisoner figures are unreliable. Inflation of enemy losses was standard, the counting methods described in the texts are open to question, and comparison between sources produces inconsistencies. Any specific total should be treated as an order-of-magnitude indication at best.

What the figures do reliably indicate is that the events were large. A state does not construct extensive victory monuments over minor skirmishes, and the settlement policy that followed implies a population substantial enough to be worth distributing across multiple locations. The Libyan presence in the Delta after these wars was numerically significant, and the subsequent political history confirms it.

### The pattern across the Ramesside period

Taken together, the western fortresses, the two great invasions, and the settlement policy form a coherent sequence. Egypt faced sustained westward pressure across several reigns, defeated the resulting invasions militarily, and converted the defeated into a component of its own military system rather than removing them.

That was a rational policy with a foreseeable cost, and Egypt was not the only ancient state to make the same trade. The cost was realized slowly, across two centuries, and by the time it arrived nobody could have connected it to decisions made under Merneptah.

## The Question of Numbers and Settlement

How many Libyans actually settled in Egypt, and where, is a question that bears directly on how the dynasties came to power.

### The distribution of settlement

Textual and onomastic evidence points to Libyan settlement concentrated in the Delta, particularly the western and central regions, with a presence extending into Middle Egypt and as far south as Herakleopolis. Personal names of Libyan origin appear in documents from these areas in numbers that increase across the Third Intermediate Period, and the same names recur in temple appointments and in the titles of local officials, which indicates settlement that had become permanent and socially embedded rather than a garrison presence.

The pattern makes military sense. Settlements were placed where the threat had come from and where the manpower would be needed, which is to say along the western approaches and across the Delta. It also placed them, unintentionally, in the region that would become the political center of Egypt once the New Kingdom's Theban orientation faded.

### Estimating the scale

No census exists, and any figure is an inference. The indicators available are the prisoner numbers in Ramesside inscriptions, which are unreliable but large; the geographic spread of Libyan personal names; the number of distinct Libyan chiefdoms visible in later sources; and the military capacity those chiefdoms could evidently field.

The reasonable conclusion is that the Libyan-descended population was large enough to constitute a distinct and militarily dominant social stratum across the Delta, without being large enough to displace the Egyptian population or alter the region's language and culture. Egyptian remained the language; Egyptian religion remained the religion; Libyan identity persisted as a matter of descent, title, and military role.

### The military tenure system

Land granted in exchange for service obligation is a system with well-understood dynamics. It converts soldiers into landholders with a stake in the country, which produces loyalty and reduces the cash cost of maintaining a force. It also produces a hereditary military class whose obligations and privileges pass to sons, and whose leaders control both land and armed men.

Once such a class exists, the state's ability to command it depends on the state's own strength. A strong central authority directs it; a weak one negotiates with it; and a collapsing one is replaced by it. Egypt after the New Kingdom moved through all three positions in roughly two centuries.

### Why the settlers kept their identity

Assimilation was extensive in religion, language, and material culture, and negligible in the areas that mattered politically. The reason appears to be structural rather than sentimental. Libyan chiefly authority was the basis of the settlers' military organization, and their military organization was the basis of their land tenure and status. Abandoning the chiefly structure would have meant dissolving into the general Egyptian population and losing exactly the privileges that made the community worth belonging to.

Identity persisted, in other words, because it was institutionally load-bearing. That is a general pattern in settled military populations across many societies, and Egypt's Libyans followed it closely.

## The Later Kings and the Slow Unravelling

The reigns between Shoshenq I and the Kushite arrival are individually obscure but collectively tell a clear story, and it is worth following.

### Osorkon I and the inherited position

Shoshenq I's successor inherited a functioning arrangement: family members in the great offices, Thebes under a royal High Priest, and a treasury enriched by the Levantine campaign. Records of large donations of precious metal to temples are associated with his reign, which fits a period of unusual liquidity.

What the reign did not do was change the structure. The relatives placed in office by the founder had by now produced their own heirs, each with a claim on his father's position, and the process of multiplication was already running.

### Osorkon II and the jubilee

Osorkon II built the festival hall at Bubastis whose reliefs record a royal jubilee, one of the more informative surviving depictions of that ceremony. A king celebrating a jubilee is a king asserting that his rule has been long and legitimate, and the monument is a substantial one.

His reign also shows the strains. Arrangements for the succession and for the distribution of offices among his sons appear in the record, and the pattern of dividing responsibilities among royal relatives continues, with the consequences that pattern reliably produces.

### The Theban conflicts

The Chronicle of Prince Osorkon, carved at Karnak, is the fullest surviving account of the period's internal politics. It describes a royal son intervening at Thebes, imposing order, facing renewed opposition, and returning repeatedly across a span of years.

The document is exceptional in Egyptian royal inscription for admitting difficulty at all. Most such texts record only success. That this one records repeated cycles of revolt and suppression suggests either that the difficulties were too well known to omit or that the text's purpose was to justify severity rather than to claim triumph.

Either way, the substance is clear: the royal house could not hold Thebes without periodic force, and force did not settle the question permanently.

### The proliferation

By the eighth century BCE the situation had passed the point where a royal house could plausibly claim national authority. Two royal lines used cartouches simultaneously. Great Chiefs held Delta cities as independent powers. Local rulers held Middle Egyptian centers. Thebes was governed through its own establishment.

Individual reigns in this phase are difficult to date and sometimes difficult to place, and the chronological reconstructions differ. What is not in doubt is the direction of travel: each generation produced more autonomous authorities than the last, and no mechanism existed to reverse the process.

### The view from Piye's stela

The Kushite Victory Stela offers a snapshot of the endpoint. It names rulers with royal titulary and chiefs without, describes them submitting individually, and depicts a political landscape in which the largest single authority, Tefnakht, had assembled a coalition rather than a kingdom.

Reading that document alongside the situation at Shoshenq I's accession two centuries earlier gives the clearest possible measure of what happened. One king with relatives in the great offices became a dozen authorities negotiating with each other, and the transformation happened without any external cause. It was internal, structural, and steady.

## Why This Period Is Worth Studying

A reader might reasonably ask why two centuries of political fragmentation in a poorly documented era deserve attention, and there are three good answers.

### It explains what came next

The Kushite conquest, the Assyrian invasions, the Saite restoration, and the whole configuration of the Late Period follow directly from conditions the Libyan centuries created. A fragmented Delta drew in Kush; Kushite intervention drew in Assyria; Assyrian patronage produced the Saites. None of that sequence is intelligible without understanding why Egypt had come apart in the first place.

### It shows how legitimacy actually worked in Egypt

Two hundred years of pharaohs who advertised non-Egyptian descent, held every office, and faced no ethnic revolt is decisive evidence about the nature of Egyptian identity and Egyptian kingship. Legitimacy rested on performing the role correctly, funding the temples, and serving the gods. Ancestry was, at most, a secondary consideration.

That conclusion has consequences well beyond this period. It explains why the Kushites could rule, why the Ptolemies invested so heavily in temple patronage, and why Egyptian priesthoods accommodated a long sequence of foreign regimes with comparatively little friction.

### It is a case study in institutional failure

The Libyan dynasties failed for a reason that can be stated precisely and that recurs across many societies: an authority structure based on inheritable personal followings cannot be centralized without destroying itself. Watching that dynamic run its full course across two centuries, with the endpoints documented, is genuinely instructive.

The contrast with the Saite solution completes the lesson. The same population, organized around a paid professional army and a centralized administration rather than around chiefly followings, produced a strong unified state within a generation. The variable was structure, not people, and that is the finding worth carrying away.

## Religion Under the Libyan Kings

Since legitimacy in Egypt was religious, the Libyan dynasties' relationship with the temples is central rather than incidental.

### Patronage as policy

Libyan kings endowed temples, appointed priests, restored monuments, and had themselves depicted performing the standard royal rites. Records of large donations survive, and the building record at Karnak, Bubastis, Tanis, and elsewhere is substantial for a period conventionally described as one of decline.

This was not optional. An Egyptian king's function was to maintain the cults, and a ruler who failed to do so had no claim on the office. For a dynasty whose ancestry could be questioned, conspicuous piety was the strongest available argument, and the Libyan kings made it consistently across two centuries.

### The problem of the priesthoods

Patronage created its own difficulty. The great priesthoods, particularly the High Priesthood of Amun at Thebes and the senior offices at Memphis, carried enormous wealth and standing. Filling them with royal relatives kept them in the family and simultaneously created powerful figures with independent bases.

The result was that the same office could be a mechanism of royal control in one generation and a source of rivalry in the next. The Theban High Priesthood is the clearest case, but the pattern recurs across the religious establishment.

### Local cults and Delta gods

The Libyan dynasties gave particular attention to Delta cults, notably Bastet at Bubastis, their ancestral seat. That focus reflects both the political geography of the period and the ordinary Egyptian practice of a dynasty promoting the deity of its home city.

Delta temples are less well preserved than those of Upper Egypt for the archaeological reasons discussed earlier, which means the religious activity of the period is systematically underrepresented in the surviving record. What survives at Bubastis and Tanis indicates substantial investment, and there is no reason to think those sites were exceptional.

### The Apis cult and continuity

Burial of the Apis bulls at Saqqara continued throughout the period, generating dated records that serve as a chronological backbone for modern reconstruction. Maintaining that cult required royal involvement and substantial resources, and its uninterrupted continuation across a fragmenting political landscape is a strong indicator of institutional resilience.

The general point holds beyond the Apis. Egyptian religious institutions carried on through the fragmentation with their calendars, personnel, and endowments largely intact, and that continuity is why Egyptian civilization emerged from two centuries of political division without a cultural rupture.

### The Amun establishment's own trajectory

Meanwhile the Amun establishment at Thebes was undergoing its own transformation, from a hereditary High Priesthood that behaved like a rival monarchy toward the God's Wife arrangement that eventually superseded it. The Libyan kings both suffered from the first arrangement and contributed to building the second, since Libyan royal women held the office and the quasi-royal status it acquired dates from the Libyan period.

That development is one of the Libyan centuries' most consequential legacies. It gave every subsequent regime a workable mechanism for controlling Thebes, and the Kushites and Saites both used it without modification.

## Trade, Wealth, and How the Chiefdoms Funded Themselves

Political fragmentation raises an obvious economic question: how did a dozen competing authorities pay for armies, temples, and courts?

### The agricultural base

The fundamental answer is that Egyptian wealth was agricultural and local. The Nile flood produced a surplus every year regardless of who claimed the crown, and any ruler controlling a stretch of cultivated land controlled the revenue from it. A Delta chief with a city, its branch of the river, and its farmland had a viable income without needing access to a national treasury.

That is why fragmentation was economically survivable in a way it would not have been in a state dependent on centralized long-distance trade. The relationship between the river and Egyptian state formation is examined in the account of [how the Nile built the Egyptian state](/2000/08/01/how-the-nile-built-egyptian-state/), and the same dynamics that allowed a unified state to form also allowed its pieces to function independently.

### The loss of imperial revenue

What did disappear was imperial income. New Kingdom Egypt had drawn Nubian gold, Levantine tribute, and the profits of long-distance exchange, and none of that flowed to Third Intermediate Period rulers in comparable quantity. Nubia was lost, the Levantine empire was gone, and Shoshenq I's campaign was a raid rather than the restoration of a tribute system.

The consequence is visible in the scale of royal projects. Nothing in the period approaches the Ramesside building programs, and the reuse of earlier stone at Tanis, whatever else it indicates, is consistent with a monarchy working within tighter resource limits than its predecessors.

### Mediterranean and Levantine contact

Trade with the Levant and the eastern Mediterranean continued at a reduced level. Egyptian objects of the period appear at Levantine sites, and imported material reaches Egypt. Phoenician cities were expanding their maritime activity during these centuries, and Delta ports participated in that world.

The eastern Delta's position on the routes to Asia gave its rulers a stake in that traffic, which is one reason the eastern cities, Tanis and Bubastis among them, were prominent in the period's politics.

### Temple economies

Temple estates remained the largest economic institutions in the country, holding land, livestock, workshops, and personnel. Their revenues supported the cults and the priesthoods, and control of temple appointments therefore had direct economic consequences.

For a chief or a king, patronizing a temple was an investment as well as a religious duty, since the institution's wealth and its network of dependents became allies. The functioning of these institutions as centers of power is examined in the account of [temples as power centers in late Egypt](/2006/07/01/temples-as-power-centers-egypt/).

### What the Tanis burials show

Against any picture of general impoverishment stand the royal tombs at Tanis, containing silver coffins, gold masks, and jewelry of the first quality. Silver was scarcer than gold in Egypt and had to be imported, so a silver coffin represents very substantial expenditure.

The correct conclusion is not that the period was rich but that it was not poor in the way the political narrative implies. Royal resources were sufficient for high-quality burial, for temple building, and for maintaining courts and armies. What they were not sufficient for was the imperial-scale projects of the New Kingdom, and the difference is one of degree rather than of kind.

## What a Reader Should Take Away

Three points survive from the Libyan centuries, and each carries beyond the period itself.

The first is that Egypt's absorptive capacity was extraordinary and had limits that were not ethnic. A population defeated in war, settled by royal decree, and given land for military service produced, within two centuries, a line of pharaohs whom nobody appears to have regarded as illegitimate on grounds of ancestry. What Egypt demanded of its rulers was that they serve the gods, fund the temples, and perform the royal role correctly, and the Libyan dynasties did all three consistently. What Egypt could not absorb was the political structure those rulers brought with them, and it was structure rather than origin that eventually broke the country.

The second is that fragmentation and decline are not the same thing. The Libyan period produced silver coffins and gold masks at Tanis, inlaid bronzes of the first rank, a festival hall at Bubastis, additions at Karnak, and the most substantial Egyptian campaign into the Levant in three hundred years. It also produced a country with several simultaneous kings. Both things are true, and an account that emphasizes only the political disintegration misses a great deal of what was actually happening.

The third is that institutions outlast politics. Through two centuries in which the number of claimants to the throne steadily increased, the temples kept their calendars, the priesthoods filled their offices, the Apis bulls were buried on schedule, scribes were trained, and craftsmen worked. Egyptian civilization survived the collapse of Egyptian unity because its carrier was religious rather than governmental, and when unity was eventually restored, first by Kushites and then by Saites, the culture they took charge of was intact and ready to be revived.

The image that captures the period best is the double title itself. A man writes his name in a cartouche, takes the titulary of Horus and the Two Ladies and the Golden Falcon, has himself depicted offering to Amun in the great temple at Karnak, and then adds, without embarrassment, that he is Great Chief of the Ma. Both halves are sincere. Both were necessary. And the second, which gave him his soldiers and his throne, was the thing that would eventually give the same throne to a dozen of his descendants at once.

## Names, Titles, and Sources of Confusion

The vocabulary of this period trips readers more than most, and a short guide prevents the common errors.

Libu, Meshwesh, Tjehenu, and Tjemehu are distinct Egyptian terms for western peoples, not synonyms, though modern accounts often collapse them. Ma is an abbreviation of Meshwesh used in later texts, and Great Chief of the Ma is the title that matters for the dynasties, appearing on royal and non-royal monuments alike throughout the period.

Shoshenq, Osorkon, Takelot, and Nimlot are Libyan-origin royal names that recur throughout the period. Multiple kings bear each name, the numbering differs between chronological reconstructions, and a Takelot II in one account may not correspond to a Takelot II in another. When reading about this period, check which reconstruction an author is following.

Bubastis and Tanis are distinct cities, both in the eastern Delta, and both associated with the Twenty-second Dynasty, which is why accounts of the period sometimes appear to place the same dynasty in two different capitals. Bubastis was the family's ancestral seat and the site of the temple of Bastet; Tanis was a principal residence and the royal burial place. Some accounts describe the dynasty as Bubastite and others as Tanite, and both are defensible.

The Twenty-second, Twenty-third, and Twenty-fourth Dynasties are not consecutive. They overlap, and the Twenty-fourth overlaps the Twenty-fifth entirely. Treating Manetho's dynastic numbers as a sequence of successive reigns produces a chronology hundreds of years too long and a picture of Egyptian politics that never existed.

Shishak, the king named in the biblical account of the campaign against Rehoboam, is generally identified with Shoshenq I, but the identification is an inference rather than a documented equation, and a minority of scholars dispute it. Presenting it as certain overstates the case.

Finally, Libyan in this context means descended from populations west of Egypt as the Egyptians classified them, and it does not correspond to the modern nation of Libya or to any single ancient ethnic identity. The dynasties were Egyptian kings whose families traced descent to those populations, several centuries after their ancestors had settled in Egypt.

One further caution concerns the phrase Third Intermediate Period itself. It is a modern scholarly label for the span between the end of the New Kingdom and the beginning of the Late Period, and it groups together the Tanite and Theban arrangement of the Twenty-first Dynasty, the Libyan dynasties, and the Kushite Twenty-fifth Dynasty. Those are three quite different political situations, and treating the period as a single condition obscures more than it clarifies. The Libyan centuries in particular have their own character, distinct from what preceded and followed them, and reading them through a label designed to mark a gap between two peaks tends to prejudge what they were.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who were the Libyan pharaohs of Egypt?

The Libyan pharaohs were kings of the Twenty-second, Twenty-third, and Twenty-fourth Dynasties, ruling roughly from 945 to 715 BCE, who descended from Meshwesh Libyan populations settled in the Egyptian Delta as military colonists during the Ramesside period. The principal line was founded by Shoshenq I and ruled from Bubastis and Tanis in the eastern Delta. Later kings include a series of rulers named Osorkon, Takelot, and further Shoshenqs, together with a rival line generally placed at Leontopolis and a short-lived western Delta kingship at Sais under Tefnakht and Bakenranef. They used full Egyptian royal titulary while also retaining the Libyan title Great Chief of the Ma, and that double identity is the defining feature of the period.

### Q: How did Libyans come to rule Egypt?

By accumulation over roughly two centuries, not by conquest. Ramesside kings defeated large Libyan incursions under Merneptah and Ramesses III and then settled the defeated as military colonists in the Delta, granting land in exchange for service. Those communities kept their own chiefs and kin-group organization while adopting Egyptian religion, names, and burial practice. Over generations their leaders accumulated land, troops, and marriage ties to the Egyptian elite and royal house. When the Twenty-first Dynasty ended, the most powerful family in the country was a Meshwesh line at Bubastis holding the title Great Chief of the Ma, connected by marriage to the outgoing dynasty, and Shoshenq I took the throne around 945 BCE without any recorded struggle.

### Q: What were the two main Libyan dynasties?

The Twenty-second Dynasty, founded by Shoshenq I around 945 BCE and based at Bubastis and Tanis, was the principal line and held genuine authority over the whole country under its early kings. The Twenty-third Dynasty emerged later, generally understood as a branch of the same Libyan family, and ruled simultaneously with the Twenty-second rather than after it, which is why the two produce overlapping king lists. Its principal seat is usually placed at Leontopolis in the Delta, though its location and the sequence of its kings are debated. A third line, the Twenty-fourth Dynasty at Sais under Tefnakht and Bakenranef, was short-lived and belongs to the very end of the period, when it attempted to reunify the north by force.

### Q: How did the Libyans divide Egypt?

Through the structure of their own authority rather than by deliberate partition. Libyan power rested on kin groups and personal followings rather than on delegated royal office, so when kings placed sons and brothers in charge of cities, troops, and priesthoods, those relatives became independent powers whose sons inherited the same claim. The crown could not simply recall what it had granted, because doing so required defeating the holder. Every generation therefore added new autonomous centers. By the middle of the eighth century BCE the Delta contained several rulers using royal titles and a larger number of Great Chiefs holding cities such as Mendes, Sebennytos, and Busiris, with further local rulers at Herakleopolis and Hermopolis controlling stretches of the Nile corridor.

### Q: Where did the Libyans come from?

From the region immediately west of Egypt, the desert margins and Mediterranean coastal strip of what is now Libya and the western Egyptian desert. They were neighbors rather than distant invaders, and Egyptian texts distinguish several groups among them: Tjehenu and Tjemehu from the Old Kingdom onward, and Libu and Meshwesh in the Ramesside period, the last being the group from which the ruling dynasties descended. They were pastoral and semi-pastoral peoples organized around chiefs and kin groups rather than a centralized state, keeping cattle and cultivating where water allowed. The long-term pressure pushing them eastward toward the Nile appears to have been environmental, as the drying of the Sahara steadily contracted the pastures their economy depended on.

### Q: How did Libyan rule differ from Egyptian rule?

In its foundation rather than its outward forms. The Libyan kings used the complete pharaonic apparatus, taking the five-part royal titulary, writing their names in cartouches, building and restoring temples, and being buried in Egyptian style, as the rich royal tombs at Tanis demonstrate. Underneath that, their actual authority rested on kinship and personal followings rather than on an appointed, revocable bureaucracy. They governed through relatives who held power in their own right instead of through officials who held it from the crown. Classic Egyptian administration treated hereditary local power as a symptom of state decay; the Libyan system had it built in from the start, which made the state militarily robust and administratively prone to splitting.

### Q: Did the Libyans fight to control Egypt?

They fought each other constantly and never fought a war of conquest against Egypt. There is no record of a Libyan invasion that put the dynasties on the throne; they arrived through settlement and dynastic succession. Once in power, conflict among Libyan-descended rulers became the period's characteristic form of warfare. The Chronicle of Prince Osorkon, carved on the Bubastite Portal at Karnak, documents repeated intervention, revolt, and renewed disorder at Thebes across many years. Later, Tefnakht of Sais assembled a coalition and expanded eastward and southward, absorbing rivals, and it was that expansion that provoked the Kushite campaign. Piye's Victory Stela describes the resulting warfare as city by city fighting between roughly comparable forces.

### Q: How did Libyan chiefs become pharaohs?

Through steady accumulation of land, troops, offices, and marriage connections across generations. Libyan military settlements held land by service obligation under their own leaders, which gave those leaders an armed following and an economic base. During the Twenty-first Dynasty, Libyan-descended families rose in the Delta military hierarchy, and the family at Bubastis held the position of Great Chief of the Ma across several generations while also acquiring Egyptian titles and marrying into Egyptian elite and royal lines. By the time the Twenty-first Dynasty ended, that family commanded the most powerful military bloc in Egypt and had a legitimate connection to the ruling house. Shoshenq I's accession followed Egyptian dynastic convention, and the chiefly title continued in use alongside the crown.

### Q: Who was Shoshenq I?

Shoshenq I founded the Twenty-second Dynasty around 945 BCE and was the most effective ruler the Libyan period produced. He inherited the position of Great Chief of the Ma, was connected by marriage to the outgoing Twenty-first Dynasty, and took the throne without recorded conflict. His reign combined an attempt to reunify the country by installing family members in senior religious and military positions, notably his son as High Priest of Amun at Thebes, with a substantial building program including work at Karnak, and a military campaign into the southern Levant recorded on the Bubastite Portal with an extensive list of place names. A fragment of a stela bearing his name was recovered at Megiddo. He died before the Karnak decoration commemorating the campaign was finished.

### Q: Who were the Meshwesh?

The Meshwesh were one of the Libyan peoples named in Egyptian texts from the Ramesside period onward, alongside the Libu, and they are the group from which the ruling dynasties of the Libyan period traced their descent. Their name is frequently abbreviated in later texts to Ma, which appears in the title Great Chief of the Ma carried by Libyan kings and chiefs throughout the period. They appear in Egyptian records first as opponents in the Ramesside Libyan wars, sometimes in coalition with groups identified among the Sea Peoples, and afterward as settled military colonists in the Delta. Their organization was chiefly and kin-based rather than territorial, and it was that structure, carried into Egypt and retained, that shaped the political character of Libyan rule.

### Q: What was the Great Chief of the Ma?

Great Chief of the Ma was a Libyan tribal title, referring to leadership of the Meshwesh, which Libyan rulers in Egypt continued to use alongside the full pharaonic titulary. Its retention is the single most revealing feature of the period. An Egyptian king's authority derived in theory from the gods and from the office; a Libyan chief's derived from his kin group and the armed followers who owed him personal allegiance. The Libyan kings needed both, because the pharaonic title supplied legitimacy while the chiefly title supplied soldiers. Non-royal Great Chiefs also held Delta cities such as Mendes, Sebennytos, and Busiris throughout the period, and by its later phase these figures constituted a landscape of independent powers that no king could subdue.

### Q: Did Shoshenq I invade the Levant?

Yes, and it is the best-attested Egyptian campaign into Asia for centuries. A relief and topographical list on the Bubastite Portal at Karnak record the expedition and name a long series of places in the southern Levant, covering routes through the Negev, the coastal plain, and northward toward the Jezreel Valley region. A stela fragment bearing his name found at Megiddo provides independent physical confirmation of Egyptian presence there. The campaign is generally identified with the biblical account of an invasion by a king named Shishak during Rehoboam's reign, though the identification is an inference and Jerusalem does not appear securely in the Karnak list. Strategically it achieved little lasting: no administration or garrison network followed, and no successor repeated it.

### Q: Why did Libyan rule become so fragmented?

Because the system generated new independent power centers automatically. Authority rested on lineage and personal followings rather than on revocable royal office, so every senior appointment created a figure whose position his sons would inherit as of right. A king who placed three relatives in charge of cities with troops created three future dynasties, and the number of autonomous rulers therefore grew with each generation. Classic Egyptian administration prevented this by making offices non-heritable and rotating officeholders, but no Libyan king could impose that reform, because hereditary chiefly authority was the basis of his own throne and his relatives were his army. Stronger kings slowed the process; none could reverse it without dismantling the source of their own power.

### Q: What weapons and tactics did Libyan armies use?

Egyptian depictions of Libyans in combat show bows, javelins or throwing spears, swords, and shields, with cloaks rather than armor. Once settled in Egypt and serving as Egyptian troops, they used standard Egyptian equipment including the composite bow, bronze weapons, and the khopesh. Chariotry remained in use during the period, and Piye's Victory Stela mentions chariots among the forces of the northern rulers alongside considerable attention to horses. No distinctive Libyan military technology or tactical innovation is attested. Their advantage was organizational and demographic rather than technical: cohesive kin-based units with strong internal loyalty, available in quantity, in a country whose native military establishment had declined substantially after the New Kingdom.

### Q: Were the Libyan pharaohs considered foreign?

Their Libyan descent was openly acknowledged, since they announced it themselves by retaining the title Great Chief of the Ma, but they were not treated as foreign occupiers. They held every Egyptian office including the high priesthoods, built and endowed temples, married Egyptian elite families, wrote in hieroglyphs, and were buried in Egyptian style. No Egyptian revolt framed as resistance to foreign rule is recorded, and the conflicts of the period were between Libyan-descended rulers rather than between Egyptians and Libyans. The sharpest attack on their legitimacy came from the Kushites, whose Victory Stela criticized the northern rulers for ritual deficiency rather than for ancestry, which shows that legitimacy in Egypt rested on correct religious practice rather than descent.

### Q: What ended Libyan rule in Egypt?

The Kushite conquest ended Libyan royal supremacy. Tefnakht of Sais had assembled a coalition and pushed south into Middle Egypt, which drew Piye north around 727 BCE; the Kushite campaign defeated the coalition and subordinated the northern rulers, and Shabaka's defeat of Tefnakht's son Bakenranef around 716 BCE ended the last independent Libyan royal line. Libyan-descended dynasts were not eliminated, however. They continued as vassals under Kushite overlordship and later under Assyrian patronage, and the Saite Twenty-sixth Dynasty that eventually reunified Egypt and presided over its Late Period revival descended from the same western Delta line as Tefnakht. Libyan-descended families lost the crown in the eighth century BCE and recovered it in the seventh.

### Q: How many kings ruled Egypt at the same time?

At the height of the fragmentation, several. Piye's Victory Stela, which lists the rulers who submitted during the Kushite campaign, names multiple men using royal titulary simultaneously across the Delta and Middle Egypt, alongside a larger number of Great Chiefs of the Ma and local rulers who did not claim kingship. Four men with royal titles at once is a reasonable reading of that document. This is why the conventional dynastic numbering misleads so badly for this period: the Twenty-second, Twenty-third, and Twenty-fourth Dynasties overlap one another, and the Twenty-fourth overlaps the Twenty-fifth entirely, so adding their lengths in sequence produces a chronology hundreds of years too long and a political picture that never existed.

### Q: What was the Twenty-fourth Dynasty at Sais?

The Twenty-fourth Dynasty was a short-lived royal line in the western Delta, comprising Tefnakht and his son Bakenranef, active roughly between 727 and 715 BCE. Tefnakht is the one Libyan-descended ruler who came close to reversing the fragmentation: he assembled a coalition, extended his authority across much of the Delta, and pushed south into Middle Egypt, besieging Herakleopolis and threatening the approaches to Upper Egypt. That expansion triggered the Kushite intervention, and Piye defeated the coalition and received Tefnakht's submission. Bakenranef was subsequently defeated by the Kushite king Shabaka. The irony of the period is that the ruler with the right strategy for reunification was destroyed by the outsiders who then accomplished it themselves within a generation.
