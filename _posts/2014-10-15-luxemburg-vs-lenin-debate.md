The quarrel between Rosa Luxemburg and Vladimir Lenin is usually introduced to readers as a contest between two temperaments, and the introduction does both of them an injury before the evidence is even heard. On one side stands the organiser, suspicious of enthusiasm, drilling his faction into an instrument that could seize and hold power. On the other stands the tribune of the masses, trusting the energy of the crowd, allergic to committees, waiting for history to move through spontaneous action rather than through plans drawn up in exile. Luxemburg against organisation, Lenin against the crowd: the pairing is neat, memorable, and false in nearly every particular that matters. Luxemburg founded parties, led one for most of her adult life, wrote its programme, edited its press and disciplined its factions with a polemical ferocity her opponents remembered for decades. Lenin, for his part, wrote some of the most perceptive pages any European socialist produced about the creative power of mass action during the revolution of 1905, and repeatedly urged his own organisation to throw its doors open to workers flooding toward politics. A debate between two party builders about how a party should be built is a harder subject than a fable about temperament, and it is also a far more instructive one.

What follows reconstructs the argument as it actually unfolded across fifteen years, from her essay on the organisational questions of Russian social democracy in 1904 to the manuscript on the Russian Revolution she wrote in a prison cell in 1918 and declined to publish. Three corrections organise the whole treatment, and a reader who carries away nothing else should carry these. First, the disagreement about organisation was a disagreement about a specific design, a central committee empowered to prescribe tactics to the movement, and not a disagreement about whether organisation should exist. Second, their sharpest and least reconcilable dispute was not about committees at all but about the national question, the right of nations to self-determination, which he defended as a democratic demand and she rejected as a snare, in an argument rooted in the politics of partitioned Poland and never settled between them. Third, the English title under which her 1904 essay circulated for decades, a title posing Leninism against Marxism as rival doctrines, was supplied by an American editor in the early nineteen sixties and appears nowhere in anything she wrote. The framing of the debate, in other words, was partly manufactured by its packaging, and recovering the argument requires peeling the packaging away.

The guide is built around a single findable object and a single named judgement. The object is the agreement and disagreement grid, set out in full further on, which lines the two positions up issue by issue, revisionism, war, organisation, spontaneity, the national question and parliament, and adds a column for what the subsequent record suggests about each row. The judgement is the mutual confirmation: the pattern, visible only when the whole fifteen years are read together, by which each thinker's principal criticism of the other was borne out by events, her warning about what an over-centralised party becomes when it rules, his warning about what befalls a movement that reaches the decisive hour without an organisation able to act. Two failure modes, each identified in advance by the person best placed to see it, each confirmed in the other's country rather than in their own. A debate that ends that way has no winner available for coronation, and this guide crowns none. It reports both bodies of text accurately, steelmans both cases, and lets the grid do the arguing.

## A quarrel misread on both sides

The caricature survives because it serves too many readers at once. Admirers of Lenin have used the spontaneist Luxemburg as a foil, the gifted agitator whose distrust of discipline supposedly left her followers helpless when the German crisis came. Admirers of Luxemburg have returned the compliment, casting Lenin as the engineer of a machine whose logic ran from the committee room of 1904 to the single-party state, with her essay standing as the prophecy nobody heeded. Cold War publishing rewarded the second version especially, since a Marxist critic of Lenin who could be presented as a democrat martyred twice over, once by the German militarists who murdered her and once by the Bolshevik tradition that ignored her warnings, fitted the needs of editors who wanted the argument settled before it began. Social democrats had their own use for a Luxemburg safely dead and selectively quoted against revolutionaries of every stripe. Each camp extracted the half of the record it needed, and the halves hardened into the two figures of the fable.

Against the fable, set the bare chronology of two working lives. Luxemburg entered organised politics as a teenager in Warsaw, helped to found the Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland while still in her early twenties, and spent the German decades that followed inside the largest Marxist party on earth, the SPD, where she taught at its party school, wrote for its press and fought its leadership over strategy with the expectation that the party, reformed in its practice, would lead a revolution she never doubted was coming. Nothing in that biography belongs to an enemy of organisation. Lenin's biography, symmetrically, is unintelligible as the record of a man who despised mass action. His writings of 1905 respond to strikes, soviets and insurrection with an excitement his organisational writings rarely show; he pressed a reluctant committee culture to recruit workers wholesale, and he chastised comrades who treated the spontaneous creativity of the Petersburg Soviet as a problem to be managed. Each thinker, read whole, contains the quality the caricature assigns exclusively to the other.

What genuinely divided them was narrower, more technical and more interesting. She believed that revolutionary tactics are generated inside the movement's own experience and that a centre which prescribes them from above, however gifted its members, substitutes its own caution or its own impatience for the education the class receives by acting. He believed that a revolutionary organisation facing a police state must be built around a stable core of professional revolutionaries, centralised and disciplined, because anything looser would be penetrated, provoked and destroyed, and because socialist consciousness, in his famous formulation of 1902, does not arise from the economic struggle unaided but must be brought to it by theory organised in a party. These are real disagreements about the relationship between a party and a class. They are not disagreements about whether the class needs a party, a proposition both affirmed in everything they built, nor about whether the class can act, a proposition both affirmed in everything they wrote about strikes and revolutions.

A second solvent of the caricature is the discovery of how much else they shared. Both made their reputations fighting revisionism, the attempt associated with Eduard Bernstein to revise Marxism into a doctrine of gradual reform, and her Reform or Revolution of 1899 and his polemics of the same years breathe the same air. Both regarded parliamentary work as a platform and a measuring instrument, never as the road itself. Both opposed the imperialist war of 1914 when most of European socialism collapsed into patriotic support for its own government, she from a German prison cell, he from Swiss exile at the head of the Zimmerwald left. Both held that the war had ended an epoch and that the choice before Europe was between socialist transformation and a long barbarism of competing empires. On the largest questions of their shared world, the two were allies who never met as allies, separated by language, by party and by the accident that their sharpest exchanges were conducted in print across borders, often through intermediaries, and almost never face to face.

The personal register matters here, because later tradition has sometimes decorated the argument with an enmity the record does not support. They did meet and work in shared institutions: at the Stuttgart congress of the Second International in 1907, Luxemburg, Lenin and Martov jointly carried an amendment to the anti-war resolution committing the parties to exploit any war crisis for revolution, a collaboration neither would have entered with a contemptible opponent. Lenin's correspondence treats her interventions with the respect, occasionally exasperated, that he reserved for figures he considered serious, and his published judgement of her after her death, for all its criticism of her errors, reached for the image of an eagle among hens. Her side likewise distinguishes throughout between Lenin the thinker, whose anti-war intransigence she valued during the war years, and the organisational doctrine she opposed. Polemic in this milieu was a blood sport played by people who nonetheless recognised one another as inhabitants of the same small and endangered world. Reading the debate as a feud flattens what reading it as an argument reveals: two of the most capable minds in the movement testing, on the same problems, solutions whose difference turned out to matter enormously and whose similarity has been systematically understated.

## Luxemburg and Lenin before the quarrel

The argument of 1904 landed on ground prepared by two very different political educations, and a reader who skips the preparation will mistake differences of school and circumstance for differences of essence. Poland gave Luxemburg her first political world. The Polish socialist movement was split from birth between a patriotic current, for which the restoration of an independent Poland stood at the centre of the programme, and the internationalist current she helped to found, which held that Polish workers' emancipation lay through joint revolution with the workers of the partitioning empires, Russia, Germany and Austria, and that a Polish state restored within capitalism would change the flag over the exploitation rather than end it. The Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland, later of Poland and Lithuania, was therefore built as a party that defined itself against a national programme most of its potential audience wanted to hear. The experience marked everything she later wrote about nations: she had watched a national demand function, in her judgement, as the solvent of class politics in her own country, at close quarters, for years, before she ever addressed the question in theory.

Her German career then supplied the second education, in the possibilities and the sclerosis of a mass party. The SPD she joined in 1898 was a state within the state, with its press, its schools, its trade union allies and its parliamentary fraction, and she watched its apparatus develop the instincts of an institution with something to lose. Her great polemic against Bernstein argued that the party's theory was sound and its daily practice was quietly revising the theory away, a structure of argument she would later apply to the Bolsheviks with the valences reversed: there the theory of centralism, she feared, would revise the practice of the movement away. Teaching at the party school in Berlin placed her at the machine's centre, forming cadres, and the mass strike debates after 1905 brought her into open conflict with trade union leaders who wanted strikes kept as negotiable instruments under official control. When she wrote about organisation she wrote as an insider of the two most instructive party machines in Europe, the small Polish party built on programmatic intransigence and the great German party drifting toward caution.

Lenin's formation ran through a different bottleneck. Russian Marxism developed under an autocracy that made legal party life impossible, so that questions European socialists debated as matters of preference presented themselves to Russians as matters of survival. The circle, the illegal press, the agent network, the raid and the exile were the elements of his political childhood, and the famous argument of What Is To Be Done? in 1902, that a stable organisation of professional revolutionaries was the precondition of a party able to withstand the police, answered a Russian question before it answered any general one. His factional struggle with Martov at the second congress of the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party in 1903 turned on a single clause defining party membership, his narrower formula against Martov's broader one, and the split that followed hardened a dispute about the boundaries of an underground party into the founding division of Bolshevism and Menshevism. When Luxemburg intervened in print in 1904, she was therefore addressing a movement whose organisational obsessions were not theoretical hobbies but responses to repression, and part of the interest of her essay is that she knew this perfectly well and argued as though the Russian case, precisely because it was extreme, would become the template others copied.

The exchange also had a precise institutional setting that later summaries tend to blur. Her essay, Organisational Questions of Russian Social Democracy, appeared in the summer of 1904 in Iskra, the Russian party paper then under Menshevik control after Lenin's resignation from its board, and simultaneously in Die Neue Zeit, the German party's theoretical journal edited by Karl Kautsky. She was not a member of the Russian party and claimed no vote in its quarrels; her standing was that of the Polish party's leading theorist and a figure of the international movement intervening in a debate she believed concerned every party, because emigration and exile had made the Russian arguments portable. Kautsky's journal giving the essay its German platform signalled that the umpire generation of Marxism considered the intervention legitimate and weighty. Trotsky, then aligned against Lenin, published his own devastating organisational polemic in the same season, warning that Lenin's logic of substitution would end with a dictator substituted for the central committee, and the coincidence of the two critiques from different hands fixed 1904 in memory as the year the anti-Lenin case was stated in full, a full thirteen years before there was a Leninist state for it to be about.

One more preliminary protects the reader against a persistent confusion. The debate about organisation and the debate about spontaneity are often merged into a single quarrel, yet they began in different texts, in different years, against different opponents. The organisational critique of 1904 answers a congress and a factional pamphlet, Lenin's One Step Forward, Two Steps Back, which had codified the Bolshevik reading of the 1903 split. The theory of the mass strike, her most sustained treatment of how mass action actually develops, answers the experience of 1905 and was aimed chiefly at German trade union leaders and party executives who feared mass action as anarchy. Lenin figures in the second text hardly at all. Keeping the two fronts separate, as the grid does, is the first discipline the subject demands, and most of the caricatures in circulation begin by collapsing them into one another.


## The organisational questions essay, argument by argument

Her essay of 1904 is a short text with a long shadow, and it repays being taken apart slowly, because the compressed versions in circulation keep its conclusions and lose its reasoning. She begins by conceding the premise her later reputation denies her: social democracy, she agrees, is by its nature the party of centralism, the movement that overcomes local and particular fragmentation in favour of the unified struggle of a class. The question she poses is not whether the Russian party should be centralised but what kind of centralism a revolutionary movement can survive, and she distinguishes at once between two species that the Russian debate had fused. One species is the mechanical subordination of a party's sections to a directing centre, modelled, in her reading, on the discipline of a barracks or a factory floor, in which the centre thinks and the membership executes. The other is the coordination of a self-active movement, in which central direction expresses and concentrates an initiative that rises from below and remains answerable to it. Her charge against the Bolshevik scheme is that it builds the first species while believing it has built the second.

The essay then identifies the intellectual error she takes to underlie the design. Lenin's plan, as she reads One Step Forward, Two Steps Back, treats opportunism, the tendency of parts of the movement to dilute its aims, as a defect of personnel that the right organisational machinery can exclude, a bacillus to be quarantined by statutes, membership clauses and supervisory committees. Against this she sets a historical claim: opportunism is a stage and a product of the movement's own growth, appearing in every country as the movement acquires institutions, and it is overcome not by organisational prophylaxis but by the movement's continued development, in which errors are made, exposed and corrected in practice. A party that arms its centre against its own future deviations, she argues, will discover that the weapon works far better against initiative than against opportunism, since opportunism happily wears the uniform of discipline while initiative rarely does. The point is psychological as much as constitutional. Rules select for the temperament that flourishes under rules, and a movement that selects for obedience in the name of vigilance against heresy will, in time, be staffed at its summit by the obedient.

From this follows the essay's most quoted passage, and the one whose short fragment has become a commonplace far beyond its original setting. Discussing the infallibility claimed for a correctly constructed central committee, she remarks that the errors committed by a genuinely revolutionary movement are, in her judgement, 'infinitely more fruitful than the infallibility of the best possible Central Committee'. The fragment is usually deployed as a celebration of spontaneity, and deployed so it becomes a slogan she would not have recognised. In context it is an argument about learning. A movement that acts and errs generates its own correction, because the error instructs thousands of participants at once and the correction, when it comes, is the movement's own property, carried in its habits and its memory. A centre that prevents error by preventing action produces a membership trained to wait, and the knowledge concentrated in the committee cannot be transferred downward by circular, because it was never the membership's knowledge to begin with. Fruitfulness, not freedom in the abstract, is the criterion: she is comparing two methods of educating a class, and judging the fallible method superior as education even where it is inferior as choreography.

The essay also contains a warning about the sociology of committees that deserves to be quoted in paraphrase whenever the text is summarised. A central committee equipped with unlimited powers of intervention, she argues, will tend to become the only thinking element in the party, with the membership reduced to executive instruments, and the famous vigilance against deviation will invert into its opposite: a leadership insulated from the correction that only an active membership can supply becomes capable of the largest errors of all, committed with the full authority of the machine and discovered only in catastrophe. She instances the Blanquist tradition, the conspiracy of a dedicated minority substituting its own action for the movement of the class, as the historical form toward which such a design gravitates, and she is careful to say that she is describing a logic and not an intention. Lenin's sincerity as a revolutionary is not in question anywhere in the essay. The argument is that designs have consequences their designers do not choose, a form of reasoning she applied impartially to the German party's drift and to her own camp's illusions, and which gives the text its unsettling durability: it cannot be answered by attesting to anyone's good character.

### Was Luxemburg against organisation?

No. She co-founded and led parties across two decades and insisted that a revolutionary party owes the movement political leadership. Her argument opposed one organisational form, a central committee prescribing tactics to the movement from above, rather than organisation itself. The label of spontaneist pasted onto her ever since is a caricature.

That answer, compressed for reference use, opens the question the next section takes up in full, because everything depends on the precision of the distinction it draws. A reader who grants that she opposed one organisational form and not organisation itself will want to know exactly which features of the Bolshevik scheme she marked as dangerous, and whether her target was centralism as such or a specific variety she named with a specific label. The label exists, and the essay applies it without ambiguity. She called the design ultra-centralism, and the compound word does real work: it asserts that centralism itself is a spectrum, that the Russian party's majority faction had pushed beyond the point at which central direction strengthens a movement into the region where it begins to replace the movement, and that the excess, not the principle, was the pathology. Recovering the qualifier restores the debate to the participants, who argued about degrees and mechanisms, from the caricature, which argues about essences.

One further feature of the essay is regularly lost and should be registered before the argument moves on. She wrote as a defender of the Russian party's unity and of its revolutionary character against critics further to her right, and the essay's severity is the severity of a family argument. The German movement contained plenty of voices happy to treat the Russian quarrels as evidence of revolutionary immaturity, and her intervention refuses that comfort throughout: the Russians' problems are the movement's problems in concentrated form, which is precisely why their solution matters beyond Russia. The essay closes by projecting the issue forward, suggesting that the forms a young movement builds under repression will be studied and copied wherever repression is the teacher, so that an error constitutionalised in one country becomes an export. History was kind to that prediction even where it was unkind to others she made, and the export of organisational models, in both directions across the argument, is one of the threads the retrospective sections below pick up.

## Ultra-centralism and the alternative she proposed

Naming her target precisely is the work of this section, because the debate turns on a design specification and not on a mood. The features of Lenin's scheme that her essay isolates can be reconstructed from the text in a handful of strokes. Membership of the party is defined narrowly, around participation in a party organisation, so that the boundary between the party and the class it leads is drawn hard and policed. Authority flows downward through a hierarchy of committees whose higher instances may annul the decisions of lower ones, co-opt members and dissolve organisations. The central committee stands at the apex with powers characterised in her reading as unlimited, and the statutes are imagined as the principal instrument by which the centre guarantees the movement's political line, orthodoxy being treated as a property that correct machinery can preserve. Around the whole design sits the figure she found most revealing, the night watchman: her image for a centre whose highest activity is surveillance, watching the movement for deviations the way a watchman watches a warehouse, intervening against movement itself whenever movement threatens to become irregular.

Set against the specification the psychology she predicts for it, and the prediction is the essay's most original contribution. An organisation built to prevent the class from going wrong will, she argues, train its leading stratum in a distinctive deformation: the belief that the tactic of a great movement can be invented in a committee room and issued as instruction, and the corresponding disbelief in any knowledge arising from the struggle itself. She calls the revolutionary tactic, by contrast, something the party does not invent but discovers, the product of a continuous series of great creative acts of the experimenting class struggle, in which the unforeseen plays a larger part than the best party leadership's foresight. Notice the structure of the claim. She is not denying that leadership foresees, prepares and decides; her own career refutes the suggestion on every page. She is denying that foresight is the whole of tactics, and insisting that a structure which treats the unforeseen as insubordination will systematically misprice the information that only action in the mass generates. The organisation she wants is one built to learn at speed, with a centre strong in political leadership and weak in the power to substitute itself for the learning process.

Her positive proposal is therefore easy to miss, because it is stated as a direction of flow rather than as a rival statute book. The party's leading bodies should concentrate the political experience of the movement, articulate its aims, supply initiative in the sense of opening campaigns and posing choices, and submit their own line continuously to the test of the movement's response, with the broadest possible sphere of independent life for the local organisations and the press in which argument can proceed unconcealed. Centralism, in this register, means the coordination of the whole and the subordination of the part where coordination requires it, animated by what she calls the movement of the class itself rather than by the letter of a disciplinary code. Critics then and since have pressed the obvious question: how much of this is a design and how much a disposition, and what happens on the day the centre and the movement disagree about a decision that cannot wait? The essay does not fully answer, and the honesty of the debate requires registering the gap. Her answer in practice, visible across her German career, was that such days are rarer than organisers fear, that a leadership which has educated the movement can usually persuade it, and that where persuasion fails the leadership which overrides the movement wins the vote and loses the revolution. Whether that answer suffices is a genuine question, and it is Lenin's strongest ground, to which the next sections give full weight.

### What did she mean by the errors of the movement?

She meant that a class learns its politics by acting, so mistakes made inside real struggle instruct thousands of participants at once and correct themselves through experience. Supervised correctness teaches only waiting. The comparison judges two methods of education, not two levels of enthusiasm for discipline.

Between the essay's appearance and the revolution that tested every word of it, she returned to the organisational theme obliquely but consistently, and the consistency matters for the fairness of the retrospective. In the mass strike pamphlet the party's role is defined as political leadership exercised through slogans, direction and the clarification of aims, explicitly distinguished from the technical command of strikes, which cannot be decreed. In her writings on the German party's paralysis during the war she arraigned an apparatus that had converted centralism into the suppression of internal life, treating the SPD executive's discipline as the European proof of her Russian argument's mechanism: a centre that had learned to command had forgotten how to think, and a membership trained to wait for instructions waited, in August 1914, for instructions that betrayed it. The parallel is not exact, and she did not pretend otherwise, since the SPD's failing was cowardice in office rather than substitution by a revolutionary committee. But the institutional psychology rhymes, and she heard the rhyme. By the time she came to judge the Bolsheviks in power, the judgement had behind it a decade and a half of reflection on what centralised machines do to the movements that build them.

The essay's reception completes the picture of its moment. Plekhanov, the founder of Russian Marxism then moving toward the Menshevik side, welcomed the intervention. The Bolshevik press answered the Menshevik arguments at length while treating her contribution largely as an eminent foreigner's misunderstanding of Russian conditions, a dismissal that contained its own unintended compliment, since it conceded that the argument could be faulted on local knowledge rather than on reasoning. Kautsky, whose journal had published her, maintained the umpire's neutrality he was famous for, to the frustration of both factions. And the text itself began the migration that defines its afterlife: reprinted in collections, translated piecemeal, quoted by every subsequent critic of the Soviet party-state, and read by generations who encountered it under a title its author never gave it, in an edition assembled when the Cold War made its framing a weapon. The argument about the title belongs to its own section below. The argument about the substance continues here, with the question readers always ask next: how, and how directly, did Lenin answer her?


## Answers, silences and proxies: how Lenin replied

The shortest accurate answer to the question of Lenin's response is that he never published a direct reply to her essay, and the longer answer explains why the absence is more informative than most presences. His great organisational pamphlet of that season, One Step Forward, Two Steps Back, appeared in May 1904, weeks before her text, and its target was the Menshevik opposition inside the Russian party, whose case against his congress majority he anatomised paragraph by paragraph. When her essay followed in the Menshevik-controlled Iskra, he treated it in correspondence as a contribution to the faction fight by a distinguished outsider briefed by his opponents, and he answered the faction fight rather than the essay, restating his positions on membership, on the congress and on discipline as though the Polish intervention raised nothing that the Russian record did not already settle. There is evidence of irritation in the private remarks reported from those months, and none of a considered public engagement. The debate that posterity stages as a duel was, at the moment of its supposed climax, a speech answered by a silence and a change of subject.

The silence has been read in incompatible ways, and fairness requires giving each reading its due. One reading treats it as the dismissal of a man who considered organisational questions already decided by the logic of Russian illegality, and who saw in her essay the abstractions of a legal mass movement's theorist lecturing an underground party on the dangers of the very discipline that kept it alive. A second reading treats it as tactical: Lenin in 1904 was fighting for his faction's survival inside the Russian movement and had no wish to open a second front against the German party's left wing, whose goodwill he valued and whose journal had just demonstrated its weight. A third reading, more interesting than either, observes that his practice over the following years quietly absorbed parts of her objection without ever conceding the argument. In 1905, confronted by revolution, he demanded that the committees open the party to workers on a mass scale and denounced the committee mentality that treated recruitment as contamination, in language his 1904 self would have found alarming and his critics found Luxemburgist. In 1917 his party operated with an internal argumentative freedom, factions contending openly in its press and congresses over the insurrection itself, that bears little resemblance to the warehouse discipline of the night watchman image. Practice, in his hands, proved more flexible than doctrine, and the flexibility is part of the record the retrospective must weigh.

The public argument was meanwhile conducted by proxies and by parallel texts, a structure typical of the international movement's debates. Trotsky's Our Political Tasks, published in Geneva in the same year with a dedication to Plekhanov, pressed the substitution charge further than she had pressed it, foreseeing a sequence in which the organisation substitutes itself for the party, the central committee for the organisation, and a single dictator for the committee. The passage matters to this debate for two reasons. It demonstrates that her anxieties were not an idiosyncrasy but the common property of Lenin's ablest Russian critics, arrived at independently from inside the same movement. And it complicates every later use of the argument, since Trotsky subsequently joined the party he had anatomised, defended its centralism through civil war, and spent his exile explaining why the prophecy he had authored had been fulfilled by Stalin rather than by the design of 1904. The substitution passage is evidence in the debate; it is also evidence about the debate's difficulty, since its author could not settle its meaning across his own lifetime.

Lenin's mature statements on the questions she raised came obliquely and late. His 'Left-Wing' Communism of 1920 defended iron discipline in terms she would have recognised as the unmodified doctrine, insisting that the repudiation of party discipline by Western left communists played into the hands of the bourgeoisie, and contrasting the Bolsheviks' schooling in clandestinity with the legal comforts that made indiscipline affordable elsewhere. Yet the same years contain his alarm at the bureaucratisation of the Soviet apparatus, expressed with increasing desperation in his final dictated notes, where the party he built confronts him as a machine whose defects he proposes to correct by organisational rearrangements, expanding the central committee with workers, restructuring the inspectorate, precisely the category of remedy her essay had argued mistakes the location of the disease. He never connected his alarm to her text, and there is no evidence he reread it in those months. The convergence is structural rather than literary, and it is the more damning for that: the problem she had described as a logic of institutions arrived for him as an experience of government, and he met it with the tools his tradition supplied, statutes and inspectorates, while naming its symptoms with a clarity that approaches her vocabulary.

His posthumous judgement of her, delivered in the Notes of a Publicist in 1922, supplies the debate's most human document and should be weighed whole rather than mined. He catalogues her errors as his tradition counted them: the Polish independence question, the accumulation theory, the July days judgement of 1917, the party disagreements of 1904 itself. Then the register changes, and he instructs his readers that, notwithstanding the errors, 'she was and remains an eagle', adding that her works would serve as useful training for generations of communists everywhere. The eagle sentence is not a retraction and should not be inflated into one. It is something rarer in that polemical world: an acknowledgement, from the victor of the argument's political sequel, that the defeated side of the exchange belonged to the movement's first rank and that her writings formed part of its permanent patrimony. Readers assembling a feud from the record have to explain that sentence away. Readers assembling an argument can simply place it where it belongs, at the end of a fifteen-year exchange between two revolutionaries who agreed about the destination more often than the map.

### Did he ever concede anything to her case?

In print he conceded nothing, and the concession hunters who scour his works for a retraction will return empty-handed. In practice the concessions are visible: the wholesale recruitment he demanded when revolution loosened the underground's constraints, and the open internal argument his party permitted itself at its moment of decision, both depart from the strictest reading of his own organisational doctrine.

Beneath the famous exchange ran a quieter channel that the grid cannot show and the narrative should not omit: the two watched each other's work continuously and borrowed without ceremony. Her writings on the war economy and on accumulation informed the climate in which his imperialism studies were written, and his analysis of the national question after 1913 engages her Polish arguments directly, naming the SDKPiL position as the error to be refuted, which is a form of respect his polemics did not extend to minor figures. During the war their strategic judgements converged to a degree that startled contemporaries: both treated the collapse of the Second International as final, both demanded a new international built on opposition to the war, both rejected the defence of any belligerent fatherland, and both read the Russian collapse of 1917 as the opening of a European revolutionary period rather than as a local event. The Junius pamphlet she smuggled from prison and the Zimmerwald interventions he led from exile are companion documents of the same catastrophe, written by the two most uncompromising internationalists of their generation. That they never formed the alliance their positions invited is one of the might-have-beens of the period, explicable by party geography and by the fact that each was, in those years, a prisoner of circumstance in the most literal senses available, she in cell and fortress, he in exile and then in the sealed machinery of a revolution consuming all his hours.

## The mass strike and the education of struggle

If the essay of 1904 states her organisational doctrine in the negative, as a warning against a design, the pamphlet on the mass strike states it in the positive, as an account of how revolutionary action actually comes into being and what a party is for inside it. The Mass Strike, the Political Party and the Trade Unions appeared in 1906, written in the aftermath of the Russian revolution of the previous year, in which she had participated directly during her months in Warsaw before her arrest by the tsarist police. The German audience for the text was a labour movement then arguing, at its congresses, about whether the political mass strike could be adopted as a weapon in the Prussian suffrage struggle, and her pamphlet intervenes in that argument by refusing its premise. A mass strike, she argues, is not a device to be adopted, stored and deployed on a date chosen by a committee. It is the characteristic form of a revolutionary period itself, the way a class in motion expresses the accumulated pressure of its conditions, and it arrives through a process that no leadership can schedule because the schedule is written by the enemy's actions, by economic crises, by the thousand frictions of exploitation suddenly rendered intolerable in combination.

The pamphlet's central chapters reconstruct 1905 as a single unfolding education. Strikes begin over wages in one city, are answered by lockouts and troops, generalise into political demands, subside, and reignite months later at a higher level of organisation and consciousness, with each wave leaving behind committees, funds, newspapers and habits of solidarity that the next wave inherits. Nothing in the sequence obeys a plan, and yet nothing in it is chaos: the movement displays a logic that participants learn by participation, and the soviets, those improvised councils that astonished every party including the Russian ones, appear in her account not as inventions but as the natural organs a class creates when the struggle reaches a pitch at which representation becomes necessary and existing institutions will not supply it. Her famous conclusion about spontaneity follows with care. The element called spontaneous in such movements is not the absence of organisation or of consciousness; it is the name observers give to the fact that the initiative, at the decisive moments, rose from the mass of participants rather than descending from any headquarters, and that the headquarters of every party spent 1905 running to catch up with a movement that was educating itself faster than they could issue it instructions.

Against that background the party's task is specified with a precision the caricature never quotes. The party cannot make the mass strike, and a leadership that attempts to decree one discredits itself; the pamphlet is scathing about the fantasy of the strike as a folding weapon carried in the party's pocket. What the party supplies is political leadership: the clearest possible articulation of the aims toward which the movement is travelling, slogans that name the political stakes of economic struggles, direction in the sense of a compass rather than a leash, and the technical and organisational preparation, press, funds, coordination, that allows waves of action to be sustained and generalised rather than dissipated. Leadership so conceived is more demanding than command, not less, since it requires the party to understand the movement it leads well enough to speak for its future while the movement is still discovering that future in action. She compares the party's role to that of the conscious vanguard of a process whose energy it does not generate and whose timing it does not control, and the comparison quietly relocates the meaning of vanguard away from the committee that substitutes itself for the class toward the stratum that clarifies what the class is already doing. Readers who want the conceptual anatomy of that term in its Bolshevik usage will find it in the companion treatment of the [vanguard party explained](/2014/04/01/vanguard-party-explained/), which this guide deliberately does not duplicate.

The German reception of the pamphlet is part of its meaning. The trade union leadership, gathered at Cologne in 1905, had already anathematised mass strike propaganda as anarchy, and the party congress at Mannheim in 1906 settled for a formula subordinating any political strike to union consent, which she regarded as the burial of the weapon by the institutions claiming to wield it. Her polemic against that settlement, and against Kautsky's retreat from his own earlier radicalism on the question in 1910, deepened her isolation inside the German party and confirmed the pattern the organisational essay had predicted for a different machine: an apparatus with assets to protect experiences mass action primarily as a risk to the assets. The thread connecting 1904 and 1906 is thus not spontaneity as a faith but a unified institutional suspicion: organisations develop interests distinct from the movements they serve, and revolutionary strategy must be designed against that tendency in one's own camp first. Lenin's organisations were not exempted from the suspicion because they were revolutionary; the German unions were not condemned because they were reformist. The variable she tracks is institutional self-preservation, wherever it appears.

### How did the mass strike change the argument?

It moved the argument from statutes to history, because after 1905 she could point to a revolution in which mass initiative had built organs no party designed, and in which every party had been educated by events it did not lead. The organisational question acquired an empirical floor: any theory of the party now had to explain 1905.

Lenin's own writings of the revolutionary year form the necessary counterweight in this section, and they acquit him of the charge the caricature files. Confronted by the Petersburg Soviet, an organ created by striking printers and workers outside every party's programme, he argued in print that the party should not treat the Soviet as a rival to be disciplined but as an embryo of revolutionary power to be strengthened, and he urged that its non-party character was a reason to work within it rather than to boycott it. His Two Tactics of Social-Democracy, the theoretical testament of that year, insists on the revolutionary energy of the peasantry and the masses with a fervour no reader could mistake for committee fetishism, and his letters from the period scold the Bolshevik committees in Russia for their suspicion of the new worker recruits flooding into the organisation. The honest summary is that 1905 pushed both thinkers toward each other's supposed territory: she emerged emphasising the party's political leadership of a movement she had watched invent itself, he emerged emphasising the mass creativity his organisational doctrine had no obvious place for. The debate's later simplifiers had to forget 1905 twice over, once for each of them, and the forgetting is detectable in almost every short account in circulation.

What remained genuinely unresolved between them after 1905 was the weighting of a risk. She weighted the risk of strangulation: a movement supervised by a centre jealous of its authority would fail to develop the capacities revolution requires, and would discover the failure at the moment of crisis, when the class, never permitted to learn, hesitates and the initiative passes to the enemy. He weighted the risk of dissipation: a movement without a hardened organisation would spend its energy in heroic episodes, be divided by the enemy's manoeuvres, and find at the moment of crisis that enthusiasm is not a staff, a treasury or a communications network. Both risks are real, both materialised somewhere in the revolutionary decades that followed, and the retrospective sections of this guide are organised by exactly that pairing. The grid, to which the argument now turns, exists to hold the two weightings side by side with everything else the two agreed and disputed, in a form the reader can inspect at a glance and then use as a map for the deeper treatments that follow it.

## The agreement and disagreement grid

Debates conducted across fifteen years, four countries and two revolutions accumulate sediment, and the reader arriving fresh deserves one place where the whole shape lies open to inspection. The agreement and disagreement grid below is that place. Each row takes an issue on which the two left a record, states her position and his in compressed form, and adds a third column for what the subsequent record suggests, phrased as evidence rather than as verdict wherever the evidence permits. Compression is the grid's method and its acknowledged cost: every cell abbreviates arguments that the surrounding sections expound at length, and the cells should be read as signposts to those arguments rather than as substitutes for them. A student who memorises the grid has acquired a map. The territory is the prose around it.

| Issue | Luxemburg | Lenin | What the record suggests |
| Revisionism | Reform or Revolution answered Bernstein by tying reform work to a revolutionary final aim | His polemics of the same years defended orthodoxy against the revision of Marxism into gradualism | Shared ground: the anti-revisionist fight formed both reputations and aligned them before they ever disagreed in print |
| The war | The Junius pamphlet indicted social democracy's collapse and called for a new international | The Zimmerwald left demanded defeat of one's own government and a break with social patriotism | Shared ground again: the two most uncompromising internationalist voices of the war years, converging from prison and from exile |
| Organisation | Centralism of a self-active movement, with the centre leading politically and barred from substituting itself for the class | A stable core of professional revolutionaries, centralised command, discipline fitted to survival under a police state | Each design met its predicted difficulty: hers in a fortnight-old German party, his in a ruling party that resisted democratisation |
| Spontaneity | Mass initiative is the revolution's educator; the party clarifies and generalises what struggle teaches | Mass energy is indispensable and insufficient; consciousness and coordination must be organised into it | The year 1905 pushed each toward the other's emphasis, and neither position survived contact with revolution unmodified |
| The national question | Self-determination is a metaphysical formula that subordinates class politics to national movements in the epoch of imperialism | Self-determination, including secession, is a democratic demand socialists must champion while opposing particular secessions on their merits | Their deepest and least bridged divide, fought over Poland for two decades and argued past each other to the end |
| The role of parliament | A platform, a tribune and a gauge of the movement's strength, never the mechanism of transformation | An arena to be used for exposure and organisation, worthless as a road to power and dangerous when mistaken for one | Substantial agreement in doctrine, differences of emphasis in practice reflecting two very different parliamentary situations |

Reading the grid well requires noticing its geometry. The rows of agreement are not marginal courtesies; they include the questions that defined the era, the integrity of Marxism against its revision, the response to imperialist war, the limits of parliament. The rows of disagreement are real but unevenly weighted: organisation divides them by degree and design, spontaneity divides them chiefly by emphasis after 1905, and only the national question divides them at the level of principle irreconcilably. A debate whose principals agree on the era's largest questions and disagree most deeply on one they argued through the Polish case is a different object from the duel of the caricature, and the grid's first service is to make that shape undeniable. Its second service is diagnostic. The final column, read down, tells a story no single row tells: again and again the record vindicates a warning issued by one of them about a danger located in the other's project, and that pattern, the mutual confirmation, is the claim this guide has named and will spend its retrospective sections substantiating.

The grid also disciplines the writer who uses it, and a word about its limits belongs in any honest presentation. Positions held across decades are not points but trajectories, and the cells freeze trajectories at their most quotable moments: the Lenin of the organisation cell is the Lenin of 1902 to 1904, not the improviser of 1917, and the Luxemburg of the spontaneity cell wrote her pamphlet before she had watched a German mass movement accept war. Where a cell and a text conflict, the text governs. The grid is a finding aid for an argument, in the same family as the study grids readers build from the companion notes at [the Marxism study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), and its proper use in an essay is as scaffolding for comparison, dismantled as the comparison proceeds. With the map in hand, the guide now walks the terrain row by row, beginning where the disagreement ran deepest.

## The national question: the disagreement that cut deepest

Posterity stages the quarrel as an argument about committees, yet the two participants would have been surprised by the billing, because the dispute they pursued longest, returned to most often and settled least was the national question. Its roots lay in the Polish politics described earlier. The SDKPiL had been founded in rejection of the Polish Socialist Party's independence programme, on the argument that the Polish working class's future lay in joint revolution with the Russian, German and Austrian workers against the three partitioning empires, and that a restored Polish capitalist state would merely give Polish workers a Polish master. Luxemburg developed the position theoretically in her doctoral work on Polish industrial development, which demonstrated the economic integration of the Polish kingdom with the Russian market, and defended it organisationally for the rest of her life: the demand for independence was, in her vocabulary, the utopia of a class collaboration that history had already overtaken. When the Russian party's programme of 1903 included the right of all nations to self-determination, the Polish delegation's objections at the congress were an early skirmish of a war she would fight for twenty years.

Lenin's development of the opposite position was equally systematic, and its logic deserves the full statement the debate requires. Writing in 1913 and 1914, in the resolution on the national question and in The Right of Nations to Self-Determination, he argued that the right in question is a democratic demand of the same family as the rights of divorce in marriage law, his own analogy: to champion the right of divorce is not to oblige every marriage to dissolve, and to champion a nation's right to secede is not to recommend any particular secession. The socialist party retains its judgement on each case, weighing the interests of the class struggle; what it may not do is deny the right itself, because denial aligns the workers of the dominant nation with their own state's coercion and poisons the trust on which any voluntary union of workers across national lines depends. The distinction between the right and its exercise is the hinge of his position, and his polemics return to it relentlessly, charging his Polish opponents with confusing a democratic guarantee with a nationalist programme. Behind the logic stood a strategic judgement about the Russian Empire as a prison of nations, in which the great-power chauvinism of Russian workers themselves was the chief obstacle to the unity the revolution required.

Her counter-argument, stated most fully in the long series on the national question and autonomy written in 1908 and 1909, attacks the formula at its root. The right of nations to self-determination, she contends, is a phrase that dissolves under inspection, because the nation it empowers does not exist as a unitary subject: within every nation, classes want incompatible futures, and the right in practice is exercised by the bourgeoisie of the small nation, which uses it to enlist its workers behind a national project and to bargain with the empires. In the epoch of imperialism, when the great powers partition the world and small states survive as clients and buffers, the programme of national independence for the subject peoples of the old empires is either an empty phrase or a reactionary one, diverting the workers of each fragment from the only struggle that can free them, the joint overthrow of the imperial system itself. Her alternative was territorial autonomy within a democratised state for regions of distinct culture, a solution designed to secure language and schooling without enthroning the nation as the subject of history. The argument is characteristically hers in method: where he reasons from the juridical form of a right, she reasons from the class content that will fill the form, and the two methods pass each other without engaging more often than later readers have liked to admit.

### Why did Poland make the national question concrete?

Because Poland was not an example in her politics but the founding fact of them, the case on which her party existed. A party built by rejecting independence as its first article could not treat self-determination as an abstraction, and every general formula arrived in her workshop already tested against Warsaw.

The dispute's bitterness had a biographical undertow that honest treatment should acknowledge without exaggerating. For the Polish internationalists, the independence demand was not a debating point but the programme of the rival party competing for the same Warsaw workshops, and its most famous bearer, Józef Piłsudski, would end the story commanding a Polish state and fighting a war against Soviet Russia, a trajectory she would have filed under confirmations. For Lenin, the SDKPiL's stance carried a different charge: it appeared to him as the refusal of socialists in an oppressed nation to trust the workers of the oppressor nation with a democratic demand, a refusal he traced to the Poles' justified hatred of tsarism curdling into a doctrine. Each side suspected the other of generalising a local wound into a universal law, and each suspicion contained enough truth to keep the argument alive. Their exchanges on the question, hers in the autonomy essays and in her war-time writings on the crisis of social democracy, his in the 1914 pamphlet and in the 1916 theses on the socialist revolution and the right of nations, where her position is criticised under the transparent label of the Polish social democrats' errors, constitute the most sustained direct engagement between them on any subject. On organisation they had exchanged a speech and a silence; on the national question they exchanged libraries.

The war and the revolutions turned the theoretical dispute into a contest of predictions, and the retrospective habit of scoring it should be indulged here with care, because this row of the grid resists the mutual confirmation pattern that governs the rest. The collapse of the empires in 1918 produced the independent Poland his framework could accommodate and hers had argued could only be a client and a prison, and the Polish state's subsequent career, authoritarian drift, minority oppression, border wars, gave her shade arguments to cite. The Soviet state's federal forms, built under his insistence on the right of secession inscribed in constitutions, coexisted with a centralisation that emptied the forms, a sequence she would have recognised from her organisational warnings rather than from her national ones. Meanwhile the anti-colonial century that followed was fought substantially in the vocabulary of national self-determination, which his side of the argument had kept available to the movement and hers had advised it to refuse, and the refusal's cost in Asia and Africa is a charge her defenders have never fully answered. Score-keeping on this row depends on which decades the scorer privileges, which is the mark of a genuinely undecided question, and the grid records it accordingly: the deepest divide, argued past each other, unresolved.

What a student must not do with this dispute is import the organisational caricature into it, as though the national argument were the party argument in costume. The two disputes have different structures. On organisation the disagreement concerned the internal constitution of the workers' own instrument, and both parties wanted the instrument built. On the national question the disagreement concerned the subject of politics itself, whether nations or classes are the bearers of the historical claims at issue, and the parties wanted incompatible maps of the political world. The companion treatment of the [class consciousness explained](/2011/09/15/class-consciousness-explained/) article supplies the theoretical background for understanding why she regarded nationally defined consciousness as a regression from the class standpoint, and readers who follow the link will find the conceptual machinery that her Polish polemics assume. Here it is enough to fix the distinction: two debates, two structures, one pair of antagonists, and a secondary literature that profits from confusing them because confusion manufactures the duel it then pretends to referee.


## The ground they shared

The grid's agreement rows deserve expansion in prose, because agreement between famous antagonists is the material readers are least likely to have encountered and most likely to underestimate. Begin with revisionism, the controversy that made both names. When Bernstein argued at the end of the eighteen nineties that capitalism was outgrowing its crises and that the movement was everything and the final aim nothing, the young Luxemburg's Reform or Revolution replied with the argument that daily reform work draws its meaning from the revolutionary goal it serves, and that a party which abandons the goal will find its reforms transformed into the administration of the existing order. Lenin's polemics of the same period, against the Economists in Russia and the revisionists abroad, defend the same junction of theory and programme, and his What Is To Be Done? is unintelligible except as a product of the anti-revisionist climate the whole generation shared. The two entered the movement's front rank through the same door, defending an orthodoxy both understood as a fighting doctrine rather than as a catechism, and the shared formation explains a shared reflex visible throughout their later quarrel: each treats the other's error as a species of the same genus, the subordination of the revolutionary aim to the convenience of the movement's machinery.

The war then tested the agreement and found it load-bearing. The collapse of August 1914, when the great parties of the Second International voted the credits and blessed the trenches, left the internationalist remnant small enough to be counted in prison cells and exile rooms, and the two most consequential voices of that remnant were hers and his. The Junius pamphlet, written in her Berlin cell in 1915 and circulated illegally under a Roman pseudonym, arraigned the German party's surrender as the bankruptcy of a whole epoch of Marxist practice and posed the alternative that has echoed since, between socialist transformation and a relapse into barbarism administered by competing empires. His conferences of the Zimmerwald left, his insistence that socialists must work for the defeat of their own governments and turn the imperialist war into civil war, scandalised even the anti-war majority of the remnant. The texts differ in instrument, hers a pamphlet aimed at resurrecting German internationalism, his a factional campaign aimed at splitting the international and refounding it, and the difference of instrument recapitulates their organisational disagreement inside their agreement. But the substantive judgements, on the war's character, on the old international's death, on the epoch that had opened, are interchangeable across long passages, and contemporaries noticed: the wartime police of several countries filed the two names in the same dossiers.

Parliament supplies a third agreement, quieter because both positions were forged against different illusions. Her doctrine, formed in the Reichstag's shadow, treated elections as the movement's census and parliament as a tribune from which to address those outside it, while denying that a socialist majority so assembled could legislate capitalism away against the resistance of the possessing classes and their state. His doctrine, formed where parliament was a concession wrung from autocracy and periodically withdrawn, reached the same proportions by the opposite road: the Duma fraction as a platform for exposure and organisation, the institution itself as a form whose worship disarmed the workers who mistook it for the prize. Neither entertained the parliamentary road as a strategy of transformation, and neither counselled abstention as a principle, the twin errors between which their shared position is defined. The agreement matters to the debate because it fixes the location of their real dispute. Two revolutionaries who concur that power will not be voted into being, that war has ended an epoch, and that theory must not be revised into gradualism, are arguing about the engineering of a shared project when they argue about committees, and engineering arguments admit of evidence, comparison and partial truth on both sides in a way that duels of worldview do not.

A fourth agreement deserves naming because it is the one their followers most reliably forget, and it concerns the subject of the whole debate: the working class itself. Both built their politics on the conviction that the class is capable of ruling, not as a compliment paid in speeches but as an operational premise. His insistence that consciousness must be brought to the workers by theory has been read, by enemies and some friends, as a doctrine of the class's incapacity, yet the same author staked his strategy in 1917 on the soviets, organs of the class's own creation, and wrote in The State and Revolution that every cook must learn to govern, a sentence no theorist of incapacity could have signed. Her insistence that the class learns by acting has been read as a doctrine of its spontaneous sufficiency, yet the same author spent her life building the party she believed the class required in order to act politically at all. Strip the polemical varnish from both and the residue is a common faith with different anxieties attached: he feared the class would be left unarmed, she feared it would be left untrained, and each fear, inspected closely, is a tribute to the seriousness with which both regarded the other's problem.

## A title neither of them wrote

Every reader who has met her 1904 essay in English has probably met it under a false name, and the story of the name is not a bibliographical curiosity but a lesson in how debates are won before they are read. The essay's own title is descriptive and unglamorous: Organisational Questions of Russian Social Democracy. Under that title it appeared in Iskra and in Die Neue Zeit in 1904, and under that title it lived its first half century, reprinted occasionally, cited in the specialist literature, known to the historians of the Russian movement as a document of the faction struggle. Its transformation began when the American historian and former communist Bertram D. Wolfe assembled her two great Russian texts, the organisational essay and the posthumous manuscript on the Russian Revolution, into a single volume published by the University of Michigan Press in 1961, and gave the first of them a new title of his own manufacture: Leninism or Marxism? The volume carried that phrase on its cover as the book's governing question, and the essay has circulated in English under Wolfe's interrogative ever since, in reprint after reprint, syllabus after syllabus.

Consider what the retitling does to a reader before the first page is turned. Her title announces a contribution to a discussion: organisational questions, within a named movement, at a named moment. Wolfe's title announces a war of doctrines, Leninism against Marxism itself, and positions the essay as the founding indictment in that war, written by Marxism's authentic representative against the usurping system. The reader so prepared reads a family argument as a schism, a discussion of committee powers as a verdict on a regime that did not exist when the words were written, and a text whose author spent the war years converging with Lenin on the epoch's largest questions as though it were the charter of an opposition tradition. Wolfe, a scholar of genuine learning whose own journey out of the communist movement gave the volume its framing energy, was entitled to his interpretation; editors make arguments with titles as legitimately as authors make them with chapters. The objection is not to the existence of his framing but to its invisibility: generations of readers have absorbed the framing as though it were the author's, and have then cited the essay, thus framed, as evidence that the framing was hers. The circle is perfect and perfectly common in the transmission of famous texts. Breaking it requires nothing more exotic than checking the title page's history, a discipline the companion biographical treatments model for both figures, the [rosa luxemburg intellectual biography](/2014/08/01/rosa-luxemburg-intellectual-biography/) for her publishing record and the [lenin intellectual biography](/2014/03/01/lenin-intellectual-biography/) for the parallel transformations of his texts in translation.

### How did the retitled essay reach English readers?

It reached them as a book of the Cold War, in an edition of the early nineteen sixties whose editor supplied the famous title and bound the essay together with her prison manuscript on the Russian Revolution. Read as a pair under that cover, two occasional texts became a single prophetic indictment, and the packaging taught the reading.

The retitling also explains a puzzle in the debate's reception, the disproportion between the essay's fame and its brevity. As an intervention in the Russian faction struggle of 1904 it was one voice among many, respected and unanswered. As Leninism or Marxism? it became a scripture, the text to which every subsequent critique of the party-state could appeal for a founding authority inside the Marxist tradition itself, and its judgements were accordingly read with a weight their author, who revised her view of the Bolsheviks substantially in 1918 and refused to publish her own critique of their revolution, would have found astonishing. Cold War liberalism needed a Marxist witness against Leninism and found her; the Trotskyist tradition needed a revolutionary critic of bureaucracy who was neither social democrat nor anarchist and found her; the New Left needed a Marxism with the authoritarian stain argued out of it by one of its martyrs and found her. Each finding was a partial reading dressed as a recovery, and each leaned on Wolfe's title to stabilise the interpretation. None of this diminishes the essay, whose argument survives the removal of its packaging intact, as the earlier sections of this guide have tried to show. It diminishes only the packaging's claim to invisibility, and it instructs the reader of famous debates to ask, of every canonical confrontation, who built the stage on which the confrontation is performed.

Her own practice as an editor of other people's meanings supplies the fitting coda. She was a ferocious guardian of texts, a reviewer who treated mistranslation and tendentious excerpting as political acts, and her polemics repeatedly pause to restore an opponent's sentence to its paragraph before answering it. A guide that follows her in this owes its readers the same restoration for her: the essay restored to its title, its venue and its year, the manuscript restored to its drawer, and the debate restored to the two people who conducted it, neither of whom ever posed the question in the form Wolfe's cover made immortal.


## The manuscript she would not publish

In the autumn of 1918, in the prison at Breslau where the German state had confined her for her opposition to the war, Luxemburg wrote the text that would do more than any other to define her posthumous relationship with Lenin's revolution. The manuscript known as The Russian Revolution examines the Bolsheviks' first year in power with an intimacy no other foreign Marxist could have matched, and its opening judgements confound every attempt to recruit it as an anti-Bolshevik tract. The Bolsheviks, she writes, have rendered the immortal service of leading the first successful assault on the citadel of capital; their October rising saved not only the Russian revolution but the honour of international socialism itself, and the party of Lenin stands, in her assessment, as the only party in Russia that grasped the real interests of the revolution in its first period. The failures she then catalogues are therefore the failures of comrades in an enterprise she claims as her own, and the manuscript's critical chapters derive their force from that identification: this is the movement's senior internationalist auditing the movement's first government, in the conviction that the audit is a service she owes it.

The audit itself has three counts, each traceable to the organisational argument of 1904 while addressing the new subject of state power. The land policy and the national policy of the Soviet government draw her first fire, on grounds consistent with her lifelong positions: the distribution of the estates to the peasants, she argues, creates a new and powerful class of small proprietors standing as a bulwark against socialisation, and the slogan of national self-determination, hoisted by the Bolsheviks into a governing principle, hands the revolution's borderlands to bourgeois national movements that will turn them against it. Then the manuscript reaches the count for which it is remembered, the question of democracy. The dispersal of the Constituent Assembly, the restriction of the suffrage, the suppression of hostile newspapers and organisations: she contests each measure not as a liberal protesting at revolution but as a revolutionary protesting at the strangulation of the revolution's own life. Her reasoning deserves its famous sentence, given here in its shortest honest fragment: 'freedom is always freedom for the one who thinks differently'. Around that fragment the manuscript builds an institutional argument identical in structure to the essay of fourteen years before. A public life deprived of open contest, she warns, falls asleep; the bureaucracy remains the only active element; and a dictatorship exercised by a party machine in the name of the class becomes, whatever its intentions, the rule of a clique, with terror as its instrument and corruption as its atmosphere. The prophecy's mechanism is the night watchman inverted: the centre that began by supervising the movement ends by replacing the public altogether.

And then the fact on which this guide insists, because it disciplines everything that may legitimately be concluded from the manuscript: she did not publish it. The text breaks off unfinished, its argument against the Bolshevik land and national policies qualified in its own later pages by her recognition that the German revolution's failure had left the Russians isolated with choices no one should envy, and after her release in November 1918 she declined to issue it, telling Paul Levi and others in substance that her assessment had been overtaken by her growing conviction that the Bolsheviks' errors were the errors of a revolution abandoned by its expected European reinforcements, above all by the German movement whose failure she was fighting in the streets of Berlin. Levi published the manuscript in 1922, after her death and against the judgement of her closest political heirs, several of whom protested that its author had set it aside as superseded. The publication detonated exactly the argument this guide has been conducting: her enemies in the communist movement treated the decision to suppress as an admission, her canonisers treated the manuscript as her last word, and the truth, that it is an unfinished audit its author chose not to deliver because the situation it audited had changed under her, is less useful to every camp than the legends they preferred.

### Why did she leave the Russian manuscript unpublished?

Because the audit had been written for a situation that dissolved beneath it, as the German failure she was combating became, in her judgement, the principal explanation of the Russian deformities she had catalogued. Publishing the indictment while withholding its revised context struck her as a blow struck at the wrong target.

Weigh the refusal itself as evidence in the debate, since the debate's participants are entitled to have their acts read with the same care as their texts. A theorist convinced that her organisational critique had been vindicated in full, and anxious chiefly for her standing in the argument, would have published the manuscript in the winter of 1918 as the crown of her career, the prophecy delivered with its fulfilment attached. She instead spent her last weeks building the German party whose absence she blamed for Russia's isolation, co-founding the Communist Party of Germany at the turn of the year, and the priorities of those weeks are a verdict she delivered with her life rather than with her pen: the Russian questions would be settled, if they were settled at all, by the success of revolution in the West, not by polemics addressed to a besieged government from a Berlin rostrum. Her final writings on Russia breathe an impatience with Western critics who graded the Bolsheviks from safety. None of this retracts the manuscript's warnings, which she never disowned and which her party's press continued to voice in her name. It relocates them: from the arsenal of an opposition to the internal memoranda of a movement, which is where she had always insisted the argument of 1904 belonged. The distinction between a critique delivered inside a shared enterprise and the same critique marketed outside it is one of the debate's most exportable lessons, and it is taught by her conduct more plainly than by anything she wrote.

The manuscript's posthumous career completes the irony the retitling section described. Bound by Wolfe with the organisational essay under his interrogative title, the two texts were read for decades as movements of a single symphony, the young theorist's warning and the dying prisoner's confirmation, and the reading is not baseless: the continuity of the institutional argument across the fourteen years is real, as this guide has shown. What the binding concealed was the discontinuity of judgement, the distance between the author of 1904, addressing a faction in an underground party, and the author of 1918, addressing a revolution she called her own and refusing to publish against it. A reader who restores the drawer from which the manuscript was taken reads a harder and better debate: one in which the critic of Lenin could also be, in the same season, the defender of Lenin's revolution against its cultured despisers, and in which the categories of the later quarrels, pro and anti, dissolve on contact with the evidence.

## Class consciousness and the meaning of spontaneity

No word in the debate has suffered more from its friends than spontaneity, and a section devoted to cleaning it is owed to any reader who intends to use the debate rather than merely cite it. In the polemical shorthand the word came to denote a faith: the belief that the working class, left to itself, generates socialist consciousness and revolutionary strategy by instinct, so that parties are at best midwives and at worst obstacles. Luxemburg held no such belief, and the texts refute it in her own vocabulary. Her doctoral and journalistic work treats consciousness as a historical product, formed in struggle, certainly, but formed also by theory, by the press, by the party school where she herself taught, by the whole apparatus of education a serious movement builds. What her theory of the mass strike claims is narrower and stranger: that the sequence and scale of mass action in a revolutionary period cannot be derived from a plan, because the plan's authors do not control the variables, the provocations, the crises, the moods of regiments and the price of bread, and that the movement's participants, schooled by each wave of action, repeatedly outrun the strategic imagination of their own leaderships. Spontaneity in her usage names the location of initiative within a process, not the absence of thought within it. The soviets of 1905 were spontaneous in her sense and were also, within weeks, debating programmes, electing executives and issuing decrees: the initiative was unplanned, the consciousness was real, and confusing the two claims is the master error of the secondary literature.

His usage of the same word, usually overlooked, is closer to hers than the quarrel's mythology allows. The spontaneity Lenin combats in What Is To Be Done? is a specific political tendency, the worship of the elemental economic struggle by the Economist current, which he accuses of bowing before the very process it should lead, and his counter-claim is that trade union struggle left to itself produces trade union consciousness, the desire for reforms within the system, rather than the political consciousness that aims at the system itself. The claim is about the content that unaided struggle generates, not about the energy it generates, which he never doubted and repeatedly celebrated. Read with that precision, the two positions approach a formulation both could have signed: struggle teaches, and what it teaches depends on the political instruments present within it; a party that merely registers the struggle's lessons will lag behind them, and a party that imagines it can replace them will suffocate them. The companion article on [democratic centralism explained](/2014/06/01/democratic-centralism-explained/) examines the constitutional form in which his tradition eventually codified its side of that formulation, and readers tracing the organisational thread forward will find there the sequel this guide is charged not to retell.

### Is the spontaneity label a fair one for her theory?

It is fair about the origin of initiative and unfair about everything else, since her account assigns the party the decisive tasks of articulation, direction and preparation, and treats unled mass action as a resource that dissipates without them. The label survives because it is shorter than the theory.

Where the two genuinely continued to differ, beneath the shared formulation, was in their estimates of a single quantity: the reliability of the class's self-correction under pressure. Her political psychology, formed by 1905 and confirmed for her by the German collapse of 1914, held that the gravest errors in the movement's history had been committed not by masses in motion but by apparatuses in office, and that the bias of any prudent strategy should therefore run toward unleashing the class's activity and trusting its education to the process. His psychology, formed by the underground and confirmed for him by the improvisations of 1917, held that the class in motion is magnificent and uneven, capable of the soviet and of the pogrom within the same season, and that the bias of strategy should run toward the organisation that can generalise the class's best moments and survive its worst. These are not positions that evidence can fully reconcile, because they encode different weightings of the same catastrophes, and the student who sees that the debate terminates in a weighting rather than in a fact has understood it better than the partisans of either camp usually manage. The grid records the row as a difference of emphasis hardened by polemic into a difference of principle, and the record of both revolutions, examined next, gives each weighting its supporting catastrophe.


## The retrospective: two records and the mutual confirmation

Every long debate in political theory is eventually handed to history for marking, and this one was handed over with unusual speed, because within fifteen years of the essay of 1904 both parties had faced the test their argument had theorised: the making of a revolution, his successfully in Russia, hers unsuccessfully in Germany, with the manuscript on the Russian Revolution standing between the two events like a commentary written in advance on both. The retrospective temptation is to score the tests and declare the argument settled, and the guide's named claim, the mutual confirmation, exists to describe what the scoring actually finds. Each thinker's principal criticism of the other's project was borne out by the record of the country where that project was tried. Her criticism, that an over-centralised party in power would substitute itself for the class and strangle the public life on which its own correction depended, describes the trajectory of the Soviet party-state with an accuracy its rulers spent decades contesting. His criticism, pressed against her whole tradition, that mass revolutionary energy without an organisation built in advance to lead it would dissipate at the decisive moment, describes the German catastrophe of January 1919, in which she herself was murdered, with an accuracy her admirers have spent decades softening. Neither confirmation cancels the other. They compose a single finding: the debate had identified, from opposite sides, the two characteristic failure modes of revolutionary organisation, and the century obligingly exhibited both.

Take the German test first, in the detail it deserves, since it is the test his side cites and the one her side must not be permitted to summarise away. The Communist Party of Germany was founded at the turn of 1919, in the interval between the November revolution that had destroyed the monarchy and the January fighting that destroyed the party's leadership. It was, in the phrase the brief for this debate fixes on, a fortnight old when the crisis came: a federation of Spartacus League cadres, Bremen left radicals and assorted revolutionary shop stewards, without a national apparatus, without a tested press network, without the local roots a decade of patient construction might have laid down, and divided at its founding congress over the most elementary tactical questions, including whether to participate in elections to the National Assembly, a vote the young membership carried against the leadership's advice. When the dismissal of the Berlin police president Eichhorn provoked mass demonstrations and the occupation of the newspaper quarter in January, the movement's dynamics outran every organisation claiming to lead it, hers included. Luxemburg judged the rising premature, said so in the party's councils and in print, and refused, when the fighting was lost, to flee the city or to disown the workers who had risen. Government troops under social democratic authority hunted the leadership through Berlin, and on the fifteenth of January she and Karl Liebknecht were arrested, beaten and murdered, their bodies consigned to a canal and a mortuary. The fuller narrative belongs to the companion [german revolution marxist analysis](/2014/11/01/german-revolution-marxist-analysis/), and is deliberately not retold here; what belongs to this guide is the organisational balance sheet. At the decisive moment there existed no German organisation capable of doing what Lenin's had done in October: appraising the hour, concentrating force, timing the blow and surviving the counterstroke. The absence is a fact, and it is the fact his side of the argument had always predicted would matter most.

Now take the Russian test with the same unsparing symmetry. The party that took power in October 1917 was, by the standards of her essay, already a formidable centralised instrument, and the circumstances of civil war, foreign intervention, famine and economic collapse pressed it, year by year, along the trajectory her mechanism described. Rival socialist parties were suppressed; the soviets, whose initiative the revolution had celebrated, were hollowed into administrative shells as real decisions migrated upward into the party's committees; in 1921, at the Tenth Congress, factions within the party itself were banned as an emergency measure that hardened into a constitution; the apparatus of professional officials, accountable upward and irremovable from below, acquired the corporate solidity she had predicted for the night watchman's heirs. The Kronstadt rising of that year, sailors of the revolution demanding soviet democracy, was suppressed by the government the sailors had helped to install, an episode her manuscript's warning about terror as the instrument of a besieged clique reads, in retrospect, like a caption composed beforehand. None of this requires denying the siege conditions that formed the context of every decision, and the guide does not deny them: isolation, invasion and ruin are the strongest part of the defence, as the objections section below states in full. What the record establishes is narrower and harder: that the institutional consequences she forecast from the design followed the design into power, through the hands of leaders who did not intend them and in several cases deplored them, and that no mechanism internal to the design proved capable of reversing them once the emergency that justified each step had passed.

The mutual confirmation, stated with the precision the claim requires, is therefore a claim about warnings rather than about blueprints. It does not assert that her organisational alternative would have governed Russia better, a counterfactual no evidence can carry; the Russia of 1918 might have crushed a looser party faster, and the honest historian must leave that door open. It does not assert that his organisational alternative would have saved the German revolution, whose defeat had causes, the loyalty of the army's officer corps, the social democratic government's willingness to use it, the Allies' blockade, that no party design abolishes. What it asserts is that each antagonist located, with remarkable accuracy, the specific way in which the other's project would fail if it failed, because each understood the other's project from inside a shared tradition and pressed on its genuinely weakest joint. Her joint in his structure was the missing mechanism of correction from below. His joint in her structure was the missing instrument for the concentration of force at the decisive hour. Two failure modes, each real, each named in advance by the opponent best qualified to name it: that is the debate's yield, and it is a more useful inheritance than a verdict, because a movement instructed by it knows what to build against, in both directions at once.

### Whose warnings did the record confirm?

Both, which is the whole of the claim, and the answer resists every attempt to halve it. Her warnings describe the ruling party's trajectory after power was taken; his describe the unled movement's fate when the crisis arrived before the instrument did. The confirmations are symmetrical in form and asymmetrical in everything else, including who survived to read them.

One further element of the retrospective is usually missed, and it strengthens the claim by complicating it: each thinker, late in life, registered the approach of the failure mode they had assigned to the other, and registered it in their own camp. His final dictated notes circle obsessively around the bureaucratisation of the Soviet state and the uncultured apparatus strangling the revolution's intentions, and propose remedies whose organisational character she would have diagnosed in a sentence. Her final weeks are a frantic act of party construction, the founding congress, the programme she drafted for it, the daily paper she edited, as though the theorist of the movement's self-activity had concluded, at the end, that the instrument's absence was the emergency of the hour and her remaining weeks its only workshop. The convergence at the close does not dissolve their disagreement, which remained real in doctrine to the last page each wrote. It does suggest that the disagreement was always between two halves of a single problem, the problem of how a class organises its own emancipation without the organisation becoming the emancipation's substitute or its casualty, and that both halves were visible to both minds whenever events forced the whole problem into view.

## The objections at full strength

A debate guide earns its authority in the sections where it argues against itself, and two objections, one against each party, stand above the rest in the literature and in the logic of the record. They are stated here at the strength their ablest proponents would demand, answered as the evidence allows, and left with the concessions each answer owes.

The objection against her position, in its strongest form, runs as follows. Theory is answerable to the test its author lived, and her test was January 1919. The German working class rose, the old order's authority lay in fragments, and the movement she had spent her life educating possessed no instrument capable of converting the crisis into power: no disciplined national party, no agreed strategy, no leadership empowered to act for the whole. She herself, on this reading, stands inside the indictment rather than above it, since the Spartacus League's looseness was her tradition's organisational ideal in practice, and her own judgement that the rising was premature concedes the absence of the very preparation her doctrine had deprioritised for twenty years. The rising she could not lead killed her. A doctrine of organisation that ends with its author murdered by the counter-revolution her followers could not organise against has been refuted in the only court revolutionary theory recognises, and the subsequent century of her admirers has been an extended effort to appeal a verdict delivered by events.

The answer begins by accepting the objection's centre of gravity, which is real: the organisational deficit of the German left in 1919 is a fact, and it is the strongest empirical point on his side of the entire debate. What the answer contests is the attribution. The Communist Party of Germany was a fortnight old at the crisis because the German revolutionary left had spent the war divided inside a social democratic party whose apparatus suppressed it, and the causes of the deficit run through the whole history of German social democracy, its legalism, its union bureaucracy, its wartime treason in her analysis, rather than through the organisational writings of the woman who had fought that apparatus for two decades. She had demanded, in season and out, exactly the independent revolutionary party whose absence the objection deplores, and the founding congress she addressed in her last fortnight was the attempt to build it under fire. Moreover the counterfactual the objection smuggles, that a Bolshevik-style party could have taken and held power in the Germany of 1919, faces the military and international arithmetic of that winter: a victorious German soviet republic would have confronted the Allied blockade, the intact officer corps and a social democratic government already demonstrating its willingness to shoot, with none of the territorial depth that saved the Russian experiment. The objection establishes that her tradition underbuilt the instrument. It does not establish that the instrument, built, would have prevailed, and the distance between those findings is the distance between a criticism and a refutation.

The objection against his position, in its strongest form, is the mirror image and equally formidable. The party he built took power and could not subsequently be democratised by any means internal to its design; the emergency measures of the civil war were never dismantled but normalised; the ban on factions became the constitution of a regime in which the correction of error from below, the mechanism her essay had identified as the movement's only school, was reclassified as treason; and the outcome, within a decade of his death, was a bureaucratic tyranny exercising terror against the party's own founders, an outcome his design's defenders must either attribute to the design or to a run of accidents so comprehensive that the design's irrelevance becomes the alternative indictment. On this reading the essay of 1904 is not a contribution to a debate but the debate's whole content, delivered early: everything after is annotation, and the European century's verdict on Leninism was written by a Polish theorist before Leninism existed.

The answer, again, begins with concession. The sequence from emergency to constitution is documented beyond serious dispute, the mechanism of substitution operated much as forecast, and apologists who treat each stage as a fresh improvisation under fresh necessity owe an explanation of why the necessities never ended when the emergencies did. What the answer contests is the inference from sequence to design, on two grounds. The first is causal crowding: the Russia of 1918 to 1921, invaded, blockaded, famine-stricken, its industry destroyed and its working class dispersed into the army and the villages, subjected any conceivable government to pressures that deform looser structures as reliably as tighter ones, and the Menshevik and anarchist alternatives were not crushed by Bolshevik centralism alone but by the same civil war that crushed every political form in the territory. The second ground is the elasticity the Bolshevik party displayed before the emergency constitution hardened: the open factional warfare of 1917, the coalition negotiations, the tolerated opposition press of the first months, suggest a design less monolithic in practice than its statutes, and therefore a trajectory driven by circumstance further than the blueprint reading allows. The objection establishes that his design contained no brake against the substitution it enabled. It does not establish that the design, alone among the forces present, drove the car, and the distance between those findings is likewise the distance between a criticism and a refutation. The symmetry of the two answers is not a rhetorical device imposed by this guide. It is the shape of the evidence, and it is why the mutual confirmation, rather than a verdict for either side, is the conclusion the record supports.


## The Polish party as her organisational evidence

Arguments about organisation are usually conducted in the subjunctive, and one reason hers deserve a hearing beyond their elegance is that she ran the experiment. The Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland and Lithuania, the party she co-founded and whose leading theorist she remained, was a small, illegal, intensely programmatic organisation operating across the three partitions of a country that did not exist on the map, and its quarter century of life constitutes the practical record against which her organisational writings should be audited. The party held its cadre together through the repressions that followed 1905, maintained a press in three languages, contested the influence of the much larger Polish Socialist Party on the terrain of the Warsaw and Łódź workshops, and preserved an internationalist programme against the most powerful emotional current in its political environment, the desire for national restoration. Whatever its limits, it was not the achievement of a movement allergic to organisation, discipline or central direction; its statutes and its practice were those of a party that knew precisely what it excluded and why.

The audit must also record the costs her critics itemise, because the Polish party exhibits her tradition's characteristic weaknesses in miniature. Its intransigence on the national question, the very principle of its foundation, confined it to a minority of the Polish working class even at the height of its influence in 1905 and 1906, when its membership and its strike leadership briefly rivalled the patriotic party's; its refusal of the independence slogan left the emotional treasury of national feeling to its rivals, and the sequel wrote the moral in state form, since the Poland that emerged in 1918 was built by Piłsudski's legionaries and diplomats, not by either workers' party. Her defenders reply that a party which had purchased size at the price of its programme would have dissolved into the national camp it existed to oppose, and that smallness with a programme is a stage rather than a failure. The reply is respectable and incomplete in exactly the way the German retrospective suggested: at the decisive moments of Polish history, 1905 and 1918, the instrument her tradition built was too small to determine the outcome, and the shortfall was not accidental but structural, the price of a set of choices her theory ratified. The Polish evidence thus cuts in both directions at once, which is what makes it evidence rather than decoration. It acquits her of the spontaneist charge conclusively. It leaves open, as the live question of her politics, whether programmatic purity and organisational reach can be maximised together or whether her tradition habitually traded the second for the first.

Set the Russian evidence beside it in the same spirit. The Bolshevik organisation, audited as an instrument rather than as a doctrine, performed the feats its design advertised: it survived fourteen years of illegality without dissolution, preserved a leadership and a press through war and exile, expanded in the months of 1917 from a sect of tens of thousands into the governing party of a revolution, and operated in October with a coordination no rival formation approached. The same audit records the design's carrying costs across that whole period rather than only after power: the factional regime of permanent polemic that consumed the exile years, the splits and expulsions by which unity was manufactured, the periodic revolts of the organisation's own worker members against the committee culture the 1904 essay had anatomised, revolts documented by historians of the party's internal life and suppressed, each time, in the name of the discipline the revolts questioned. Neither party's record is a parable. Both are case files, and the debate conducted at the level of the case files is the debate this guide has attempted to reconstruct, in which designs have track records, track records have prices, and the prices were paid by the movements that could least afford them.

## The war years: convergence under fire

Between the pre-war arguments and the revolutions stands the catastrophe that rearranged both thinkers' positions and revealed the depth of their agreement: the collapse of European socialism into the First World War. The sequence deserves treatment in its own right because the debate's later chapters are unintelligible without it. In August 1914 the parliamentary parties of the Second International, led by the mighty German SPD, voted the war credits and delivered their working classes to the slaughter as patriotic contingents. Luxemburg, who had spent the pre-war years warning that the party's apparatus would choose its own preservation over its programme at the crisis, watched the prediction execute itself in a single afternoon, and her wartime writings, produced in prison and in hiding, move steadily toward the conclusion that the International was dead and must be refounded on the basis of the minority that had refused the credits. Lenin, from his Swiss exile, reached the structurally identical conclusion faster and stated it more brutally, treating the collapse as the bankruptcy of an entire epoch of the movement and organising the Zimmerwald left as the nucleus of a new international. For four years the two most famous names of revolutionary Marxism pursued, in isolation from each other and under police attention in both empires, the same project: the reconstruction of internationalism from the remnant.

The convergence had limits that map the surviving disagreement with unusual clarity. His wartime strategy was organisational in its essence: split the parties, expel the social patriots in form as they had expelled themselves in fact, and build the new international as a disciplined federation of revolutionary rumps, on the calculation that clarity of demarcation was the precondition of any future mass influence. Hers remained pedagogical in its essence even under prison conditions: the Junius pamphlet addresses the German working class and its party as a single erring subject to be recalled to its own traditions, and the Spartacus letters she wrote for the illegal opposition aimed to educate a movement out of its betrayal rather than to amputate the betrayers and start again. Both strategies failed in their own terms during the war itself, since neither a split nor a recall materialised in time to stop the killing, and both succeeded posthumously in the sense that the post-war communist movement was built from the human material their wartime intransigence had preserved. But the wartime chapters fix the debate's mature form. By 1917 the two were no longer arguing about whether the old movement's forms had failed; the trenches had settled that. They were arguing, in advance and in the abstract, about what would be built on the ruins, and the revolutions gave each of them a ruin of their own to build on within two years.

One wartime text of hers belongs specifically to this debate because it contains her most considered pre-revolutionary judgement of the Russian party and its leader. The Junius pamphlet's discussion of the war's lessons praises the Russian social democracy's revolutionary record across the pre-war decades and singles out the Bolsheviks' intransigence as the standard the German party had failed to meet, a judgement written by the author of the ultra-centralism critique at the height of her own persecution by the state the German party was serving. The passage is routinely omitted from the anthologies of the quarrel, for the same reason Wolfe's title is routinely retained: it complicates the duel. A critic who can write that sentence in 1915 is not the prosecutor of a rival doctrine but a comrade auditing an ally's machinery while saluting his conduct, and the whole wartime record supports the more demanding reading. When the February revolution came and his April Theses scandalised even his own party's establishment by demanding the transfer of power to the soviets, her response from prison, conveyed through intermediaries and visible in her correspondence, was a recognition of kinship across the old divide: here was the revolutionary intransigence she had demanded from German platforms for a decade, practised at last by the man whose committees she had warned against. The organisational argument survived the recognition intact, as the manuscript of the following year proves. But it survived as an argument inside a camp, and the camp's existence is the wartime convergence's permanent contribution to the record.


## Fifteen years of argument: how the quarrel developed

Debates between major thinkers are usually narrated as single exchanges, a challenge and a reply, because narration prefers scenes to processes. This one resists the compression, since its meaning changes as its context changes, and a chronological pass through its fifteen years, gathering threads the thematic sections have treated separately, shows a quarrel that passed through at least four distinct phases and meant something different in each.

The first phase, from the congress of 1903 to the revolution of 1905, is the phase of the essay and the silence examined earlier: an argument about the constitution of an underground party, conducted on his side as a faction struggle and on hers as an international intervention, with the mass strike still a German theoretical topic rather than a Russian memory. Its stakes were prospective and its tone, for all its sharpness, was the tone of comrades disputing the architecture of a house neither expected to inhabit soon. The second phase opens with 1905 and runs to the war. The Russian revolution rearranged the evidence for both parties, elevating her mass strike analysis into the movement's common property and driving his organisational practice toward the open recruitment and soviet enthusiasm the thematic sections described. Simultaneously the national question moved to the centre of their direct engagement, as his pre-war writings on self-determination took her Polish doctrine as their principal Marxist target. The phase's characteristic document is not a reply but a pair of parallel constructions, each thinker building, from the shared experience of a failed revolution, a system the other would have annotated in the margins. Stuttgart in 1907, where they collaborated on the anti-war resolution, belongs to this phase and captures its doubleness: operational allies, theoretical antagonists, neither condition cancelling the other.

The third phase is the war itself, treated in its own section above: convergence under persecution, the shared project of refounding internationalism, conducted by a prisoner and an exile whose channels of communication ran through smuggled manuscripts and neutral capitals. Its characteristic fact is the suspension of the quarrel without its resolution, the organisational and national arguments held in reserve while the epoch's catastrophe consumed their authors' energies. The fourth phase, the shortest and the most consequential, runs from February 1917 to January 1919 and transforms the debate's genre: the argument about a party becomes an argument about a state. His April Theses and October insurrection converted his organisational doctrine into a government; her prison manuscript audited that government with the doctrine of 1904 in one hand and her solidarity with its revolution in the other; her refusal to publish the audit, and her death at the hands of the German counter-revolution while building the party she had concluded Germany lacked, closed the exchange biographically while leaving every one of its questions open. Posterity inherited the debate in this final phase's lighting and has mostly read the earlier phases backward from it, which is the single most consequential error in the secondary literature: the essay of 1904 read as a prophecy of 1936, the pamphlet of 1906 read as a brief against October, the national essays read as preliminaries to a schism. Restoring the phases restores the arguments, each aimed at the target its moment presented, and it is the restoration this guide has attempted throughout.

Two continuities run beneath the phases and deserve explicit statement, because they explain why the debate rewards the long reading. The first is methodological: across fifteen years and every change of circumstance, she argues from the movement's process to its forms, asking what a practice teaches the class that practises it, while he argues from the enemy's pressure to the movement's requirements, asking what a form must withstand. Process against pressure: the two questions are both indispensable to any real organisation, and the phases can be read as a competition between them for priority in which the lead changes with events, 1905 favouring her question, 1917 favouring his, the civil war years favouring hers again with a vengeance neither lived to see completed. The second continuity is the identity of the ultimate subject. Neither thinker, in any phase, treats organisation as an end or spontaneity as a virtue in itself; both treat every question as a form of the single question of how the working class becomes capable of ruling a society, the class's self-education toward power. A debate whose participants share the question and divide over its engineering is the most instructive kind political theory affords, and the phases, laid end to end, show the engineering judgements being revised by events in both directions, which is what living doctrine looks like from the inside.

## How the debate was inherited

The afterlife of the quarrel inside the movements that claimed the two names is a study in selective inheritance, and a brief survey equips the reader to recognise the selections when they recur, as they do in nearly every subsequent argument about revolutionary organisation. The communist parties built under the Third International canonised his side as constitution: the organisational principles she had criticised were codified as the definition of a Leninist party, her essay was treated in the official literature as a Menshevik episode of her youth, and her memory was honoured in the carefully bounded form Lenin's eagle sentence had authorised, the great revolutionary whose errors the movement had lovingly catalogued. The German party she co-founded spent its first years arguing over her inheritance in precisely those terms, with Paul Levi's publication of the Russian manuscript in 1922 serving as the detonator of a crisis about whether her critique could be spoken inside the house she had built; Levi's expulsion for the publication taught the movement the answer its institutions preferred. Meanwhile the social democratic tradition that had imprisoned her performed its own appropriation, elevating the critic of Bolshevism into a witness for parliamentarism she would have arraigned from the same platform, an inheritance claim her Junius pamphlet refutes on every page.

The middle of the century added the Cold War packaging described in the retitling section, and the nineteen sixties added a further heir: the New Left, which recovered her as the patron of a Marxism of participation against the bureaucratic Marxisms of both blocs, and read the mass strike pamphlet as a charter for movements that distrusted parties altogether, a reading that survives contact with her party-building biography no better than the others. Trotsky's tradition, heir to the substitution prophecy of 1904 and to the party of 1917 simultaneously, conducted the debate inside itself for decades, its historians dividing over whether her warnings described a danger the revolution succumbed to or a stage it passed through, and the division maps with suspicious neatness onto the factional needs of each historian's moment. The pattern across all the inheritances is the one the grid was built to break: each heir takes a row, elevates it into the whole, and discards the rows that complicate the claim. The corrective is not a superior inheritance but a refusal of the method, a determination to hold the whole table in view, agreements and disagreements and confirmations together, which is less satisfying than a patron saint and considerably more useful.

Scholarship, as distinct from inheritance, has moved in the corrective's direction across the past half century, and its findings deserve a summary a student can rely on. The publication of her collected works and correspondence in critical editions, and of his in successive documentary series, has dissolved several legends at once: the feud legend, damaged by the Stuttgart collaboration and the wartime convergences the letters document; the spontaneist legend, unsustainable against the Polish record and the party school years; the prophecy legend in its simple form, complicated by the manuscript's unfinished state and its author's decision to shelve it. What survives the scholarship is the debate itself, restored to its phases and its proportions, and the mutual confirmation as the pattern the restored record displays. A reader who wants the two lives that carried the argument, in the depth the debate presupposes, has the companion biographies named in the grid's margins; a reader who wants the argument's modern restatements will find them wherever organisations still dispute the priority of process against pressure, which is to say wherever the question of the party is honestly alive.


## Consciousness from without: the doctrine beneath the design

Beneath the organisational dispute sits a theoretical claim that explains why the dispute could not be settled by drafting better statutes, and a reader who isolates it holds the key to the whole debate. In What Is To Be Done? Lenin argued that the working class, by its own effort alone, arrives at trade union consciousness, the understanding that workers must combine against employers for better terms within the existing order, while socialist consciousness, the understanding of the order as a whole and of its supersession, arises from the scientific comprehension of society developed by theory, and must therefore be brought to the movement, in the pamphlet's notorious preposition, from without. The formulation quoted Karl Kautsky's commentary on the Austrian programme, and its author spent later years qualifying its polemical sharpness, notably in 1905 when he allowed that the formula had been bent in one direction to correct an opposite bending. But the claim's hard core survived every qualification, because it expressed his deepest conviction about the division of labour in a revolutionary movement: theory is produced by intellectual work organised somewhere, and a party is, among other things, the institution that keeps that production connected to the class whose emancipation the theory describes.

Her relationship to the doctrine is the subtlest element in the entire debate, because she shared more of it than her polemical position allowed her to say. No major Marxist of the generation believed more fervently in theory's authority: her whole career is a defence of the programme against its dilution, her party school teaching formed cadres in exactly the doctrine whose transmission the from-without formula describes, and her contempt for movements that flattered the prejudices of their audiences was boundless. What she contested was the formula's institutional corollary, the suggestion that consciousness resides in a centre which the class receives. Her alternative account, worked out across the mass strike writings, treats consciousness as arising within struggle under the party's articulation: the party brings the programme, the slogans and the historical memory, the struggle brings the experience that makes the programme legible to millions, and neither term of the exchange can replace the other. The difference from his formula is real but located precisely: not in the value assigned to theory, on which they were at one, but in the imagined direction of the pedagogical flow, one-way in the strictest reading of his pamphlet, reciprocal in hers. The organisational designs each thinker favoured can be derived from their pedagogies almost mechanically, which is why the debate about committees was always, at bottom, a debate about how a class learns.

### What did the formula about consciousness actually claim?

It claimed that economic struggle alone generates demands the existing order can absorb, so the critique of the order as a whole must be carried into the movement by theory organised in a party. The claim concerns the content struggle produces unaided, not the energy it produces, which its author never questioned.

The pedagogical reading also dissolves the debate's most durable slander against each party. Against him, the slander holds that the from-without doctrine expresses contempt for the workers' intelligence; against the evidence of his 1905 and 1917 practice, in which the class's improvised organs repeatedly outran his own expectations and he said so in print, the slander cannot stand, and the doctrine is better read as a claim about where theories come from than about where intelligence lives. Against her, the slander holds that the reciprocal pedagogy expresses a romantic faith that the class teaches itself everything; against the evidence of a lifetime spent teaching it, in classrooms and editorials of legendary severity, the slander is merely silly. What remains after the slanders are cleared is a disagreement between two serious pedagogies of emancipation, each with a characteristic blindness the other diagnosed. His risks a teaching institution that forgets it is also a student, hers a learning process whose lessons arrive too late for the hour that needs them, and the reader who recognises those risks as permanent features of any movement's life has extracted from the debate its most transferable content.

## The shared laboratory of the first Russian revolution

Every theoretical debate has a laboratory in which its claims were mixed with reality, and for this one the laboratory was the Russian revolution of 1905, the dress rehearsal, in the phrase both traditions adopted, for the greater events of 1917. The year's sequence is the common evidence base of the entire argument, and its main exhibits deserve a consolidated statement. A massacre of petitioning workers in January ignited a strike wave that rolled across the empire in rhythms no committee charted; in the autumn a general strike, beginning with the Moscow printers and the railwaymen, paralysed the state and extracted the October Manifesto; in Petersburg the strikers' coordination hardened into the Soviet of Workers' Deputies, an organ elected in the factories, led in its public sessions by Trotsky, and exercising for fifty days the functions of an alternative authority in the capital; in December the Moscow rising, the year's armed climax, was suppressed by artillery and guards regiments, and the long reaction began. Two future antagonists of 1917 spent the year as the revolution's students, she in Warsaw until her arrest in the spring of 1906, he in Petersburg and exile directing a party that the events repeatedly surprised.

The exhibits support both pedagogies, which is why both thinkers mined the year for the rest of their lives. For her account, 1905 demonstrated the creative precedence of mass action: the soviet, the period's most consequential institutional invention, appeared in no party's programme and was recognised by the parties only after the factories had built it, while the general strike displayed the wave logic her pamphlet theorised, each crest educating the participants for the next. For his account, the year demonstrated the costs of organisational arrears: the Petersburg Soviet perished in December partly for want of the military organisation and the peasant and garrison work the Bolsheviks had been unable to build in time, the Moscow rising fought in isolation its leaders had not chosen, and the revolution as a whole displayed a spontaneity magnificent in energy and repeatedly bankrupt in coordination. Both readings are true of the record, and the thinkers knew it, since each spent the post-1905 years adjusting: she codifying the party's leadership role with new emphasis, he insisting that the party learn to work in every legal opening and mass organisation the reaction permitted. The laboratory's verdict, if the metaphor may be pressed once, was not that one theory had failed its test but that the phenomenon under study had two variables, and each theorist had hold of one.

### What did the soviets prove, and what did they leave unproven?

They proved that a class in motion generates representative organs of genuine authority faster than any party can design them. They left unproven whether such organs can survive a determined counter-revolution without an organised force devoted to their defence, and the empire's artillery answered that question provisionally in the negative.

There is a historiographical moral in the laboratory that students of the debate should absorb early. Because both antagonists treated 1905 as evidence, their later writings can be cross-examined against a shared record, and the cross-examination disciplines the partisanship of interpreters: claims about what she believed mass action could do, or about what he believed parties were for, can be tested against what each wrote while the factories' chimneys were still warm. The debate's modern literature is at its best when it performs that test and at its worst when it substitutes the events of 1917, or of 1936, for the evidence of the laboratory, since the later events are precisely what the argument is about and cannot be imported as neutral premises. Hold the sequence firmly: the laboratory came first, the revolutions second, the legends third, and an interpretation that respects the order will find the debate harder, fairer and more instructive at every step.


## Party and democracy: the question underneath

Running beneath every section of this guide is a question neither antagonist ever treated in isolation and both treated as decisive: the relationship between a revolutionary party and democracy, inside the movement first and inside the state the movement might found. Her position is the more fully theorised of the two, because the organisational essay and the Russian manuscript are, read consecutively, a single treatise on the subject. Internal party democracy, in her account, is not a liberal ornament bolted onto a revolutionary instrument but the instrument's operating system: the mechanism by which errors are caught while they are still cheap, by which leaders are educated by the led, and by which the class rehearses in its own organisation the self-government it claims to be preparing for society. A party that suppresses its internal public life is therefore not purchasing efficiency at the price of a scruple; it is disabling the only error-correction machinery available to it and guaranteeing that its mistakes will be discovered by catastrophe rather than by argument. The manuscript's extension of the argument to the state follows without a seam: a revolution that suppresses the public life of the society it rules deprives itself of the same machinery at the moment its decisions acquire the largest consequences, and the soviets themselves, emptied of electoral life, become the bureaucratic shell she described.

His position on the same question is usually rendered as its simple negation, and the rendering survives only by selective quotation. The Lenin of The State and Revolution, written in hiding in the summer of 1917, theorises the Commune model with an enthusiasm for direct democracy, rotation of office, officialdom paid at workmen's wages and the armed people replacing the standing army, that no reader of the organisational writings would predict; the text is the record of a thinker for whom soviet democracy was not a façade but the revolution's institutional discovery, the form in which the class could rule because the form abolished the separation between rulers and ruled. Against that text stand the practices of the government he then led, the dispersal of the Constituent Assembly after elections it lost, the progressive suppression of rival parties and press, the emergency constitution of the civil war, and the distance between the two records is the central problem of his legacy, argued over by his defenders as the measure of circumstance's power and by his critics as the revelation of the design's true content. Her manuscript is the founding document of the second reading, which is why it, and not the essay of 1904, is the text his tradition has never digested. What the guide's discipline requires is the holding of both records open at once: the democratic theory he wrote and the substitution she forecast, the emergency he governed through and the mechanism she named, neither record permitted to dissolve the other. Partisans close one of the books. The debate, properly read, is the friction between them.

A shared premise deserves excavation at this depth, since it distinguishes both thinkers from many of their respective followers. Neither regarded democracy as a value external to socialism, a concession to be traded against the movement's success; both regarded the self-activity of the working class as socialism's substance, so that a victory achieved by methods that extinguished the class's activity would be, in her vocabulary, a caricature of the aim and, in his own best texts, a betrayal of the Commune standard he had set his government. Their quarrel about the party is fierce precisely because the stake was common. Where they divided was over the conditions under which self-activity survives: she insisting that it survives only where it is constitutionally protected against the movement's own apparatus, he insisting that it survives only where the apparatus is strong enough to defend the space in which it can occur, against enemies who respected no constitution at all. The civil war tested his premise and the party-state tested hers, and the tests, as the retrospective established, returned their symmetrical and uncomfortable results.

## Reading the two bodies of text

A guide that has spent its length reconstructing an argument owes its readers a map of the evidence, and the primary texts of this debate form a reading path with a natural order. Begin with the two short documents of 1904, his One Step Forward, Two Steps Back and her Organisational Questions, read as a pair and in that sequence, so that her essay answers the text it actually answers rather than the legend of it. Follow with the paired masterpieces of the revolutionary years, her Mass Strike pamphlet of 1906 and his Two Tactics of 1905, which display the two minds working the same laboratory from different benches, and with his What Is To Be Done? read whole rather than in the extracts the polemics circulate, since the pamphlet's argument about Economism is the context its famous formula requires. The national question is best approached through his 1914 pamphlet on self-determination against her autonomy essays of 1908 and 1909, the pair of texts in which the engagement is most direct. The debate's final phase requires her Russian Revolution manuscript, read entire and unfinished as it stands, against his State and Revolution, the two books of 1917 and 1918 whose unplanned dialogue about democracy and power constitutes the argument's summit. His Notes of a Publicist of 1922, with the eagle passage in its full and ambivalent context, makes the fitting terminus.

The secondary literature sorts itself, for a reader's purposes, into the inheritances surveyed earlier and the scholarship that corrects them, and the practical advice this guide offers is to read one monument of each inheritance against the scholarship's grain. The official communist historiography's Luxemburg, the Trotskyist tradition's internal argument over her warnings, the Cold War liberal edition that retitled her essay, the New Left's patron of participation: each is a primary source for the history of the debate's uses, valuable exactly insofar as the reader remembers that a use is being documented. The modern critical editions and the biographies built on them restore the documentary floor, and the companion treatments in this series carry the two lives at the length they require. What no secondary source can supply is the friction of the primary texts themselves, the experience of watching two first-rate minds disagree inside a shared world, and the guide's consistent recommendation, here as everywhere in the series, is to treat every summary, this one included, as scaffolding for that encounter rather than as its replacement.

One editorial caution completes the map, and it returns the reader to the retitling with which the guide's middle sections were concerned. Famous texts circulate in famous editions, and famous editions have editors, translators and decades attached to them; the organisational essay reached the English-speaking world inside Wolfe's framing, the Russian manuscript reached it through Paul Levi's act of posthumous disobedience, and several of his texts reached it through the collected works of a state that had interests in his image. None of this invalidates the texts. All of it instructs the reader to keep the transmission in view as part of the evidence, asking of any canonical confrontation the questions this guide has asked throughout: who published this, under what title, in whose interest, and against whose decision. Debates are inherited through institutions, like everything else the two antagonists ever studied, and their own debate is not exempt from the law they spent their lives formulating for other people's.


## Two styles of leadership in practice

Doctrine is one witness to a thinker's organisational theory; the conduct of meetings, factions and crises is another, and the debate gains credibility when both witnesses are heard. Her leadership style, documented across the German and Polish movements, was the style of the public argument pressed to its limit: the mass meeting addressed for hours, the congress resolution fought clause by clause, the opponent answered in print at a length that left nothing standing, and, beneath the ferocity, a consistent refusal to settle by machinery what could be settled by persuasion. Colleagues who watched her work the SPD's congresses describe a leader who treated the assembled delegates as the party's sovereign body in fact rather than in formula, preparing her interventions as education, accepting defeats at the rostrum as data about the movement's education rather than as occasions for procedural revenge. The same style marked her war work, the Spartacus letters composed as lessons for a class she believed was being misled but never written off, and her conduct in the German revolution's councils, where her opposition to the January rising was argued openly, lost, and then honoured by her refusal to abandon the defeated. A leadership that persuades or accepts the verdict is the organisational essay enacted as temperament, and its risks are the essay's risks in biographical form: persuasion is slow, verdicts arrive late, and the hour of the rising does not consult the educator's timetable.

His leadership style was the style of the instrument tuned in private and played in public with total commitment: the faction organised as a school of cadres, the line worked out in correspondence and committee before it was carried to the congress floor, the willingness to stand in a minority of one at his own party's meetings and to argue the minority into a majority by the force of the analysis, as in April 1917, when his theses were initially voted down by the Petrograd organisation he led and were party doctrine within the month. The April episode is the standing refutation of the crudest version of the night watchman charge, since a machine incapable of internal correction does not reverse its line under argument at the summit of a revolution; and it is equally the standing illustration of her subtler charge, since the correction travelled through the person of the leader rather than through the sovereign deliberation of the membership, and a movement whose error-correction depends on the presence of a genius at the switchboard has solved its problem for exactly one lifetime. Both observations belong in the record, and the debate between the styles is best understood as a debate about where correction should live: distributed through the movement's public life, slowly and reliably, or concentrated at the point of decision, swiftly and hazardously. Modern organisations of every kind conduct the same argument under other names, and the antiquity of this one's documents should not disguise the familiarity of its structure.

The styles converged, once, on the only occasion when the two worked the same institution at the same time, and the episode has been cited earlier in this guide for its bearing on the feud legend; it bears equally on the present question. At Stuttgart in 1907 the amendment they jointly carried with Martov committed the International's parties to exploit a war crisis for the acceleration of revolution, and the drafting was a small masterpiece of the two methods combined: her insistence that the resolution educate the parties about the crisis's meaning, his that it bind them to a course of action when the crisis came. The resolution then failed its test in 1914, when the parties voted the credits regardless, an outcome each interpreted, characteristically, as the other's warning fulfilled: for her, the apparatuses had strangled the education the resolution was meant to deliver; for him, the absence of organisations built to enforce it had left the words undefended. A single document, two autopsies, and between them the whole debate in miniature.

## October and after: the argument becomes a state

The final phase of the debate demands a consolidated narrative, because its events are the ones through which most readers first approach the whole subject and the ones most encrusted with the inheritances. In October 1917 the party he had built over fourteen years took power in Petrograd in the name of the soviets, on a programme of peace, land and bread whose audacity she saluted from her cell in the words the manuscript section quoted: the immortal service, the honour of international socialism saved. The government that followed confronted, in sequence, the dispersal of the Constituent Assembly in January 1918 after elections in which the Bolsheviks won a minority of the national vote, the treaty of Brest-Litovsk in March, which she criticised as a capitulation fattening German imperialism while accepting that the alternative might have been the revolution's immediate destruction, the descent into civil war, the Red Terror proclaimed after the attempts on the government's leaders, and the progressive restriction of the political arena to a single party and then, in 1921, to a single permitted temper within it. Each stage had its defenders' case, grounded in the siege; each stage enacted a clause of her audit, several of them before the audit was even written, since the manuscript of autumn 1918 was composed while the sequence was still unfolding and reads, in consequence, less like prophecy after the fact than like a dispatch from inside the mechanism. The distinction matters to her credit and should be kept: the organisational essay of 1904 is prophecy, the manuscript of 1918 is diagnosis, and the debate is strongest where the two are not confused.

Germany's revolution, beginning in the same November that freed her from Breslau, supplied the counterpoint in real time, and the simultaneity is the retrospective's sharpest instrument. While the Russian party-state was demonstrating the costs of an organisation that had outgrown correction, the German movement was demonstrating the costs of correction without an organisation: a working class in motion from Kiel to Munich, councils sprouting in the soviet form across the Reich, and no national revolutionary leadership capable of giving the motion a strategy, the Spartacus League weeks old as a party when the January crisis arrived. She spent the interval as she had spent her life, educating at speed: the programme she drafted for the founding congress, with its insistence that the new party would never take power except by the clear will of the great majority of the working class, is the organisational essay's democratic doctrine restated as a founding constitution, and her speeches of those weeks argue against every shortcut, putschist or parliamentary, with the same patience. The patience was murdered with her on the fifteenth of January, and the debate lost, at forty-seven, the antagonist whose side of it has had to be reconstructed ever since from texts she did not live to revise. He survived her by five years, long enough to watch the German party's later catastrophes, the failed October of 1923 among them, and to dictate the notes in which the bureaucratisation she had forecast confronts him as his own government's defining problem. The exchange thus closed without a closing statement from either party, which is perhaps why it has never closed at all.

A note on proportion belongs at the end of the narrative. The debate's final phase occupies two years of two crowded lives, and the guide has given those years their due weight without permitting them to annex the whole. The organisational question was posed in 1904 about a party of exiles; the national question was posed across two decades about the map of Europe; the mass strike question was posed in 1906 about the education of a class. The state question of 1918 inherited all three and added the terrible variable of power. Reading the debate backward from its final phase, as the inheritances do, converts three arguments into one and flatters the reader with a verdict. Reading it forward, phase by phase as this guide has done, returns something less comfortable and more valuable: a set of connected problems, worked by two of the ablest minds the movement produced, whose solutions failed in instructively opposite ways and whose failures, taken together, map the terrain any successor must cross.


## The umpires: Kautsky and the centre ground

No account of the debate is complete without the figure who stood, for its first decade, as the acknowledged umpire of Marxist orthodoxy, since the antagonists' positions were partly defined against his. Karl Kautsky, editor of Die Neue Zeit, executor of the Marxist tradition's literary estate and the German party's resident theorist, published her essay in 1904 without endorsing it, corresponded with him without joining him, and was claimed by both camps as the authority whose eventual verdict would settle the argument. The verdict never arrived in the form either wanted. Kautsky's own organisational instincts were those of the great legal party he served: democratic in form, centralised in administration, hostile to factional splits and to mass actions that escaped the calendar of congresses, and his trajectory across the debate's phases, from umpire to wartime centrist to the most famous literary antagonist of the Bolshevik government, traces the collapse of the centre ground on which the early argument had been conducted. By 1918 the debate had new poles: his Terrorism and Communism arraigned the Soviet regime from the standpoint of the parliamentary democracy he had come to defend, and the replies it provoked from the Bolshevik side, including Trotsky's defence of terror and Lenin's polemic against the renegade of 1918 vintage, belong to a different quarrel, in which her position, democratic and revolutionary at once, was precisely the one the new alignment had no shelf for.

Her relationship with the umpire is itself a chapter of the debate, because she began as his collaborator and ended as his most dangerous internal critic, and the evolution mirrors her organisational theory's application to the German machine. The mass strike controversy of 1910, in which Kautsky counselled the party to husband its strength and avoid confrontation with the Prussian state while she demanded the suffrage campaign be pressed with the weapon of the strike, displayed the pattern her essay had forecast for a leadership insulated from its movement: the theory of the centre adjusted itself, year by year, to the convenience of the apparatus, until the pope of Marxism was revising the doctrine in the direction of the caution his office required. She named the process without mercy in private and with precision in print, and the German left's subsequent history, in which the centre she and Kautsky had disputed became the wartime majority that imprisoned her, gave the diagnosis its evidence. For the purposes of this guide the umpire's fate carries a methodological instruction. The debate between Luxemburg and Lenin was not conducted in a vacuum sealed against third positions; it was conducted inside a movement whose centre of gravity was drifting, and part of what each antagonist meant by organisation was a defence against the drift, her defence being the movement's self-activity perpetually renewed from below, his being the hardened cadre that could hold a line the centre would not. The century's judgement on the umpire, whose party presided over the catastrophe both had spent their lives trying to prevent, is the one verdict in the whole affair that approaches unanimity, and it belongs to the record.

## Holding the whole table in view

The guide has circled its subject through biography, text, laboratory and retrospect, and the sections remaining are best used to fix the method a reader carries away, since the debate's content and its method of study are here the same thing. Hold the whole table in view: the phrase names the discipline the grid embodies and the discipline both antagonists, at their best, practised on each other. It means refusing the excerpt that convicts without the paragraph that complicates, the phase torn from the fifteen years, the row torn from the table, the warning torn from the solidarity that delivered it. Applied to her, the method returns a party builder who theorised the party's dangers; applied to him, an organiser who theorised the class's self-government and then governed through an emergency that consumed the theory's conditions; applied to their quarrel, a shared project argued across the engineering of emancipation, in which the agreements are load-bearing, the disagreements are specific, and the deepest of them, the national question, is the one the popular version of the debate barely mentions. A reader who has absorbed the method owns something more durable than a position on a century-old argument: a way of reading any famous confrontation for the agreements its staging conceals and the packaging its transmission imposed.

The mutual confirmation, the claim this guide set out to name and test, can now be stated in its final form. Two revolutionaries, formed in the same tradition and divided by its hardest practical problem, each identified the characteristic failure mode of the other's solution with an accuracy that events, in the other's country, proceeded to verify: the party that substitutes itself for the class and cannot be corrected, the movement that reaches its hour without the instrument the hour requires. The verification is not a score draw arranged by an umpire's charity; it is the record's own shape, visible in the German January and in the Soviet decade, in the fortnight-old party and in the faction ban, and it explains the debate's otherwise puzzling durability, its refusal to stay settled in any generation. Movements keep rediscovering the argument because organisations keep exhibiting the failure modes, alternately and sometimes in sequence, and each rediscovery sends its readers back to the two antagonists who named them first and named them at each other. That is the sense in which the quarrel belongs to the permanent equipment of political thought rather than to the history of a faction fight in 1904, and it is the sense in which this guide has presented it: not as a duel with a winner withheld out of politeness, but as a map with two cliffs marked, surveyed by the two cartographers who each saw one cliff clearly and fell, in the end, toward the one they had marked for the other.

One discipline remains to be stated for the reader who intends to use the map rather than merely admire it, and it concerns the order in which the debate's materials should be consulted when a live question arrives, as live questions have a way of doing at the least convenient moments. Begin with the grid, to establish which row of the argument the question occupies and what the two positions on that row actually were, since most errors in the debate's application are errors of row, a national answer supplied to an organisational question. Move next to the primary text on the relevant side, read whole, because the excerpts in circulation have usually been selected by the inheritances for their convenience. Only then consult the retrospective evidence, asking the mutual confirmation's question in its exact form: not which antagonist the record crowns, but which failure mode the situation is drifting toward, and what each antagonist's warning would prescribe against it. A reader who works in that order will find the debate functioning as its authors would have wished, not as a mausoleum of settled scores but as a workshop of usable judgements, maintained by two minds who disagreed about nearly everything except the importance of the work.


## The Russian chorus: the debate inside his own movement

The organisational argument of 1904 is often staged as a foreign intervention in a Russian quarrel, and the staging understates how crowded the Russian side of the stage already was. The membership clause over which the 1903 congress split had its Russian theorists before it had its Polish critic: Martov, whose broader formula for party membership she effectively defended, argued that a movement growing toward mass influence could not define itself as a conspiracy of the enrolled; Axelrod warned the congress generation that a centralised apparatus of professional revolutionaries would reproduce, inside the workers' party, the tutelage the party existed to abolish in society; Plekhanov, the movement's founder, travelled from Lenin's side of the clause to the opposition's within a year, carrying the prestige of the founding generation with him. Her essay entered this chorus as its most systematic voice, not as a solo. The point disciplines two common errors at once. It refutes the dismissal, convenient to his later tradition, of the organisational critique as an outsider's misunderstanding of Russian conditions, since the critique's elements were native to the Russian debate and articulated by its founders. And it refutes the canonisation, convenient to hers, of the essay as a lone prophecy, since its author was conscious of speaking within a chorus and said so, presenting her argument as the international movement's common sense applied to a Russian design.

The chorus matters for a further reason: its members' subsequent trajectories rehearse the debate's outcomes in biographical miniature. Martov led the Menshevik internationalists through the revolution with a consistency that cost him everything except his honour, denouncing Bolshevik terror from within the socialist camp while refusing every alliance with the counter-revolution, and dying in exile in 1923 with his party suppressed by the state his old factional opponent governed. Axelrod's warnings were republished by every subsequent opposition as scripture and heeded by none in power. Plekhanov died in 1918 denouncing the October rising as a violation of the historical stages, a judgement the Bolsheviks filed as the founder's apostasy and posterity has filed more gently as the tragedy of a theorist outlived by his own movement. Trotsky, the chorus's most gifted voice of 1904, crossed to the party he had anatomised, built its army, defended its terror, and spent his exile composing the argument that the substitution he had forecast had been accomplished not by the design of 1904 but by the bureaucracy of the Thermidor, a distinction whose ingenuity measures the difficulty of the original prophecy for the man who made it. A reader assembling the debate from its principals alone misses this penumbra of corroboration and complication, in which nearly every position later taken on the argument was first occupied by a Russian who had watched the design assembled at close quarters.

The chorus also supplies the debate's most underused control group, since its members shared the principals' world without sharing their eventual power or martyrdom, and their judgements therefore register the argument's force independent of its outcomes. When Axelrod, in exile and obscurity, restated the tutelage warning in the nineteen twenties, he was not prophesying and he was not campaigning; he was recording that the mechanism described in 1904 had operated in the manner described, in the judgement of a man who had opposed it before it had a state to operate on. When the Menshevik historians in emigration compiled their documentary record of the revolution's first years, the suppression of the socialist opposition parties, the emptying of the soviets, the growth of the apparatus, their volumes became, whatever their authors' intentions, the evidentiary annex to her manuscript, assembled by the very current she had defended in 1904 against the design she criticised. Evidence that arrives from witnesses with no victory to defend carries a weight the principals' own texts cannot, and researchers who neglect the chorus deprive themselves of the debate's least interested testimony.

The same control function operates in reverse for his side of the argument. The Russian chorus contained the era's most articulate advocates of the broad, loose party of the whole class, and its members' conduct in 1917 is the experiment his organisational writings had predicted: formations that had refused the discipline of demarcation discovered, in the year of decision, that they could neither hold their own ranks nor negotiate from strength, and the Menshevik organisation that had defined itself against his membership clause in 1903 spent the revolution dissolving between a government it would not join and a counter-revolution it would not serve. Martov's integrity through that dissolution is not in question here, and his party's fate does not convict its members of anything except the organisational judgement he had spent a career opposing. The chorus thus testifies in both directions, which is the mark of a genuine control group, and a reader who consults it leaves with the debate's symmetry confirmed by the only witnesses who paid for both sides of it.

## The economics underneath the politics

Political debates between Marxists are rarely only political, and this one has an economic substructure that surfaces at its decisive points. Her theory of accumulation, published in 1913 as The Accumulation of Capital, argued that capitalist reproduction requires a non-capitalist environment into which surplus value can be realised and from which the system's expansion draws its sustenance, making imperialism the competitive struggle for the shrinking remainder of that environment and locating the system's terminal crisis in the environment's exhaustion. His theory of imperialism, written in 1916 under the acknowledged influence of Hilferding and Bukharin and in critical dialogue with hers, relocated the mechanism inside the capitalist economies themselves, in the fusion of bank and industrial capital and the export of capital that followed, and drew from it the political conclusion she resisted: that the uneven development of the system would break its chain at the weakest link and make national revolutionary ruptures, and with them the national question, the operative terrain of the epoch. The economic disagreement is the national disagreement's engine room. If the system's crisis is global and simultaneous in tendency, as her accumulation argument implies, then the fragmentation of the struggle into national secessions is a diversion from the only front that matters; if the system's development is uneven and its ruptures national in form, as his imperialism argument insists, then a doctrine of national rights is not a diversion but an instrument for exploiting the fractures. Neither antagonist wrote the economics as a commentary on the politics, and neither would have accepted so crude a derivation, yet the architecture is visible in the finished structures, and students who trace it understand why the national argument could not be compromised: each side's politics rested on a political economy the other regarded as refuted, and the refutations themselves, hers of his concentration on monopoly, his tradition's charge that her reproduction schemes contained a technical error about the departments, were conducted with the same totality as everything else between their schools.

A parallel substructure runs beneath the organisational argument and completes the pattern. His economics of the party, if the phrase may be risked, treats organisation as capital: a stock of accumulated capacity, built in advance of the crisis, depreciating under repression, yielding its return at the moment when the movement's spontaneous income peaks and must be invested or lost. Her economics of the party treats organisation as a flow: a capacity regenerated continuously by the movement's activity, incapable of being stockpiled because its substance is the class's own educated initiative, which decays in storage exactly as a strike fund's meaning decays when the strikes are forbidden. The metaphors are this guide's, not theirs, and are offered as instruments for the reader rather than as attributions. Used carefully, they predict each side's characteristic judgements across the whole debate: the stock theorist will always favour demarcation, reserves and the defence of the apparatus; the flow theorist will always favour openness, activity and the renewal of the ranks; and the retrospective's two failure modes translate without remainder, the stock confiscated by its own custodians, the flow dissipated for want of a reservoir at the flood. A debate whose substructures align so completely across its surface disputes is not a bundle of separate quarrels but a single, coherent division within a tradition, and its coherence is the final reason it rewards the whole-table reading this guide has practised.


## The peasantry and the land: the shared blind spot

A debate guide confined to the questions its principals argued loudest would inherit their silences, and the loudest shared silence in this one concerns the class that formed the majority of the population in the country where the argument was settled. Russia in 1904 was a peasant empire, and any organisation claiming to make its revolution would live or die by its relations with the villages. His treatment of the question was strategic and central: the Two Tactics of 1905 built the entire revolutionary prospect on an alliance in which the proletariat would lead the peasantry through the democratic revolution against the autocracy and the landlords, and the land programme, confiscation of the great estates, was designed as the alliance's cement. Her treatment was oblique for most of her career, the Polish and German countrysides figuring in her work chiefly as reservoirs of political backwardness, until the Russian manuscript forced the question on her and produced her most controversial judgement: the Bolsheviks' slogan of land to the peasants, she argued, had surrendered the countryside to small property, creating overnight a vast class of proprietors whose interest in defending their plots would stand as the revolution's internal frontier for generations. The judgement scandalised readers who filed her as the democrat of the pair, since it arraigned the revolution for conceding the one demand its rural majority actually made, and it remains the count of her audit on which subsequent scholarship has been kindest to his side: the land settlement of 1917, whatever its long costs, purchased the regime's survival through the civil war by giving the villages a revolution they would defend against the Whites' restoration.

The asymmetry is instructive about both frameworks. His organisational doctrine, so often taxed with indifference to the lived movement, was joined throughout to a sociological attentiveness toward classes outside the proletariat, an attentiveness that treated alliances as quantities to be calculated and concessions as instruments; the party of the from-without doctrine proved, in the villages, remarkably willing to learn its programme from the peasants' own seizures of the estates, ratifying in the land decree a distribution it had not designed. Her doctrine of the movement's self-education, by contrast, operated with a restricted definition of the movement: the industrial class educating itself through struggle is the manuscript's implicit subject, and the peasantry appears as an object of policy rather than as a subject whose activity might also educate. Neither framework was refuted by the land question, but each was exposed at the edge of its favourite category, and a complete map of the debate should mark the territory where both run out: the agrarian majority of the old empires, whose revolution neither the committee nor the mass strike, as theorised in 1904, had fully imagined.

## Error, correction and the education of leaders

One thread has run through every section of this guide without yet receiving its own treatment, and drawing it together clarifies why the debate retains its hold on readers with no stake in its century. Both antagonists were, before anything else, theorists of how political movements learn, and their disagreement reduces, at the deepest level, to rival accounts of where knowledge resides while a movement is learning it. In her account, knowledge is generated at the periphery and validated in action: the strike committee discovers what the programme means by attempting it, the local organisation reads its own town better than any centre can, and the leadership's highest function is to gather the discoveries, generalise them into line and slogan, and return them to the movement as its own clarified experience. Error in such a system is not waste but tuition, and the system's health is measured by the speed at which correction travels upward. In his account, knowledge is generated where theory is produced and validated by its grip on the whole: the centre exists because the view from any single factory gate is partial, because the enemy's strategy is visible only in aggregate, and because the movement's discoveries, ungathered, remain local property and die with the local struggle. Error in such a system is a cost to be minimised by foresight, and the system's health is measured by the quality of the gathering institution and the discipline with which its conclusions are executed.

Neither account is refuted by the other's evidence, because each describes a real circuit in any functioning movement, and functioning movements run both circuits at once or fail in one of the two directions the retrospective catalogued. The debate's enduring value to a reader lies in the precision with which the two accounts specify their circuits' failure points. Hers fails at aggregation: discoveries that never become line, energy that never becomes decision, a movement educated in everything except the concentration of its force at the appointed hour. His fails at feedback: conclusions that cannot be appealed by experience, a centre whose information degrades as its authority grows, a movement whose tuition is confiscated in the name of sparing it error. An organisation designed by a reader of the whole debate would be engineered, self-consciously, against both failures at once, with protected channels for correction travelling upward and protected instruments for decision travelling down, and the protection in each case constitutional rather than customary, since both antagonists learned that customs are what emergencies eat first. Whether such a design is achievable is a question the twentieth century left open and the twenty-first has not closed; that the design problem can be stated so exactly is the debate's bequest, and it is stated nowhere more exactly than in the exchange between the woman who trusted the movement's errors and the man who trusted the committee's foresight, each of whom understood the other's trust well enough to name its price.

## The personal record: opponents, not enemies

Forum versions of the debate reliably ask whether the two hated each other, and the question, naive as it sounds, opens a documentary record worth assembling in one place, because the answer disciplines the whole interpretation. They moved in the same small world for two decades: the congresses of the Second International, the exile colonies of Zurich, Munich, Kraków and Paris, the editorial offices and party schools of a movement whose senior figures could be seated in a single hall. She attended the London congress of the Russian party in 1907 as a representative of the Polish organisation, in the very citadel of his faction's struggle, and the protocols record no discourtesy; both addressed the International's extraordinary congress at Basel in 1912, summoned as the Balkan wars threatened the general conflagration, and the anti-war manifesto they jointly supported there became the standard against which 1914 was judged a betrayal. The Stuttgart amendment of 1907, their one documented legislative collaboration, has been cited in this guide for its substance; it belongs here for its form, since revolutionaries do not ordinarily draft together with opponents they despise, and the drafting was remembered in both camps as businesslike.

The written traces run in the same direction. His marginalia and correspondence engage her arguments at the level of an antagonist whose agreement would be worth having, and his published references across the years, even at maximum polemical distance on the national question, treat the Polish doctrine as an error of the serious, to be refuted by argument rather than dismissed by label. Her side maintains the distinction with equal consistency: the wartime writings separate the man, whose intransigence she praised while his party's machinery stood arraigned in her earlier work, from the organisational doctrine, and the prison manuscript's severities are addressed to a government's measures in the grammar of a comrade's audit, not an enemy's indictment. The two people who emerge from the personal record are recognisably inhabitants of the same moral universe as their texts: severe, unsentimental, capable of a ferocity in print that never quite becomes contempt, and united, beneath every dispute, by the shared and unusual conviction that the argument mattered more than the relationship. Posterity's feud was assembled because feuds are narratable; the record supports an argument, conducted at the highest level of seriousness by two people who expected to be answered. A debate read in that expectation, with each side's case stated as its author would have demanded, is the debate this guide has attempted to write, and the personal record is offered as the final exhibit in its defence.

## Why the argument refuses to stay settled

It remains to ask why this particular quarrel, among the hundreds the movement's heroic age produced, is the one each generation reopens, and the answer returns the reader to the claim with which this guide began its retrospective. Most famous debates decay into their verdicts: the record accumulates on one side, the loser's position becomes a period piece, and the exchange survives as a monument to a question nobody needs to ask again. This debate's questions have never stopped being asked, because the organisations that ask them keep reproducing the occasions. Wherever a movement builds an apparatus to act on history, the apparatus acquires the interests she anatomised, and someone reaches for the essay of 1904. Wherever a movement's energy outruns its instruments at a moment of crisis, the deficit he anatomised presents its bill, and someone reaches for the pamphlets of 1902. The two reaches are the debate's immortality, and they guarantee that its texts will be read as interventions in living arguments long after their authors' world has vanished, with all the distortion that such reading brings and all the vitality that makes the distortion worth correcting each time.

The correction, performed once more in summary, is the whole guide in miniature. Restore the titles their editors stole and the manuscripts their authors shelved. Restore the agreements the staging conceals and the phases the legends collapse. Restore the national question to its place as the deepest divide and the organisational question to its proportions as a dispute about design between two builders. Restore the warnings to their symmetry and the records to their evidence, the fortnight-old party beside the faction ban, the eagle sentence beside the ultra-centralist charge. What survives the restoration is not a smaller debate but a larger one, an argument capacious enough to contain two revolutions and honest enough to explain why both ended as they did, and a reader who closes the file at this point owns the instrument the series exists to distribute: the grid, the claim, and the discipline of holding the whole table in view.

## For students and exam candidates

Students meet this debate in examinations as a comparison question, and the marks live in the distinctions the caricature destroys. Open by separating the two disputes the tradition fuses: the organisational argument of the essay of 1904, about the design of a party, and the strategic argument of the mass strike pamphlet, about how mass action develops and what leadership means inside it. State her target precisely, a central committee prescribing tactics to a movement, and use her own label for it. Then deploy the agreement and disagreement grid as your essay's skeleton, spending your analysis where the rows are uneven: agreement on revisionism, war and parliament, division by degree on organisation, division by principle on the national question. The paired treatment in [luxemburg trotsky exam answers](/2014/12/15/luxemburg-trotsky-exam-answers/) drills the comparison format against a neighbouring debate. Close with the mutual confirmation as your evaluation, since it converts a verdict question, who was right, into an analytical one, which warnings the record sustained, and markers reward the conversion.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The grid at the centre of this guide was built to be stolen for the classroom, and the natural seminar format is a structured debate with the rows assigned as briefs: one group defends the organisational design against the ultra-centralism charge using the Russian underground's conditions as its evidence, another prosecutes the charge from the essay's text, and a third argues the national question, the row on which compromise proved impossible, from the Polish materials. Insist that advocates quote their principal's words rather than the tradition's summaries, and reserve the seminar's final movement for the packaging question, Wolfe's retitling and Levi's publication, so that students handle the transmission of the debate as part of its substance. The organisational question in its constitutional form can then be followed into the companion treatment of [democratic centralism explained](/2014/06/01/democratic-centralism-explained/), which gives the seminar its second session and its essay title.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Researchers should treat this debate as a case study in the politics of textual transmission as much as in organisational theory. The primary record is unusually well bounded: two short texts of 1904, the paired revolutionary writings of 1905 and 1906, the national essays and pamphlets of 1908 to 1916, the prison manuscript its author shelved, and a posthumous judgement of six sentences that both traditions have mined beyond its weight. Each text has an editorial history that belongs in the footnote apparatus of any serious treatment, the venues of first publication, the Menshevik Iskra and Kautsky's journal, the circumstances of Levi's disobedient publication of the manuscript in 1922, and the Wolfe edition of 1961 whose supplied title reframed the essay for the Anglophone world. Journalists covering modern movements will find the debate's failure modes more reportable than its doctrines: the apparatus that cannot be corrected and the movement that cannot act are recognisable far beyond the tradition that named them, and the mutual confirmation offers a frame for that recognition which neither canonises nor buries its subjects.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did Luxemburg and Lenin disagree about?

Three questions divided them across fifteen years of argument. The first was organisational: how centralised a revolutionary party should be, and whether a central committee may legitimately prescribe tactics to the movement it leads. The second concerned mass action, where she held that revolutionary initiative rises from the class's own experience of struggle while he stressed the organisation that must be built to receive and direct that energy. The third, and by far the deepest, was the national question, where he championed the right of nations to self-determination as a democratic demand and she rejected the formula as a surrender of class politics to national movements. Around those three disputes stood a wide territory of agreement, on revisionism, on the war and on the limits of parliament, which the popular version of the quarrel almost never reports.

### Q: Was Luxemburg against organisation?

No, and the documentary record makes the charge untenable. She helped to found the Social Democracy of the Kingdom of Poland as a young woman, led it for a quarter of a century, taught at the German party's school in Berlin, edited party newspapers and drafted the programme of the Communist Party of Germany in the last fortnight of her life. What she opposed was a particular architecture of organisation, in which authority flows only downward, statutes police opinion, and a central committee treats the membership's independent activity as a danger to be supervised. Her own parties were disciplined and programmatic to a degree her critics called sectarian. The accurate summary is that she was an organisational thinker who believed the wrong design could ruin the right cause, not a romantic who believed causes needed no instruments.

### Q: What is the mass strike argument?

The argument, developed in her pamphlet of 1906 from the experience of the Russian revolution of the previous year, holds that a mass strike is not a weapon a leadership can manufacture, store and discharge on a chosen date. It is the characteristic form of a revolutionary period, arising from the accumulated grievances and confidence of millions, moving in waves that subside and return at higher levels, and educating its participants faster than any programme could. Organisations that imagine they can decree such a movement will discredit themselves; organisations that dismiss it as chaos will be left behind by it. The party's genuine tasks are different: to articulate the political aims toward which the movement is travelling, to generalise local struggles into common action, and to prepare the press, funds and coordination that let each wave leave durable gains behind it.

### Q: Did Luxemburg call Lenin's approach ultra-centralist?

Yes, the term is hers and it appears in the essay of 1904 as the precise name of her objection. The compound matters. Centralism itself she accepted as a defining feature of social democracy, the principle that overcomes the fragmentation of local struggles into a unified class movement. The prefix marks the point of excess: a centralism that ceases to coordinate a self-active movement and begins to replace it, in which the central committee becomes the only thinking element and the membership its executive instrument. By choosing a word of degree rather than a word of kind, she located the dispute where it actually lay, in a disagreement about how far central direction may go before it inverts into its opposite, and later summaries that drop the prefix convert her engineering argument into the caricature of an essence.

### Q: What did they disagree about on the national question?

He defended the right of nations to self-determination, including secession, as a democratic demand that socialists must champion, while reserving the party's judgement on whether any particular secession deserved support. His analogy was divorce law: defending the right to divorce obliges nobody to dissolve a marriage. She answered that the formula empowers a fiction, since a nation contains classes with opposed interests and the right is exercised in practice by the small nation's bourgeoisie, which enlists its workers behind a national project and bargains with the empires. In the age of imperialism, she held, independence programmes for the subject peoples were either empty or reactionary, and she proposed territorial autonomy for cultural rights instead. Poland, her founding case and his standing counterexample, kept the argument concrete and unreconciled for twenty years.

### Q: Was her essay really called Leninism or Marxism?

No. Her essay appeared in 1904 in the Russian paper Iskra and in the German theoretical journal Die Neue Zeit under the title Organisational Questions of Russian Social Democracy, a heading that announces a contribution to a movement discussion. The famous alternative was supplied nearly six decades later by the American editor Bertram D. Wolfe, who bound the essay together with her prison manuscript on the Russian Revolution in a volume published in 1961 and placed his own interrogative title over the collection. The substitution reframed a family argument about committee powers as a doctrinal war between Leninism and Marxism itself, and generations of English readers absorbed the frame as though it were the author's. Recovering the original title is the first step in recovering the essay.

### Q: What did Luxemburg and Lenin agree about?

More than the quarrel's reputation suggests. Both built their early reputations resisting revisionism, the revision of Marxism into a doctrine of gradual reform, and both held that daily reform work drew its meaning from the revolutionary aim it served. Both rejected the parliamentary road as a strategy of transformation while using elections and legislatures as platforms and gauges of strength. Both opposed the imperialist war from its first day, she in the Junius pamphlet written in prison, he at the head of the Zimmerwald left, and both pronounced the old International dead and demanded its refoundation. Both rested their politics on the conviction that the working class was capable of ruling society. The disagreements that fill the anthologies were conducted across this shared foundation, which is why the argument reads, in full, as an engineering dispute within a common project.

### Q: Whose criticism was borne out by events?

Each one's criticism of the other, which is the pattern this guide names the mutual confirmation. Her warning that an over-centralised party would substitute itself for the class was borne out in the Soviet record after power was taken: rival parties suppressed, soviets hollowed into administrative forms, factions banned inside the ruling party itself, and a bureaucracy that no internal mechanism proved able to correct. His warning that revolutionary energy without an organisation built in advance would fail at the decisive hour was borne out in Germany, where the party she co-founded was a fortnight old when the January crisis of 1919 arrived, no instrument existed capable of leading it, and she was murdered by the counter-revolution the movement could not defeat. Two failure modes, each forecast by the opponent best placed to see it.

### Q: Is spontaneity a fair description of her position?

Only in a narrow sense that the label usually violates. She used the idea to locate initiative: in a revolutionary period the impetus for mass action rises from the participants' own experience rather than descending from a headquarters, and leaderships spend such periods catching up with movements already educating themselves. She never held that the class needs no theory, no programme and no party; her life was spent supplying all three, in classrooms, newspapers and party schools of legendary rigour. Her mass strike pamphlet assigns the party the decisive work of articulating aims, generalising struggles and preparing organisation, withholding from it only the power to invent the movement itself. Spontaneist, as a summary of that position, mistakes the origin of energy for the absence of leadership, and she would have marked the mistake as the caricature it is.

### Q: Did Lenin respond to Luxemburg directly?

Not in print, and the asymmetry shaped the debate's afterlife. His organisational pamphlet of 1904, One Step Forward, Two Steps Back, appeared before her essay and answered his Menshevik opponents inside the Russian party; when her intervention followed in the Menshevik-controlled Iskra, he treated it in correspondence as a distinguished outsider's contribution to a faction fight he considered already settled, and answered the fight rather than the essay. The direct engagements between them came later and on other ground, above all the national question, where his writings on self-determination take her Polish doctrine as their principal Marxist target. His fullest published judgement arrived in 1922, in the Notes of a Publicist, where he catalogued her errors as his tradition counted them and then instructed his readers that, notwithstanding everything, she was and remains an eagle whose works would train generations.

### Q: How does the mutual confirmation reading change the Luxemburg and Lenin quarrel?

It changes the question the quarrel is used to answer. Read as a contest, the debate demands a winner, and two generations of partisans have supplied one by excerpting the half of the record their side requires. Read through the mutual confirmation, the debate becomes a diagnostic instrument: each antagonist, pressing on the other's project from inside a shared tradition, located the joint at which that project would fail, and the record of the two revolutions sustained both diagnoses, hers in the ruling party that outgrew correction, his in the movement that reached its crisis without an instrument. The quarrel then instructs rather than recruits. Its yield is a pair of failure modes to be designed against simultaneously, and its famous antagonists become, what their texts support, the two most accurate critics each other's tradition has produced.

### Q: What should a student separate between their organisational and strategic disagreements?

Three separations keep an examination answer out of the caricature. Separate first the texts: the organisational critique answers the Russian congress and the factional pamphlet of 1904, while the mass strike argument answers the revolution of 1905 and the German union leaders, with Lenin barely present in the second. Separate second the levels: the organisational dispute concerns the internal constitution of the party, who decides and who corrects, while the strategic dispute concerns the party's posture toward action it does not control, and a position on one does not dictate a position on the other. Separate third the trajectories: both thinkers modified their emphases under the pressure of 1905 and 1917, he toward open recruitment and soviet initiative, she toward the party's leadership and preparation, so that answers which freeze either figure at a single date misrepresent both.

### Q: Why did Luxemburg refuse to make her critique a public split?

Because she judged the critique an internal service to a revolution she claimed as her own, and because the situation it audited was changing under her pen. The manuscript on the Russian Revolution was written in prison in the autumn of 1918, left unfinished, and set aside after her release; in her last weeks she argued, in substance, that the Bolsheviks' deformities were principally the product of their isolation, above all of the German movement's failure, whose repair in the streets of Berlin was the work she chose instead. Publishing the indictment without its revised context would have armed the revolution's enemies, the social democratic government then hunting her among them, against comrades whose rising she had saluted as the salvation of international socialism's honour. Paul Levi's posthumous publication in 1922, against her closest heirs' protests, converted an internal memorandum into the public split she had declined to make.

### Q: How did each side's later record bear on the other's warnings?

With an exactness that still startles readers who meet the record whole. The organisation he built took power and then exhibited, stage by stage, the substitution her essay had described as a logic rather than an intention: opposition parties and press suppressed during the civil war, the soviets reduced to administrative shells, factions prohibited inside the ruling party in 1921, and an officialdom accountable upward that his own final notes attacked without finding a remedy his tradition could supply. The tradition she built reached its crisis in Germany with the deficit he had always predicted would be fatal: a revolutionary party founded a fortnight before the January fighting, no national apparatus, no agreed strategy, a rising its own leadership judged premature and could neither lead nor halt, and its founders murdered by troops serving a social democratic government.

### Q: What did the two agree on that their followers often forget?

They agreed on the destination and on most of the map's largest features, and their followers forget it because the quarrel is more useful to them than the alliance. Both were formed by the fight against revisionism and held that reform work without a revolutionary aim decays into administration of the existing order. Both treated parliament as a tribune and a census, never as the road to power. Both refused the patriotic war of 1914 when their movement collapsed into it, and both worked, she from a cell and he from exile, to refound internationalism on the refusal. Both, at Stuttgart in 1907, had jointly carried the amendment committing the International to exploit a war crisis for revolution. And both staked everything on the working class's capacity to rule, differing over the engineering of that capacity rather than over its existence.
