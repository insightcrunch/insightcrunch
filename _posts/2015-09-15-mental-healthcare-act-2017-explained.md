## The Question the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 Answers

The Mental Healthcare Act 2017 begins from a proposition that the law it replaced never entertained: a person with mental illness is presumed able to decide about their own treatment, and anyone who says otherwise must prove it. Every structure in the Act, the advance directive, the nominated representative, the chapter of enforceable rights, the rules on admission, the review boards, is machinery built around that single presumption. The statute this article explains is therefore best read backwards from its premise. Under the Mental Health Act 1987, the operative legal question was how a person could lawfully be taken into a psychiatric hospital and kept there. Under the Act of 2017, the operative question is whether the person can decide, and only after that question is answered against the person does any machinery of compulsion come into play, fenced with time limits, reporting duties and review. That reversal, from custody to capacity, is the claim on which the whole statute stands, and it is the thread this article follows through the text, the institutions, the enforcement record and the honest limits of what the Act has delivered.

A note on method before the substance. This article states the law from the text of the Act, citing the section for every proposition that carries weight, and it attributes every figure about the world outside the text to the source that published it: the parliamentary answers are identified by their question numbers and dates, the survey figures to the National Mental Health Survey, and the litigation submissions as submissions. Where the Act's text contains the occasional imperfect cross-reference, of which the definition of the Board and the emergency treatment reference inside the advance directive chapter are examples, the article follows the operative provision rather than the cross-reference a point the article does not labour, since the substance is not in doubt. Readers who work with the Act professionally will find that habit, trusting the operative section over the stray reference, is the one the courts and the Boards themselves have had to adopt.

The article is a pillar treatment. It states the formal identity of the Act and the record of its making, works through the provisions that carry the weight in connected prose, describes the admission framework as a clinician or a family would meet it, sets out the treatment restrictions, explains the provision on attempted suicide and its relationship with the penal codes, gives the clinical objection to the design and the rights answer to that objection at full strength, and closes with an assessment that separates the text, which is widely admired, from the record, which is uneven. The wider map of health legislation in which this statute sits is drawn in [the thematic guide to health laws in India](/2015/06/01/health-laws-india-complete-guide/), and this article stays inside its own statute except where a cross-reference is unavoidable.

## What the Act Is: Identity, Commencement and Design

The Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 is Act 10 of 2017. It received the assent of the President on 7 April 2017 and was brought into force on 29 May 2018 by notification S.O. 2173(E), published in the Gazette on the same day. Section 126 repealed the Mental Health Act, 1987, which is Act 14 of 1987, and Section 117 armed the Central Government with a transitory power to frame interim schemes for persons still governed by the older statute, so that the change of regime would not strand anyone mid-treatment. The Act extends to the whole of India. It runs to 128 sections arranged in sixteen chapters, and its architecture is itself an argument: the chapters on capacity, advance directives, nominated representatives and rights come first, in Chapters II to V, and the chapters on admission and on offences come near the end, in Chapters XII and XV. A reader who wants to know what the statute thinks matters most can read the table of contents as a ranking.

The long title states the purpose: to provide for mental healthcare and services for persons with mental illness and to protect, promote and fulfil the rights of such persons during delivery of mental healthcare and services. The preamble then does something unusual in Indian health legislation. It recites that the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities and its Optional Protocol were adopted at the United Nations Headquarters in New York on 13 December 2006 and came into force on 3 May 2008, that India signed and ratified the Convention on 1 October 2007, and that it was therefore necessary to align and harmonise the existing laws with the Convention. The statute thus declares its own source. It is a harmonisation measure, enacted because the older law could not be reconciled with the obligations India had taken on, most sharply the guarantee in Article 12 of the Convention that persons with disabilities enjoy legal capacity on an equal basis with others, the protection of liberty in Article 14, the right in Article 19 to live independently and be included in the community, and the health guarantee in Article 25, which includes a direction that health insurance be provided without discrimination. The domestic companion statute enacted under the same obligation is the subject of [the pillar on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016](/2017/08/01/rpwd-act-2016-explained/), and the two Acts should be read as siblings: one general, one specific to mental healthcare.

Three design choices follow from that source and run through everything below. The first is that rights are stated as entitlements held by the person, not as standards of good behaviour expected of institutions, and each right is paired with a forum, usually the Mental Health Review Board, before which its denial can be taken. The second is that compulsion is drafted as an exception with a clock on it: supported admission is authorised in measured periods, reported to a board within days, and reviewed on application as a matter of right. The third is that the Act regulates by registration and standards rather than by licensing discretion: a mental health establishment must be registered with the relevant Authority, must meet notified minimum standards, and is audited, inspected and liable to have its registration cancelled, with an appeal to the High Court. The older Act licensed psychiatric hospitals through a licensing authority whose inquiry was largely about premises. The newer Act registers establishments against published standards and then polices them through audits and a complaints route that runs from the establishment to the Board to the State Authority.

## The Law Before: Custody, Licensing and the Reception Order

### What did the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 replace?

It replaced the Mental Health Act 1987, which had itself replaced the Indian Lunacy Act 1912 and was principally a statute about licensing psychiatric hospitals and regulating admission and detention through magistrates' reception orders. The 2017 Act repealed it outright by Section 126 and substituted a rights framework built on capacity, consent, advance directives and review boards.

The Indian Lunacy Act 1912 belonged to the world of the asylum. Its vocabulary was the vocabulary of its century, and its legal machinery was built around certification and confinement. The Mental Health Act 1987, which received assent on 22 May 1987 and came into force on 1 April 1993, was a genuine modernisation of that machinery and should not be caricatured. It renamed the institutions as psychiatric hospitals and psychiatric nursing homes, created Central and State Authorities for Mental Health Services to regulate and coordinate, devoted a chapter to the protection of human rights of mentally ill persons, under which no person was to be subjected during treatment to any indignity or cruelty, and built a detailed admission code. But the centre of gravity of the 1987 Act was unmistakably institutional. Its chapters ran to licensing, admission and detention, inspection and discharge, judicial inquisition regarding persons alleged to be mentally ill who possessed property, the cost of maintenance, and penalties. The person appeared in it chiefly as the subject of an admission procedure or of a property inquiry, not as the holder of a portfolio of rights against the state and the treating institution.

The admission machinery shows the premise most clearly. Alongside voluntary admission, the 1987 Act provided for admission under special circumstances on the application of a relative or friend supported by medical certificates, and for reception orders made by a Magistrate, on the application of a relative or of a police officer, authorising detention in a psychiatric hospital. Detention could then continue on the strength of those orders, and the person's route out ran through discharge procedures administered by the same institutional world that had admitted them. There was no statutory presumption of capacity, because the question of capacity was not the question the Act asked. There was no advance directive, because the idea that a person might bind their own future treatment belonged to a legal imagination the statute did not have. There was no right to insurance parity, no right to community living, no right to access one's own medical records as a statutory entitlement, and no district-level board charged with reviewing whether a detention should continue. The guardianship provisions of the 1987 Act, under which a district court could appoint a guardian for a person found incapable of looking after himself or his property, completed the picture: incapacity was a status to be declared, and once declared it carried the person's legal personality with it.

What exposed the framework was not doctrine but fire. On 6 August 2001, at Erwadi in Ramanathapuram district in Tamil Nadu, a fire in a thatched building used as an asylum attached to a place of religious healing killed more than twenty-five inmates, who could not escape because they were chained to poles or beds. The Supreme Court took suo motu cognisance in proceedings titled In Re: Death of 25 Chained Inmates in Asylum Fire in Tamil Nadu, and by its order of 5 February 2002 recorded the submission of the amicus curiae that the Mental Health Act 1987 was not being implemented by the authorities at all, and the Attorney General's concession that the Act was for the benefit of mentally ill persons and required to be implemented earnestly. The case matters to this article for two reasons. It demonstrated that the licensing premise of the 1987 Act had failed on its own terms, since chained confinement persisted in establishments outside the regulatory net altogether. And it fixed the image that the 2017 Act's prohibition of chaining, stated in Section 95 in the plainest possible words, was written to answer. When Parliament later enacted that no person with mental illness shall be chained in any manner or form whatsoever, it was legislating against a named national memory, not an abstraction.

The machinery of the 1987 Act deserves one more paragraph of detail, because the 2017 Act is unintelligible except as its answer. Its licensing chapter required every psychiatric hospital and psychiatric nursing home to hold a licence from a licensing authority, granted for five years after an inquiry into the premises, the staff and the applicant's fitness, and the Central and State Authorities for Mental Health Services created by the Act were charged with regulating, coordinating and supervising that system. Its property chapter empowered a district court, on a judicial inquisition, to find that a person was incapable of looking after himself or managing his property and to appoint a guardian of the person and a manager of the property, a jurisdiction exercised over the person's legal personality as a whole rather than decision by decision. Its admission chapters contained one provision that pointed beyond the statute's own premises: a voluntarily admitted patient could not be kept for more than twenty-four hours after a request for discharge unless two medical certificates supported a fresh admission under special circumstances, a distant ancestor of the twenty-four hour assessment hold in Section 88 of the newer Act. The 1987 text was not careless drafting. It was a considered statement of a custodial model, and it is the model, more than any single provision, that the 2017 Act repealed.

Nor was the failure only textual. In the years around and after the Erwadi fire, the National Human Rights Commission took up the condition of mental hospitals as a standing concern, and its reviews reported overcrowding, poor sanitation, custodial rather than therapeutic regimes, and patients retained in institutions long after any clinical justification for their stay had passed. The Supreme Court's oversight in the Erwadi proceedings ran in parallel, with the Court calling for reports on the implementation of the 1987 Act across the States. The record those exercises built matters to the 2017 Act because it shaped the drafting choices: a statute whose predecessor had failed through non-implementation by States was written with its own reporting lines, registers, deadlines and district forums built into the text, and with repeal coupled to a transitory power so that the change of regime would not itself become an excuse for institutional drift. Whether that armour has worked is the question the implementation record answers at the end of this article; that the armour exists at all is explained by the record of the 1990s and 2000s.

The Erwadi proceedings also produced directions, and they are worth recording because they mark the point at which supervision of the sector passed from scandal to system. The Court called upon the State Governments to report on the state of implementation of the 1987 Act within their territories, and the proceedings treated the existence of unlicensed establishments housing chained residents not as a series of local crimes but as a regulatory failure to be answered State by State. The longer consequence was a change in what governments could be asked in public: after Erwadi, the questions about mental hospitals, in Parliament, in human rights proceedings and in the press, were questions about licensing, inspection and implementation of a statute that admitted of no answer except compliance or default, and the 2017 Act was drafted in full knowledge of that change, which is one reason its own text is so unusually specific about registers, deadlines, funds and reports. A legislature that has watched a predecessor statute fail for want of machinery writes the machinery into the next one.

The deeper failure of the older regime was conceptual and the Erwadi litigation only dramatised it. A statute organised around admission has nothing to say to the person who is never admitted, and most people with mental illness are never admitted anywhere. It has nothing to say about insurance, about information, about confidentiality, about the right to see one's records, about treatment in the community, or about the person who attends a clinic as an outpatient and is entitled to be dealt with as a patient like any other. The National Mental Health Survey of India 2015 to 2016, conducted by the National Institute of Mental Health and Neuro Sciences, measured the terrain the old statute could not reach: a current prevalence of mental disorders of 10.6 per cent among adults, a lifetime prevalence of 13.7 per cent, and a treatment gap, the distance between needing care and receiving it, of between 70 and 92 per cent across disorders. A licensing statute addressed to hospitals could not close a gap that existed mostly outside hospitals. The replacement statute would have to be addressed to the person, to the insurer, to the district, and to the government itself. That is the statute the Convention required and the statute Parliament eventually enacted.

## From Convention to Statute: How the 2017 Act Was Made

The drafting road ran through two Bills. A Mental Health Care Bill was introduced in the Rajya Sabha on 19 August 2013 and was referred to the Department-related Parliamentary Standing Committee on Health and Family Welfare, whose examination of the Bill was extensive and whose recommendations pushed the text further in the direction of rights, including on the questions of advance directives, the decriminalisation of attempted suicide and the composition and powers of the review boards. The government accepted a large part of that direction and brought forward a revised Bill in 2016 rather than reviving the earlier text. The Mental Healthcare Bill, 2016 was passed by the Rajya Sabha in August 2016 with a substantial set of amendments moved by the government, and by the Lok Sabha in March 2017, receiving assent on 7 April 2017. Commencement then waited on the machinery: the Central Government had to frame rules, the Central Authority had to be established under Section 33 within nine months of assent, and States had to be put in a position to constitute their Authorities and Boards. The Act was brought into force on 29 May 2018, and the gap between assent and commencement, a little over a year, is the first entry in the implementation ledger this article returns to at the end: the statute's promises run from commencement, but its institutions had to be built after the text was already law.

The constitutional basis of the statute deserves a word, because public health and hospitals sit principally in the State List of the Seventh Schedule, and a Union statute of this reach in a State field requires its footing to be stated. The footing is the preamble itself. Article 253 of the Constitution empowers Parliament to make any law for the whole or any part of India for implementing an international treaty, agreement or convention, notwithstanding the distribution of legislative powers, and the Act's recital of the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, of India's signature and ratification on 1 October 2007, and of the necessity of aligning and harmonising the existing laws with it, states on the face of the statute the basis on which Parliament legislated. The drafting consequence is visible throughout: because the Act rests on a harmonisation obligation, its provisions are written as a single national code, uniform in its rights, its admission periods and its board structure from State to State, rather than as a model law offered for State adoption. The price of that uniformity is the implementation pattern the record shows, a national text dependent for its machinery on the very State administrations whose legislative field it entered, with State rules, State Authorities and district Boards as the points at which the uniform code meets uneven local capacity.

Two features of the passage record deserve attention because they explain the text. The first is that the decriminalisation provision survived the committee stage and both Houses without being traded away, which was not a foregone outcome: the proposition that a person who attempts suicide should be presumed to be under severe stress rather than prosecuted had been recommended by the Law Commission of India in its Two Hundred and Tenth Report in 2008, on the humanisation and decriminalisation of attempt to suicide, and had waited nearly a decade for a legislative vehicle. The Mental Healthcare Bill became that vehicle, which is why the provision sits in a health statute rather than in an amendment of the Penal Code. The second is that the insurance parity clause was enacted in absolute terms. Section 21(4) does not say that insurers shall endeavour to provide cover, or that cover shall be provided on prescribed terms; it says every insurer shall make provision for medical insurance for treatment of mental illness on the same basis as is available for treatment of physical illness. The absoluteness of that sentence is what later forced the insurance regulator to direct compliance, and what later allowed courts and complaint forums to treat a psychiatric exclusion in a post-commencement policy as untenable. Drafting choices of that kind, a presumption stated without qualification, a duty stated without a discretion, are the load-bearing decisions of a statute, and this Act made several of them deliberately.

The subordinate legislation followed commencement closely at the centre. The Central Government framed the Mental Healthcare (Central Mental Health Authority and Mental Health Review Boards) Rules, 2018, governing the Authorities and Boards, and the Mental Healthcare (Rights of Persons with Mental Illness) Rules, 2018, prescribing forms and procedures for several of the rights in Chapter V, and the Central Authority followed with regulations addressing, among other things, the manner of making advance directives and the minimum standards framework for establishments. The pattern set at the centre is part of the statute's story: the Union built its layer of the machinery on time, and the corresponding State layer, State rules framed by State Governments and minimum standards enforced through State Authorities, is where the record thins, as the implementation section documents. A drafter reading the Act should also note Section 114, the special provision for the north-eastern and hill States, which extends several of the Act's constitution and compliance timelines for those States, Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim and Tripura, together with Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir, Ladakh, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and Lakshadweep, for a period of ten years from commencement, an acknowledgment inside the text that a single timetable could not honestly be imposed on States with very different institutional starting points.

## The Threshold Question: What Counts as Mental Illness

Before capacity comes definition, because the Act's protections attach to a person with mental illness and its machinery is triggered by determinations made under Section 3. Section 2(s) defines mental illness as a substantial disorder of thinking, mood, perception, orientation or memory that grossly impairs judgment, behaviour, capacity to recognise reality or ability to meet the ordinary demands of life, together with mental conditions associated with the abuse of alcohol and drugs. The definition expressly excludes mental retardation, described in the Act as a condition of arrested or incomplete development of mind specially characterised by subnormality of intelligence, which is dealt with under the disability statutes instead. Two features of the definition carry legal weight. It is a threshold definition with the word substantial and the phrase grossly impairs doing real work: distress, eccentricity and ordinary unhappiness are outside it. And it is deliberately functional rather than diagnostic in form, describing what the disorder does to judgment, behaviour and the capacity to meet ordinary demands, rather than annexing a list of named conditions.

Section 3 then disciplines the determination itself. Mental illness is to be determined in accordance with nationally or internationally accepted medical standards, including the International Classification of Diseases of the World Health Organization in the edition notified by the Central Government. No person or authority may classify a person as having a mental illness except for purposes directly relating to treatment or as covered under the Act or another law. A determination may not be made on the basis of political, economic or social status, or membership of a cultural, racial or religious group, or on the basis of non-conformity with moral, social, cultural, work or political values or religious beliefs prevailing in the person's community. Past treatment or hospitalisation is relevant but does not by itself justify a present or future determination. And the determination of mental illness does not by itself mean that the person is of unsound mind unless a competent court has so declared. These are not decorative clauses. They are the statute's answer to a documented history, in many legal systems including this one, of psychiatric classification being used against dissenters, against women whose families found them inconvenient, and against persons whose non-conformity was mistaken for pathology. The section converts that history into five specific prohibitions on the act of classification itself, and any determination made in breach of them is vulnerable in proceedings before a Board or a court.

## The Cast of the Act: Definitions That Do Work

Section 2 repays reading because several of its definitions carry operative consequences. A care-giver is a person who resides with a person with mental illness and is responsible for providing care to that person, a definition that recognises the household as the real first institution of Indian mental healthcare and gives the care-giver defined places in the scheme, in the default order for nominated representatives, in discharge planning and in the Board's composition. A mental health professional is defined by qualification and registration, embracing the clinical psychologist, the mental health nurse and the psychiatric social worker holding the prescribed postgraduate qualifications, while a psychiatrist is a medical practitioner with a postgraduate degree or diploma in psychiatry, and the Act keeps the two categories distinct because several of its procedures, the examination pairs for supported admission among them, turn on the difference. A mental health establishment is defined widely, as a health establishment, wholly or partly meant for the care of persons with mental illness, where such persons are admitted and reside at, or are kept for care, treatment, convalescence or rehabilitation, and the definition expressly reaches government and private facilities, ayurvedic and other systems of care, and convalescence and rehabilitation homes, which is why the registration net in Chapter X is cast so much wider than the licensing net of the older Act, which reached only institutions styling themselves psychiatric hospitals or nursing homes. A medical officer in charge is the person in administrative charge of an establishment, and it is on that office, not on the treating clinician, that the Act fastens several of its duties, to inform, to report admissions to the Board and to receive complaints. The definitions also work outward. The Board is defined by reference to its constitution under the Act, and throughout the admission chapters the statute works with the idea of the least restrictive care option possible in the circumstances, which the examining professionals must certify, after taking any advance directive into account, every time a supported admission is made or extended under Section 89 or Section 90. Definitions in a statute of this kind are not a glossary; they are the load paths, and a reader who skips Section 2 will misjudge where the weight falls in every later chapter.

One expression recurs so often that it must be fixed before the substantive chapters are read: the appropriate Government. Throughout the Act the phrase distributes duties between the Union and the States by subject rather than by territory: in relation to establishments and institutions owned or controlled by the Central Government it means the Central Government, and in other cases the State Government of the State in which the establishment or the person is situated, with the Union Territory administrations standing in the State position for their territories. The distribution explains the Act's recurring double structure, a Central Authority and State Authorities, Central rules and State rules, a national register fed by State registers, and it explains why the implementation record is necessarily a State-by-State record: for most establishments, most Boards and most services, the appropriate Government whose duty a right engages is the State, and the Union's levers are the standards, the money it grants and the parliamentary questions it must answer.

## Capacity: The Presumption on Which Everything Else Stands

Chapter II contains a single section and the most important sentence in the Act. Section 4 provides that every person, including a person with mental illness, shall be deemed to have capacity to make decisions regarding mental healthcare or treatment if such person has the ability to understand information relevant to the decision on treatment or admission or personal assistance, to appreciate any reasonably foreseeable consequence of a decision or lack of decision on the subject, and to communicate the decision by means of speech, expression, gesture or any other means. Section 4(2) requires that information be given to the person in a language they understand or in sign language or by visual aids or any other means, so that they can understand it. Section 4(3) closes the door through which the presumption would otherwise leak: merely because a person arrives at a decision perceived to be inappropriate or wrong, that by itself is not a ground to conclude that the person lacks capacity. The design is worth pausing on. Capacity is presumed, not granted. It is decision-specific, turning on understanding, appreciation and communication for the decision at hand, not on diagnosis, intelligence or agreement with the treating team. It is supported rather than merely tested, because the duty to present information in an accessible form falls on the system. And it is insulated against the most common route of its defeat, the inference from an unwise choice to an incapable chooser.

The operation of the test in practice is worth spelling out, because Section 4 is applied at bedsides and admission desks rather than in appellate courts. The assessment is of the person's ability for the particular decision at the particular time, not of the person's general competence: a person may be able to decide about medication, with information given slowly and in their own language, on a morning when they are settled, and be unable to decide about discharge on an evening when they are acutely unwell, and the Act treats those as separate questions with separate answers. The information limb places the first move on the professional: before any conclusion about capacity is available, the information relevant to the decision must actually have been communicated in a form the person can use, which converts what looks like a test of the patient into a duty of the system. The appreciation limb is the one that does the clinical work, since it asks not whether the person can repeat information back but whether they can grasp what a decision, or the absence of one, will foreseeably mean for them. And the communication limb is deliberately medium-neutral, counting speech, expression, gesture or any other means, so that a person who cannot speak is not for that reason alone placed outside the presumption. Read with Section 3, which forbids treating a determination of mental illness as a determination of unsoundness of mind, the chapter completes a decoupling that older Indian law never performed: diagnosis over here, capacity over there, and a statutory wall between them that only a decision-specific assessment, honestly recorded, is allowed to cross.

The presumption also orders the rest of the admission code. Section 14(9) states that all persons with mental illness shall have capacity to make mental healthcare or treatment decisions but may require varying levels of support from their nominated representative to make decisions, and Section 14(8) provides that the very fact of having a nominated representative is not to be construed as a lack of capacity. Section 85(2) directs that all admissions, as far as possible, shall be independent admissions of persons who are in a position to make their own decisions, unless supported admission is unavoidable. The Act's consent architecture is therefore a ladder: the person decides alone where they can; the person decides with support where support is needed; a decision is made for the person only within the supported admission and treatment provisions, and even there the statute keeps capacity under review, requiring reassessment at stated intervals during a supported admission and restoring the person's own authority whenever capacity is found to have returned. Consent under this Act is not a single signature on a form; it is a continuing legal state that the system must keep checking. The comparison is instructive for readers of the wider health statutes: the consent framework for organ donation, where the authorisation of the donor or of near relatives determines what may lawfully be removed and when, is examined in [the pillar on the Transplantation of Human Organs Act 1994](/2015/09/01/transplantation-human-organs-act-1994/), and the two statutes share a premise that in Indian health law consent is a structure of graded permissions, not a formality.

## The Advance Directive: Deciding in Advance

Chapter III, Sections 5 to 13, enacts the instrument that expresses the capacity premise most sharply. An advance directive is a written statement by which a person who is not a minor specifies in advance how they wish, and how they do not wish, to be cared for and treated for mental illness, and may nominate, in order of precedence, the individuals to be their nominated representative. Section 5 makes four design choices that define the instrument. It may be made irrespective of the person's past mental illness or treatment, so it is available to anyone, not only to those with a diagnosis. It is invoked only when the person ceases to have capacity to make treatment decisions, and continues in force until the person regains capacity. A decision made by the person while they have capacity, even if contrary to the directive, must be followed instead of the directive, so the instrument is a bridge across episodes of incapacity and never a cage around a capacitous choice. And a directive contrary to law is void from the beginning. The manner and procedure of making a directive is as specified by regulations of the Central Authority, and Section 7 obliges every Mental Health Review Board to maintain an online register of the directives registered with it and to make the register available to treating professionals as prescribed. Section 8 allows the maker to revoke, amend or cancel the directive at any time in the prescribed manner, and Section 9 removes the instrument from the one situation where delay is dangerous: an advance directive does not apply to emergency treatment under Section 94.

The duties and safeguards around the directive are as carefully drawn as the instrument itself. Section 10 obliges the medical officer or mental health professional in charge, and the treating psychiatrist, to follow a valid directive, subject to Section 11. Section 11 allows a professional, relative or care-giver who doubts a directive's applicability to apply to the Board, which may review, alter, modify or cancel it, but only after hearing all concerned parties and only after considering whether the directive was made in free will, free from coercion, whether it was intended to apply to the present circumstances, whether the maker was informed of its contents and consequences, whether the maker had capacity when making it, and whether it is contrary to law. A legal guardian may make a directive for a minor, terminable when the minor attains majority. Section 12 requires the Central Authority to review the use of directives periodically and to record the numbers made, the percentage revoked or cancelled, and the profile of the persons making them, so that the instrument is itself subject to audit. Section 13 protects the professional who follows a valid directive in good faith from liability for unforeseen consequences, and protects a professional from liability for not following a directive that was never given to them. The architecture is a negotiation between autonomy and safety conducted in advance and in writing: the person's earlier capacitous self binds the system, the Board holds a narrow power to set the instrument aside for stated reasons, and the professional who honours the record is not left exposed.

A directive is only as useful as the record that can be found when it is needed, and the Act's register is a public mechanism maintained by Boards that in many districts took years to be constituted. A person making a directive therefore has a practical interest in keeping the document, the nominated representative's details and the supporting medical records together in a form that a family can produce quickly and a treating team can trust. An encrypted personal archive is the natural way to hold that bundle: guidance on keeping sensitive documents so that they stay private yet retrievable by the people who must see them is set out in [the account of how journalists use VaultBook to protect sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/), whose methods for sealed, access-controlled files apply directly to an advance directive and the papers around it. The Indian instrument also has a constitutional parallel which should be kept distinct from it. In Common Cause v. Union of India (2018), a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court recognised advance medical directives for end-of-life care as part of the right to life with dignity under Article 21. That judgment concerns refusal of life-sustaining treatment in terminal conditions generally; the Mental Healthcare Act's directive is a statutory instrument confined to mental healthcare, with its own register, its own review power and its own statutory priority rules, and neither instrument borrows authority from the other.

One further feature of the directive deserves emphasis, because it determines how the instrument behaves inside the admission code. When professionals examine a person for a supported admission under Section 89 or Section 90, the certification they must record is not merely that the statutory risk grounds exist; it is that, after taking into account the advance directive, if any, the admission is the least restrictive care option possible in the circumstances. The directive therefore enters the hardest decision the Act authorises not as background colour but as a named ingredient of the certification, and a certification that ignores a registered directive is defective on the face of the section. Section 12's periodic review by the Central Authority, recording how many directives have been made, what percentage have been revoked or cancelled, and the profile of the persons making them, is the statute's own check on whether this machinery is being used at all: an instrument that exists in the text but is never made, never registered and never consulted would show up in those figures, and the review duty ensures that someone in the system is required, at intervals, to look at them.

## The Nominated Representative: Support, Not Substitution

Chapter IV, Sections 14 to 17, creates the office through which supported decision-making operates. Section 14 provides that every person who is not a minor shall have the right to appoint a nominated representative by writing on plain paper, with a signature or thumb impression, and the appointee must not be a minor, must be competent to perform the duties of the office, and must give written consent to the appointment. Where no representative has been appointed, Section 14(4) supplies a descending order of precedence: the individual named in the advance directive takes precedence; failing that, a relative; failing that, a care-giver; failing that, a suitable person appointed by the Board; and as the last resort, the Director of the Department of Social Welfare of the State, or a person designated by that Director, appointed by the Board. The person may revoke, alter or cancel an appointment at any time, and the Board may revoke an appointment it has made where the person's interest so requires. For a minor, Section 15 makes the legal guardian the nominated representative unless the Board, on application or of its own motion, is of the opinion that the guardian is not acting in the minor's best interests or is not fit to be the representative, in which case the Board appoints the Director of Social Welfare or a designated officer instead. Section 16 empowers the Board to revoke, alter or modify the categories of appointment it has power over, including appointments under the default order and appointments for minors.

The duties of the office are set by Section 17 and they are duties of fidelity, not of independent judgment. The representative must consider the current and past wishes, the life history, values, cultural background and best interests of the person, and must give particular credence to the person's views. The representative is entitled to information about diagnosis and treatment, is involved in discharge planning, may apply for the person's admission or discharge under the admission provisions, may apply to the Board against any violation of the person's rights, and holds defined consent functions, including for research under Section 99 and for the treatment decisions that arise during a supported admission where the person requires nearly total support. The contrast with the guardianship model of the older law is deliberate and complete. A guardian under the 1987 Act replaced the person in law for the matters covered by the guardianship. A nominated representative under the 2017 Act stands beside the person, carries the person's own values into rooms the person cannot enter, and can be displaced by the person at will. The office is the statute's institutional answer to the hardest question in the whole field: how to help a person decide without deciding for them, and its answer is to make the helper chosen, accountable, reviewable and bound by the person's expressed wishes rather than by the helper's view of what the person needs.

The default order in Section 14(4) repays attention as a piece of social drafting. It begins with the person's own choice, in the directive, and moves outward through the relationships that in practice surround a person in an Indian household, the relative and then the care-giver, reaching the state, in the Board and the Social Welfare Director, only when the private circle is empty. The proviso to the sub-section, under which a representative of an organisation registered under the Societies Registration Act 1860 that works for persons with mental illness may be temporarily engaged until the Board makes an appointment, recognises a real gap in that sequence: the interval between a person arriving at an establishment with no one, and a Board being moved to appoint. Section 15 applies the same protective logic to minors from the other direction: the legal guardian holds the office by default precisely because the guardianship already exists, but the Board may displace a guardian who is not acting in the minor's best interests or is not fit to hold the office, so that even the most natural appointment under the Act is a reviewable one. No appointment anywhere in Chapter IV is beyond the person's reach or the Board's supervision, and that, more than any single duty in Section 17, is what distinguishes the representative from the guardian the older law knew.

## The Rights Chapter: Sections 18 to 28

Chapter V is the Act's promise, and it is drafted as a set of eleven rights, each with a section, each framed as an entitlement of the person. Section 18 guarantees the right to access mental healthcare and treatment from services run or funded by the appropriate Government: services of affordable cost, good quality, sufficient quantity, geographically accessible, and without discrimination on any ground. The section specifies the range the system must aim at, acute outpatient and inpatient services, half-way homes, sheltered and supported accommodation, support to families, home-based rehabilitation, hospital and community-based rehabilitation, and services for children and older persons; requires mental health services to be integrated into general healthcare at all levels; confines long-term inpatient care to exceptional circumstances, for the shortest possible duration and as a last resort; requires minimum services to be available in each district; entitles a person, where the district minimum is unavailable, to treatment at another service in the district at government cost; and, in Section 18(7), provides that persons living below the poverty line, whether or not they hold such a card, and persons who are destitute or homeless, shall be entitled to treatment free of charge at government run or funded establishments and at establishments designated by the appropriate Government. Medicines on the Essential Drug List are to be available free of cost at government establishments from the community health centre upwards, and Section 18(11) closes the section by obliging the appropriate Government to make sufficient provision of budget for mental health services, so that the entitlement is tied, at least in the text, to the money that alone can honour it.

Section 18 contains two further disciplines that are easy to miss and that matter in practice. The range of services the appropriate Government must ensure includes services for children and for older persons, which obliges the system to plan for the two age bands whose care the general hospital model most often fails, and the integration requirement, that mental health services be integrated into general healthcare at all levels, forbids the administrative habit of treating psychiatric care as a separate vertical delivered only through specialised institutions. The long-term care clause cuts the other way with equal deliberateness: a person who needs long-term in-patient care is to receive it in an establishment only in exceptional circumstances, for the shortest duration possible and strictly as a measure of last resort, a clause written against the documented reality, recorded in the oversight reports of the older regime, of residents who entered an institution in a crisis and remained for decades because no discharge pathway existed. And where the district minimum is unavailable, the section does not leave the person with an apology: the entitlement is to treatment at another mental health service in the district at the cost of the appropriate Government, with rules to be prescribed for claiming reimbursement of interim costs, so that the failure of the public facility is priced to the government rather than to the patient.

Section 19 protects the right to live in the community: to live in, be part of and not be segregated from society, and not to continue to remain in an establishment merely because of non-acceptance by the family, homelessness, or the absence of community-based facilities, with the government obliged to provide support, including legal aid, for the person to exercise the right to live in the family home, and obliged within a reasonable period to provide and support less restrictive community establishments such as half-way homes and group homes. Section 20 protects the person from cruel, inhuman and degrading treatment: the right to live with dignity, to a safe and hygienic environment, sanitation, facilities for leisure, recreation, education and religious practices, privacy, clothing that protects dignity rather than institutional uniforms, protection from forced work without remuneration, wholesome food and items of personal hygiene including sanitary items for menstruation, protection against compulsory tonsuring, facilities directed at preparing the person for community living, and protection from all forms of physical, verbal, emotional and sexual abuse. Read against the Erwadi record, every clause in that list answers a documented practice.

Section 21 guarantees equality and non-discrimination. Every person with mental illness is to be treated as equal to persons with physical illness in the provision of all healthcare, including emergency facilities of the same quality and availability, ambulance services of the same quality and standard as for physical illness, and living conditions in establishments. A child below three years of a woman receiving care in an establishment is ordinarily not to be separated from her, separation being permitted only where the treating psychiatrist is of the opinion that separation is in the interest of the child's health and safety, with continued supervised access, review every fifteen days, and the approval of the Authority if separation crosses thirty days. And then Section 21(4) states the insurance duty in one sentence: every insurer shall make provision for medical insurance for treatment of mental illness on the same basis as is available for treatment of physical illness. The enforcement history of that sentence is part of the Act's story. The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India, by a circular dated 16 August 2018, directed all insurers to make provision for medical insurance for treatment of mental illness with immediate effect in compliance with Section 21(4), and followed it with a letter of 22 October 2018 asking insurers to place an underwriting policy for mental illness coverage in position. The standardisation guidelines of 27 September 2019 then barred insurers from excluding mental illness, stress and associated disorders from health policies, and a circular of 2 June 2020 required insurers to publish the approach they adopted to coverage of persons with mental illness. The duty nonetheless met resistance at the claims counter: in litigation reported before the Delhi High Court, a policyholder whose claim for treatment of a schizoaffective disorder had been rejected by the National Insurance Company under a psychiatric exclusion in a policy issued after the Act had commenced succeeded, the Court holding that the insurer had not given effect to Section 21(4) and that policies carrying such an exclusion in the two years from May 2018 were violative of the Act. The lesson the record teaches is that parity was won in three arenas in sequence, in Parliament, before the regulator, and in the courts, and that the last of the three was needed because the first two did not by themselves move every underwriting desk.

The remaining rights are the machinery rights, the ones that make the headline rights usable. Section 22 guarantees the right to information: the person and the representative are entitled to be informed about the provisions of the Act and the criteria for any admission, about the right to apply to the Board for review, and about the nature of the illness, the proposed treatment plan and the known side effects of treatment, in a language and form the person understands, with a duty to convey the information promptly where it could not be given at admission. Section 23 guarantees confidentiality in respect of mental health status, physical healthcare and treatment, extending to electronic and digital records, with enumerated exceptions that include release to the nominated representative, release to other treating professionals, release necessary to prevent harm to the person or violence to others, release on the order of the Board, the Authority, a High Court, the Supreme Court or another competent statutory authority, and release in the interests of public safety, together with a bar in Section 24 on releasing any photograph or information to the media without the person's consent. Section 25 guarantees access to basic medical records in the prescribed form, subject to a withholding power where disclosure would cause serious mental harm to the person or a likelihood of harm to others, balanced by a duty to inform the person of the right to apply to the Board for release of the record. Section 26 protects personal contacts and communication: the right to refuse or receive visitors and telephone and mobile calls at reasonable times, to send and receive sealed mail and electronic mail, to have mail from a named person withheld at the person's request, and to unsupervised contact with defined categories including judges, Board and Authority members, Members of Parliament and of State Legislatures, the nominated representative, legal representatives and the treating practitioner. Section 27 entitles the person to free legal services to exercise any right under the Act, and places a duty on the magistrate, the police officer, the person in charge of a custodial institution and the medical officer or professional in charge of an establishment to inform the person of that entitlement and to provide the contact details of the legal aid service: the entitlement runs through the legal aid machinery created by [the Legal Services Authorities Act 1987](/2021/06/15/legal-services-authorities-act-1987/), and the duty to inform is the hinge that connects the mental health statute to that machinery. Section 28 completes the chapter with the complaints right: a person, or the representative, aggrieved by deficiencies in care, treatment and services in an establishment may complain to the medical officer or professional in charge, then to the Board, and then to the State Authority, without prejudice to any remedy available in a court of law.

Two of the machinery rights reward a closer look because they govern situations that arise daily and are rarely litigated. The confidentiality right in Section 23 is not a professional courtesy restated; it is a statutory duty owed by every person who comes to know the information, covering mental health status, physical healthcare and treatment, and expressly including records stored in electronic and digital form, and its exceptions are a closed list rather than a discretion. The consequence is that the commonest breaches, a diagnosis shared with an employer, a discharge summary handed to relatives who have no right to it, a photograph circulated from a ward, are not merely unethical but unlawful, and Section 24 adds the specific prohibition on releasing any photograph or information to the media without the consent of the person. The records right in Section 25 is the mirror image: the person is entitled to their own basic medical records, and the establishment's power to withhold is conditioned on an opinion that disclosure would result in serious mental harm to the person or a likelihood of harm to others, with a statutory duty to tell the person of the right to apply to the Board for the record's release. Between them, Sections 23 and 25 relocate control of the clinical record from the institution to the person, which in a field where the file has historically been the institution's property is a quieter revolution than the advance directive but a broader one, since it touches every patient rather than only those who plan ahead.

## Duties of Government: Sections 29 to 32

### What does the Act require governments to provide in every district?

Section 18 requires minimum mental health services to be available in each district, and entitles a person, where those services are not available, to treatment at another service in the district at government cost. The duty sits with the appropriate Government, and the district is the unit the Act chose, which is why the district programme is its measure.

Chapter VI converts the access right into governmental programme duties. Section 29 requires the appropriate Government to plan, design and implement programmes for the promotion of mental health and the prevention of mental illness in the country, including public health programmes to reduce suicides and attempted suicides, and to do so in consultation with the Central Authority and with the health, education, social and legal services and other concerned departments. Section 30 requires programmes to reduce stigma and spread awareness of mental health and illness, including publicity for the text of the Act itself, and periodic sensitisation and training of government officials, including police officers, on their duties under the Act. Section 31 addresses the workforce, the constraint on which the whole statute ultimately runs: the appropriate Government must plan, design and implement public health programmes for human resource development, must provide training at the least for all medical officers in public health establishments and in prisons and jails to deliver basic and emergency mental healthcare, and must make efforts within ten years from the commencement of the Act to meet the internationally accepted guidelines for the number of mental health professionals relative to population. Section 32 requires coordination between ministries and departments so that prevention, promotion and long-term care form one system rather than a set of unconnected schemes. These are duties of endeavour and programme rather than of individual entitlement, and their placement matters: Parliament placed the promotion and prevention chapter between the rights chapter and the finance chapter, signalling that the rights were meant to be served by an apparatus the same statute obliged the state to build.

## The Programmes Beneath the Statute

The duties in Chapter VI do not operate in a vacuum; they ride on a programme architecture that predates the Act and that the Act assumes rather than creates. The National Mental Health Programme, adopted in 1982, was the Union's first organised attempt to carry mental healthcare beyond the large hospitals, and its district component, the District Mental Health Programme, begun in 1996 and modelled on the Bellary district experience in Karnataka, placed a small team of specialists at district level and tasked it with early detection, treatment, training of primary care staff and public education. It is that programme, in its successive expansions under the Five Year Plans, that supplies the district unit which Section 18 chooses as the measure of the access right and Section 30's awareness duties presuppose. The relationship between the programme and the statute is therefore double-edged. The Act gives the programme's goals legal form, converting a scheme that ran on budget lines and administrative instructions into duties stated in a statute, enforceable at least at the level of public accountability and answerable in Parliament. But the Act also exposes the programme, because a district team of a handful of specialists, which is the District Mental Health Programme's establishment in most districts, cannot by itself deliver the Section 18 range of acute care, rehabilitation, supported accommodation and child and old-age services. Reading Sections 18 and 29 to 32 together with the programme record is the correct way to understand both: the statute sets the standard, the programme is the instrument, and the distance between the two is a budgetary and workforce question that the implementation record measures.

The workforce question is also a training question, and the Act assigns it deliberately. Section 31 requires the appropriate Government to plan human resource development for mental health services and, as the floor of that duty, to train all medical officers in public health establishments and in prisons and jails to provide basic and emergency mental healthcare. The choice of the medical officer as the trainee of first resort reflects the structure of Indian public healthcare: in most districts the psychiatrist is a scarce specialist at the district hospital, while the medical officer at the community health centre and the primary health centre is the clinician the population actually reaches, and a system that trains that officer to recognise, stabilise and refer is the only system that can meet a treatment gap measured in tens of percentage points. The Authorities carry the complementary duty at the professional end: the Central Authority's functions include training of mental health professionals and officials, and the national institutions whose directors sit on it, the National Institute of Mental Health and Neuro Sciences at Bengaluru among them, are the apex training institutions of the field. The Act thus imagines a pipeline, specialist norms and training at the centre, generalist capacity at the periphery, and Section 31's ten-year endeavour clause is the timetable it sets for closing the distance between the two.

## The Institutions: Authorities, Registration and Review Boards

The Act builds a three-storey institutional structure. At the top sit two rule-making and regulatory authorities, the Central Mental Health Authority under Chapter VII and the State Mental Health Authorities under Chapter VIII. Section 33 required the Central Government to establish the Central Authority within nine months of assent. Its composition under Section 34 is deliberately mixed: the Secretary or Additional Secretary in the Department of Health and Family Welfare is chairperson ex officio; Joint Secretaries dealing with mental health, with the Department of Empowerment of Persons with Disabilities, with AYUSH and with the Ministry of Women and Child Development sit as members; the Director General of Health Services and the directors of the central mental health institutions are members; and the nominated membership brings in one mental health professional, one psychiatric social worker, one clinical psychologist and one mental health nurse, each with at least fifteen years of experience, two persons who have or have had mental illness, two representatives of care-givers, and two representatives of non-governmental organisations working in mental health, nominated members holding office for three years. Section 43 assigns the Central Authority the functions of registering establishments under central control, developing quality and service norms, training professionals and officials, advising the Central Government, and maintaining the national register. Each State Government was required under Section 45 to establish a State Mental Health Authority on a mirrored pattern, health secretary as chairperson ex officio, with state officials, professionals, persons with lived experience, care-givers and non-governmental representatives, and Section 55 gives the State Authority the registration, standards, training and supervisory functions within the State, together with the power to adjudicate the penalties under Section 107. Finance for both tiers is structured through dedicated funds under Chapter IX, fed by grants and audited through the ordinary public audit machinery.

Registration is the second storey. Chapter X, Sections 65 to 72, provides that no person or organisation shall establish or run a mental health establishment unless it is registered under the Act, a category the Act defines widely to include health establishments and rehabilitation homes where persons with mental illness are admitted and reside or are kept for care, treatment, convalescence and rehabilitation. Registration proceeds in two stages: a provisional certificate issued on application without prior inquiry, valid for twelve months and renewable, and a permanent certificate granted after the establishment submits evidence of compliance with the minimum standards specified by the Authority, public notice of the application for thirty days inviting objections, consideration of responses, and an order within the period the section prescribes, with registration deemed granted if the Authority neither communicates objections nor decides in time. Section 65(6) set the system a deadline of its own: the Authority was to specify the minimum standards for different categories of establishments within eighteen months of commencement. Section 67 requires an audit of every registered establishment every three years and empowers the Authority, after show cause and hearing, to cancel registration where minimum standards are not maintained, where the persons in management have been convicted under the Act, or where the establishment violates the rights of persons with mental illness, with immediate restraint on operations where there is imminent danger to the persons admitted. Section 68 adds inspection and inquiry, including the power to enter and search premises suspected of being run as an unregistered establishment, and Section 69 gives an aggrieved establishment an appeal to the High Court within thirty days. Every establishment must display its certificate of registration, the Authority must maintain a digital register, and Section 72 obliges every establishment to display the contact details of the concerned Board and to provide the forms and free telephone access by which an admitted person can apply to that Board.

### What standards must a mental health establishment meet to stay registered?

An establishment must satisfy the minimum standards specified by the Authority for its category, covering facilities and services, qualifications of personnel, records and reporting, and any other conditions the regulations impose. Compliance is continuing: establishments are audited every three years, and registration may be cancelled for failure of standards, for conviction of the management, or for violating patients' rights.

The third storey is the Mental Health Review Board, the institution on which the Act's promises most directly depend, because it is the forum to which nearly every individual grievance runs. Section 73 requires the State Authority, by notification, to constitute Boards for a district or a group of districts. Section 74 composes each Board of a chairperson who is a District Judge, or an officer of the State judicial services qualified to be appointed as a District Judge, or a retired District Judge; a representative of the District Collector or District Magistrate; two members, one a psychiatrist and one a medical practitioner; and two members drawn from persons with mental illness, care-givers, and representatives of organisations of such persons or of non-governmental organisations working in mental health. Members hold office for five years or until the age of seventy, and are appointed by the chairperson of the State Authority. Section 76 requires decisions to be taken by consensus where possible and otherwise by majority, the chairperson holding a casting vote, with a quorum of three. Section 77 opens the Board's doors wide: a person with mental illness, the nominated representative, or a representative of a registered non-governmental organisation acting with the person's consent may apply against a decision of an establishment or a violation of rights, no fee is charged, and applications may be made orally or by telephone in exceptional circumstances by a person admitted in an establishment. Section 80 requires the Board to endeavour to dispose of applications within ninety days, with shorter periods prescribed for specified categories such as appointments of representatives, challenges to admissions of minors and reviews of admissions. Sections 78 and 82 complete the design by treating the Board's proceedings as judicial proceedings and arming it with the functions this article has already met: maintaining the register of advance directives, reviewing and where necessary cancelling directives, appointing representatives where the statutory order requires, permitting or refusing supported admissions beyond thirty days, reviewing admissions on application, visiting establishments and reporting violations to the Authority. An appeal from the orders of the Authority or the Board lies to the High Court under Section 83. The Board is thus part tribunal, part ombudsman and part registrar, and the Act's wager is that a district-level body with a judicial chair, clinical members and lived-experience members can hold together functions that the older law scattered between licensing authorities, magistrates and no one at all.

The Board's procedural design shows the same concern for the applicant's position as its composition. An application may be made by the person, by the nominated representative, or by a representative of a registered non-governmental organisation working in the field with the person's consent, so that an admitted person who cannot act alone is not dependent on the establishment's goodwill for access to the forum. Proceedings before the Board are deemed to be judicial proceedings within the meaning of the Penal Code provisions the Act names, which disciplines false evidence and insult or interruption in the hearing room. The Board is directed to endeavour to hear and dispose of applications within ninety days, with the Act prescribing shorter periods for the categories where delay is itself the injury: the appointment of a representative for a person who has none, challenges touching the admission of minors, and the reviews of supported admissions on which a person's liberty directly turns. Establishments are conscripted into the access machinery by Section 72, which obliges each of them to display the Board's address and telephone numbers conspicuously, including on its website, and to provide the forms and free telephone access an admitted person needs to apply. A right of review that the reviewed person cannot reach is a formality; the Act's answer is to put the Board's phone number on the ward wall and the cost of the call on the establishment.

The functions Parliament assigned the two Authorities explain why their constitution mattered so much to the Act's early years. The Central Authority is charged with registering establishments under central control and maintaining the national register, with developing quality and service norms and minimum standards guidance for categories of establishments, with promoting and conducting training for professionals, government officials and others, with advising the Central Government on mental health policy, and with collecting and publishing information. The State Authority performs the corresponding functions within the State, registers and supervises establishments, receives complaints that climb the Section 28 ladder, adjudicates the penalties under Section 107, and constitutes the Boards. Between them, the Authorities hold nearly every lever the Act creates that is not held by a Board or a court: standards, registration, training, data and penalty adjudication. An unconstituted State Authority therefore does not merely leave a committee unformed; it leaves the registration system without a registrar, the standards without an author, the penalties without an adjudicator and the Boards without a constituting body, which is why the State Authority figures in the parliamentary answers are the leading indicator of everything else in the implementation record. The registration chapter also contains a coordination clause of practical importance: an establishment already registered under the Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act 2010 or another State law may apply on the strength of that registration with an undertaking that it fulfils the Authority's minimum standards, a recognition that the same premises are often regulated twice over and that the mental health register was meant to capture, rather than duplicate, the existing stock of facilities.

### Where does a person complain when a right is denied?

The first step is the establishment itself: Section 28 takes a complaint about deficiencies in care, treatment and services to the medical officer or mental health professional in charge. The second step is the Mental Health Review Board, which hears applications against decisions of establishments and violations of rights without fee. The third step is the State Mental Health Authority.

Alongside the ladder, the specific right carries its own machinery: an insurance refusal runs to the insurer's grievance process, the regulator and the Ombudsman, and court remedies remain open throughout.

## Money and Accounts: Chapter IX

Chapter IX, Sections 57 to 64, gives the Authorities their financial skeleton, and its dry provisions matter because several of the implementation failures in the record are, at root, accounts not opened and funds not constituted. The Central Government and each State Government are to make grants to their Authority, and each Authority maintains a fund, the Central Mental Health Authority Fund and the State Mental Health Authority Fund respectively, into which grants, fees and other receipts are credited and from which the Authority's expenses, including the functioning of the Boards at State level, are met. Annual accounts are prepared and audited through the Comptroller and Auditor General's machinery, annual reports are laid before Parliament or the State Legislature, and the Authorities may accept grants, gifts and donations for the purposes of the Act. The connection to the implementation record is direct: the parliamentary answer of 4 August 2026 reported the State Mental Health Authority Fund as constituted in 26 States and Union Territories, a figure that measures something more concrete than intention, since an Authority without a fund cannot pay its members, staff its secretariat or fund the Boards it constitutes. The chapter is also the honest limit of what statutory drafting can do about money: the Act can structure a fund, require audit and compel reporting, but the size of the grants that flow into the fund is a budget decision taken each year outside the statute, which is why Section 18(11)'s budgetary duty and Chapter IX's machinery must be read together, the first stating the obligation and the second providing the vessel, with the annual appropriations determining whether either has content.

## Admission and Discharge: Sections 85 to 99

The admission code is where the capacity premise meets the ward, and it is drafted as a graduated system. Section 85 defines the independent patient, a person admitted who either has capacity to make treatment decisions or requires minimal support from a nominated representative, and directs that admissions be independent wherever possible. Section 86 admits such a person on their own request, subject to conditions that protect the request from abuse: the person must have a mental illness of such severity that admission is required, must be likely to benefit from admission and treatment, must have understood the nature and purpose of admission, must have requested admission of free will, and must have capacity or need only minimal support. No treatment may be given without informed consent, and the establishment is expressly forbidden to require the consent or presence of a nominated representative, relative or care-giver as a condition of an independent admission, a clause aimed at the common hospital practice of making a family member the real gatekeeper of a voluntary patient's stay. Section 88 then guarantees the exit: an independent patient is to be discharged immediately on request, subject to one narrow safeguard. Where a mental health professional is of the opinion that the person may meet the criteria for supported admission, the professional may prevent discharge for twenty-four hours, and no longer, to allow assessment; at the end of that period the person must either be admitted under Section 89 or discharged. The twenty-four hour clause is the statute refusing both extremes: no open-ended power to retain a voluntary patient on clinical instinct, and no obligation to release a person into a crisis that the assessment procedure exists to evaluate.

Section 87 governs the admission of minors, and it is the most procedurally dense of the provisions. A minor may be admitted only on the application of the nominated representative, who for a minor is ordinarily the legal guardian. Two examiners, two psychiatrists, or a psychiatrist and a mental health professional or medical practitioner, must independently examine the minor on the day of admission or within the preceding seven days and conclude that the mental illness is of such severity that admission is required, that admission is in the minor's best interests with due regard to the minor's wishes where ascertainable, that the minor's needs cannot be met at a less restrictive setting, and that community-based alternatives have failed or are unsuitable. The minor must be accommodated separately from adults, and the representative or an appointed attendant must stay with the minor throughout the admission, with a female attendant where the representative of a minor girl is male. Treatment requires the representative's informed consent. The establishment must inform the Board within seventy-two hours of the admission; the Board may visit and interview the minor and review the records; an admission continuing for thirty days must be informed to the Board immediately and the Board must review it within seven days and every thirty days thereafter; and if the representative withdraws support, the minor is to be discharged.

### How is a child admitted to a mental health establishment under the Act?

Only on the application of the nominated representative, and only after two independent examinations certify severity, best interests, unmet need and the failure of community alternatives. The child is kept separately from adults with an attendant present throughout, the Board is informed within seventy-two hours, and admissions crossing thirty days are reviewed by the Board.

Supported admission is the Act's name for admission without the person's consent, and Sections 89 and 90 divide it by duration. Section 89 authorises admission for a period not exceeding thirty days on the application of the nominated representative, supported by independent examinations by one psychiatrist and one mental health professional or medical practitioner conducted on the day of admission or within the preceding seven days. The examiners must find the three elements that recur through the code: a mental illness of such severity that the person has threatened or attempted, or is threatening or attempting, to cause bodily harm to himself, or has behaved or is behaving violently toward another person or has caused another person to fear bodily harm, or has shown or is showing an inability to care for himself to a degree that places the individual at risk of harm to himself; that the person is ineligible for admission as an independent patient because of the support required, extending to very high support needs; and, after taking any advance directive into account, that admission is the least restrictive care option possible in the circumstances. Treatment during a supported admission takes the directive into account or proceeds on the person's informed consent with the representative's support, and where the person requires support approaching total support, the representative may give temporary consent, recorded as the Act requires, with the person's capacity reviewed every seven days. The admission must be reported to the Board within three days where the person admitted is a woman or a minor and within seven days in other cases, the person, the representative or a non-governmental organisation may apply to the Board for a review, the Board's findings within seven days are binding, and a person discharged from a supported admission may not be readmitted under Section 89 within seven days of discharge, the beyond-thirty-days route being the only alternative. Section 90 governs admission beyond thirty days. It requires independent examinations by two psychiatrists within the preceding seven days, findings in the same risk categories stated in the stronger form of conduct shown consistently over time, certification that admission remains the least restrictive option after considering any directive, and continuing ineligibility for independent care. The admission must be reported to the Board within seven days, and the Board must, within twenty-one days of the admission or readmission, permit it or order discharge, examining in doing so the need for institutional care and whether a community setting could provide the treatment instead, and may require a community-based treatment plan to be submitted. An admission permitted under the section may run for ninety days in the first instance, may be extended by one hundred and twenty days, and thereafter by one hundred and eighty days at a time, each extension requiring fresh compliance with the section, and where the Board refuses permission or the permitted period expires, discharge follows. The representative's temporary consent in a Section 90 admission is reviewed every fortnight.

Around this spine sit the movement provisions. Section 91 permits leave of absence on conditions, Section 92 deals with absence without leave, applying to persons admitted under orders in the prisoners chapter, and requires the police to take an absent person into protection and return them, and Section 93 regulates transfer between establishments, requiring the consent of the Central Authority for transfers between States and notice with reasons to the person and the representative. Section 94 authorises emergency treatment by any registered medical practitioner, at a health establishment or in the community, with the representative's informed consent where the representative is available, only where immediately necessary to prevent death or irreversible harm to the person, the infliction of serious harm by the person to himself or others, or serious damage to property flowing directly from the illness; it includes the power to transport the person to the nearest establishment for assessment; it expressly forbids the use of electro-convulsive therapy as emergency treatment; and it limits emergency treatment to seventy-two hours or the completion of assessment, whichever is earlier, extended to seven days only in an emergency or disaster declared by the appropriate Government. Section 98 requires discharge planning for every discharge, prepared in consultation with the person, the representative, the family or care-giver and the psychiatrist who will treat the person afterwards, so that discharge is a planned transition into community care rather than the end of the file.

The admission code should also be read for what it assumes about time, because its numbers are its safeguards. A supported admission under Section 89 runs for thirty days at most; the report of a woman's or a minor's admission reaches the Board in three days, of others in seven; the Board's review findings bind within seven days of an application; a discharged person cannot be returned under Section 89 for seven days; the Board's decision on a Section 90 admission must come within twenty-one days; capacity during a supported admission is reviewed every seven days under Section 89 and every fortnight where consent runs through the representative under Section 90; a minor's admission is reported in seventy-two hours and reviewed every thirty days; emergency treatment ends at seventy-two hours or on assessment. The cumulative effect is that no coercive state under the Act is allowed to age without meeting a fresh decision-maker. That is the code's deepest difference from the reception order regime it replaced, under which an order, once made, could carry a detention forward indefinitely on its own authority. Under the 2017 Act, time itself is made to work for the person: every period expires, every extension must be earned by fresh examination and fresh certification, and the default at every expiry is discharge.

## Treatment the Act Forbids or Fences

Section 95 lists the prohibited procedures, and the list is short enough to quote in substance in full. A person with mental illness shall not be subjected to electro-convulsive therapy without the use of muscle relaxants and anaesthesia. Electro-convulsive therapy shall not be performed for minors, subject to a single exception: where the psychiatrist in charge is of the opinion that it is required, it may be performed with the informed consent of the guardian and the prior permission of the Board. Sterilisation of men or women, when intended as a treatment for mental illness, is prohibited. And a person with mental illness shall not be chained in any manner or form whatsoever. Section 96 fences psychosurgery: it may be performed only with the informed consent of the person and the approval of the Board. Section 97 prohibits seclusion and solitary confinement outright, and regulates physical restraint narrowly: restraint may be used only where it is the only means available to prevent imminent and immediate harm to the person or others, only when authorised by the psychiatrist in charge, only for the period strictly necessary, never as punishment or deterrent and never for want of staff, with the method, nature, justification and duration recorded immediately in the medical notes, the nominated representative informed within twenty-four hours, the person kept where they can harm neither themselves nor others under regular supervision, and every instance reported monthly to the Board, which may direct an establishment to desist from a persistent and wilful practice of violation. Section 99 regulates research: free and informed consent is the rule, and where a person is unable to give free and informed consent but does not resist participation, the research may proceed only with the permission of the State Authority and the consent of the nominated representative, on conditions that include the research being necessary for the person or the class the person belongs to and not being capable of being carried out on persons able to consent for themselves. The nominated representative's consent for research is personal to the office, and Section 17 confers it expressly, which places the representative's research function alongside the treatment functions rather than inside them. Consent may be withdrawn at any time, and the withdrawal has the effect the Act prescribes of ending the person's participation, while research using anonymised case notes and records is preserved so that the consent regime does not retrospectively disable the ordinary audit and study of clinical material. Section 106 confines every professional to their own field, barring a mental health professional from discharging functions or recommending medicines or treatment they are not authorised for within their profession. These provisions share a drafting method: the Act does not ask establishments to be kind; it enumerates the practices that history had shown and removes them from discretion, leaving the Board and the Authority to police a list rather than a standard.

Chapter XV backs the scheme with offences that are deliberately modest in amount and specific in target. Section 107 penalises the running of an establishment without registration on a graduated scale, not less than five thousand rupees extending to fifty thousand for a first contravention, fifty thousand to two lakh rupees for a second, and two lakh to five lakh rupees for subsequent contraventions, with a penalty up to twenty-five thousand rupees for knowingly serving as a mental health professional in an unregistered establishment, the penalties being adjudicated by the State Authority and recoverable as arrears of land revenue. Section 108 punishes contravention of the Act generally, with imprisonment up to six months or fine up to ten thousand rupees or both for a first offence, and for subsequent offences imprisonment up to two years or a fine of not less than fifty thousand rupees extending to five lakh rupees or both, and Section 109 deals with offences by companies. The modesty of the fines has been criticised, fairly, as pricing non-compliance cheaply for large institutions; the sharper teeth of the scheme are regulatory rather than penal, cancellation of registration under Section 67 and the exposure of violations to the Board, and readers should weigh the offences chapter as a backstop rather than as the Act's enforcement centre.

## Police, Magistrates, Prisons: The Duties of Other Agencies

Chapter XIII assigns the Act's obligations to the agencies that meet mental illness outside the clinic. Section 100 obliges the officer in charge of a police station to take into protection any person found wandering at large whom the officer has reason to believe has a mental illness and is incapable of caring for himself, and any person the officer has reason to believe is a risk to himself or others by reason of mental illness. The officer must inform the person, or the representative where ascertainable, of the grounds on which the person is being taken into protection, and must take the person to the nearest public health establishment as soon as possible and in any case within twenty-four hours for assessment by a medical officer. No person taken into protection under the section may be detained in a police lock-up or prison in any circumstances. If the assessment finds no mental illness of a nature or degree warranting admission, the police are to take the person to his residence or, where he is homeless, to a government establishment for homeless persons, and in the case of a homeless or wandering person the station is to lodge a first information report of a missing person and trace and inform the family. The section converts the police, in this narrow class of encounters, from a detention authority into a transport and tracing authority, with the lock-up expressly closed to them. Section 101 requires the police to report to the Magistrate a person with mental illness being ill-treated or neglected in a private residence, and allows any person to make such a report. Section 102 empowers the Magistrate, on such a report, to order the person to be conveyed to a public mental health establishment for assessment and treatment, or to authorise admission for assessment for a period not exceeding ten days. Section 104 requires the person in charge of a custodial institution, including beggars' homes, orphanages, women's protection homes and children's homes, to take a resident who has or is likely to have a mental illness to the nearest government run or funded establishment for assessment.

Section 103 deals with prisoners, and it does so by integration rather than by building a parallel system. Orders for the admission of prisoners under the Prisoners Act 1900, the service Acts and the Code of Criminal Procedure operate as authority for admission to a mental health establishment; transfer to a psychiatric ward within the prison's medical wing satisfies the section where such a ward exists, and otherwise the prisoner is transferred to an establishment with the Board's permission. The medical officer of the prison must send the Board a quarterly report certifying whether any prisoners with mental illness are in the prison, the Board may visit the prisoner and inquire why the prisoner is being kept in prison rather than transferred, and reports on the condition of persons detained under the section are to reach the Board every six months. The appropriate Government is required to set up a mental health establishment in the medical wing of at least one prison in each State and Union Territory. Section 105 completes the chapter at the boundary with the courts: where in any judicial process proof of mental illness is produced and challenged by another party, the court must refer the question to the Board, which, itself or through a committee of experts, forms its opinion and reports it to the court. The scheme across the chapter is consistent: wherever the older law gave another agency an independent power over the person, the new Act either narrows that power to assessment and transport, or routes the question through the Board.

Section 105 deserves a final word because it quietly changes the position of mental illness inside ordinary litigation. Courts had long received contested assertions about a party's or a witness's mental condition with little structured assistance, relying on certificates of uneven quality. The section replaces that practice with a reference: where proof of mental illness is produced in a judicial process and is challenged, the court refers the question to the Board for the district, and the Board, itself or through a committee of experts constituted for the purpose, forms and communicates its opinion. The opinion is the Board's, arrived at through a body that includes a judicial chairperson, a psychiatrist and a medical practitioner, which gives the receiving court an institutional assessment in place of a partisan certificate. The provision does not make the Board a court of appeal over medical questions generally; it operates only where proof of mental illness is both produced and disputed in a pending process, and within that band it standardises the route by which such proof is tested.

## Attempted Suicide and Section 115

Section 115 is two subsections long and is the provision of the Act most often met by readers outside the field. Stated exactly, Section 115(1) provides that notwithstanding anything contained in Section 309 of the Indian Penal Code, any person who attempts to commit suicide shall be presumed, unless proved otherwise, to have severe stress and shall not be tried and punished under the said Code. Section 115(2) casts a corresponding duty on the appropriate Government to provide care, treatment and rehabilitation to a person having severe stress who has attempted suicide, to reduce the risk of recurrence of the attempt. Three points of precision matter and are commonly lost. The Act does not repeal Section 309: the repeal clause, Section 126, repeals only the Mental Health Act 1987, and Section 115 operates by way of a non-obstante presumption that displaces the penal provision in practice for the class it covers. The presumption is rebuttable, expressed with the words unless proved otherwise, so the section decriminalises by shifting the evidential starting point rather than by striking the offence from the Code. And the decriminalisation is paired with a duty: the state's answer to an attempt is specified by the statute itself as care, treatment and rehabilitation directed at reducing recurrence, and Section 29(2) reinforces that duty by requiring prevention programmes that include public health programmes to reduce suicides and attempted suicides. The legal position, stated plainly, is that an attempt to commit suicide is not a prosecutable offence in India because the presumption of severe stress in Section 115 stands in the way of trial and punishment, and the state owes the person care rather than a charge-sheet.

Section 115 sits in Chapter XVI among the Act's closing provisions, and its neighbours explain how the whole scheme is held together. Section 116 bars the jurisdiction of civil courts over any matter the Authority or a Board is empowered to determine, channelling disputes into the Act's own forums, with the High Court's appellate and constitutional jurisdiction untouched. Section 117, already noticed, governed the transition from the repealed Act. Section 120 gives the Act overriding effect over inconsistent laws, the clause that secures the registration chapter against conflicting State regimes and the rights chapter against inconsistent institutional rules. The rule-making powers that follow distribute the subordinate legislation between the Central Government, the State Governments and the Authorities' regulation-making powers, which is why the implementation record is properly measured, as this article measures it, in rules notified and regulations made as much as in bodies constituted. The placement of the suicide provision in this company is itself a small lesson in Indian legislative practice: a reform of the criminal law's operation, achieved through a presumption and a duty inside a health statute, catalogued between a jurisdictional bar and a repeal clause, because the Mental Healthcare Bill was the vehicle Parliament had.

The history gives the provision its weight. Section 309 of the Penal Code had made attempt to commit suicide an offence punishable with imprisonment up to one year or fine or both. A Division Bench of the Supreme Court in P. Rathinam v. Union of India (1994) struck the provision down as offending Article 21, and a Constitution Bench in Gian Kaur v. State of Punjab (1996) overruled that decision and upheld Section 309, reasoning that the right to life did not include a right to die. The Law Commission of India, in its Two Hundred and Tenth Report of 2008, recommended that the provision be repealed, observing that suicide is a manifestation of disease and despair calling for treatment rather than punishment, and Section 115 is the statutory fruit of that recommendation, arriving by the health route a decade later. The interface with the successor penal code must be stated as the codes state it: the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023, which replaced the Penal Code, contains no offence corresponding to Section 309, and the only adjacent provision is Section 226, which penalises an attempt to commit suicide made with the intent to compel or restrain a public servant in the exercise of lawful power, for which community service is among the available punishments. The full comparison of the two codes, including the treatment of this omission, is worked through in [the comparison of the Indian Penal Code and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita](/2011/02/01/ipc-vs-bns-comparison/); for the purposes of this Act the position is simply that Section 115 emptied Section 309 of operation, and the successor code then left the emptied provision out altogether, so that the decriminalisation accomplished by a presumption in a health statute was completed by an omission in the penal code.

## The Clinical Objection and the Rights Answer

The Act has been contested since the Bill stage, and the contest deserves to be stated in its strongest form on both sides, without this article adjudicating it. The clinical objection, pressed by the Indian Psychiatric Society in its submissions during the Bill's passage and developed by practitioners writing in the Indian Journal of Psychiatry, runs as follows. A strong presumption of capacity, combined with a binding advance directive, can in practice block treatment in an acute crisis: a person in the grip of an episode may refuse the very treatment that would restore the capacity the statute presumes, the directive made in calmer times may forbid the intervention the treating team judges necessary, and the remedy the Act offers, an application to the Board, operates at the speed of a tribunal rather than the speed of a ward. On this view, the Act imports a model designed for legal systems with dense professional coverage into a country where a single psychiatrist may serve a vast population, multiplies procedural steps around admission until families give up and take the person home untreated, and exposes practitioners to legal risk for clinical judgment exercised in good faith. The objection is not a defence of the old regime: its holders do not ask for reception orders back, and they concede the abuses the Act was written against. It is an argument about sequencing and dosage, that rights machinery must be calibrated to the workforce that has to operate it, and that where calibration fails, the person the Act means to protect is the person who pays.

A connected strand of the clinical and family critique concerns the household. In India the family, and not the state or the market, is the principal provider of long-term care for a person with severe mental illness: housing, supervision, medication adherence, money and crisis management are carried overwhelmingly by relatives, and care-givers' organisations argued through the Bill process that a statute drafted around individual autonomy, advance directives and supported decision-making speaks the language of a person who has an effective state to claim against, while the Indian reality is a family carrying the load with almost no respite, no allowance and no statutory recognition beyond the definition of care-giver. The critique continues that the Act's suspicion of substituted judgment, carried into the nominated representative's fidelity duties, can leave families formally peripheral at the moment of admission and discharge even though they will receive the person back at the gate, and that Section 98's consultation of the family in discharge planning is a thin provision against that structural fact. Defenders of the Act answer that the default order in Section 14(4) places relatives and care-givers at the front of the representative queue precisely because the family is the care system, that discharge planning, information rights and the representative's application rights run to the family in practice, and that the alternative model, in which the family's authority over the person was the organising principle, was the model whose failures the Erwadi record exposed. The disagreement is recorded here in the same spirit as the capacity debate: it is a live argument about the fit between a rights text and a family-based care economy, and any honest account of the Act must carry it.

The rights answer, developed by disability rights organisations and by scholars of mental health law including the Centre for Mental Health Law and Policy, begins from the record rather than from the ward. The historical alternative to the capacity framework was not benign clinical discretion; it was detention without process, treatment without consent, chaining in establishments outside any regulatory net, and a licensing statute that the Erwadi proceedings showed had not been implemented at all. On this view, the presumption of capacity is not a courtesy but the legal form of a factual truth, that most people with mental illness can and do make most of their own decisions, and the advance directive is not an obstacle to treatment but the person's own instruction for the intervals when they cannot speak. The Board mechanism exists precisely to review directives and contested admissions, and where Boards are slow or absent the failure is one of implementation, of States not constituting or staffing the bodies the Act requires, rather than of the design. Supporters add a structural point: the Act does not prohibit treatment in crisis, since Section 94 authorises emergency treatment for seventy-two hours, Section 89 authorises supported admission on certified risk grounds, and Section 11 allows a directive to be set aside by the Board where it was coerced, uninformed or inapplicable; what the Act prohibits is compulsion without a record, without a time limit and without a review forum. The two positions agree on more than their rhetoric suggests: both accept the rights chapter, both accept that the workforce is the binding constraint, and both accept that an unconstituted Board is worse than either model fully built. They disagree about whether the statute should have waited for the system, and that disagreement is recorded here because the Act's readers will meet it in every serious commentary on the statute.

A third objection cuts across both and should be stated plainly, because no reading of the Act is honest without it. A right to access is only as real as the services that exist to be accessed. The National Mental Health Survey's treatment gap of 70 to 92 per cent was not caused by the absence of a rights statute and cannot be closed by one. The constraint is arithmetic: the number of psychiatrists, clinical psychologists, psychiatric social workers and psychiatric nurses relative to a population of India's size, a gap Section 31 addresses only as a duty to make efforts within ten years of commencement to meet internationally accepted guidelines. District services on the scale Section 18 imagines require budgets, training pipelines and a primary-care integration that a statute can mandate but cannot manufacture. The Act's defenders do not deny this; their claim is the narrower one that a legal entitlement changes what governments can be held to, in budgets, in parliamentary answers and in court, and that claim is testable against the implementation record, to which this article turns.

## The Implementation Record: Text Against Delivery

The Act's own deadlines frame the record. The Central Authority was to be established within nine months of assent; the Act commenced on 29 May 2018; minimum standards for establishments were to be specified within eighteen months of commencement; the workforce duty in Section 31 runs for ten years from commencement. The most reliable public measure of the record is the set of answers the government has given in Parliament, and the figures must be used as the answers state them, at their dates, without merging counts from different sources into a single series. In the Rajya Sabha, in an answer given in 2021 to Unstarred Question No. 1681 by the Minister of State for Health and Family Welfare, Dr Bharati Pravin Pawar, the government stated that 27 States and Union Territories had established State Mental Health Authorities and 8 States had constituted Mental Health Review Boards, and that among State rules, only the draft rules of Karnataka had been approved, while the Central Government's rules and regulations under the Act had been framed and notified. On 4 August 2026, in the Rajya Sabha's answer to Unstarred Question No. 1865, the Minister of State, Prataprao Jadhav, stated on information furnished by the Central Mental Health Authority that 32 States and Union Territories had constituted State Mental Health Authorities, 26 States and Union Territories had constituted Mental Health Review Boards, only 8 States and Union Territories had notified State Mental Healthcare Rules, and 26 States and Union Territories had constituted the State Mental Health Authority Fund. The Mental Healthcare Act Tracker maintained by the Centre for Mental Health Law and Policy, which follows constitution of the bodies State by State, has at its own count reported figures that differ in detail from the parliamentary answers, recording, for instance, Boards in a smaller set of States at the time of its reporting; the divergence is itself informative, because it reflects how unevenly the constitution of Boards has proceeded between a State notifying a body and that body sitting, hearing and deciding.

Three conclusions can be drawn from the attributed record without straining it. The first is that the Authority tier was built faster than the Board tier, which is the wrong way round for a rights statute, because the Board is the forum the individual rights run to; for years after commencement, in a majority of States, a person seeking review of an admission, or registration of an advance directive with the Board that Section 7 obliges to keep the register, faced a forum that existed on paper in the Act but not in the district. The second is that rule-making lagged structure: a count of only eight States and Union Territories with notified State rules, in the 2026 answer, eight years after commencement, means the prescribed forms, fees and procedures on which registration and several rights depend were missing in most of the country for most of the Act's life. The third is that the money followed the same pattern, with the State Authority Fund constituted in 26 States and Union Territories on the 2026 answer. Courts have pressed the point at intervals, the Punjab and Haryana High Court among them, taking up the constitution of Authorities and Boards in proceedings on the Act's implementation, and the trajectory in the parliamentary answers, from 8 States with Boards in 2021 to Boards reported across 26 States and Union Territories in 2026, shows movement that is real and slow in equal measure. The litigation record adds texture that the parliamentary answers cannot supply. In proceedings before the Supreme Court brought by the National Platform for the Rights of the Disabled concerning implementation of the Act, submissions recorded in the hearings put the position in granular terms: that only ten States and Union Territories had notified minimum standards for establishments, that Mental Health Review Boards were functional in a minority of the country's districts against a requirement that runs district by district, and that the stock of institutional beds fell well short of the numbers the norms require. Those figures are submissions in litigation rather than official answers, and they are recorded here as such; their importance is that they were made in the forum where the Act's promises are ultimately enforceable, and that the Court's continuing oversight of implementation is part of the mechanism by which the gap between text and delivery is being closed, district by district and standard by standard. At the programme level, the Union's National Tele Mental Health Programme, launched in October 2022 with its Tele-MANAS helpline service, represents the service-delivery direction the Act's prevention and access duties point toward, a national telephonic first point of contact feeding into the district system; it is a programme, not a provision of the Act, and it is noticed here only because the implementation record of a statute includes the instruments governments build under its pressure.

Nothing in the record supports either of the two easy verdicts, that the Act is a dead letter or that it is a completed reform; the record supports the duller and more useful verdict that the statute's institutions are being built years behind its text, and that the gap between the two is where the Act is actually lived.

## The Act Beside Its Neighbours

No statute in this field operates alone, and three relationships fix the Mental Healthcare Act's position in the wider health code. The first is with the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016, the general statute enacted under the same Convention. The two Acts divide the territory by subject: the disabilities statute addresses disability across categories, including mental illness as a specified disability, and governs equality, education, employment, social security and the certification of disability, while the mental healthcare statute governs the healthcare relationship itself, capacity for treatment decisions, advance directives, admission and the rights of the person inside the treatment system. The sharpest point of contact is decision-making support. The disabilities statute provides, in Section 14, for limited guardianship, a joint decision-making arrangement granted by the district level authority under which a limited guardian decides in consultation with the person, for a specified period and purpose. The mental healthcare statute's nominated representative is a different instrument for a different setting: chosen by the person, revocable at will, bound by the person's wishes, and never a grant of decision-making power over the person by an outside authority. A person may in principle meet both instruments, the limited guardian for the transactions of civil life and the representative for treatment, and the statutes are drafted so that the treatment instrument, being the more specific and the more closely tied to the person's own nomination, governs inside the establishment.

The second relationship is with the Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act 2010, the general registration statute for clinical establishments in the States that have adopted it. The mental health Act's registration chapter, as noticed earlier, accepts an establishment's registration under that Act or a corresponding State law as the basis for its own registration on an undertaking of compliance with the mental health standards, and the two registers then run in parallel: the general register policing the establishment as a clinical facility, the mental health register policing it as a place where persons with mental illness are admitted, audited under Section 67 and answerable to the Board for the rights of the persons inside it. The third relationship is with the ordinary law of medical consent and negligence, which the Act supplements rather than displaces. Informed consent to treatment, battery and negligence in the civil and consumer courts, and professional discipline before the medical councils continue to operate on their own principles; what the Act adds is the statutory frame around them for this class of treatment, the capacity presumption that determines whose consent counts, the directive that binds the future, the representative who supports the decision, and the Board to which the failures of the frame can be taken without the cost of a suit. Read in that company, the Act is best understood as the special law of a single treatment relationship, laid over the general law of medicine and the general law of disability, and prevailing, by Section 120, wherever the general law is inconsistent with it.

## Assessment: A Strong Text and an Uneven Record

Measured against its own namable claim, the Act did what it set out to do in law. Capacity replaced custody as the organising question: Section 4 presumes the person's ability to decide, Section 5 hands the person an instrument for binding the system in advance, Section 14 surrounds the person with a chosen supporter rather than a substituted guardian, Chapter V states eleven rights as entitlements with forums attached, Sections 89 and 90 put compulsion on a clock with a board at the end of it, Section 95 removes chaining and the other enumerated abuses from the field of discretion altogether, and Section 115 withdrew the shadow of prosecution from a person in crisis and replaced it with a duty of care. As a piece of legislative design it is coherent in a way few Indian health statutes are: premise, instruments, institutions and offences all point the same way, and the provisions that matter most are drafted without the qualifying discretion that usually empties such statutes of force. The honest verdict must hold the two halves together. The text is a strong one. The record, on the government's own parliamentary answers, is of institutions constituted late, rules notified in a minority of States years after commencement, and a workforce duty whose ten-year clock runs against a shortage no answer has suggested is closing. A reader who wants the measure of the Act should therefore refuse both the celebration and the dismissal, and should watch the four numbers by which its future will be told: the Boards constituted and sitting, the State rules notified, the minimum standards enforced through registration audits, and the treatment gap the next national survey measures against the 70 to 92 per cent of 2015 to 2016.

The Act's effects outside its own machinery should be recorded before the verdict is closed. Insurance products were redesigned after the regulator's directions of 2018 to 2020 removed the psychiatric exclusion from compliant policies, so that the parity duty changed the standard contract a policyholder buys, not merely the arguments available when a claim is refused. Admission practice in registered establishments runs on the Act's forms and clocks even where no Board has ever been moved, because the reporting duties and the twenty-four hour, thirty day and twenty-one day periods structure the file from the first hour. Police training under Section 30 has made the Section 100 duties part of the standard instruction in States that have run the sensitisation programmes, changing the destination of a wandering person from the lock-up the section forbids to the public health establishment it commands. None of these effects required a reported judgment. They are the statute working as statutes usually work, by altering the paperwork, the training and the default settings of the institutions it governs, and an assessment that counted only litigation would miss most of what the Act has done.

This article addresses the law of mental healthcare: the statute, its institutions and its record. It is legal information, not legal advice, and it is not a source of clinical guidance. A person seeking help for themselves or for another person should approach a qualified mental health professional or a recognised helpline, and a person in immediate danger should contact the emergency services.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What rights does the Mental Healthcare Act give a person with mental illness?

- A: Chapter V of the Act gives eleven rights. They are the right to access mental healthcare and treatment from government run or funded services, free of charge for persons below the poverty line, destitute or homeless persons (Section 18); to live in the community without segregation (Section 19); to protection from cruel, inhuman and degrading treatment (Section 20); to equality and non-discrimination, including insurance parity (Section 21); to information about the law, admission criteria and treatment (Section 22); to confidentiality (Section 23); to access basic medical records (Section 25); to personal contacts and communication (Section 26); to free legal aid (Section 27); and to complain about deficiencies in services (Section 28).

### Q: How does an advance directive work under the Mental Healthcare Act?

- A: An advance directive is a written statement under Section 5 by which a person who is not a minor records in advance how they wish, and do not wish, to be cared for and treated for mental illness, and may name individuals in order of precedence to be the nominated representative. It can be made whether or not the person has had mental illness or treatment before. It takes effect only when the person ceases to have capacity to make treatment decisions and stays in force until capacity returns. A decision the person makes while they have capacity overrides the directive. Professionals must follow a valid directive, subject to the Board's power under Section 11 to review, alter or cancel it on stated grounds.

### Q: Who is a nominated representative?

- A: A nominated representative is the person who supports a person with mental illness in exercising legal capacity and making treatment decisions under Chapter IV of the Act. Any person who is not a minor may appoint one in writing on plain paper with a signature or thumb impression; the appointee must be an adult, competent for the role, and must consent in writing. The representative's duties under Section 17 are to consider the person's current and past wishes, life history, values, cultural background and best interests, and to give particular credence to the person's own views. Having a representative is not treated by the Act as evidence of incapacity, and the appointment can be revoked or changed by the person at any time.

### Q: Does the law require insurance to cover mental illness?

- A: Yes. Section 21(4) of the Act provides that every insurer shall make provision for medical insurance for treatment of mental illness on the same basis as is available for treatment of physical illness. The duty is stated without qualification and took effect with the Act's commencement on 29 May 2018. The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India directed all insurers by a circular dated 16 August 2018 to comply with the provision with immediate effect, and its standardisation guidelines of 27 September 2019 barred the exclusion of mental illness from health policies. A policy issued after commencement that excludes psychiatric treatment therefore conflicts with the statute, and claims rejected on such an exclusion have been held against insurers in litigation.

### Q: What did the Mental Healthcare Act change about attempted suicide?

- A: Section 115 provides that, notwithstanding Section 309 of the Indian Penal Code, a person who attempts to commit suicide shall be presumed, unless proved otherwise, to have severe stress and shall not be tried and punished under that Code. The Act did not repeal Section 309; it displaced prosecution by a rebuttable presumption, and it paired decriminalisation with a duty on the appropriate Government to provide care, treatment and rehabilitation to reduce the risk of recurrence. The Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023 later omitted any equivalent of Section 309 altogether, retaining only the narrow offence in Section 226 of attempting suicide to compel or restrain a public servant in the exercise of lawful power.

### Q: What does a Mental Health Review Board decide?

- A: A Mental Health Review Board is the district-level body constituted under Section 73, chaired by a District Judge or an officer qualified to be one, with a district administration representative, a psychiatrist, a medical practitioner, and members drawn from persons with mental illness, care-givers and their organisations. It maintains the register of advance directives, reviews and may alter or cancel directives, appoints nominated representatives where the statutory order requires, hears applications against decisions of establishments and violations of rights without fee, reviews supported admissions, and must permit or refuse admissions beyond thirty days under Section 90, deciding within twenty-one days. Its proceedings are judicial proceedings, and an appeal from its orders lies to the High Court.

### Q: When does the Mental Healthcare Act allow admission without consent?

- A: Admission without the person's consent is the supported admission of Sections 89 and 90. It requires an application by the nominated representative and independent examinations, by a psychiatrist and another mental health professional or medical practitioner for Section 89, or by two psychiatrists for admission beyond thirty days, finding a mental illness of such severity that the person has threatened, attempted or is threatening harm to himself, has behaved violently toward another or caused fear of bodily harm, or is unable to care for himself to a degree that places him at risk; that the person needs very high support and is ineligible for independent admission; and that admission is the least restrictive care option, after taking any advance directive into account.

### Q: Has the Mental Healthcare Act been implemented?

- A: In part, and unevenly. The Act commenced on 29 May 2018 and the Central Authority was established under it. The government's own parliamentary answers give the measure: in the Rajya Sabha in 2021, the answer to Unstarred Question No. 1681 recorded 27 States and Union Territories with State Mental Health Authorities and 8 States with Mental Health Review Boards; on 4 August 2026, the answer to Unstarred Question No. 1865 recorded 32 States and Union Territories with State Authorities, 26 with Review Boards, and only 8 with notified State Mental Healthcare Rules. The institutions have therefore been built years behind the text, and implementation remains the Act's weakest point.

### Q: What is the legal test of capacity under Section 4 of the Mental Healthcare Act?

- A: Section 4 deems every person, including a person with mental illness, to have capacity to make mental healthcare and treatment decisions if the person can do three things: understand information relevant to the decision, appreciate any reasonably foreseeable consequence of a decision or of not deciding, and communicate the decision by speech, expression, gesture or any other means. Information must be given in a language the person understands, or in sign language or by visual aids. The test is decision-specific, so a person may have capacity for one decision and not another. Section 4(3) adds that arriving at a decision others perceive as inappropriate or wrong is not, by itself, a ground to conclude that the person lacks capacity.

### Q: Can a person with mental illness be chained or kept in seclusion in a mental health establishment?

- A: No. Section 95 provides that a person with mental illness shall not be chained in any manner or form whatsoever, and Section 97 prohibits seclusion and solitary confinement. Physical restraint is permitted only in a narrow band: where it is the only means available to prevent imminent and immediate harm to the person or others, when authorised by the psychiatrist in charge, for no longer than strictly necessary, never as punishment or deterrent and never because of staff shortage. The method, nature, justification and duration must be recorded immediately in the medical notes, the nominated representative must be informed within twenty-four hours, and every instance must be reported monthly to the Mental Health Review Board.

### Q: What can a policyholder do if an insurer refuses to cover treatment for mental illness?

- A: The policyholder can challenge the refusal at several levels. Section 21(4) obliges every insurer to provide insurance for mental illness on the same basis as physical illness, so a rejection resting on a psychiatric exclusion in a policy issued after 29 May 2018 can first be taken to the insurer's grievance cell, then to the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India's grievance system and the Insurance Ombudsman, and also to a consumer commission as a deficiency in service. The Delhi High Court has held in reported litigation that an insurer which rejected a claim under such an exclusion had not given effect to Section 21(4), so the statutory duty supports the complaint at each of these forums.

### Q: How is an advance directive registered, and can the person who made it change or cancel it?

- A: An advance directive is made in writing in the manner specified by regulations of the Central Mental Health Authority, and Section 7 requires every Mental Health Review Board to maintain an online register of the directives registered with it and to make the register available to treating mental health professionals. Registration matters because Section 13 protects a professional from liability for not following a directive that was never given to them. The maker keeps control of the instrument: Section 8 allows the person who made a directive to revoke, amend or cancel it at any time in the prescribed manner. Separately, a professional, relative or care-giver may apply to the Board under Section 11, which may review, alter, modify or cancel a directive after hearing the parties.

### Q: Who becomes the nominated representative if a person has never appointed one?

- A: Section 14(4) supplies a descending order of precedence. First comes the individual named in the person's advance directive. Failing that, a relative acts. Failing that, a care-giver. Failing that, a suitable person appointed by the Mental Health Review Board. As the last resort, the Board appoints the Director of the Department of Social Welfare of the State, or a person designated by that Director. A representative of an organisation registered under the Societies Registration Act 1860 that works for persons with mental illness may be engaged temporarily until a Board appointment is made. For a minor, the legal guardian is the representative unless the Board finds the guardian is not acting in the minor's best interests or is unfit, and appoints the Social Welfare Director instead.

### Q: What is the difference between an independent admission and a supported admission?

- A: An independent admission under Section 86 is the person's own act: a person with capacity, or needing only minimal support, who has understood the nature and purpose of admission and requests it of free will is admitted on that request, cannot be treated without informed consent, and may obtain discharge under Section 88 immediately on asking, subject only to a twenty-four hour assessment hold. A supported admission under Sections 89 and 90 is admission without the person's consent, on the nominated representative's application, after independent examinations certify severity, risk and the need for very high support, for a maximum of thirty days under Section 89 and longer only with the Review Board's permission under Section 90.

### Q: How long can a supported admission last, and who reviews it?

- A: A supported admission under Section 89 may last up to thirty days. It must be reported to the Mental Health Review Board within three days where the person admitted is a woman or a minor, and within seven days in other cases, and the person, the representative or a non-governmental organisation may ask the Board to review it, the Board's findings within seven days being binding. Beyond thirty days, Section 90 applies: two psychiatrists must certify the grounds afresh, the admission is reported to the Board within seven days, and the Board must within twenty-one days permit the admission or order discharge. A permitted admission runs ninety days in the first instance, then may be extended by one hundred and twenty days, and thereafter by one hundred and eighty days at a time.

### Q: Can electro-convulsive therapy be given to a minor under the Mental Healthcare Act?

- A: Only through a double gate. Section 95 prohibits electro-convulsive therapy for minors as the rule, and permits it as an exception only where the psychiatrist in charge of the minor's treatment is of the opinion that electro-convulsive therapy is required, the guardian gives informed consent, and the prior permission of the Mental Health Review Board is obtained. For all patients, the same section prohibits electro-convulsive therapy without the use of muscle relaxants and anaesthesia, and Section 94 adds that electro-convulsive therapy may not be used as emergency treatment at all. The provision was among the most debated in the Bill, and the enacted text resolves the debate by keeping the treatment available for a minor only under clinical opinion, guardian consent and prior Board permission together.

### Q: What must the police do when they find a person with mental illness wandering in a public place?

- A: Section 100 requires the officer in charge of a police station to take into protection a person found wandering at large whom the officer has reason to believe has a mental illness and is incapable of caring for himself, or who is believed to be a risk to himself or others by reason of mental illness. The officer must inform the person, or the nominated representative, of the grounds, and must take the person to the nearest public health establishment for assessment as soon as possible and within twenty-four hours. The person may not be detained in a police lock-up or prison in any circumstances. If assessment finds no illness warranting admission, the police take the person home or, if homeless, to a government establishment for homeless persons, and lodge a missing person report to trace the family.

### Q: Is a person with mental illness entitled to free legal aid?

- A: Yes. Section 27 entitles a person with mental illness to free legal services to exercise any right under the Act. The entitlement is made operational by a duty to inform: the magistrate, the police officer, the person in charge of a custodial institution, and the medical officer or mental health professional in charge of an establishment must each inform the person of the entitlement to free legal services under the Legal Services Authorities Act 1987 and provide the contact details of the legal aid service. In practice the application is made to the District Legal Services Authority, which provides a legal aid counsel. Legal aid also supports the right in Section 19 to live in the family home.

### Q: Does the Mental Healthcare Act cover a prisoner who develops mental illness?

- A: Yes, through Section 103. Admission orders made under the Prisoners Act 1900, the armed forces Acts and the Code of Criminal Procedure operate as authority for admitting a prisoner to a mental health establishment; where the prison has a psychiatric ward in its medical wing, transfer to that ward satisfies the section, and otherwise the prisoner is transferred to an establishment with the Board's permission. The prison medical officer must send the Board a quarterly report certifying whether any prisoners with mental illness are in the prison, the Board may visit and ask why a prisoner is being kept in prison rather than transferred, and condition reports are due every six months. The government must also set up a registered mental health establishment in the medical wing of at least one prison in each State and Union Territory.

### Q: Can a person who walks in voluntarily leave a mental health establishment when they wish?

- A: Yes, as the rule. A person admitted as an independent patient under Section 86 is to be discharged immediately on request under Section 88, without needing the medical officer's consent. There is one safeguard: if a mental health professional is of the opinion that the person may satisfy the criteria for supported admission, because the person appears unable to understand treatment decisions and needs substantial or very high support, or has shown the risk grounds the section lists, the professional may prevent discharge for up to twenty-four hours to allow an assessment for supported admission. At the end of that period the person must either be admitted under Section 89 on its own certified grounds or be discharged.
