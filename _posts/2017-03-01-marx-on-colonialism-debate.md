Did Marx support colonialism, and was he a racist? The questions arrive together, in search boxes and seminar rooms alike, and they are usually answered by selecting a single text and presenting it as the whole record. The honest answer has four parts, and no part cancels another. In 1853 Marx published newspaper articles arguing that British rule in India, brutal in its motives and its conduct, would nevertheless prove historically progressive in its effects. In private correspondence he used racist language, and this article will state that plainly. From the late 1850s his published position changed substantially, as rebellion in India and war in America gave him colonial peoples as actors to report rather than as backdrops to describe. Finally, in letters of 1869 and 1870 on Ireland, he reversed the reasoning of his earlier years completely and produced the founding Marxist analysis of national antagonism as the mechanism by which a working class is divided and disarmed. An assessment that omits any of the four is not an assessment. It is a selection, and selection is the commonest method by which this record is misrepresented in both directions, by prosecutors who stop at 1853 and by defenders who start at 1870.

One matter of method belongs at the front, because this article establishes a standard the series will keep. Marx's correspondence contains language that is racist and, in the best known instance, antisemitic as well. Where a reader needs to know what was said, this article states that the material exists, characterises it accurately and specifically, and cites the letter so that anyone may verify it. It does not reproduce the epithets themselves. Reproduction would add nothing to the reader's understanding that the characterisation does not supply, and the characterisation will not be softened to compensate for the omission. The language was racist; saying so in full sentences, with the date and the recipient and the occasion named, is a more serious indictment than a quotation dropped onto the page for shock. Readers who want the primary text will find it identified precisely enough to locate in any collected edition. What they will not find here is the pretence that the words were something else.

The plan of what follows mirrors the shape of the record. The journalism of 1853 comes first, stated in its strongest form and with its famous formulation intact, because the later development cannot be measured unless the starting point is given accurately. Then come the factual premises underneath the journalism, and the finding of later historiography that those premises were wrong, a finding delivered in large part by Marxist historians of India itself. The private language follows, stated early in the article rather than buried, since burying it would read as evasion. The change of position from the late 1850s, and the Ireland reversal of 1869 and 1870, receive the fullest treatment, because they are the parts most often missing from accessible accounts and because the reversal is, in this article's judgement, the most consequential thing Marx wrote on any colonial question. A table called the position over time then orders the whole sequence so that the movement, rather than any single text, becomes the object of assessment. The scholarly debate over the record, opened by Edward Said in 1978, answered by Aijaz Ahmad in 1992, and rebuilt on new evidence by Kevin Anderson in 2010, is presented with both sides at full strength. The verdict comes last and grades the parts separately, because the evidence genuinely falls into parts that deserve different grades.

## Marx on colonialism: a correspondent and his brief

The India articles were journalism, written to order and against deadlines, and their occasion explains both their strengths and their limits. Marx had been engaged in 1851 by Charles Dana, managing editor of the New-York Daily Tribune, the largest circulation newspaper in the United States, to supply a stream of articles on European politics and economics. The arrangement was his family's principal income for a decade, and it made him a working reporter of the European scene: revolutions and counterrevolutions, budgets and trade figures, the politics of every court and parliament. India came within his brief in the spring of 1853 because the British Parliament was debating the renewal of the East India Company's charter, the legal instrument under which a trading corporation governed a subcontinent. The debate generated select committee hearings, official reports, and a flood of testimony about Indian land tenures, Indian taxation, and the condition of Indian manufacture. Marx read the parliamentary material the way a good correspondent reads a dossier, which is to say closely and critically, and out of it he built two long articles for the Tribune. The first, on the British rule in India, appeared on 25 June 1853. The second, on the future results of that rule, appeared on 8 August 1853. Together they remain the most quoted, and the most fought over, pages he ever wrote about the world beyond Europe.

Journalism of this kind had a discipline its modern critics sometimes forget. Marx was not free to invent his India. He had never been there, and everything he knew came through documents, which meant the documents available in London in 1853: the reports of the very administration whose rule he was judging, the testimony of its officials, the travel accounts of earlier centuries, and a European philosophical literature that treated Asia as Europe's opposite and predecessor. His originality lay in what he did with the dossier, not in the dossier itself. Where the parliamentary speakers debated whether company rule had been efficient or corrupt, Marx asked a different question: what was this rule doing to the social order it governed, and what would history make of the destruction? The answer he gave was unlike anything in the hearings. It condemned the conquerors' motives in the harshest terms available to him and then, in the same breath, assigned their conquest a world-historical function. That combination, held in a single argument and never resolved into comfort for either side, is what the next section states in full.

### What was Marx actually writing, and for whom?

He was writing paid newspaper correspondence for the New-York Daily Tribune, the American paper that employed him as its European correspondent from 1851, and the India articles of June and August 1853 were occasioned by the parliamentary debate over renewing the East India Company's charter, which supplied the reports and testimony he worked from.

The circumstances matter for how the articles should be weighed, though they excuse nothing in them. A correspondent writes for readers who need a distant scene made intelligible by Friday, and Marx's readers were Americans for whom India was a name on a map and a source of cotton politics. He wrote as an analyst, not as an administrator or a traveller, and he was explicit with himself, in his notebooks of the period, about the poverty of the record: the archive of empire was nearly the only archive there was. This dependence cuts in two directions when the articles are assessed. It explains how a writer capable of savage independence toward every European government could reproduce, as settled fact, the colonial file's account of the Indian village. It does not make the reproduction accurate, and the section on his factual premises below treats the dependence as part of the charge rather than as a mitigation of it. The point of recovering the circumstances is narrower. These were not treatises composed in leisure for a philosophical audience; they were interventions in a live parliamentary moment, written by a man whose larger theoretical work, the critique of political economy, was still mostly unwritten. The stage scheme they apply to India was his working framework of 1853, applied under deadline. What he did to that framework in the decades after is the burden of the second half of this article.

There is a further circumstance that belongs in the record before the argument itself is stated. The Marx of 1853 was an exile in London, stateless, watched by police agents, and dependent on the Tribune's fees to feed a household that buried children in those years for want of money and medicine. None of this is offered as pathos. It is offered because the standard picture of these articles, in both the prosecution and the defence literatures, treats them as the serene deliverances of a system builder, and they were not. They were written in a Dean Street lodging by a correspondent racing the mail steamer, inside a framework he shared with nearly every serious European thinker of his generation, from sources compiled by the empire he despised. Holding all of that in view at once is the beginning of accuracy about them. Their judgements were nonetheless his judgements, published under his name and never retracted in his lifetime, and they will be stated now without varnish.

## How the journalism was made: the reading room, the byline, and the steamer

The material production of the correspondence deserves a section of its own, because the controversy's participants habitually write as though the articles descended from a study in a university that did not exist. Marx worked in the reading room of the British Museum, walking distance from the Dean Street rooms in Soho where the family lived in the early eighteen fifties in two rooms of a lodging house, in poverty of a kind that the letters record without self pity: the pawnshop holding the children's shoes and his own coat in the weeks the Tribune's payment was late, the manuscripts copied out in Jenny Marx's hand for the mail steamer, the articles sometimes written by Engels in Manchester and sent down for dispatch when illness or the pressure of the great theoretical drafts made London production impossible, a division of labour the Tribune's readers never suspected and which scholarship later had to disentangle article by article. Charles Dana, an exacting and sympathetic editor, nonetheless cut, retitled, and occasionally rewrote, and used some contributions as unsigned editorials, so that the corpus of the correspondence reached print filtered through a second intelligence with its own politics, a fact the modern editions labour to mark. None of this is picturesque decoration. It fixes the epistemic conditions of the colonial writings with precision. The India of the articles was constructed in a circular reading room in Bloomsbury, from blue books and debates, by a man racing steamers, and the contrast with the Ireland of the letters, constructed from residence, acquaintance, and organisational combat in the same city, is not a contrast of talent or of virtue. It is a contrast of evidence. The tragedy of the 1853 articles, if the word is not too large, is that their author knew the difference perfectly well in every other domain: no reader of Capital's chapters on the working day, built from factory inspectors' reports tested against the inspectors' own evasions, can doubt that he understood how interested archives lie. On India he read the inspectors and believed them, because India was, for the correspondent of 1853, a subject among twenty, and the belief cost him nothing at the time. The bill arrived posthumously, with interest, and it is still being presented. The life inside which this production was sustained, and the newspaper years in their biographical order, are traced in the [karl marx intellectual biography](/2013/05/01/karl-marx-intellectual-biography/).

## The background in the revolutionary years: the world market and its heavy artillery

The India articles did not spring from a blank page, and the framework they applied had a famous earlier statement that the assessment must include, because prosecutors quote it as readily as the articles themselves. In the Communist Manifesto of 1848, written with Engels on the eve of the European revolutions, the bourgeoisie is described as the great revolutionary class of the modern world: battering down all Chinese walls with the heavy artillery of cheap commodities, drawing even the most barbarian nations into civilisation, remaking the world after its own image, and rescuing populations from what the text calls the idiocy of rural life. The tone of those pages is deliberately provocative, a celebration of dissolution delivered in the accents of its enemies' alarm. The Manifesto's bourgeoisie is no hero; it is praised the way a flood might be praised by a man who hated the drowned village's priest and landlord and believed the water, however filthy, carried the seeds of a freer order. But the structure of the judgement is the same structure that will govern the India articles five years later: precapitalist societies figure as stagnation to be broken, the breaking is assigned to the most ruthless agent available, and the violence of the process is discounted against the dynamism it releases. Anyone who wants to understand why the articles of 1853 took the shape they did has to reckon with the fact that their author had already written the script, in its most compressed and quotable form, before he had read a page of the parliamentary evidence on India.

Two things should be said about that script before it hardens into a caricature. The first is that the Manifesto's geography of progress was the common sense of its century wearing revolutionary dress. Liberal political economy, the utilitarian administrators of India, the Hegelian philosophy of history, and the socialist sects all shared, beneath their quarrels, the picture of a dynamic Europe and a stationary elsewhere; they differed over who should hold the solvent and what should replace the dissolved. Marx's version differed from the administrators' in its destination, since the civilisation the bourgeoisie was spreading was itself, on his account, a temporary order pregnant with its own gravediggers, and the colonised were cast as future gravediggers rather than as permanent pupils. That difference mattered enormously to the colonised readers who later took the text up. It did not alter the picture of the East that the text assumed, and the picture, as the historiography section showed, was false. The second thing is that 1848 also contains the beginnings of the countercurrent that would eventually overturn the script. The young Marx of the revolution years was already a partisan of Polish independence against the partitioning empires, already treating a nation's subjugation as a chain on the nations that profited from it, in speeches on Poland delivered in Brussels in 1847 where he argued that a nation cannot become free while continuing to oppress another. The seed of the Ireland letters is present in the Polish speeches, twenty-two years early. It coexisted with the Manifesto's staircase without disturbing it, because Poland was European and its oppressors were the feudal empires the revolution also targeted, while Asia remained assigned to the staircase's lower steps. The whole subsequent history of his thinking on the colonial question can be told as the slow migration of the Polish insight eastward and southward, until it reached India and was applied, by other hands more than by his, to the empire he had once cast as history's solvent. The migration took the rest of his life, and it was never completed in a form he published as doctrine. But its existence forbids the simple story in which a Eurocentric youth is replaced, at some datable moment of conversion, by an anti-colonial elder. Both elements are present from the start, unevenly distributed across the map, and the development consists in the geography changing.

## The double mission: brutality with a progressive verdict

The central formulation sits in the second article, and it has organised every subsequent argument about Marx and empire. England, Marx wrote, had to fulfil a double mission in India: "one destructive, the other regenerating". The destructive mission was the annihilation of the old society of Asia, its village order, its handicraft industries, its political forms. The regenerating mission was the laying of the material foundations of a modern society in their place: railways, telegraphs, a unified administration, a press, an army organised on European lines, and eventually an Indian class educated in European science who would, he predicted, one day throw off the foreign yoke itself, since the benefits of the new order would be reaped by Indians only when they had grown strong enough to seize them or when the British working class had displaced the British ruling class. The argument is therefore not that conquest was good. It is that conquest was a crime that history would use. England in India was, in his account, driven by the meanest of motives, the profits of a corporation and the markets of Lancashire, stupid in its manner of ruling, and guilty of impoverishing a civilisation. And it was simultaneously the unwitting agent of the only transformation that could, on his theory, release India from the stagnation he believed he saw in its past. Both sentences must be carried together, because the argument is precisely their conjunction.

Stripped of its rhetoric, the argument has three moving parts, and each will matter when the verdict is graded. The first part is a description of the conquered society: India before the British was, on his account, a land of self-sufficient villages beneath an unchanging despotism, without a history of internal transformation, its economy and its politics frozen in forms thousands of years old. The second part is a theory of how societies change: productive forces develop, old orders dissolve when they block that development, and the dissolution, however cruel, is the precondition of advance. The third part is an application: British rule, whatever its motives, was dissolving the frozen order and importing the machinery of the dynamic one, and therefore stood on the progressive side of the historical ledger even as it filled the moral ledger with crimes. Notice where the weight falls. The conclusion about progress is only as sound as the first part, the description. If Indian society was not in fact frozen, if it contained commercial, agrarian, and political dynamism of its own, then the conquest was no solvent of any stagnation, but the violent interruption of a development, and the entire construction collapses into a rationalisation of plunder. Marx's critics within the historiography of India have pressed exactly there, and the section after next shows that they are right about the description. For now the logical structure needs to be plain: the double mission is a conditional argument, and its condition failed.

### Did Marx support colonialism?

in 1853 he described British rule in India as brutal in its motives and conduct but historically progressive in its effects. His factual premises about Indian society were wrong. From the late 1850s his position changed substantially, and on Ireland he reversed it entirely.

That compressed answer is the whole article in four sentences, and each of its clauses will be tested in what follows: the description of the 1853 verdict, the failure of its premises, the substantial change, and the Irish reversal. It is placed here, at the head of the evidence, because a reader who keeps it in view cannot be recruited by either of the excerpting armies. The support in question was a judgement about a process, delivered in a newspaper, inside a theory of history that the same writer spent his last decades dismantling in the relevant respects. Whether the judgement deserves the name of support at all is a question the verdict section takes up in its own terms; the prior question, what was actually claimed, belongs to the articles themselves, and the close reading below is designed to let the articles answer it before the critics are heard.

### What did the double mission actually claim?

It claimed that British rule in India was performing two tasks at once: destroying an old social order that Marx believed incapable of transforming itself, and laying the material foundations of a new one, through railways, unified administration, and modern industry, whose fruits Indians would eventually claim for themselves.

The moral register of the articles deserves its own paragraph, because the progressive verdict is often quoted as though it were delivered in the tone of an imperial annual report. It was not. The first article dwells on the destruction of the Indian handloom industry, the ruin of the weavers, the tax burden that ground the peasantry, and the spectacle of a civilisation being dismantled for revenue. Marx wrote that the misery inflicted by the British on India was of an essentially different and infinitely more intensive kind than anything India had suffered before, because earlier conquerors had at least settled, taxed, and left the productive order standing, while the British broke the order itself. His contempt for the hypocrisy of the conquerors is on every page: the same England that wept over suffering elsewhere administered famine taxes in India. Readers who quote the progressive conclusion without the accompanying fury are excerpting as tendentiously as readers who quote the fury and suppress the conclusion. The article's difficulty, and the reason it still generates heat a century and three quarters later, is that Marx refused to let the moral verdict do the work of the historical one. He thought a thing could be an atrocity and a solvent at once, and he thought history was full of such things. Whether the solvent part survives the correction of his facts is a separate question, and it is the question his framework, applied honestly, answers against him: remove the frozen India of his premises, and the double mission loses the only ground on which its second mission stood.

There is one more element of the 1853 position to register before leaving it, because it is the element that will change most completely. In 1853 Marx treated the colonial question as a question about the colonised society's stage of development. India interested him as a case: the place where the theory of social formations met a society that seemed to lack the internal springs of change. The colonised appear in the articles overwhelmingly as sufferers and as raw material, not as political agents whose own actions might alter the sequence. Indian resistance, when it appears at all, appears as reaction, the spasms of the old order. Within four years he would be reporting a great rebellion in India with unmistakable sympathy for the rebels' grievances, and within sixteen he would be arguing that a colonised people's national movement was the lever that could move the working class of the colonising country itself. The distance between the India of the articles and the Ireland of the letters is the distance this article exists to measure. It cannot be measured at all by readers who know only the first text, which is why the double mission has had to be stated here at full length and in its own terms before any criticism is allowed near it.

## The argument he was intervening in: the charter debate

The articles were an intervention in a specific parliamentary quarrel, and recovering the quarrel restores a dimension the excerptors lose, because Marx's formulations were aimed at targets his first readers could see. The East India Company's charter, the instrument by which a corporation of merchants exercised sovereignty over the subcontinent under the distant supervision of a government board, fell due for renewal in 1853, and the renewal debate in Parliament assembled every interested voice in the British connection with India: the Company's directors defending their patronage and their dividends, the Manchester manufacturers demanding the opening of the Indian market and the improvement of cotton supply, the missionary interest demanding access for conversion, the reforming administrators demanding railways, law codes, and education, and a small company of critics, heirs of the impeachment generation, for whom the whole system was organised plunder wearing a charter. The consensus of the debate, across nearly all of these voices, was that India was to be governed for ends set in London, and that the ends differed only in their mixture of profit, improvement, and salvation. Marx's intervention took the debate's own evidence, the hearings' revelations about taxation, land, and manufacture, and drew from it a conclusion no speaker in the chamber would own: that the system was at once a crime against the Indian population and the solvent of the Indian social order, that its apologists were hypocrites and its humanitarian critics were sentimentalists, and that the future it was preparing would be claimed neither by the Company nor by Parliament but by the Indians themselves or by a transformed Britain. The intervention's audacity should not be domesticated by familiarity. A stateless exile, writing for an American newspaper, informed the British public that its Indian empire was a criminal enterprise whose historical justification would be executed by its victims. The justification clause is what the prosecution quotes; the criminal charge is what the debate heard; and the prediction is what the following century, in its broadest outline, delivered. All three were in the intervention because all three were in the argument, and any reading that amputates the intervention into a position paper on progress has already stopped reading journalism as journalism, with an occasion, an enemy, and a deadline.

## Reading the two articles closely: what is actually on the page

Because the controversy runs on excerpts, the articles themselves deserve a slower reading than either camp usually gives them. The first article opens not with the village but with the money: the revenues of India, the interests clamouring in London for the charter's renewal, the question of who profits. Only then does it descend to Hindustan's social foundations, and the famous passages come in a deliberate order. First the dissolution: the village communities, broken by the tax gatherer and the soldier, by the steam engine and the competition of English cotton, their union of agriculture and domestic industry uprooted across the whole surface of the country. Then the judgement on what was dissolved, the idyll that was no idyll, the communities that were despotism's solid foundation and that had confined the human mind within the smallest possible compass. Then the conqueror's character: a rule actuated by the vilest of motives, stupid in its methods, inflicting a misery of a kind and intensity beyond all earlier conquests because it destroyed the very framework of the society it taxed. And only at the end, the turn for which the article is remembered, the insistence that the question was not whether England had meant well but whether mankind could fulfil its destiny without a fundamental revolution in the social state of Asia, so that England, whatever its crimes, had served as the unwitting instrument of that revolution. The closing image denies the reader any comfort the turn might have offered: human progress so far, he wrote, resembled a hideous pagan idol that would drink the nectar only from the skulls of the slain. A writer constructing an apologia for empire does not close on that image. A writer rejecting empire does not build the turn that precedes it. The article's scandal is its refusal to be either document, and excerptors of both camps have spent a century and a half amputating the half they cannot use.

The second article is the cooler and in some ways the colder of the two, because it looks forward. Its machinery is predictive: the railways, which he called the forerunner of modern industry in India, would dissolve the hereditary divisions of labour on which caste rested; the telegraph and the steamship would bind the country into a single political and economic body for the first time; a native army trained by the British would become the instrument of its own liberation; a free press, introduced for the first time in Asia, would create a public; and from the new productive forces would arise an Indian society capable of appropriating the fruits of the transformation. The conclusion then divides those fruits' ownership with care. The bourgeoisie, he wrote, had neither emancipated nor materially improved the condition of the mass of the Indian people, and it would not; Indians themselves would come into the fruits of the new order only when one of two things had happened, when the ruling classes of Britain had been supplanted by the industrial proletariat, or when the Indians had grown strong enough to throw off the English yoke altogether. Read whole, the sentence is not a promise that empire will deliver progress as a gift. It is a prediction that the instruments empire installs for its own purposes will be turned against it, by Indians, at the first opportunity of strength. That prediction was vindicated in its general shape by the history of the following century, in which the railways, the press, the universities, and the English language itself became the infrastructure of the national movement that ended the Raj. What was not vindicated was the premise underneath the prediction, the frozen India that required foreign machinery to move at all, since the national movement's own historians would show a country that had been anything but frozen before the machinery arrived. The article thus contains, side by side, the best and the worst of its author's colonial thinking: an eye for the self-subverting dynamics of domination that the Ireland letters would perfect, and a blindness about the colonised society's own dynamics that the historiography would expose. Excerpting has flourished precisely because the whole is this uncomfortable.

## The village community and the solid foundation of despotism

Underneath the double mission sits a picture of India, and the picture is painted in the first article with a confidence that its sources did not warrant. At the base of the society Marx described stood the village community: a small, self-sufficient unit of agriculturalists and hereditary artisans, producing nearly everything it consumed, governed by custom and caste, reproducing itself identically across centuries while dynasties rose and fell above it like weather above a landscape. These communities, he wrote, had "restrained the human mind within the smallest possible compass", making it the unresisting instrument of superstition and depriving it of every historical energy. Above the villages stood the state, and between the two there was, on his account, almost nothing: no cities of consequence as independent powers, no landed aristocracy in the European sense, no private property in land of the kind that elsewhere generated classes, law, and conflict. The state's function was taxation and public works, above all irrigation, and its form was what he called Oriental despotism, later systematised in his notebooks as the Asiatic mode of production. Because the villages supplied their own wants and reproduced their own order, conquest changed nothing beneath the surface: a new dynasty taxed what the old dynasty had taxed, and the social material below remained, in his word, unresisting and unchanged. India, on this account, had no history in the active sense. It had a succession of conquerors and a permanent society, and the second fact was the explanation of the first. The category at the centre of the picture, the Asiatic mode of production, is dissected in the series' companion study of the [mode of production explained](/2010/02/15/mode-of-production-explained/), where its logic is set out in full; what matters here is its provenance and its fate.

Every element of this picture can be traced to a shelf in the library of empire, and tracing it matters because the picture, not the theory, is where the articles fail. The absence of private property in land came from the seventeenth century traveller François Bernier, whose account of Mughal India described the emperor as proprietor of the soil, a description later readers generalised into a theory of the East. The self-governing village, stable beneath all conquest, came from the British administrative literature itself: officials of the Company, surveying the territories they were learning to tax, described the village communities in terms that one of them, Charles Metcalfe, made famous, as little republics having nearly everything they wanted within themselves and lasting where nothing else lasted. The frame into which these reports were fitted, Asia as the childhood of history, stationary while Europe matured, came from Hegel's lectures on the philosophy of history, which Marx had absorbed in his student years along with the rest of his generation. James Mill's History of British India, written by a man who never visited India and was proud of the fact, supplied a contemptuous certainty about Indian institutions that its readers mistook for knowledge. Marx tested these sources against each other and found them consistent, which they were, since they drew on the same administrative needs and the same European assumptions. What he could not test them against, from a London lodging in 1853, was India itself.

### Where did Marx's picture of the Indian village come from?

It came from the archive of the empire he was criticising: from Bernier's seventeenth century account of Mughal landholding, from the reports of British officials who described the villages as self-sufficient little republics, from James Mill's history, and from Hegel's lectures, which cast Asia as stationary and Europe as history's active agent.

The picture had a function inside his larger theory, and the function explains why he held it with such confidence. Marx was constructing, in those years, a comparative account of social forms: the ancient world of Greece and Rome, the feudal order of medieval Europe, the capitalist order emerging around him. Each form had its own logic of property and its own engine of change. Asia, as the reports presented it, seemed to offer a fourth form with no engine at all, a society organised for permanence, and that made it theoretically precious as the limiting case, the proof that stagnation was possible where the communal village and the despotic state locked together. In the notebooks published after his death as the Grundrisse, the Asiatic form receives extended comparative treatment as one of the archaic configurations from which class societies developed elsewhere. The category did real work in his system. It also did real damage outside it, licensing generations of readers to treat half the world as a waiting room. The category of Oriental despotism had a strange afterlife of its own: in 1957 the former Marxist Karl Wittfogel built a vast polemic upon it, arguing that hydraulic despotism explained the communist states, so that a concept Marx had assembled from colonial files ended its career as a weapon against his own tradition, wielded on the basis of much the same static picture of Asia he had inherited. Concepts assembled from bad evidence do not stay in the hands that assembled them.

One qualification is owed to precision before the demolition begins. Marx's village was not quite the simple idyll of the administrative reports, and his treatment was not simple contempt. He saw the communities as both solidary and stifling: their members were bound to one another by a real communal organisation, and bound down by a division of labour fixed at birth, sanctified by religion, and enforced by the village's own authorities. The same paragraph that mourns their dissolution as a human catastrophe calls their foundations the bedrock of despotism and superstition. This doubleness is the articles in miniature, and it is why neither the celebratory nor the prosecutorial excerpting of them survives a full reading. But doubleness about a falsehood is still a falsehood twice inflected. The villages existed; their portrait did not. What later scholarship did to the portrait is the next subject, and it is fatal to it.

## The prehistory of a category: despotism and stationariness before Marx

The picture of the East deployed in the articles was not Marx's invention, and its genealogy matters because the structural objection, to be answered fairly, must be aimed at the right depth. The idea that Asia's governments were despotic by nature and its societies stationary by essence descends through the central line of European political thought. Montesquieu's Spirit of the Laws of 1748 gave the idea its classical form: despotism as the government proper to Asia, explained by climate and terrain, ruling through fear over peoples fitted by their conditions for servitude. Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations described China as a stationary state that had long ago acquired the full complement of riches its institutions permitted, and treated the description as an economic commonplace. The utilitarian generation bureaucratised the picture: James Mill's history made Indian civilisation a rude draft of society awaiting Britain's revision, and the administrators who governed in the picture's light generated the reports that returned it to Europe as fact. Hegel's lectures then raised the whole construction into metaphysics, assigning Asia to the childhood of the world spirit, where history, properly speaking, had not yet begun, since the state existed without the subjective freedom whose emergence was history's plot. Marx inherited this library entire, as every educated European of his generation did, and his theoretical generation added the final element: a sequence of social formations through which the plot could be told as science. Originality in such conditions is not the refusal of the inheritance, which no contemporary achieved in full, but the uses to which it is put, and the assessment of the articles turns on the distance between the inheritance's standard uses and his. The administrators used the picture to warrant permanent tutelage; the liberals, to warrant improvement under supervision; Marx used it to predict the order's destruction by its own instruments and the eventual self-emancipation of its subjects, a use the picture's owners found scandalous. That the picture itself was false, and that its falseness, not its use, is what the evidence condemns, has been this article's finding. The genealogy adds only this, that the falseness was civilisational before it was personal, which is Said's strongest point restated in the archive's own terms, and that escaping a civilisation's picture of the world is the work the later record shows being done, slowly, against resistance from the picture's persistence in its author's own early keystones.

## The category at work: the Asiatic form in the notebooks of the eighteen fifties

The village analysis of the articles was not a passing journalistic device; it was load bearing in the theoretical construction Marx was assembling in the same decade, and the construction shows both why he trusted it and what it cost him. In the winter of 1857 and 1858, in the notebooks posthumously published as the Grundrisse, he worked through the forms that precede capitalist production in a comparative survey of extraordinary range: the classical city, the Germanic dispersion, the Slavonic commune, and the Asiatic form, in which the community appears as the substance and the individual as a mere accident of it, property is communal at the base, and the despot stands above the communities as the comprehensive unity that appropriates the surplus and organises the works, irrigation above all, that no village could undertake alone. The treatment is not contemptuous in the manner of the articles. It is almost tender toward the communal forms themselves, which figure as humanity's original shelter, the matrix from which property and individuality slowly differentiate. But the Asiatic variant is defined, within the survey, by the completeness of its closure: because the community never dissolves, because the individual never separates, because the state absorbs the surplus without transforming the base, the form reproduces itself outside history's current, and its members appear as children of a permanent present. The comparative frame gives the stagnation thesis its most sophisticated expression, and it reveals the thesis's true foundation. The closure of the Asiatic form is not an empirical finding that might have been revised by a better travel account. It is a requirement of the comparative architecture, the fixed point against which the dynamism of the other forms is measured. Evidence from India could no more have disturbed it in 1857 than evidence about the heavens could disturb a proof in geometry, because the category was doing structural, not descriptive, work. That is the deepest answer to the question of why a mind so suspicious of received categories received this one without suspicion: it was not a report he believed but a keystone he needed, and keystones are examined last.

The excavation of the Grundrisse in the twentieth century complicated the scholarly politics of the whole question in a way worth recording. Readers who encountered the notebooks in their postwar editions found a Marx far more comparative, tentative, and multilinear in private than the published doctrine of the movement suggested, working through alternative forms with an openness that the finished articles conceal. The notebooks therefore cut against both camps in the debate over 1853. Against the prosecution: the same winter's work that grounds the Asiatic category also grounds a lifelong, serious engagement with communal property as a real form with its own dignity and logic, not a mere absence of Europe. Against the defence: the private openness never issued, in the colonial case, in a published correction during the years when it might have disciplined the articles' authority; the published Marx of the nineteenth century is the Marx of the Tribune and of Capital, and it is the published Marx whom the world, including the colonised world, had to read. The distinction between the workshop and the shop window runs through this entire assessment, and it will matter again for the notebooks of his final decade, where the balance of credit reverses: there, the private labour is the achievement, and the absence of publication is the tragedy.

## The scheme canonised: the famous Preface and its four modes

Between the articles and the reversal stands the text in which the stage scheme received its canonical form, and the assessment is incomplete until it is confronted, because more readers have met the scheme there than anywhere else. In the Preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, published in 1859 as the summary of the method its author had reached, Marx set out the sequence in a single sentence: in broad outline, the Asiatic, ancient, feudal, and modern bourgeois modes of production may be designated as progressive epochs in the economic formation of society. The sentence is the staircase in its most quotable form, and its presence in the one text Marx published as the authorised abstract of his whole approach gave the scheme an authority that the journalism could never have conferred. Generations of readers, friendly and hostile, took the Preface as the system's table of contents, and the Asiatic mode rode in the sequence's first carriage into every textbook. The assessment must therefore record, against the comforting thought that the scheme was a youthful indiscretion of the newspaper years, that its author restated it, deliberately and in a methodological preface, in 1859, six years after the India articles and two years after the sympathetic reporting of the rebellion. The development this article has traced was not a steady march. The scheme survived its author's better knowledge for years, restated in the genre that claimed the most authority, while the journalism that contradicted it accumulated uncollected. What finally dislodged it was not a second preface but the Russian question's concrete evidence and the commune's stubborn specificity, together with the Irish analysis's demonstration that relations, not stages, explained the politics that mattered. The Preface stands in the record as the strongest single exhibit for the structural objection, since it shows the scheme functioning as the method's public face at the height of its author's powers. It also stands, by its very brevity, as a warning about the genre: a preface's outline, written to orient readers of an economics treatise, hardened into a cosmology by its readers, and its author, who protested in 1877 against exactly that hardening in the Russian case, never issued the protest as a corrected edition. The four modes govern the textbooks still, wherever the notebooks are not read against them. The method whose compressed outline the Preface supplies, and the long controversy over what that method commits its holders to, are set out at length in the series' [historical materialism complete guide](/2010/01/15/historical-materialism-complete-guide/), to which this section's argument is a footnote.

## The factual problem: an India that was never frozen

The verdict of subsequent historiography on the 1853 description is not in serious dispute, and it was delivered in large part by historians working inside the Marxist tradition in India, which gives the correction a particular weight: the tradition repaired its founder's account using his own methods against his own sources. The pre-colonial Indian economy, as reconstructed by this scholarship, was neither static nor self-sufficient in the fashion of the articles. It contained extensive commercial networks, monetised markets, merchant capital with its own institutions of credit and insurance, textile industries producing for export across the Indian Ocean world, agrarian regions of markedly different structures, and states whose fiscal and military competition drove continuous change. India in the centuries before Plassey was one of the great manufacturing and trading economies of the world, a fact registered in the trade statistics of the very companies that conquered it. D. D. Kosambi, whose Introduction to the Study of Indian History of 1956 founded a materialist historiography of ancient India, reconstructed a subcontinent of dynamic change: the spread of plough agriculture, the formation of states, the rise and transformation of caste as a historical process rather than an eternal essence. Irfan Habib's studies of the Mughal economy documented agrarian relations, commodity production, and technological practice that the static model cannot accommodate, and his essays state the methodological moral directly: the notion of an unchanging India was an artefact of the colonial archive, produced by administrators who needed the society they taxed to be legible, customary, and still.

The consequences for the 1853 argument are exact, and they should be stated without flinching. Marx's conclusion that British rule was historically progressive rested on the premise that the society it destroyed could not develop from within. The premise is false. A society with merchant capital, export manufacture, monetised exchange, and competing states was a society in motion, whatever direction that motion might have taken without conquest. The destruction of its industries by machine competition and coercive trade policy was therefore not the solvent of a stagnation but the violent foreclosure of possibilities, including the possibility, unprovable either way, of an indigenous path to industrialisation of the kind another Asian society, Japan, later travelled from a comparably commercial starting point. This does not merely dent the double mission argument. It removes the conditional on which the argument was expressly built, by the standard Marx himself supplied, since his claim for conquest was never moral but always developmental. The category of Oriental despotism has travelled the same road: abandoned as a description of Asian societies by the scholarship, surviving mainly as an object in the history of European ideas, a specimen of how the coloniser's filing system became the colonised world's supposed essence. Perry Anderson, the most formidable Marxist historian of the following generation, concluded in his own comparative work that the Asiatic mode of production should be consigned to the lumber room of the discipline, so incoherent had the assembled evidence shown it to be. When a framework's own best practitioners retire its category, the founder's use of it cannot be defended as ahead of its time. It was of its time, and its time was the high noon of the archive of empire.

### How did later historiography test the claim of a stagnant India?

By reconstructing the pre-colonial economy from Indian records and material evidence rather than from administrators' reports, and the reconstruction, much of it by the Marxist historians Kosambi and Habib, showed commercial networks, export manufacture, credit institutions, and competing states: a society in motion, not the frozen order the articles describe.

Fairness requires one distinction to be kept bright through this demolition, because the demolition is sometimes made to prove more than it can. Marx's premises were wrong, but they were the standard premises of his intellectual world, held in some form by liberals, utilitarians, and Hegelians who had none of his sympathy for the colonised and drew from the same picture conclusions far more comfortable for empire than his. John Stuart Mill, who spent his career at India House administering the system, shared the static picture and lacked the fury. The failing was therefore not idiosyncratic villainy but the ordinary epistemic condition of European thought about Asia, which is precisely what makes the case theoretically interesting rather than merely biographical: a thinker of genius, armed with a method that taught him to interrogate the categories of political economy until they confessed their historical origins, did not interrogate the category of the East, because the archive that supplied it was the only archive open to him and its consistency felt like confirmation. The scholarly name for this condition belongs to the debate section below. Its plain name is that he believed the file. The file was compiled by men who were paid to rule the society it described, and no method, however sharp, protects a reader who cannot leave the reading room. That is a lesson about evidence that outlasts the case, and it is one of the two or three durable things this whole controversy teaches.

A second distinction concerns the direction of the error's harm. A wrong description of Indian society in a newspaper article did not conquer India, tax it, or deindustrialise it, and the ledger of colonial harm belongs to the governments and companies that did those things, not to a stateless journalist in London. The harm of the 1853 articles is of a different kind and should not be inflated into equivalence: they gave a progressive warrant, from the left, to a process whose victims could least afford warrants, and they modelled for later readers a way of seeing colonised societies as objects of history rather than its subjects. That is a real harm of influence, and the verdict section will grade it as such. But the habit of treating Marx's articles as though they were acts of state, while the actual correspondence of the actual administrators gathers dust, is one of the ways this controversy is kept at the level of theatre. The record deserves better, in both directions.

## What the demolition leaves standing: deindustrialisation and the drain

The demolition of the static India should not be allowed to overshoot into a demolition of everything the articles saw, because two of their observations survived the correction of their premises and were taken up, in developed form, by the historiography that corrected them. The first concerns the fate of Indian industry under British rule. Marx described the destruction of the handloom weavers' world by machine made imports as a catastrophe consciously administered, and Indian nationalist economists, above all Dadabhai Naoroji and R. C. Dutt, built on such accounts the theory of the drain of wealth and the deindustrialisation of India: a country that had clothed much of the world reduced, by tariff policy, by the tax burden, and by the enforced openness of its markets to British goods while its own goods faced barriers abroad, to an exporter of raw materials and an importer of manufactures. Twentieth century economic historians contested the thesis vigorously, arguing that handloom production adapted and survived in niches, that the statistical basis for a collapse of industry was thin, and that the weavers' suffering, real as it was, had been generalised beyond the evidence. The debate runs on, in refined forms, among specialists, and this article takes no side in its statistical trenches, in keeping with the protocol that contested figures are described qualitatively rather than brandished. What can be said is that the question Marx posed in 1853, what this rule was doing to the productive life of the society it governed, was the right question, and that the nationalist tradition that posed it after him was reading him, among others, when it learned to pose it. A wrong description can still contain a live problem, and the problem of what conquest did to India's development has outlived every answer so far given to it.

The second survivor is the analysis of the transfer itself. Beneath the double mission's philosophy sits a colder piece of reporting: the insistence that India's wealth was being systematically drawn off to England, through the charges of government, the guaranteed returns of railway capital, the pensions and remittances of the services. The young correspondent of 1853 already tracked the money with a bookkeeper's suspicion, and the old man of 1881, in the Danielson letter discussed below, would state the transfer as the central fact of the Indian connection and call it a bleeding process. Between those poles the analysis of extraction grew into one of the permanent instruments of anti-colonial economics, reaching its classical form in the drain theory of the Indian nationalists and its modern descendants in the literature on unequal exchange and dependency. None of this rescues the progressive verdict of 1853, which depended on the static premise and falls with it. It does mean that the articles bequeathed two opposed legacies to the colonised world's own movements: a warrant for the conqueror's modernising mission, quoted by every subsequent apologist of tutelage, and a method for auditing the conqueror's accounts, used by every subsequent movement of liberation. The same twenty pages supplied both. That is not a paradox to be resolved by choosing the truer Marx. It is the record, and the habit of choosing is the disease this article was written against.

## The private language: the notorious letter, and Engels on nationality

The published record is only part of the evidence, and the unpublished part is uglier. Marx's private correspondence, written to Engels in the confidence of a lifelong friendship and published long after both men were dead, contains language that is racist by any standard that can be applied to it, his own included. The best known instance, because it concentrates the whole problem in a single page, is a letter of 30 July 1862, written after a visit to London by Ferdinand Lassalle, the German socialist leader who was Marx's collaborator, rival, and competitor for the leadership of the German workers' movement. In the course of mocking his guest, Marx reached for Lassalle's Jewish birth and for his appearance, and the mockery is conducted in epithets: an antisemitic slur directed at Lassalle as a Jew, and a racial slur directed at him as a man Marx speculated, on no evidence beyond his looks and his dislikes, to be of African descent. The letter treats the supposed ancestry as itself a joke and a discredit, which is the point that removes any shelter the conventions of private joking might be thought to offer. The prejudice is not incidental decoration on an argument; for the length of the passage, it is the argument, doing the work of diminishing a political opponent by assigning him to peoples the writer expects his reader to hold in contempt. The letter is printed in the standard edition of the correspondence, the forty-first volume of the Marx and Engels Collected Works, where any reader may verify this characterisation in full. It will not be quoted here, for the reason given at the outset: the reader now knows what the letter contains, what kind of language it uses, at whom it is aimed, and where it can be inspected, and nothing analytic would be added by putting the words themselves on this page.

Precision about the instance matters, because the controversy around it generates two opposite falsifications. The first falsification enlarges it: the letter is quoted, in the polemical literature, as though it were Marx's considered writing on race, a public doctrine rather than a private sneer, and the whole of his published work is then read through it. The second falsification shrinks it: the letter is described as banter, as irony, as the ordinary roughness of nineteenth century correspondence, as anything except what it is. Both moves should be refused. A private letter is evidence about the man, and it is also evidence about the gap between the man's practice and his theory's demands, since the theory he was building left no room for explaining a rival's politics by his blood. That the author of a doctrine of human emancipation could write this page, in this tone, to this correspondent, is a fact about the limits of the doctrine's hold on its own author, and it belongs in any honest assessment of him. It does not belong in an assessment of the doctrine's truth, which is a different question with a different article assigned to it, and conflating the two is the third falsification, practised by both camps when convenient. The 1862 letter discredits Marx's claim, were he to make it, to have lived his own universalism. It neither proves nor disproves anything in the critique of political economy, and this article will grade it exactly there: a serious entry in the biography, a null entry in the logic.

### What does the notorious letter contain, and where can it be verified?

Written to Engels on 30 July 1862 about his rival Ferdinand Lassalle, the letter abuses Lassalle with an antisemitic slur on his Jewish birth and a racial slur on his features and imagined descent. It stands in volume forty-one of the Collected Works.

The letter is the worst of the correspondence, but it is not alone in kind, and honesty about the pattern is preferable to a defence built on its singularity. The private letters of both Marx and Engels contain, scattered across decades, the casual racial vocabulary of their century: peoples ranked, national characters derided, individuals diminished by origin. Much of it is the background noise of an age in which such speech carried no social cost among European intellectuals, and establishing that fact explains the letters without excusing them, since the same decades produced contemporaries, including men inside the workers' movement, who managed the subject without the slurs. One related exhibit belongs to the published record and is in some ways more consequential than any letter, because it was written for print and never disowned: Engels's articles of 1849 on the struggles of 1848 in central Europe. Writing in the Neue Rheinische Zeitung, in The Magyar Struggle of January 1849 and Democratic Pan-Slavism the following month, Engels divided the peoples of the Habsburg lands into historic nations, bearers of progress, and what he called peoples without history, the smaller Slavic nationalities, Czechs, Slovaks, Croats, Serbs and others, whom he portrayed as inherently counterrevolutionary remnants, instruments of tsarism by their mere existence, destined to disappear in the coming revolutionary storm and, in passages of genuine ferocity, to deserve their disappearance. The articles were aimed at a political target, Bakunin's appeal to the Slavs, and they rested on a real observation: several Slavic movements in 1848 had indeed served the counterrevolution against the Hungarian and German revolutions. But the form of the argument assigned the guilt not to governments or parties or decisions but to peoples as such, to their ethnic substance, and predicted their extinction as a historical good. That is the ethnicisation of politics in the movement's own newspaper, from the pen of Marx's closest collaborator, in the tradition's founding year.

The 1849 articles had the political consequences the brief for this article insists on recording. They supplied later generations of the workers' movement with a template for dismissing the national claims of small peoples as reactionary by definition, and the template was used, in the nationality disputes that ran through European socialism for seventy years, against movements whose claims deserved hearing on their merits. The template also provoked the tradition's own most searching internal correction: Roman Rosdolsky, a Ukrainian Marxist historian writing in exile after the Second World War, devoted a book length study to Engels and the problem of the nonhistoric peoples, demonstrating from the record of 1848 that the Slavic movements Engels condemned had contained democratic and revolutionary currents his articles flattened into ethnic destiny, and concluding that the doctrine of peoples without history was a Hegelian inheritance the movement should discard. Rosdolsky's study, published in 1964 after his death, remains the model of how this material should be handled: the facts stated in full, the inheritance named, the correction argued from evidence rather than performed as loyalty. It is worth adding that Marx himself, in his mature political work on Poland and Ireland, moved steadily away from the 1849 template toward its opposite, the position that national oppression is a material relation between peoples that poisons the class politics of the oppressor nation itself. That movement is the subject of the sections to come. It does not retract the letters, which were private and stood unretracted because unknown, and it does not soften the articles of 1849, which were public. It does establish that the tradition's founder, on the national question, spent his later decades arguing against the premise his own household had printed in the year of revolutions.

### What did Engels write about the smaller Slavic peoples in the revolutionary years?

Writing in the Neue Rheinische Zeitung during the revolutions, in The Magyar Struggle and Democratic Pan-Slavism, Engels sorted the Habsburg peoples into historic nations and peoples without history, and cast the smaller Slavic nationalities as counterrevolutionary in their very nature, remnants destined to disappear.

A word, finally, about why this section has been written in the manner it has, since the manner is itself one of the article's claims. The two available styles for handling this material are exhibition and burial. Exhibition quotes the epithets at length, derives its energy from the shock of them, and teaches the reader nothing except that the writer of the article is willing to shock; it also manufactures a page on which the slurs circulate afresh, detached from any analysis, available for harvesting by anyone who wants them. Burial describes the correspondence in reassuring generalities, prejudices of the time, unfortunate phrases, and trusts that the reader will not check; it is the style of the authorised biography, and it collapses the moment a hostile reader opens the Collected Works. Characterisation with citation is the third style, and it is the only one compatible with the rest of this article's method: state what the document is, what it contains, in language that does not flinch, name its date and its place in the published record, and let the verdict rest on facts the reader can verify independently. Under that discipline, the 1862 letter is exactly as damning as it should be and not one degree more theatrical. The language was racist and antisemitic. The man who wrote it was building a theory that condemned the premises of such language. Both statements are true, both are now on the record with their evidence identified, and the assessment can proceed to the parts of the record where the same man reasoned about colonial peoples in public, at length, and better.

### Why does this article characterise the letter's language instead of reproducing it?

Because the reader's need is analytical: to know what kind of language the letter uses, against whom, and where it can be verified. A precise characterisation with a full citation supplies all of that, while reproducing the epithets would add nothing except the slurs themselves to the page.

## Engels in the year of revolutions: context and brutal shorthand

The articles of 1849 require their context to be understood, though the context mitigates nothing essential and the assessment has already graded them. Europe in that year was a battlefield of collapsing revolutions. The Hungarian republic under Kossuth was fighting for its life against the Habsburg armies; the Habsburg commanders, in turn, were deploying the empire's Slavic regiments, and above all the Croat forces of Ban Jelačić, against the revolutions in Vienna and Hungary; the Tsar's army would presently march into Hungary to finish the work. In Prague, a Slav congress had gathered in June 1848 to articulate the national programme of the empire's Slavic peoples, and Bakunin's subsequent Appeal to the Slavs had called for a revolutionary federation of Slavic nations allied, if necessary, with any power against the Germans and Magyars. Engels, writing as the revolution's partisan and its military correspondent, sorted the nations of the empire by the side they had taken in the war he was reporting. The great historic nations, Germans, Magyars, Poles, had made the revolution; the smaller Slavic peoples, as he read their conduct, had supplied the counterrevolution with its soldiers and its legitimacy, and were in his analysis the domestic garrison of tsarism, the external enemy's internal ally. The shorthand of historic and nonhistoric peoples was his instrument for converting that military judgement into a philosophy of history, and the instrument was Hegel's, borrowed from the lectures in which world historical peoples carry the spirit for an epoch and the rest are material. Everything that is objectionable in the articles flows from the conversion. A judgement about governments, regiments, and moments, defensible or not on the evidence of 1848, is reissued as a judgement about peoples, their natures, and their futures, and the reissue licences the passages in which whole nationalities are pronounced the refuse of peoples and consigned to extinction with satisfaction.

His later practice moved against the articles as surely as Marx's did, and the symmetry deserves recording because the household is the unit of the early doctrine. Engels spent the following decades as the workers' movement's most persistent advocate of Polish independence, supported the Irish cause with his own reporting and his unfinished history, and in his late correspondence on the national question treated small peoples and their national claims with a patience the young military correspondent would have found unintelligible. He never formally retracted the articles of 1849, which stood in the canon to be quoted by later dogmatists, exactly as the 1853 articles stood to be quoted against the Irish letters. The pattern is the household's pattern and the movement's: development in practice and in later writing, silence in the form of retraction, and a canon left in disorder for heirs to sort. Rosdolsky's correction, discussed above, was necessary precisely because the sorting was not done by the authors. An assessment that demands of the dead a standard of self-revision they never practised will grade every founder of every tradition at the same failing mark, which may be just; but the grading in this article has deliberately distinguished the parts that were revised in substance from the parts that were not, and by that standard the 1849 articles occupy a middle position, repudiated by their author's later politics, unretracted in their text, and more dangerous in their afterlife than the private letter, because they were printed.

### Did Engels ever move beyond the doctrines of the revolutionary years?

In practice, steadily: the advocate of the nonhistoric peoples became the movement's advocate for Poland and for Ireland, travelled the evicted districts of Ireland in 1869, and began a history of Ireland that opens with its land and people. The articles themselves were never retracted, and their canonisation is part of the problem they pose.

## The record begins to move: rebellion in India

The change, when it came, came through events rather than through reading, and its first exhibit is the Indian rebellion of 1857. The rising that British memory named a mutiny began with sepoys of the Company's own army and spread into a broad revolt across northern India, with dispossessed princes, peasants, artisans, and soldiers making common cause against the foreign power. Marx reported it for the Tribune through the autumn of 1857, in articles on the revolt and on the investigation of the tortures inflicted by Company tax collectors, and the reporting does not read like the articles of 1853. The premise has shifted under his pen. He insisted to his American readers that what was occurring was not a mere military mutiny, a breakdown of discipline within an army, but a national revolt, the accumulated resentment of a conquered people finding its occasion. He detailed the fiscal tortures that had preceded it with the anger of a prosecutor, observed that the atrocities of the rebels, endlessly catalogued in the London press, were the reflex of atrocities the conquest had practised for a century, and remarked that there is something in human history like retribution, whose instrument is forged not by the offended party but by history itself. The Indians of these articles are no longer the passive material of a social formation awaiting dissolution. They are political actors in arms against an empire, and their grievances are treated as evidence about the empire's character rather than as spasms of a dying order.

The change should not be overdrawn, because precision about its limits is what separates an account of development from a redemption story. The Marx of 1857 did not withdraw the framework of 1853, did not revisit the village analysis, and did not predict Indian liberation as a good in itself; his sympathy ran with the rebels against the Company, in the spirit of an enemy of his enemy, and his expectation remained that the destruction of the old order, by whatever hand, served the future. What had changed was the distribution of agency. A theory that had treated colonial peoples as the objects of a history made elsewhere was being forced, by the telegraph reports of sieges and massacres, to register them as makers of events that London had to answer. In the same years his American journalism took up the second great colonial question of the century, slavery in the United States, and there the movement of his thought is unmistakable and entirely in one direction. Writing on the American Civil War for the Viennese press and the Tribune from 1861, Marx treated the war as a struggle over slavery at its root, supported the North against the slaveholders' republic, pressed the case for emancipation as a war measure, and in 1864 drafted the International Working Men's Association's address to Abraham Lincoln, congratulating the American working class that re-election had been secured on an antislavery platform. The founder of historical materialism was, by the eighteen sixties, a partisan of the enslaved against the slaveholders in print and in organisation. Set beside the letter of 1862, private and unworthy, the public record of the decade draws the exact shape of the contradiction this article has been assembling: a politics that moved, in its public reasoning, steadily toward the side of the oppressed, and a private idiom that lagged, unreformed, behind the politics. Neither half of that shape is the whole man, and an assessment that cannot hold both is not finished.

### What did Marx write when India rose in rebellion?

Reporting the rebellion for the Tribune, he rejected the official description of a mere military mutiny and called the rising a national revolt, traced its causes to the fiscal tortures and exactions of Company rule, and treated the rebels' violence as the reflex of a century of conquest's own violence.

There is a further document of the transition that belongs here because it shows the mechanism of the change at work. In 1858 and 1859, as the rebellion was crushed and the Crown absorbed the Company's territories, Marx wrote on the annexations and on the opium trade with a bitterness that has no equivalent in the charter debates: the history of British rule had become, for him, a ledger of crimes to be itemised rather than a process to be interpreted. The interpretive distance of 1853, the willingness to stand back and assign the atrocity its historical function, was narrowing year by year as the reports accumulated, and what replaced it was not sentiment but a different analytical object. He was ceasing to ask after the stage of development of the society under rule, and beginning to ask what the colonial relation did to the politics of both societies, the ruled and the ruling. That second question is the one the Irish letters answer, and it is a better question, because its evidence was available to him in London itself, in the faces of Irish labourers at English docks and in the votes of English workers who hated them. The road from the India articles to the Ireland letters runs through this change of question, and it was travelled by a man in his fifties, in public, against the grain of his own most famous formulations, which is a rarer intellectual event than either his prosecutors or his defenders usually allow.

## The hardening verdict: the later India journalism

The reporting of the rebellion's aftermath completes the journalistic record on India, and its tone settles a question the defence and the prosecution both leave vague: whether the sympathy of 1857 was an episode or a direction. Through 1858 Marx followed the settlement the British imposed on the country they had reconquered: the abolition of the Company and the transfer of its sovereignty to the Crown under the Government of India Act, the proclamations by which the new regime promised religious neutrality and respect for property while confiscations proceeded, the annexation of Oudh whose proclamation by Lord Canning he dissected as an instrument for separating the landholders from the population they might have led. The articles on the annexation and on the treatment of the conquered provinces are written in the idiom of the torture reports rather than of the charter debate: the empire appears as a system of spoliation administered by proclamation, and its reforming legislation as the continuation of conquest by legal means. Nothing in these pages revives the compensating doctrine of 1853. The solvent is gone; what remains is the audit. A reader who arrays the India file in order, 1853, 1857, 1858 and 1859, watches a correspondent's categories fail under the weight of his own reporting and be replaced, piecemeal, by the categories of the later work: extraction instead of development, resistance instead of stagnation, the relation instead of the stage. The replacement was never announced, and the file therefore lacks the one document the controversy has always demanded of him, a recantation that would let the archive close. It must be graded as it stands: a direction, sustained over the last six years of the correspondence on India, away from every distinctive proposition of the two famous articles, without a single published sentence acknowledging the journey. Whether that silence was the indifference of a journalist to his back pages, the pride of a system builder, or the simple arithmetic of a man with a masterwork unfinished and a family to feed, the record does not say. The direction, which the record does say, is what the assessment has weighed.

## The rebellion's afterlife: mutiny, war of independence, and Marx's place in the quarrel

The rising of 1857 acquired, in the century after it, a second life as an object of historiographical war, and Marx's reporting became a small but telling exhibit in that war, which makes it a fitting place to test his journalistic judgement against the tribunal of later scholarship. The British official memory named the event a mutiny, a military crime enlarged by fanaticism, and the naming did political work: a mutiny has grievances about cartridges and discipline, while a rebellion has causes in the order that produced it. Indian nationalist historiography answered in kind, beginning with Vinayak Savarkar's banned history of 1909, which named 1857 the first war of Indian independence, and the naming did its own work, recruiting the dead of the rising into a nation still to be made. Professional historians then complicated both inheritances: R. C. Majumdar, the most sceptical of the nationalist school's elders, concluded that the rising was neither first, nor national, nor a war of independence in the full sense, but a congeries of revolts with different causes and no common programme, while the documentary volumes assembled under P. C. Joshi's editorship for the centenary recovered the social depth of the rebellion, its peasant and artisan masses, its proclamations, its local leaderships, in a detail that made the mutiny label untenable. The mature consensus, insofar as the quarrel permits one, holds that 1857 was a great popular revolt against foreign rule, regionally uneven, ideologically various, and national more in its object than in its organisation. Against that consensus the Tribune reporting of the autumn of 1857 scores remarkably well. Its author, reading the same telegraphic fragments as the London press that coined the mutiny language, rejected the label at the time, located the causes in the fiscal and annexationist record of the Company, and treated the rebels as political actors with grievances rather than as a crime wave. He scored well because, for once, the interested archive was not his only source: the event generated evidence faster than the administration could file it, in the form of sieges, proclamations, and a terror whose scale reported itself. The contrast with 1853 could not be sharper, and it is diagnostic for the whole record. Given events, his judgement about colonial peoples was acute and early. Given only the file, he filed the file's conclusions under his own name. An assessor looking for the single sentence that explains both the articles and the letters could do worse than that.

## The American war: slavery, labour, and the sentence in Capital

The American Civil War occupies a place in this record that neither camp in the colonial controversy uses as it should, because it is the case where the analysis of a racial order and the politics of emancipation meet in his work at full maturity, before Ireland. From the war's outbreak Marx insisted, against the near unanimous reading of the European press, that slavery was not one issue among others but the issue: that the rebellion was a slaveholders' revolt for the extension and security of their system, that the North's temporising on emancipation was a military as well as a moral error, and that the war would be won only when it became a war on the institution itself. He followed the diplomacy with an analyst's patience, worked to prevent British recognition of the Confederacy by mobilising working class opinion against intervention, and treated the English mill workers, starved of cotton by the blockade and yet refusing to demand its lifting at the price of slavery's victory, as the decade's great exhibit of class solidarity across a racial line. The theoretical deposit of the war years appears in Capital itself, in a single sentence that has outlasted most of the volume: "Labour cannot emancipate itself in the white skin where in the black it is branded." The sentence is the Irish doctrine in American dress, arrived at independently and earlier: a labour movement organised within a racial hierarchy carries the hierarchy inside itself as its limit, and the emancipation of the favoured section is blocked by the unfreedom of the degraded one. Place the sentence beside the letter of 1862 and the contradiction that structures this whole article stands in its most compressed form. The theorist who wrote that sentence into his masterwork understood, at the level of principle, exactly what racial division does to a working class. The correspondent who wrote the letter of the same decade deployed racial contempt against a rival without apparent friction. The assessment does not get to choose between the sentence and the letter; it gets to record that the same intelligence produced both, and that the sentence, unlike the letter, was argued, published, and bequeathed.

### What did the American war change in his analysis of race and labour?

It gave him a case in which a racial order was the foundation of a modern economy and the central question of a great war, and he drew from it the principle later generalised in the Irish letters: a working class divided by a racial line cannot emancipate its favoured section while the line stands.

## The eighteen sixties in print: Mexico, and the education of a correspondent

Between the revolt in India and the reversal on Ireland stands a decade of journalism in which the education visible in 1857 continued, and one episode fixes its character. In 1861 the government of Napoleon III, using a debt dispute as its pretext, launched a French expedition against Mexico, first in company with Britain and Spain and then, when its partners withdrew, alone, with the transparent aim of erecting a client empire in the Americas under an imported Austrian archduke. Marx covered the enterprise for the press in 1861 and 1862, and his verdict on it was delivered without a trace of the double mission's double bookkeeping: he called the intervention one of the most monstrous enterprises in the annals of international history, a piece of buccaneering by a regime that needed foreign glory to varnish domestic usurpation. No solvent was discerned in the French bayonets, no regeneration predicted from the puppet throne, no stage assigned to the Mexicans that an invasion might help them climb. The difference from 1853 is not that Mexico was European and India Asian; it is that by 1861 the writer had a decade of colonial reporting behind him, had watched the Company's torturers and the opium clippers and the suppression of the Indian revolt at close quarters in the daily press, and had absorbed the lesson his own later method would codify: that the character of a conquest is given by the relation it establishes, not by the civilisation the conqueror claims to carry. The editorials on Mexico are minor pieces next to the Irish letters, but they are the hinge on which the public record turns, the point at which the fury of 1853 is no longer balanced by a compensating entry on history's other side.

The decade's journalism had a second schoolroom, and it stood on the other side of the Atlantic. The American war drew from Marx some of his most sustained reporting, and its colonial dimension was not incidental to his analysis but central to it. He treated slavery as the pivot of the conflict years before the North's government dared to, argued that the South's society rested on the enslavement of one race as its foundation, and analysed, in terms the Irish letters would refine, how the existence of slavery corrupted the labour politics of the free states and held the white worker's movement in tutelage to the slaveholders' republic. When emancipation came as a war measure he recognised it as the war's meaning, and the International's address to Lincoln, drafted by Marx, congratulated the American workers on a victory won under the banner of labour's own emancipation. A man does not arrive at that address from the letter of 1862 without remainder, and this article has already refused to dissolve the remainder. What the decade shows is the public reasoning and the private idiom travelling in opposite directions at increasing speed, and a biographer who reports only one of the two journeys is writing fiction in either case.

## The Eastern Question: the other file of the eighteen fifties

India and China were not the only non-European files on the correspondent's desk, and the third file disciplines the assessment in a useful direction, because it shows the young Marx applying the Polish pattern, solidarity with national movements against a reactionary empire, well beyond Europe's eastern frontier while the Indian file was still governed by the staircase. The Eastern Question, the diplomatic name for the slow decomposition of the Ottoman Empire and the rivalry of the powers over its estate, filled his correspondence through the Crimean years. His fixed point in that file was Russia: the Tsar's empire figured in his analysis as the great reserve of European counterrevolution, the power whose expansion every revolution had reason to fear, and his journalism supported, in consequence, the peoples caught between the empires with a warmth the India articles never attempt. The national movements of the Danubian principalities, the Greeks, the Slavic Christians of the Balkans, appear in these pages as historical actors with claims, their emancipation treated as a European interest against both the Sultan's decay and the Tsar's ambition. The Ottoman order itself he described as a rotting structure, held together by the jealousies of its would-be heirs, and his contempt for the diplomacy that preserved it was total. The file has its own discreditable exhibit, which honesty requires in the margin: the series of articles in which he undertook to expose Lord Palmerston as a lifelong agent in Russian pay, a conspiracy construction built from genuine diplomatic evasions and prosecuted with a certainty the evidence could not carry, which stands as the decade's warning that the correspondent's judgement, where his hatreds were engaged, could overrun his method as surely as any archive could capture it. Set the three files side by side and the pattern of the eighteen fifties clarifies. Where a national movement existed that he could see and name, Poles, Balkan Christians, Irish in embryo, his politics found it and sided with it against empire. Where the society reached him only as an administrative abstraction, India above all, the staircase governed and the people vanished into their stage. The variable was never sympathy, which the record shows in erratic supply everywhere. The variable was evidence of agency, and the whole later development of his colonial thought can be written as the expansion of the class of peoples in whose agency the evidence compelled him to believe.

## China in the same years: opium, rebellion, and a solvent refused

India was not the only Asian society on his desk, and the Chinese writings of the same years are a control experiment the assessment should not waste, because they show the same framework producing a different balance when the evidence differed. In June 1853, in the article on revolution in China and in Europe, Marx took up the Taiping rebellion, the vast civil war then consuming the Qing empire, and treated it as a world event: the impact of British trade and British opium had cracked the celestial empire's isolation, and the rebellion's explosion would react back upon Europe's own economy and politics, an Eastern crisis as the possible detonator of a European one. The Chinese of this article are not a frozen backdrop. Their society is shown in convulsion from within, its rebellion driven by the dislocations of foreign intrusion and its own dynastic decay together, and its future is treated as an open question with European consequences. The static premises of the India articles sit beside this analysis in the same month's correspondence without visible embarrassment, which tells the assessor something important: the framework's handling of Asia was not a uniform doctrine but a set of reports of uneven quality, strongest where events supplied him actors, weakest where only the archive supplied him scenery. Five years later the Chinese file deepened. In the autumn of 1858 he wrote a short history of the opium trade for the Tribune, tracing the enforced commerce from the East India Company's Bengal monopoly through the first Opium War to the settlement then being imposed in the second, and the articles are prosecutions from first line to last: a trade in poison, maintained by war against a state attempting to suppress it, defended in London by the cant of free commerce, and corrupting every institution it touched. No double mission appears. The solvent is refused outright, because the evidence before him, the record of the trade itself, admitted no regenerating column in the ledger. The China writings therefore qualify every simple story told about the India articles in both directions. Against the defence: he could write about an Asian empire without the frozen scenery when the material gave him movement, so the scenery in the India articles was a choice the sources invited rather than a necessity the framework imposed. Against the prosecution: the hand that wrote the double mission also wrote, in the same decade, the century's most widely read English language indictment of the opium wars, and a doctrine of uniform Orientalist capture cannot explain the difference between the files.

## Poland: the apprenticeship of the national question

Ireland was the completion of an education, not its beginning, and the earlier terms were served on Poland. The Polish question ran through Marx's entire political life like a watermark. He had spoken for Polish independence, against the three partitioning empires, in the revolutionary years themselves; the founding meeting of the International in London in September 1864 took place in an atmosphere of solidarity with the Polish uprising of the previous year, crushed by Russia with a savagery that had revolted European opinion; and the International's early programme and addresses returned to Poland as the standing test of whether the workers' movement meant its universalism. His reasoning about Poland anticipated, in embryo, the reasoning he would later perfect on Ireland. Russia's despotism rested, in his analysis, on its western conquests; the Polish nation in chains was the outer bastion of that despotism and simultaneously the lever against it, since a free Poland would break the reactionary power that loomed over every European revolution. The structure is the Irish structure, two decades early: a subject nation's emancipation valued not only as justice to the subject but as the precondition of liberation in the oppressor countries themselves, with the oppressor's workers named as beneficiaries of a freedom they might be tempted to resent. Poland even supplied the maxim, delivered in the Brussels speeches of 1847, that no nation wins its own freedom while holding another nation in subjection, a sentence the confidential communication of 1870 would restate for Ireland as doctrine.

Why, then, did the maxim wait twenty years for its Irish application, and longer still for any application beyond Europe? The reasons are instructive, because they are the same reasons that structured the 1853 failure. Poland was a European nation with a documented history of statehood, a nobility, a literature, a place in the revolutionary imagination of 1848; it fitted, effortlessly, the category of a historic nation temporarily subdued, and solidarity with it cost the framework nothing. Asia, in the scheme he inherited, had been assigned to the opposite category, and the categories did his seeing for him until events battered them. Ireland occupied the instructive middle ground between the two assignments: a European people, colonised for centuries, Catholic and Celtic in the English imagination's contemptuous construction, present in England itself as a migrant workforce whose treatment in the English labour market could be observed at first hand by a resident of London. Ireland was close enough to be unignorable and colonised enough to force the general question. The education proceeded by proximity, from Poland the abstraction to Ireland the neighbour to the Russian commune the scholarly problem, and each step required the framework to surrender a category it had treated as natural. This is not the pattern of a man applying a finished colonial doctrine, reactionary or emancipatory. It is the pattern of a mind whose universalism was real but whose information was European, being dragged by politics and evidence toward conclusions its early system had no room for, and finding room by breaking the system open at the joints.

## Capital on the colonies: blood, fire, and the last chapter

Any assessment confined to the journalism misses the theoretical masterwork, and the masterwork complicates the assessment in the direction of severity toward empire. The first volume of Capital, published in 1867, closes its account of the origins of capitalism with chapters on the expropriation of the peasantry and on the colonial system, and the colonial passages are among the most violent in the book. The discovery of the Americas, the extirpation and enslavement of their indigenous peoples, the hunting of human beings for their skins and the conversion of Africa into a preserve for the commercial hunting of black skins, the plunder of India and the slave trade's management of Liverpool's prosperity: these are catalogued, in prose of controlled fury, as the chief moments of primitive accumulation, the process by which capital came into the world, in his phrase, dripping from head to foot, from every pore, with blood and dirt. No developmental alibi is offered for any of it. The colonial system is presented as a hothouse of accumulation, a machinery for transferring wealth by violence from the periphery to the counting houses of Europe, and the national debt, the tax system, and the great chartered companies are named as its instruments. A reader who met Marx only in these chapters, and there are generations who met him chiefly there, would find it unintelligible that the same writer had once assigned British rule in India a regenerating mission, and the juxtaposition is the puzzle at the centre of this article in its sharpest form: the author of 1853 and the author of 1867 are the same man, and Capital's primitive accumulation reads, in part, like a prosecution brief against the premises of the Tribune articles, assembled by their author without acknowledgement.

The final chapter of the volume sharpens the point into a paradox the assessment must record. It is devoted to the modern theory of colonisation, and its butt is Edward Gibbon Wakefield, the theorist of systematic colonisation, whose schemes for settling the colonies with a managed supply of wage labour had influenced policy across the empire. Marx's treatment of Wakefield is contemptuous and admiring at once: contemptuous of the colonial project, admiring of the theorist's inadvertent honesty, since Wakefield's difficulties in Australia, where land was too cheap for wage labour to be had at any price until the state artificially raised its price, had forced him to confess in print what European political economy concealed, that capital is not a thing but a social relation, which must be manufactured where it does not exist by separating the producer from the land by force. The colonies, in this closing argument, are valuable to the theorist precisely because they strip the disguise: in the colony, the violence that Europe's economy presents as natural law stands confessed as policy. It is a remarkable place for the book to end, and it confirms the trajectory this article has been tracing through the journalism. By 1867 the colonies have moved, in Marx's analysis, from the waiting room of history to the X-ray room of capital, the place where the system's secrets are most legible. The double mission is not formally retracted in these pages, and the Asiatic category survives in the book's footnotes and its predecessor drafts. But a framework in which colonialism's essence is plunder, and in which the colony reveals the metropolis's truth, has no logical room left for the progressive solvent of 1853, and Marx never returned to the solvent doctrine in print after Capital. The letters on Ireland, two and three years later, would draw the political conclusion the theory had already reached.

### What does the chapter on primitive accumulation say about the colonies?

It counts the colonial system among primitive accumulation's chief moments, and catalogues the enslavement and extirpation of the Americas' peoples, the slave trade, and India's spoliation as the violent transfer that assembled capital, excusing none of it as a stage of development.

## The wages of empire: an early letter on the English workers' feast

One further document of the eighteen fifties belongs to the prehistory of the reversal, because it shows the household reasoning about empire's effect on the metropolitan working class a decade before Ireland forced the doctrine into the open. In October 1858 Engels wrote to Marx reflecting on the English proletariat's political quietism, and his explanation ran through the empire: the English working class, he observed, was becoming more and more bourgeois in its outlook, a development he found, for a nation that exploited the whole world, to a certain extent explicable, since its workers shared, in their standard of life and their expectations, in the feast of England's monopoly of the world market and the colonies. The observation is the seed of a theory, later developed by others under the name of the labour aristocracy, but its interest here is structural. Already in 1858 the household's analysis contained the proposition that colonial exploitation deforms the politics of the exploiting nation's workers, bending them toward the order that feeds them. What the Irish letters add, twelve years on, is the mechanism and the remedy: the deformation works not through comfort alone but through the antagonism of a divided workforce, and it is broken not by prosperity's end but by the political victory of the colonised nation whose labour underwrites the division. Read together, the letter of 1858, the Irish analysis of Capital's accumulation chapter, and the correspondence of 1869 and 1870 form a single developing argument about empire's domestic wages, conducted across every genre the household used, and it is the most original contribution the record makes to the theory of politics. The articles of 1853 have no equivalent progeny inside the theory itself. Their author's fame in the colonial question rests, in the end, on the work in which he analysed his own country's workers as empire's beneficiaries and its hostages, and prescribed, as the solvent of their condition, the freedom of the people his youthful journalism had never discussed at all.

## The Irish in England: the workforce inside the gates

The reversal of 1869 and 1870 had a material setting that the letters presuppose and that the assessment should make explicit, because the analysis was built from the labour market of the country Marx lived in. Ireland's catastrophe of the eighteen forties, the famine that killed and exiled on a scale this article, by its protocol, describes without figures, drove a vast migration into the industrial districts of Britain: into Liverpool and Manchester, Glasgow and the London docks, the navvy gangs of the railways and the building trades. The Irish in England were concentrated, by exclusion and by poverty, in the worst paid and most dangerous employments, housed in the districts the sanitary reformers wrote their reports about, and available to employers as the standing reproach and instrument against English labour's demands: the people who could be brought in to break a strike, to fill a mill at lower wages, to demonstrate to the English operative that his standard of life was a privilege revocable at the dock gate. English working class culture returned the pressure with a contempt that the music halls, the comic press, and the pulpit serviced as diligently as the Meyer and Vogt letter charges. Marx lived inside this arrangement for twenty years before he theorised it. He had read it in Engels's Manchester book of 1845, observed it in the streets of the city he inhabited, and worked with its products in the International, where Irish branches and Irish organisers were a living presence rather than a foreign question. The letters' originality is not the discovery that English workers despised the Irish; any journalist in London could report that. It is the assignment of function and beneficiary: the antagonism is analysed as a piece of social machinery, maintained because it works, dividing the one class whose unity threatens the order, and paying its English operators in the satisfactions of national membership rather than in wages, which it more probably costs them. That analysis required a resident's evidence and a conspirator's attention to who profits, and it could not have been written from the files of a colonial office, because the files of the English state did not describe England to itself in those terms. The contrast with 1853 is once again the contrast between an archive and an acquaintance.

### Why did the antagonism of English and Irish workers matter so much to the argument?

Because it was the mechanism by which the colonial relation reproduced itself inside the English working class: Irish labour, held cheap by conquest and famine, disciplined English labour's demands, while English workers, taught contempt for the Irish, defended the very arrangement that held their own movement divided and impotent.

## Ireland: the great reversal

The Ireland reversal is the centre of this article, as it is the centre of the whole record, and it must be set out in full. Begin with the position reversed. Through most of the eighteen sixties Marx's view of the Irish question followed the logic an English socialist might have been expected to hold: Ireland's emancipation would arrive as a consequence of the English working class taking power, since a workers' government in London would have no use for holding a subject nation, and the Irish should therefore subordinate their national struggle to the common struggle of the British movement. He had once thought the outright separation of Ireland from England impossible; by 1867 he was writing to Engels that he now thought it inevitable. What turned a revised expectation into a reversed theory was the Fenian movement, the Irish republican organisation whose uprising of 1867 failed, whose prisoners filled English jails, and whose cause detonated, literally, at Clerkenwell prison in London in December of that year. The Fenians forced Ireland from the margin of English politics to its centre, and they forced Marx, who worked with Irish members inside the International and debated the Irish question in its General Council, to think the relation through again from the ground. His letters of the winter of 1869 and 1870 record the result, and the result is an inversion: not Ireland waiting on England, but England waiting on Ireland.

The inversion is stated with complete clarity in a letter to Ludwig Kugelmann of 29 November 1869. Marx reported that he had become convinced, against his earlier view, that the decisive blow against the English ruling classes "cannot be delivered in England but only in Ireland". The reasoning ran through the material structure of English power. Ireland was the stronghold of the English landed aristocracy, the class whose rents were drawn from Irish land and whose political weight rested on the Union; the exploitation of Ireland was not a byproduct of that aristocracy's wealth but one of its principal sources, and its greatest moral strength besides. To strike the aristocracy where its income and prestige were anchored was therefore to strike the keystone of the whole English order, and the Irish national movement, far from being a distraction from the social struggle, was the lever by which the social struggle in England itself could be moved. The General Council's confidential communication of March 1870 carried the argument into the International's official reasoning: every nation that oppresses another, it held, forges its own chains, and a people that subjugates a foreign people cannot emancipate itself. The sequence of Marx's earlier position is thereby exactly reversed. Irish emancipation was no longer the dividend of an English victory. It was the precondition of one.

### What had Marx expected about Ireland before the reversal?

He had expected Irish emancipation to follow from an English working class victory, as one consequence among others of a workers' government in London with no interest in holding a subject nation. Ireland, in that scheme, waited on England, and its national movement was counselled to subordinate itself to the common struggle.

The second movement of the reversal is deeper than the first, and it is the one that founds a tradition of analysis. In April 1870 Marx wrote to Sigfrid Meyer and August Vogt, his correspondents in America, setting out why the English working class, the best organised in Europe, remained politically impotent despite its numbers and its unions. The cause, he argued, was the antagonism between English and Irish workers, an antagonism manufactured and maintained by the ruling class through every instrument at its disposal: the press, the pulpit, the comic papers, the whole apparatus by which the English worker was taught to regard the Irish worker as a competitor who depressed his wages and his standard of life. The English worker, in Marx's account, divided the proletariat into two hostile camps and in doing so made himself an accomplice in his own subjection: he cherished religious, social, and national prejudices against the Irishman, behaved toward him much as the poor whites of the American South behaved toward the enslaved population whose labour underpinned their poverty, and felt himself, in relation to the Irish worker, a member of the ruling nation, thereby turning himself into a tool of his own rulers against Ireland and strengthening their domination over himself. Then the conclusion, which deserves to stand as the most important sentence he ever wrote on the subject of race and class: the English working class "will never accomplish anything before it has got rid of Ireland". National antagonism, on this analysis, is not a prejudice floating free of material relations, nor a survival destined to fade as enlightenment spreads. It is a mechanism, deliberately serviced, by which the cheaper labour of the oppressed nation is used to discipline the labour of the oppressor nation, while the oppressor nation's workers are paid for their discipline in the coin of status, in the feeling of membership in a ruling people. Every later Marxist analysis of racism as a structure dividing labour, rather than as a mere error of the mind, descends from this letter and from the confidential communication that surrounds it.

Measure the distance travelled. In 1853, colonial peoples were the objects of a history whose subject was European development; their societies were described from the files of their conquerors; their resistance figured as the death spasm of obsolete forms; and the question asked of a colony was what stage it occupied and what solvent would move it. In 1870, a colonised people has become the strategic subject of the whole analysis: its national movement holds the lever of the oppressor country's revolution, its grievances are the key to the oppressor's politics, and the psychology of the oppressor's workers, their felt membership in a ruling nation, is analysed as a material force with material beneficiaries and a deliberate maintenance crew. The question put to a colony is no longer what stage it occupies. It is what the colonial relation does to both peoples, who profits from the antagonism it generates, and what alliance could break it. Nothing in the theory of 1853 required this second analysis, and much in the theory of 1853 obstructed it, since a scheme in which the colonised have no history cannot easily grant them the decisive historical role the letters assign to Ireland. The letters were written against the grain of the founder's own framework, by the founder, which is why the pack of texts this article examines cannot be honestly summarised by its first item. The Ireland reversal is this article's namable claim, and it is worth stating in the exact form the evidence supports: Marx's most consequential writing on colonial questions is not the journalism of 1853 but the letters of 1869 and 1870, in which he inverts his earlier order of emancipation and identifies national antagonism as the machinery by which a working class is rendered impotent; any assessment of his position that rests on the earlier articles alone is assessing a position he spent his last decades abandoning.

### Why did Ireland, rather than England, become the lever in the argument?

Because Ireland was the stronghold of the English landed aristocracy, whose rents and political weight rested on the Union, so that the Irish national movement struck the keystone of English class power directly, while the English working class, its victory long expected first, remained divided against itself by the antagonism the colonial relation produced.

Two qualifications keep the reversal honest, and both belong in the record. The first is that the reversal was strategic as well as moral, and Marx never pretended otherwise: Ireland mattered to him decisively because it was the weak point of the English order and the division point of the English proletariat, and his solidarity with the Irish cause, genuine as the Fenian amnesty campaigns showed it to be, was argued throughout in terms of what Irish freedom would do for the common struggle rather than as an abstract debt of justice. His own formula was that Irish emancipation was not a question of abstract justice or humanitarian sentiment but the first condition of the English workers' own emancipation, and readers who want a purer motive will have to look elsewhere; the argument's strength is that it needs no purity, since it grounds the alliance in the structure of the antagonism itself. The second qualification is that the reversal, for all its depth, was never systematised. Marx wrote no book on Ireland, no treatise on national oppression; the analysis lives in letters, council resolutions, and confidential communications, occasional pieces produced inside a political organisation's debates, and it coexisted in his mind, unreconciled, with survivals of the older framework that the Russian drafts would only begin to dislodge a decade later. The tradition that inherited both the letters and the framework has been arguing about their relation ever since, which is one reason the table that follows, and the scholarly debate after it, are necessary. A reversal scattered through correspondence is easy to miss, and for a century of summary writing about Marx on empire, it was missed with remarkable consistency.

## Ireland inside Capital: the evidence of the accumulation chapter

The Irish analysis is often treated as a political afterthought to the economics, a matter of letters and resolutions external to the masterwork, and the premise is false in a way that matters for the grading. The first volume of Capital, in the long chapter on the general law of capitalist accumulation, pauses over Ireland at a length that makes the country one of the book's principal exhibits. The analysis there is demographic and brutal: the clearances of the famine years and after, the consolidation of farms, the conversion of tillage to pasture, the engineered emigration by which Ireland exported its people as it exported its cattle, and the resulting remittances and rents flowing eastward across the channel. Ireland appears in the chapter as the agricultural district of England, a country whose function within the accumulation of the neighbouring island was to supply cheap food, cheap labour, and a surplus population whose pressure disciplined wages on both sides of the water. The theoretical significance of the exhibit is that the colonial relation is doing analytical work inside the theory of accumulation itself, not decorating its margins: the reserve army of labour, the concept the chapter exists to establish, is shown in its Irish form as a population managed by policy, starved into motion, and deployed by employers across a national line that the analysis names as such. When those letters then draw the political conclusion, that the antagonism of the two workforces is the secret of the English class's impotence, they are extending into strategy a relation the economics had already established as mechanism. The sequence is the exact reverse of the sequence of 1853, where a borrowed description carried a theory that never tested it. Here the theory is built from the inspected case, Ireland under the Union, generalised with reluctance, and published in the masterwork before it was announced in the correspondence. Readers who date the reversal from the letters are therefore three years late. The reversal was typeset in 1867, in the book its author staked his life on, and the letters merely drew its organisational consequences.

## The lever as a doctrine: what the Irish argument claims, and where its limits lie

The Irish letters reward the same slow analysis as the articles, because their doctrine is usually quoted as a slogan and rarely examined as an argument with conditions. Stated formally, the argument has four propositions. First, a ruling class's power is anchored in specific material strongholds, and the colonial possession may be one of them, as Ireland was the stronghold of the English landed aristocracy. Second, the colonial relation manufactures, inside the dominant nation's working class, an antagonism toward the colonised that functions as the ruling class's surest protection, because it divides the only force capable of displacing it. Third, the dominant nation's workers are compensated for their resulting impotence with a status good, the feeling of membership in a ruling nation, which they will defend against their own material interest until the antagonism is broken politically. Fourth, the sequence of emancipation must therefore run from the colony inward: the subject nation's freedom removes the anchor, dissolves the antagonism's material basis, and frees the dominant working class to act as a class for the first time. The argument's elegance is that it assigns every actor intelligible motives and requires no one's virtue: the Irish are to be freed not because English workers are generous but because English workers cannot be free otherwise. Its limits are as instructive as its strengths, and the assessment should state them as plainly as Marx's heirs have sometimes declined to do. The doctrine is a strategy for a specific configuration, a colony bound into the same state and the same labour market as the metropolis, and it does not mechanically transfer to colonies held at a distance, by different mechanisms, from peoples the metropolitan workers never meet; the later tradition's attempts to universalise the lever into a law of all anti-imperialist strategy produced both insights and mechanical absurdities. The doctrine also presumes that the antagonism, though manufactured, can be dissolved by the national victory it counsels, whereas the subsequent history of partitioned and postcolonial labour markets shows antagonisms outliving the flags that made them, a complication the letters do not anticipate. And the doctrine, for all its power, is silent on the question the 1853 articles at least faced: what the colonised society is to build on the ground the lever clears, a silence the Zasulich drafts would later fill for the Russian case but which no Irish text of Marx's addresses. Graded as what it is, a strategic analysis of genius for a specified configuration, the doctrine stands. Graded as a universal key, it fails in its author's own terms, since his mature method forbade universal keys, and the distinction between the two gradings is the whole difference between reading Marx and reciting him.

## Two pens on Ireland: Engels travels, and the household divides its labour

The Irish reversal is signed by Marx, but it was a household production, and the partner's contribution deserves its paragraph because it corrects the picture of a solitary theorist reasoning his way to a new position. Engels had been travelling to Ireland since the eighteen forties, when his partner Mary Burns, an Irish working class woman of Manchester, had taken him into the Irish districts of the English industrial city and, in the phrase of a later biographer, had shown him the colony inside the metropolis decades before he saw the colony itself. His book on the condition of the English working class, written in 1845 from that Manchester apprenticeship, already treats the Irish immigration as a force within English class formation, with an ambivalence the later letters would criticise in others: the Irish are at once the reserve that depresses English wages and a leaven of passion in an English movement he found too tame. In May 1869 Engels travelled in Ireland itself with Lizzie Burns, Mary's sister and his partner after Mary's death, and his letters home from the journey read like field notes for the reversal being drafted in London: the ruined cabins and evicted townlands, the soldiers in every town, the contrast between the green fertility of the land and the poverty of its people, the conclusion, recorded at the time, that the Irish had been robbed of all the conditions of human development and that English rule stood exposed in Ireland as naked domination unmediated by any of the hypocrisies available at home. In 1870 he began a history of Ireland, never finished, whose surviving fragment opens with the land and its people rather than with the formations of any scheme. The division of labour in the household that year is almost too neat: Engels gathering the evidence of the colony with his eyes, Marx forging it into the strategic doctrine of the letters, Jenny Marx exposing the prison regime to the French public. Those letters are the doctrine's canonical text, but their authority in the movement came partly from this texture of witnessed fact around them. A theory of national oppression written by men who had walked the evicted townlands landed differently from a theory of Asian stagnation assembled from the India Office files, and the difference in epistemic standing between the two bodies of work is a difference this article has been enforcing throughout: where he had evidence, the later Marx is formidable; where he had only the archive of the interested, the young Marx is the archive's echo. The colonial question divided his work precisely along the line between what he had seen argued by its victims and what he had read in the files of its administrators.

## The objection from the Irish side: a lever for whom?

The reversal has its own internal critics, and the strongest of them speaks from the colonised side of the relation, a vantage the article has so far assigned to the analysis rather than against it. The objection runs as follows. In the Irish letters, Ireland's freedom is argued for, tirelessly and brilliantly, as the precondition of somebody else's emancipation: the English working class's. The Irish are the lever, the stronghold, the keystone, the secret; the metaphors are a strategist's metaphors, and the beneficiary named at the climax of every argument is the movement in England. An Irish reader is entitled to ask whether the reversal reversed anything fundamental, or whether it merely repriced Ireland within the same economy of usefulness in which 1853 had priced India: the colony valued, in both decades, for its function in a history whose protagonist lives elsewhere. The objection is not a debating point. It identifies a real feature of the letters' rhetoric, and it has been pressed, in various registers, by Irish historians protective of the national movement's autonomy against every philosophy that would make Ireland a means. His own formula, subordinating abstract justice to Irish freedom as the precondition of England's social emancipation, can be quoted in the objection's support by anyone who stops reading at the formula. The answer available in the full record is threefold, and it should be given without heat. First, the argument's addressee was the English movement, and arguments are shaped by the resistance they must overcome: to men tempted to see Ireland as a distraction, the demonstration that Ireland was the condition of their own cause was the only rhetoric equal to the occasion, and the confidential communication states the justice of the Irish cause in its own terms alongside the strategy. Second, the doctrine's practical consequence, in the General Council fight and the amnesty campaign, was the expenditure of the movement's scarce authority on Irish prisoners and Irish claims at real cost to itself, a record of conduct that the instrumentalist reading cannot explain, since levers are not usually defended at a loss. Third, the letters' analysis, once made, escaped its occasion: the proposition that a people which oppresses another forges its own chains assigns the oppressor nation the subordinate place in the moral ledger, and Irish and later anti-colonial readers took the doctrine up as their own property, as the Connolly lineage shows, which they would hardly have done had it read, in their hands, as a plan for their use. The objection nonetheless stands as a permanent caution in the margin of the reversal, and this article records it unanswered in its deepest form: the letters never address the Irish as their primary reader, and the society Ireland was to build after the lever had moved England is a subject on which their author, for all his solidarity, had nothing like the Zasulich drafts' patient curiosity. Solidarity and strategy coincided in him; the record shows both, and the grading has been made on both.

## The Irish debate in practice: the International, the prisoners, and the price

The reversal was not a private opinion confided to letters; it was fought for, inside an organisation, at a price, and the fighting is part of its evidentiary value. Through the autumn of 1869 the General Council of the International debated a resolution on Ireland drafted in Marx's spirit, calling on English workers to support the amnesty of the Fenian prisoners and declaring the Irish policy of the English government a policy of calculated antagonism. The debate was hard. English trade union leaders on the council, men who had built the organisation, resisted the elevation of the Irish question to the centre of the movement's strategy, and the resolution passed against real reluctance. Marx's private correspondence of those months is explicit about why he forced the issue: the English working class, he wrote, would remain in tutelage to its rulers until it broke its solidarity with the policy of oppression in Ireland, and the International's task was to make that break conscious. His daughter Jenny, writing for the French newspaper La Marseillaise in 1870 under a pseudonym, published a series of reports on the treatment of the Fenian prisoners in English jails: the solitary confinement, the insane asylums to which broken prisoners were consigned, the deliberate cruelties of the regime. The reports, which drew on the prisoners' own smuggled testimony, caused a scandal, forced questions in Parliament, and remain among the most effective pieces of agitational journalism the Marx household produced. They are relevant here because they show the reversal embodied in practice: the family that the prosecution remembers for a private letter of 1862 was, eight years later, spending its labour and its safety on the exposure of a colonial prison regime, in print, under a false name because the work was dangerous.

The price, when it came, was organisational, and Marx paid it knowingly. The Irish stand deepened every fissure in the International: it estranged English union officialdom, whose leaders drifted from the council in the following years, and it confirmed the organisation's reputation in the English press as a foreign conspiracy in Irish and Continental interests. Historians of the International have argued ever since about whether the Irish priority hastened the association's unravelling in Britain. What is not arguable is the direction of the choice. Faced with a trade between the respectable centre of the English labour movement and the claim of a colonised nation whose cause that centre found embarrassing, Marx chose the colonised nation, argued the choice as strategy rather than charity, and forced it through the movement's governing body by the strength of the Irish analysis itself. The confidential communication and the Meyer and Vogt letter were written inside this fight, which is why they have their peculiar hardness: they are not seminar conclusions but the theoretical yield of a political battle, an attempt to explain to cooperators why the embarrassing question was in fact the decisive one. Theory made under that pressure has a different evidentiary standing from theory made at leisure. It was tested against the resistance of the very audience it described, the English workers' own representatives, and it was not withdrawn when the test proved costly.

### What happened when the Irish resolution reached the General Council?

The council debated it through the autumn against the reluctance of leading English trade unionists, and passed it: amnesty for the Fenian prisoners, condemnation of the government's Irish policy, and an appeal to English workers to break their solidarity with the oppression of Ireland as the condition of their own movement's advance.

There is a coda to the Irish story that belongs to the assessment, because it shows the reversal surviving its author and becoming a tradition's common property. When James Connolly, the Irish socialist who would die before a British firing squad in 1916, came to write the history of the Irish working class and to construct the case that the cause of Ireland and the cause of labour were one cause, the materials he built with were substantially the materials of the Irish correspondence: national oppression as the prop of the possessing classes, the manufactured antagonism between the two islands' workers as the secret of both countries' subjection, emancipation of the colony as the precondition of emancipation in the metropolis. Connolly did not learn from Marx that Ireland mattered; he learned the analytical form in which its mattering could be argued to workers who had been taught to resent it. The line from the Meyer and Vogt letter to the labour politics of the colonised world runs through such readers, and it is the longest line any text in this article casts forward. Nothing comparable descends from the articles of 1853, whose progeny are textbooks and polemics rather than movements. Longevity is not proof of truth, and this article has graded the letters as analysis rather than as scripture. But when a position's influence is part of what is being assessed, the influence ledger of the reversal, measured in the movements it armed, is not a marginal entry.

## Russia and the end of the single road

The last movement of the record concerns Russia, and it completes the demolition of the 1853 scheme from inside Marx's own hand. In November 1877, replying to a Russian critic who had conscripted the chapter on primitive accumulation in Capital into a universal law, Marx wrote a letter to the editorial board of the journal Otechestvennye Zapiski protesting against the conversion of his account of how capitalism arose in Western Europe into a historico-philosophical theory of a path fatally imposed on every people. Events of a strikingly analogous kind, he insisted, occurring in different historical environments, lead to entirely different results, and each formation must be studied on its own terms before any comparison is drawn. The letter was not sent in his lifetime and was published after his death, but its doctrine is unambiguous: the unilinear staircase of 1853, on which every society climbs the same steps in the same order, is repudiated by its own author as a method. Four years later the repudiation acquired a concrete case. Vera Zasulich, a Russian revolutionary then in exile, wrote to Marx in February 1881 with a question on which Russian socialists were splitting: was the Russian rural commune, the obshchina, with its common ownership of land, doomed to dissolve before capitalism as the universal scheme required, or could it serve as the foundation of a different development? Marx took the question with a seriousness that honours him. He drafted four long replies, working through the commune's history and its dual nature, its communal ownership undermined by the individual production growing inside it, before sending a short letter in March 1881. The sent letter held that the analysis in Capital gave no reason either for or against the commune's vitality, because the historical inevitability of capitalist genesis described there was "expressly restricted to the countries of Western Europe"; the drafts went further, holding that the commune could become the fulcrum of a social regeneration in Russia, spared the torments of a capitalist dissolution, if the forces corroding it were removed.

The four drafts, discovered in his papers and published in 1924, are among the most remarkable documents of his last years, because they show the multilinear method being practised rather than preached. The commune is not assigned to a stage and sentenced. It is analysed in its specificity: its communal property and individual cultivation, its subjection to state taxation, its isolation as both weakness and protection, its survival into an epoch when capitalism existed elsewhere and its achievements could be appropriated without its birth pangs being repeated. In January 1882 Marx and Engels together signed a preface to the Russian edition of the Communist Manifesto stating the conclusion publicly: if a Russian revolution became the signal for revolution in the West, the common ownership of land in Russia could serve as the starting point of a communist development. The sequence that began with the frozen villages of Asia ends, thirty years on, with a communal village form, closely studied and in its own country, held capable of opening a road that bypasses the stage through which the 1853 articles had required every people to pass. The wheel had come fully round. A communal form was no longer the solid foundation of despotism; under changed conditions, it might be the foundation of an emancipation, and the difference lay not in the form but in the historical environment and in the analyst's willingness to study the case rather than grade it against a universal staircase. His letters from Algeria in the spring of 1882, written during his final illness, breathe the same air: the French settlers' treatment of the Arab population is described with open contempt, and the colonial relation is seen from the side of its victims without a whisper of the double mission. He died in March 1883 with the old scheme in ruins by his own hand and the new method scattered, like the Irish analysis, through letters and drafts that the century after him would have to excavate.

### What did Marx tell Zasulich about the Russian commune?

That the necessity of capitalist development described in Capital was expressly restricted to Western Europe, so the Russian commune was not condemned by his theory to dissolve; the drafts he prepared went further, treating the commune as a possible fulcrum of social regeneration once the pressures dissolving it were taken away.

The excavation is itself part of the story, and it explains the shape of the modern debate. The Zasulich drafts slept in the archives until David Ryazanov published them in 1924, in a Soviet Union whose official doctrine was hardening into exactly the unilinear staircase the drafts repudiate; the 1877 letter surfaced in print in the following decade through Engels's papers. For most of the twentieth century, therefore, both the prosecutors and the defenders of Marx on colonialism argued from the public record of the 1850s, the Tribune articles and the Manifesto's notorious praise of bourgeois conquest, while the texts that complicate everything sat in editions few readers opened. The scholarly debate to which this article now turns was made possible by the slow publication of the whole record, notebooks included, and its modern form begins when a literary critic of genius read the 1853 articles with fresh eyes and found in them not an aberration but a civilisation's way of seeing.

## The drafts read in Russia: Danielson, the populists, and the uses of a refusal

The Russian writings had readers before they had editors, and the first readings set the pattern of the controversy to come. Nikolai Danielson, Marx's correspondent and the Russian translator of Capital, belonged to the populist world that regarded the commune as Russia's distinctive inheritance and its possible salvation, and the correspondence between London and St Petersburg across the eighteen seventies was a genuine intellectual partnership: Danielson supplied the statistics, the famine reports, the fiscal evidence of a countryside being squeezed, and Marx, who learned Russian in his fifties for the purpose, supplied the questions. The Zasulich letter arrived in that world as a thunderclap precisely because its author refused the use both factions wished to make of him. The Westernisers wanted Capital read as a sentence of death on the commune and a warrant for Russia's passage through capitalism; the populists wanted it read as a blessing on the commune as socialism's Russian cradle. The letter gave neither faction its warrant. It confined the verdict of Capital to the West, remitted the commune's fate to the concrete balance of forces corroding and sustaining it, and left Russian socialists in possession of a problem rather than a prophecy. Georgi Plekhanov, the founder of Russian Marxism, who had begun as a populist and broken with the doctrine of the commune, treated the drafts' doctrine with the embarrassment of a man whose polemics required a more obliging founder, and the ambivalence of the heirs is once again the record's familiar shape: the late work was authoritative where convenient and archival where not. The pattern reached its completion under the codified doctrine that the next century imposed, with its mandatory five stage scheme, and the drafts, though published, were quarantined by commentary. It is one of the ironies the assessment should savour without leaning on it: the texts in which Marx refused to let his book be turned into a universal catechism were managed, by the church that canonised the book, with exactly the techniques he had refused. The refusal itself survived the management, in print, waiting for Shanin's generation to read it as the programme it was.

## The Russian road's scholars: the editorial recovery and the late Marx debate

The Russian writings of 1877 to 1882 generated, once published, a scholarly field of their own, and its contours complete the map of the modern debate. The recovery was editorial before it was interpretive: Ryazanov's publication of the Zasulich drafts in 1924, in the Marx-Engels Archive he directed before his own destruction in the purges, put the four drafts into circulation among specialists, where they sat for decades as an embarrassment to the codified doctrine of the state that had published them, since a Marx who refused a universal path was unusable by a party whose legitimacy rested on having followed the correct one. Western scholarship reached the texts slowly, through the collected editions, and the field they founded took shape in the nineteen eighties around the work collected by Teodor Shanin under the title Late Marx and the Russian Road, which argued that the last decade constitutes a distinct period of the thought, with its own centre of gravity: the commune studies, the ethnological notebooks, the multilinear drafts, amounting to a second great research programme beside the critique of political economy, devoted to the diversity of social forms and the openness of their futures. Japanese scholarship, in the work of Wada Haruki and his colleagues, had reached convergent conclusions from the notebooks independently, and the convergence of two scholarly traditions on the same documentary base gave the late Marx thesis a solidity that interpretive fashions rarely achieve. The thesis has its disciplined critics, who warn against manufacturing a final conversion from drafts their author never chose to publish, and who note that the sent letter to Zasulich is deliberately minimal beside the drafts' explorations. The warning is incorporated in this article's weighing scheme, which credits the drafts as evidence of belief rather than of doctrine. But the critics and the proponents now argue over the same enlarged record, on ground the notebooks have fixed, and that is the permanent change the recovery made: after the Russian road debate, the unilinear Marx of the textbooks is a position that must be argued against documents, not a default that can be assumed. The stage scheme of 1853, which the movement's codifiers had projected backward over the whole career, stands revealed as what the chronology always said it was: the framework of the first decade, dismantled by its author in the last, and preserved between those dates chiefly by the institutions that found the dismantling inconvenient.

## Algiers: the last journey and the view from the other side

In the final spring of his life, Marx did something he had never done in the decades of writing about the colonised world: he lived, for nearly three months, in a colony. Ordered to a warm climate by his doctors after the death of his wife, he travelled in February 1882 to Algiers, then fifty years into the French conquest of Algeria, and the letters he wrote from there to Engels and to his daughters are the last first-hand testimony of the development traced in these pages. What struck him, and what he reported with the eye of the young Manchester investigator rather than the system builder of 1853, was the double society the conquest had built: the European settlers, whom he described with open disgust as arrogant, idle on expropriated land, and treating the native population with a brutality thinly veiled by legal forms, and the Arabs themselves, whose communal dignity, hospitality, and sense of equality among themselves he recorded with respect and with interest in the property forms that sustained them. He followed the colonial courts, retailed with contempt the conduct of a French military judge whose insolence toward Arab defendants he witnessed in report, and remarked on the hatred the settlers' regime was storing up for itself. The theoretical point is implicit and should be drawn explicitly, since he left it in letters rather than doctrine: the man who in 1853 had looked at Asia through the conquerors' files was in 1882 looking at a conquest from inside the conquered society's streets, and he saw what the files had hidden from him, a people with a civilisation of their own being dispossessed by men his younger self might have cast, sight unseen, as the agents of a regenerating mission. No recantation was written on his return, and within a year he was dead. But the Algiers letters complete the trajectory with an almost novelistic symmetry: the record that began with an exile reading the empire's archive in London ends with the exile, dying, watching the empire at work in Algiers and writing home in disgust. Assessors who freeze the record at 1853 are not merely selecting against the development. They are selecting against the direction of the man's whole later life.

## The last notebooks: evidence gathered at the rate of a dying man

The multilinear turn of the last years was not a mood; it was a research programme, and its material remains can be inspected. In the final decade of his life Marx filled notebook after notebook with excerpts and commentaries on the societies his early work had graded from a distance: on communal landholding in India and Algeria, drawn from the studies of the Russian jurist Maxim Kovalevsky, whose work on communal possession he read with a pencil that grew steadily more sceptical of general theories, his own included; on Lewis Henry Morgan's Ancient Society, the American ethnologist's reconstruction of kinship and communal property among the Iroquois and in the ancient world, which reached him in 1877 and which he excerpted at a length that shows the excitement of a man finding documentary ground for a possibility his system had once foreclosed, that communal forms were not a single stagnant essence but a family of arrangements with histories of their own; on Sir Henry Maine's lectures on the village communities of East and West, with marginalia that quarrel, sharply and with relish, wherever the jurist's Indian evidence is bent to flatter the British administration that employed him; on travellers, administrators' reports, and parliamentary papers concerning India, read now against the grain their compiler intended. The ethnological notebooks, published in full only in 1972, run to hundreds of pages of this labour, undertaken by a man in failing health who was publishing almost nothing. The direction of the excerpts is unmistakable to any reader who works through them in sequence. The curiosity that the young Marx directed at the categories of European political economy is directed, in the old Marx, at the diversity of communal organisation itself, and the judgements harden against every writer, friendly or hostile, who reaches for a universal solvent before the evidence is in. The notebooks are the workshop in which the 1853 framework was taken apart. They were private, like the letter of 1862, and an assessment that counts the one against the man must in fairness count the other for him, though with the weights properly distinguished: the letter expressed a prejudice in a moment; the notebooks express a decade of methodical self-correction, and in the grading of a life's work, labour outweighs lapse.

The same late period produced, on India itself, a judgement that belongs in the record as the practical obituary of the double mission. Writing to his Russian correspondent Nikolai Danielson in February 1881, Marx computed what the connection with England now cost India: the rents, the dividends on railways built for strategic rather than Indian purposes, the pensions, the charges of wars fought for imperial ends, the value of commodities sent to England without equivalent, an annual unrequited transfer which, he wrote, amounted to more than the entire income of India's sixty million agricultural and industrial labourers, and which he named, in the private idiom of a letter, a bleeding process. Whatever is made of his arithmetic, and the contested figures protocol of this article has already declined to rest any argument on colonial numbers, the shape of the judgement is what matters. India in 1881 is not a stagnant formation awaiting its solvent. It is a society being drained by a relation, whose poverty is produced and renewed by the connection itself, and whose development is blocked not by its communal past but by its colonial present. Invert the terms of 1853 and the inversion is complete: stagnation was the diagnosis then, and empire the cure; extraction is the diagnosis now, and empire the disease. He never published a recantation of the articles, and the absence of one is a fact the verdict has already weighed. But the man who wrote the Danielson letter, the Zasulich drafts, and the Algiers correspondence in the same final years had travelled as far from the Tribune articles as a thinker can travel from his own published work while continuing to sign his name to the journey, in the only currencies the dying use: letters, drafts, and notebooks, left for others to publish.

### What do the notebooks of the last decade show him doing?

Working through the evidence on communal societies at first hand, in Kovalevsky on India and Algeria, in Morgan on the Iroquois and the ancient world, in Maine argued with line by line, and moving steadily from the grading of formations by a universal scheme toward the study of each formation in its own structure.

## Inside the notebooks: Kovalevsky argued with, Maine annotated, Morgan absorbed

The notebook evidence deserves a closer description, because the claim that the late Marx changed his mind about communal societies is only as strong as the marginalia that document it, and the marginalia are specific. With Kovalevsky's study of communal landholding, excerpted in 1879, Marx worked through the evidence on India and Algeria holding by holding, and his comments quarrel with the author's governing suggestion that communal property in India survived chiefly as a shell shaped by feudal and state pressure from above. Against that reading, Marx's notes insist on the commune's internal vitality and on the distinction between forms of common property that a conqueror's fiscal convenience preserved and forms that the community's own organisation sustained; the direction of the quarrel is unmistakable, since the younger Marx of the articles had treated the commune precisely as the state's creature and the despotism's base. With Maine, the most eminent British jurist of India, the pencil is hostile in a different register: where Maine's lectures bend the Indian evidence toward the moral that British administration was dissolving a stagnant tenurial world into healthy individual property, the notes interrupt with sarcasm at the jurist's expense and with counterinstances drawn from the same reports, the fury of a reader watching the archive being cooked by its custodian, the very operation the 1853 articles had failed to detect. With Morgan the register changes again, to absorption: the Iroquois gens, communal property in the ancient world, the sequence of kinship forms, excerpted and reorganised with a care that shows the material being fitted for use, the use it eventually found in Engels's Origin of the Family, built in part from these notebooks after their author's death. Three authors, three tempers: correction of an ally, contempt for an administrator, apprenticeship to a scientist. What unifies the pages is a single methodological posture, the refusal of the prefabricated sequence. Each formation is to be got right in its own articulations before any road is drawn through it, and the roads, where the notes sketch them at all, branch. The scholar who reconstructed this workshop, Anderson, drew the warranted conclusion: the multilinear Marx is not an invention of charitable editors but the documented occupant of the notebooks, and the debate's older question, whether the late development was real, is closed. The live questions are what the development was worth, given that it never reached print as doctrine, and what it does to the framework it revised, and those are the questions the sections on the objection and the verdict exist to answer.

## The category after its author: suppression, weaponisation, retirement

The Asiatic mode of production had a career after 1883 that belongs to this article, because the career is the category's final examination. Within the Marxist tradition the concept was first an embarrassment and then a danger. It fitted awkwardly into the sequence of formations the popularisers codified; worse, in Russian hands it invited an obvious and explosive comparison, since a category defined by a despotic state standing over self-sufficient villages, drawing revenue without developing production, could be pointed at the Russian state itself, old or new, with very little adjustment. The discussions of the concept among Soviet scholars in the late nineteen twenties were therefore not academic. In 1931 a conference at Leningrad effectively closed the question: the Asiatic mode was condemned as a Trotskyist and anti-Leninist construction, its defenders were silenced or worse, and the official scheme that replaced it, five formations in universal sequence, from primitive communism through slavery, feudalism, and capitalism to socialism, bore a family resemblance to the staircase of 1853 with the Asiatic landing removed. The irony is complete and should be stated flatly: the multilinear drafts in which Marx had dismantled the staircase lay unpublished or unregarded in the archives, while a state ruling in his name canonised the staircase and suppressed, as heresy, the category that had once served to grade the world he knew least. Categories have no loyalty. They serve the institutions that license them, and the Asiatic mode's fate in the Soviet Union is the clearest demonstration in this whole record that a concept's political meaning is fixed by its use, not by its author's intention.

The concept's second career was as a weapon against that same state. Wittfogel's Oriental Despotism of 1957, written by a former communist turned prosecutor of communism, rebuilt the category into a theory of hydraulic society: states founded on the management of irrigation, total in their power, changeless in their structure, of which the Soviet Union and China were presented as the modern instances. The scholarship of the book was immense and its portrait of Asia was, in the judgement of the historians who followed, recognisably the portrait of the colonial archive once more: a civilisation defined by despotism and stagnation, awaiting explanation by its difference from a dynamic West. The concept had now served, in the space of a century, a revolutionary journalist grading an empire's conquest, a revolutionary state suppressing an inconvenience, and a cold warrior arraigning that state, each time carrying Asia's static portrait at its core. The historians of Asia, meanwhile, had simply left the room. Working from Indian, Chinese, and Ottoman records, they built accounts of commercialisation, state formation, and social conflict in which the category had no work to do, and the retirement Perry Anderson pronounced within Marxist historiography, that the concept should be withdrawn from circulation, ratified what the archive based scholarship had already practised. A concept fails in two ways: by being refuted, and by ceasing to be needed by the people who study its object. The Asiatic mode failed in both ways, and its double failure is the empirical verdict on the premises of 1853, delivered by the only tribunal with jurisdiction, the subsequent study of the societies concerned.

### What happened to the Asiatic mode of production in the century after Marx?

It was codified awkwardly, then condemned and suppressed in the Soviet discussions closed in 1931, rebuilt by Wittfogel in 1957 as a weapon against the communist states, and finally retired by historians, Marxist and otherwise, whose archive based work on Asian societies found no use for it.

## Weighing the evidence: genre, audience, and the problem of the drawer

One methodological question remains before the sequence can be tabulated, and honest readers of the same shelf divide over it: how should the different kinds of text in this record be weighed against one another? The record contains journalism written for money against deadlines, private letters written in heat to a single trusted reader, confidential communications written to be acted on by an organisation, drafts written for no reader at all and abandoned in a drawer, and notebooks written as the quarry of future work. A court of biography and a court of theory weigh these genres differently, and much of the controversy dissolves, on inspection, into a quarrel about the weights. The prosecution's weighting privileges the published and the spontaneous: the articles because they were public doctrine, the letter of 1862 because the spontaneous private voice is treated as the truest one. The defence's weighting privileges the considered and the final: the drafts and the last letters, on the principle that a thinker is his most considered self and that the last word corrects the first. Neither weighting is innocent. The prosecution's rule would convict every major nineteenth century figure on the correspondence of his worst afternoon, and it mysteriously loses interest in spontaneity when the spontaneous document is a letter denouncing a colonial judge. The defence's rule would acquit almost anyone of almost anything, since somewhere in most archives a finer sentiment can be found, and it conveniently forgets that the drawer was not a publisher: the drafts corrected nothing in the public record while their author lived, and the tradition that inherited the record inherited the public part first. The weighing used in this article, and defended here so that its verdicts can be audited, distinguishes the questions before it weights the answers. For the question of what political effect the record had, the published texts weigh most, in their order of publication, because effect runs through print. For the question of what the man believed, the whole archive weighs, with considered work outweighing heat and with the trajectory counting as evidence in itself. For what the theory contains, the texts weigh by their argumentative rigour rather than their date, which is why the Irish letters, occasional in genre and magisterial in argument, are permitted in this article to outweigh treatises. Every grading in the verdict section can be traced to this scheme, and a reader who adopts a different one will reach a different verdict from the same evidence, which is not a failure of the method but its point: the controversy becomes tractable the moment its participants state their weights.

## How the record reached its readers: editions, anthologies, and the selected Marx

A controversy about texts is also a controversy about editions, and the shape of this one was set by the order in which the record became available. The articles of 1853 were journalism, scattered in an American newspaper, and they entered the canon slowly: reprinted in collections of the Tribune correspondence, they were available to any diligent reader from early in the twentieth century, while the letters that qualify them, the Irish correspondence, the Zasulich drafts, the letter on the Russian question of 1877, and above all the notebooks, emerged piecemeal across the following hundred years, through the labours of editors in Moscow, Amsterdam, and Berlin, interrupted by wars, purges, and the custodians' own doctrinal preferences. The Zasulich drafts surfaced in 1924. The Grundrisse, with its comparative treatment of pre-capitalist formations, was published in Moscow in 1939 and 1941 and reached a wide readership only after the war. The ethnological notebooks waited until 1972. Anthologies did the intervening work of selection, and selection is interpretation by other means: the Moscow collection of the colonial writings assembled one Marx, Shlomo Avineri's widely used anthology of 1968, Marx on Colonialism and Modernization, assembled another, framing its texts with an introduction that presented the double mission with scholarly sympathy as a coherent developmental doctrine, and the textbooks assembled a third, in which the 1853 articles stood alone in a chapter on Marx and the non-European world, unaccompanied by 1870 or 1881. Each generation's controversy about Marx on colonialism was therefore partly a controversy about which edition's Marx was on the table. Said wrote in 1978, before the notebooks were widely digested, and his Marx is the Marx of the published canon of that date; Anderson wrote in 2010 with the whole shelf open, and his Marx is correspondingly larger. This is not an excuse for either. It is a reminder that the assessment of a writer whose papers took a century to publish is a moving target by construction, and that confidence in any decade's summary of this record, including the present article's, should be discounted by the knowledge that the archive has surprised every previous confidence it has been offered.

## The archive still growing: the complete edition and the moving target

A final evidentiary caution belongs in the record, because it conditions every confidence expressed in these pages, this article's included. The publication of Marx's papers is not finished. The historical-critical complete edition, the Marx-Engels Gesamtausgabe, has been appearing since the nineteen seventies under international scholarly direction, printing the manuscripts, the letters in their full extent, and above all the excerpt notebooks, volume by volume, in a form no previous edition attempted. Whole categories of evidence on which the modern debate about the colonial writings rests, the notebooks on communal property, on ethnology, on the non-European world, entered general circulation through this edition's labours, and volumes bearing on the record examined here are still in preparation. The pattern of the past century licenses a prediction about the next: each substantial enlargement of the archive has revised the received picture, sometimes in the founder's favour and sometimes against it, and the enlargements are not exhausted. An assessment written in full view of that pattern will present its verdicts as this one has tried to do, graded, dated, and tied to a stated body of evidence, so that the readers of some future edition can see exactly which findings a new volume confirms and which it retires. The excerptors, by contrast, will go on quoting 1853, since an excerpt requires no archive at all. Between those two futures for the record, the laborious one and the theatrical one, the evidence itself has already chosen sides often enough to justify the labour.

The edition's bearing on this article's central exhibit deserves one precise remark. The Irish correspondence survives in multiple drafts and council papers whose variants the new edition prints synoptically, and the variants matter to the weighing rules defended above: they show the reversal being sharpened in revision, its formulations about the aristocracy's stronghold and the workers' antagonism strengthened between draft and dispatch, which is the behaviour of a considered doctrine rather than of a rhetorical improvisation. Evidence of that grain cannot settle the old quarrels by itself. It can, and increasingly does, deny the quarrels their favourite premise, that the record is a small fixed shelf about which everything knowable is known. The shelf grows. Judgements that wish to outlast the next volume will be written, as this one has been, with their evidence showing.

## The position over time: the sequence is the evidence

The table below is this article's findable artifact, and its purpose is disciplinary. Every error in the literature on Marx and colonialism reduces, on inspection, to a disordering of time: a text detached from its year, a position quoted without its successor, a letter treated as a treatise or a treatise treated as a last word. The position over time prevents the disorder by making the sequence itself the object on the page. Read down the rows, and what is being assessed is not a set of opinions but a trajectory, with its premises, its turning points, and its unfinished end. The table also enforces the protocol the historiography section established: no figures for colonial mortality, famine deaths, or economic extraction appear anywhere in this article, because such figures are contested at their foundations and the argument requires none of them; where scale matters, the character of the violence is described qualitatively and the reader is routed to the historiography for the numbers and their disputes.

A table is an argument about what counts as a unit, and this one's units are chosen to defeat the two standard misreadings. Against the prosecutorial misreading, which takes the 1853 row as the man, the table insists that a row is a position at a date, and that the man's positions include rows he wrote later that contradict the row being prosecuted; a defendant is entitled to be judged on the whole of the evidence, including his own revisions of himself. Against the devotional misreading, which takes the final rows as absolution, the table insists that the ugly rows are rows too: the 1862 letter sits in the sequence in its proper year, in the middle of the decade of the antislavery journalism, and no arrangement of the surrounding rows removes it or converts it into something else. What the sequence shows, taken whole, is neither a racist who happened to write about economics nor a prophet maligned by juvenilia. It shows a thinker whose public analysis of colonial questions developed, substantially and against his own earlier framework, from a unilinear scheme that treated colonised peoples as history's material to a relational analysis that treated them as its potential makers, and whose private language never underwent the corresponding development. The development was real, unfinished, and confined to the public work. The verdict at the end of this article grades those clauses separately, because they are separate.

### How should a reader use the position over time table?

Row by row and in order, treating each entry as a position held at a date rather than as the man's settled doctrine, and watching two things the sequence makes visible: the premises change as better evidence and closer contact reach him, and the private language of the middle row does not change at all.

## The scholarly debate: Said, Ahmad, and the evidence of the notebooks

The modern argument about this record was opened by a book that was not principally about Marx. Edward Said's Orientalism, published in 1978, reconstructed the European discourse of the Orient: the interlocking scholarship, literature, and administration through which the West produced the East as its static, sensual, despotic other, available for rule. In a celebrated passage of that book, Said turned to the 1853 articles and found them continuous with the archive he was describing. Marx's India, on Said's reading, was assembled from the same Orientalist stock as the administrators' India: the Asiatic mode as a concept restored the very stasis the discourse required, the village communities figured as the East's immemorial essence, and even Marx's sympathy for Indian suffering was, Said argued, finally overborne by a redemptive economic determinism in which Asia had to be remade by Europe to enter history at all. The conclusion Said drew was delicate and has often been coarsened by his followers: not that Marx was a colonialist, but that the discourse of Orientalism was stronger than any individual dissenter, that it captured the vocabulary and the categories even of the thinker most committed to overturning the order the discourse served, and that Marx's articles are therefore among the most instructive exhibits in the whole archive, precisely because their author should have been immune. The strongest critical reading of the colonial record takes this form, and the next section will state it at the full strength Said gave it.

The reply came in 1992 from Aijaz Ahmad, in the chapter of his book In Theory devoted to the question and titled as a clarification of Marx on India. Ahmad, a Marxist literary scholar writing from within the tradition he shared with Said's target, charged the reading with the sins it attributed to its object. Said, he argued, quoted selectively, leaning on the articles of 1853 while passing over the journalism of 1857 and after, in which the same correspondent treated the Indian revolt as a national rising and the colonial power as a torturer; he ignored the development of the position entirely, so that a thinker whose views changed across thirty years was frozen at his most vulnerable text; and he subsumed Marx under a discourse, Orientalism, conceived as a single transhistorical structure running unchanged from Aeschylus to the modern area studies institute, a conception so total that no evidence from within the period could count against it and no writer could ever be found outside it. For Ahmad, the flattening was the method's confession: a theory of discourse that cannot register the difference between a colonial administrator's minute and a revolutionary's journalism, between 1853 and 1870, between the double mission and the Ireland letters, has purchased its explanatory sweep at the price of the distinctions history exists to make. The exchange between Said and Ahmad remains the load bearing wall of the modern debate, and it is not a polite one: each accuses the other of the characteristic scholarly vice, the one of building a category too large to be wrong, the other of reading a career as though it were a file of clippings.

### How did Said place the India articles within a European archive?

By reading them as exhibits of Orientalism, the discourse through which Europe produced the East as static and despotic: the Asiatic mode of production, he argued, restored the archive's founding assumption, and Marx's evident sympathy for Indian suffering was finally overborne by a scheme in which Asia could enter history only through European transformation.

The third act of the debate belongs to the archive itself. Kevin Anderson's Marx at the Margins, published in 2010 and built on the excerpt notebooks Marx filled in his last years, on the ethnological notebooks published only in 1972, and on the journalism and correspondence in their full extent, argued that the development the older literature had glimpsed in the Irish and Russian texts was broader and deeper than either camp had documented. Working through the notebooks on India, on Ireland, on Algeria, on the Russian commune, on the Iroquois and other communal societies, Anderson reconstructed a late Marx whose thinking on non-Western societies had become genuinely multilinear: the unilinear sequence of stages, never as rigid in the early work as its summary in textbooks suggests, gives way in the later writings to a differentiated analysis in which communal forms, colonial violence, and national movements each receive their specific weight, and in which solidarity with colonised peoples is a recurring practical commitment rather than a deduction. Anderson's study has become the standard reference for the late record, and its effect on the debate has been to shift the ground from interpretation to evidence. Where Said read a discourse and Ahmad defended a corpus, Anderson edited the chronology: after the notebooks, any account of Marx on the non-Western world that stops at 1853 is not an interpretation but an omission, and the argument must be conducted over the whole shelf. That conclusion, notably, does not acquit the early articles, whose premises Anderson's evidence confirms as the weakest part of the record. It convicts the summary.

### What did Ahmad's reply charge Said with doing?

With building his case on the journalism of 1853 and passing over the reporting of 1857 and after, with freezing a thinker whose position developed across thirty years at the weakest point of his record, and with subsuming him under a conception of Orientalism so total and transhistorical that no evidence could count against it.

The debate's present state can be stated with reasonable confidence, now that the evidence base has stabilised. On the facts of the early articles, Said's side has the better of it: the premises were the archive's premises, and the historiography of India has buried them. On the shape of the career, Ahmad and Anderson have the better of it: the development is documented, substantial, and fatal to any one-text summary. On the question underneath both, whether the framework of historical materialism itself, rather than its founder's early application of it, carries the defect Said diagnosed, the debate remains open and is taken up in the next section in its strongest form. That is where the controversy stops being literary and becomes theoretical, and it is where this article's own verdict will have to be earned rather than announced. The wider confrontation between the Marxist tradition and postcolonial theory, of which this exchange is the sharpest single episode, is mapped in [marxism vs postcolonial theory](/2017/05/15/marxism-vs-postcolonial-theory/).

## After the notebooks: where the scholarly question now stands

The debate's most recent chapter confirms the pattern this article has described, in which the extension of the archive, rather than the sharpening of polemic, is what moves the question. The ethnological and excerpt notebooks, once absorbed into the standard picture, promptly generated a second literature built upon them: the ecological reading of the late Marx, in which the notebooks on communal agriculture, soil, and the metabolic relation between society and nature, studied by John Bellamy Foster at the turn of the century and extended in Kohei Saito's work on the notebooks of the final years, present a thinker whose last research programme was turning against the productivism his early framework shared with its century, toward the conditions of a sustainable metabolism and the communal forms that might sustain it. The ecological literature is not this article's subject, and its claims are contested in their turn, sometimes for the familiar reason that drafts are being promoted into doctrine. Its existence is nonetheless evidence in the present case, of a kind neither Said nor Ahmad could have cited: the late record continues to prove fertile in directions its author's century could not have foreseen, which is the behaviour of a research programme still alive rather than of a closed system being defended. The scholarly question, as it now stands, has accordingly migrated. Whether the late turn happened at all, the founding question of the older debate, is settled by the documents. The live questions concern its weight and its limits: how far the multilinear turn reconstitutes the theory of history rather than merely supplementing it, whether the productivism of the framework survives its founder's corrections, and how the tradition that canonised the staircase should now reread the canon it assembled. Those questions will be argued for another generation, properly, over the whole shelf. What they will not survive is a return to the one-text summary, since the shelf itself is now the common property of all parties, and any summary that ignores most of it announces itself as polemic before its first argument is heard.

## The epigraph problem: how the debate was framed before it began

The scholarly debate carries a small emblem on its title page that repays attention, because it compresses the whole argument into a bibliographical fact. Said placed at the head of Orientalism, as the book's epigraph, a sentence of Marx's: they cannot represent themselves; they must be represented. The sentence comes from The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, where it describes the smallholding peasants of France, a class unable, in Marx's analysis, to articulate its interests in its own name and therefore delivered to representatives who rule it as its master. Said's appropriation is exact and audacious. Lifted from the French peasantry to the Orient, the sentence becomes the motto of his entire argument: the East, in the discourse he is anatomising, is precisely that which cannot represent itself and must therefore be represented by the West's scholars, novelists, and administrators, whose representations then pass for the thing itself. The emblem cuts in two directions, and both cuts belong to the record. Against Marx, it indicts by association: the founder of the critique of political economy supplies the epigraph for the anatomy of Orientalism because his own sentences, transposed, state its premise, and Said's chapter on the India articles argues the transposition is no accident, since Marx's Asia too is spoken for by a European science that assigns it its role. In Marx's partial defence, the emblem also testifies to the sentence's analytical power, since Said found no better instrument, in the whole literature he surveyed, for naming the operation of representation as domination; the critic of the discourse reached, for his sharpest tool, into the workshop of the discourse's most famous alleged prisoner. Ahmad's rejoinder fastens on exactly this doubleness: a reading that must borrow its motto from its defendant has already conceded the defendant's doubleness, and a method that treats the borrowing as proof of captivity has arranged its evidence so that even exculpation incriminates. The epigraph problem is therefore the debate in miniature. The texts are double, the readings are interested, and the evidence, patiently ordered by date and genre, remains the only court that has ever moved the argument forward, which is why the notebook scholarship, dry as it sounds, altered the debate more than either polemic did.

## The record read in the colonised world: India answers back

The last body of evidence about these texts is the use made of them by the people they described, and it belongs in the assessment because the brief for this article forbids the comfortable assumption that the colonised world received Marx only as the author of the double mission. Indian readers encountered the Tribune articles early and argued with them in the open. The nationalist economists who built the drain theory at the turn of the twentieth century worked in a milieu where Marx's reporting on the destruction of the handloom industry and the burden of the home charges circulated as ammunition against the Raj, cited by men who had no use for his philosophy of history and every use for his bookkeeping. When the centenary of the 1857 rebellion arrived in 1957, the Indian communist historian P. C. Joshi edited a volume of essays reassessing the rising, in which Marx's journalism of 1857, his insistence that the event was a national revolt rather than a mutiny, figured as an honoured precursor of the Indian historiography that treated the rebellion as a war of independence. The pattern repeated across the colonised world with local variations: movements took from the corpus what their situation verified, the anatomy of extraction, the strategy of the divided working class, the Irish doctrine that the colony's freedom was the metropolis's precondition, and left the stage scheme standing in the library, where Indian historians of Kosambi's and Habib's generation would eventually take it apart with better evidence than its author ever had. At the Second Congress of the Communist International in 1920, the Indian revolutionary M. N. Roy argued with Lenin himself over the colonial theses, insisting, against the strategy of alliance with national movements, on the independent organisation of the colonised masses, a debate conducted entirely within the framework the Irish letters had founded. None of this reception history acquits the articles of 1853; the same Indian tradition that used the journalism corrected its premises. What it establishes is the fact the structural objection has the most difficulty digesting: the corpus functioned, in the hands of the colonised themselves, predominantly as an instrument against empire, wielded by readers who were neither dupes nor innocents about its defects, and who graded its parts, in practice, exactly as this article has graded them, keeping the method and the Irish doctrine, discarding the staircase and the scenery.

## The comparison that disciplines the grading: Mill and the liberal empire

A comparison with the century's liberal conscience on empire disciplines every grade this article has awarded, because it separates the failures Marx shared from the uses that were his alone. John Stuart Mill spent his working life in the examiner's office of the East India Company, rising to its chief, drafting despatches for the government of India, and, when the Company's rule was abolished in 1858, composing the memorandum in its defence that remains the ablest brief ever written for government by a corporation. In On Liberty, published the following year, Mill laid down the doctrine of tutelage in its canonical liberal form: despotism is a legitimate mode of government in dealing with barbarians, provided the end be their improvement. The sentence is not an embarrassment torn from a private letter; it is the considered doctrine of liberalism's greatest philosopher, published as philosophy, in the treatise that defines the tradition's account of freedom. Set the two records side by side in the eighteen fifties. Both men held the picture of a backward East and a dynamic Europe; both believed in stages of civilisation; neither questioned that India's future lay through a transformation on lines Europe had pioneered. The difference lies in everything else. Mill was empire's salaried conscience, administering its government for thirty-five years and defending its corporation in print at the moment of its disgrace, while holding that its subjects were unfit for the liberty he theorised for their rulers. Marx was empire's enemy on a newspaper's payroll, who called its Indian rule a crime in the same articles that predicted its solvent effect, took no office under it, defended no charter of it, and spent his organisational life, within the decade, on the side of its Irish and American victims. The comparison does not acquit the double mission, whose premises were the shared premises of both men. It fixes responsibility where responsibility belongs: the static picture of the East was the common property of European thought, available in its most polished form from the liberal tradition that ran the empire, and any grading of Marx that presents the picture as his distinguishing sin, rather than as the common sense he inherited and only partly escaped, has confused the man with his century in the precise manner his own method taught readers to avoid. What distinguishes him in the comparison is not innocence. It is the direction of travel, from the picture's use as a solvent doctrine toward its dismantling, against the gradient of his own early system, while the picture's polished owners travelled, comfortably, in office.

## The strongest objection: the defect is structural

The strongest objection to the whole record must now be stated at full strength, attributed to its author and his work, as the rules of this article require. In Said's Orientalism of 1978, the objection is not that Marx held prejudices, which would be a biographical triviality, nor that the articles of 1853 contain offensive judgements, which everyone concedes. The objection is that the framework itself, historical materialism as Marx then held it, encodes the colonial assumption in its load bearing members. Its stage theory arranges the world's peoples on a single staircase of development, with Europe's present as every other society's future; its category of the Asiatic mode assigns the greater part of humanity to a formation defined by the absence of the very dynamism the theory elsewhere treats as universal; its criterion of progress is the development of productive forces on the European model, so that a society's destruction can be entered in the ledger as its advancement; and its philosophy of history thereby performs, with scientific credentials, the exact operation the colonial archive performed with administrative ones, dividing mankind into the peoples who make history and the peoples to whom history is done. On this reading, the defects of 1853 are not lapses from the theory but applications of it, and the proof is that the theory's machinery, wound up and pointed at India, produced the colonial conclusion without friction and indeed with eloquence. The later development, the objection continues, does not answer the charge but illustrates it: a framework that must be struggled against by its own author for thirty years, in letters and drafts he never systematised or published, before it will yield a decent politics of the colonial question, is a framework whose default settings are the problem, and the defaults are what the world received, since it was the articles and the stage scheme, not the drafts in the drawer, that formed the tradition's public inheritance for a century. That is the objection in its strongest form. It deserves to be felt at its full weight before any answer is attempted, because the answer that follows concedes a real part of it.

The answer, in its strongest form, is the counter-argument of Ahmad's In Theory of 1992, completed by the evidence of Anderson's notebook scholarship. It runs in three parts. First, the objection proves too much: a discourse defined so capaciously that it captures, indifferently, the East India Company's minute writers, the liberal administrators, and the revolutionary correspondent who called their rule a crime and their motives the meanest, has ceased to distinguish the thing that most needs distinguishing, namely who was building the case for empire and who was building, however imperfectly, the case against its legitimacy; a net that catches every fish explains nothing about the sea. Second, the chronology refutes the structural claim in its strong form: if the framework's defaults determined the colonial politics, the framework's own author could not have derived from it, as he demonstrably did, an analysis in which a colonised people's national movement is the lever of the metropolis's emancipation and in which the stage sequence is expressly repudiated as a universal path; the drafts are not external to the framework but are the framework worked on new evidence, which is what a living method is supposed to do, and the Marx of the Zasulich drafts is not fighting his method but using its deepest rule, the concrete study of a formation in its specificity, against its own earlier generalisations. Third, the public inheritance argument cuts both ways: the tradition that received the articles also received, in the same canon and often in the same volumes, the Irish resolutions, the anti-slavery journalism, and the defence of the Paris Commune's colonial soldiers' contemporaries in a dozen struggles, and the Marxist movements of the colonised world, from India to Vietnam to South Africa, were built by readers who found in the corpus the instruments of their liberation and said so, a reception history that a purely structural indictment cannot process without treating those readers as dupes of the discourse that allegedly imprisoned their teacher.

Then the concession, without which the answer would be advocacy rather than assessment. The stage theory as stated in 1853 does carry the assumption the objection identifies. A scheme that grades societies by their distance from a European present, and that defines the greater part of the world by a missing dynamism, is not an innocent instrument misused by its author's sources; it is the assumption itself, formulated. The honest defence is therefore not that the framework was innocent but that it was corrigible, and was corrected, from resources the framework itself supplied: the materialist rule that a formation's own structure, studied concretely, outranks any imported sequence. Whether a theory that requires its founder's thirty year struggle to reach that correction should be described as containing its own antidote or as having been overcome by its author is, in the end, a question about how theories are individuated, and reasonable readers of the same record will divide on it. This article divides as follows. The 1853 application stands condemned by the evidence, and the stage scheme in its unilinear form stands condemned with it. The method that produced the correction, the insistence on studying each social formation in its concrete relations and on finding the mechanisms of division and rule inside the relation between peoples rather than in their essences, is the same method at a better level of information, and it is the method the letters still teach. Frameworks are not acquitted or convicted as wholes by serious readers. They are disassembled, and their parts are graded, which is the operation the final section now performs on the whole record.

### Which parts of the record does the verdict separate?

Four: the published position of 1853, whose premises have failed and whose progressive verdict falls with them; the private language, which is racist and unexcused; the development after 1857, which is substantial; and the Ireland letters, which found an analysis of national antagonism that the subsequent tradition has never had to withdraw.

## The treatise that was never written: the plan of the six books and its missing volumes

The occasional character of the colonial record, its dispersal across journalism, letters, drafts, and notebooks, is not an accident of temperament but a consequence of an architecture, and the architecture deserves a paragraph in any assessment that proposes to weigh the record fairly. In 1858, in the first excitement of the economic work, Marx sketched for his correspondents the plan of the whole: six books, on capital, on landed property, on wage labour, on the state, on foreign trade, and on the world market and crises. The colonial questions of this article belong, by that plan, to the fifth and sixth books, where the state in its external relations and the world market in its totality were to receive the systematic treatment that the surviving work gives to capital alone. Those books were never written. The decades went to the first book's endless revision, to the International's politics, to the journalism that paid for both, and to the illnesses that rationed the working day. The consequence for the assessment is structural and cuts against every party to the controversy. There exists no Marxist doctrine of colonialism, in the sense of a considered treatise in which the articles, the letters, and the drafts were weighed against one another by their author and resolved into positions; there exists a trail of interventions across thirty years, made under the pressure of events, whose coherence must be constructed by the reader because it was never constructed by the writer. Construction is therefore unavoidable, and it is also contestable, which is why this article has laboured to make its construction auditable: its periodisation is stated, its weighing rules are stated, its grades are assigned part by part. The rival constructions, the prosecution's and the devotion's, are equally constructions, distinguished not by their freedom from method but by their refusal to declare one. A reader who understands that the treatise does not exist will stop asking which text contains the doctrine and start asking the only question the evidence supports, which is what the trail shows. Trails show direction, terrain, and the places where the traveller stopped and turned. This one shows all three with unusual clarity, and the assessment has followed it accordingly.

## What remains open: the questions the record cannot answer

An honest assessment ends by registering what its evidence cannot settle, and this record leaves four questions open that no industry in the archives will close. The first is the counterfactual of publication. Had Marx lived to systematise the Irish and Russian work into the treatise on politics he never wrote, the tradition's public inheritance would have been different in ways that can be imagined but not computed; the drafts' existence licenses the question and forbids its answer, and polemic that fills the silence with certainty, in either direction, is fiction wearing footnotes. The second is the question of the prejudice's depth. The correspondence is a finite sample of a private voice across forty years, densest where its author was angriest, and no sampling of it can establish whether the epithets of 1862 were the voice of his convictions or the reach of a cruel man for the cruelest instrument at hand in a quarrel; the distinction matters to biography and cannot be resolved from the documents, which is why this article graded the letter as conduct, where the evidence is complete, rather than as a window into an interior, where it is not. The third open question is the framework's, carried over from the objection: whether a method is individuated by its founding formulations or by its demonstrated capacity for self-correction, a question in the philosophy of theories that the Marx case illustrates with unusual clarity and cannot by itself decide. The fourth is the question the whole record poses to its readers rather than about its author: what are the equivalents, in the information order of the present, of the colonial archive of 1853, the interested files whose consistency masquerades as confirmation, and which of our own confident descriptions of distant societies are being assembled, in good faith and with powerful methods, from sources compiled by the powers that administer them? That question is not rhetorical decoration. It is the use of the case, and a reader who has followed the trajectory from the reading room to Algiers will recognise it as the question its subject, at his best, taught his readers to ask of every file, including, in the end, his own.

## Why the selection persists: the polemical economy of a dead man's record

It is worth asking, before the verdict, why the excerpting this article has resisted remains the controversy's dominant method a century and a half after the record closed, because the answer explains the record's strange immortality better than any property of the texts themselves. The colonial writings are not, in bulk, large: two famous articles, a shelf of journalism, some letters, some drafts. Their polemical yield, however, is extraordinary, because they stand at the one point where several of the modern world's standing arguments intersect and can be fought by proxy. For critics of the Marxist tradition, the articles and the letter are a founding stain, evidence that the doctrine's universalism was counterfeit at the source, serviceable in every subsequent dispute about movements and states that claimed the inheritance. For the tradition's institutions, the same texts are an embarrassment to be managed, by silence where possible and by the development narrative where necessary, since conceding them at full strength seems to arm the enemy. For scholars of empire, the case is a laboratory specimen of how knowledge and power interlock, small enough to master and famous enough to matter. Each constituency therefore has an interest in a simple record, and simplicity is manufactured by selection. The complexity defended in this article, the trajectory, the four parts, the graded verdicts, serves none of the standing armies, which is precisely why it must be restated by disinterested labour whenever the controversy is reopened. There is a lesson in the record's persistence for readers of any contested thinker. Texts do not survive because they are settled; they survive because they are useful unsettled, and the usefulness guarantees that the settlement, though available in the evidence all along, will never be permitted to become the common account. The most a single article can do is what this one has attempted: to make the full record, in its order, with its worst page characterised and its best pages quoted, easier to consult than the excerpt, so that the next selection, by whichever army, is at least made against a visible alternative.

## The five misreadings, corrected in order

The controversy generates the same five errors with such regularity that they deserve correction in a single place, each against the section of this article that carries its evidence. The first misreading holds that Marx endorsed colonialism without qualification. It survives only by suppressing the articles' own moral vehemence, in which the conquest of India is a crime of greed and cruelty, and by suppressing the closing condition of the second article, in which the fruits of the transformation belong to the Indians only when they throw the yoke off or when Britain's rulers are displaced. What he endorsed, if the word must be used, was a historical function he attributed to a process he condemned, which is a different and more uncomfortable position, and it deserves to be opposed in its actual form. The second misreading, the mirror image, holds that he condemned colonialism without qualification from the start. It survives by suppressing the double mission itself, the progressive verdict delivered in plain sentences in a public newspaper, which no rereading can soften into irony. The third misreading treats the 1853 position as his settled doctrine, a timeless Marx on empire available for quotation. The position over time table is the standing refutation: the doctrine changed, substantially, on the record, in the direction opposite to the summary. The fourth misreading proposes that the private language can be separated from the assessment of the man, quarantined as gossip beneath a thinker's notice. It cannot, because the assessment of a man includes his conduct toward the peoples his theory concerned, and the 1862 letter is conduct in writing, addressed to his closest collaborator, unretracted because unpublished rather than because withdrawn; what can be separated, and what this article has separated, is the letter's bearing on the man from its bearing on the truth of his economics. The fifth misreading is the subtlest and the most consequential for students: that his account of Indian society was descriptive, a report of what India was, rather than derived, a construction assembled from the reports of the power that ruled it. Once the derivation is seen, the articles change their evidentiary species. They stop being a window on India and become a window on the information order of empire, on what a brilliant and hostile reader in London could and could not know in 1853, and that is the capacity in which they retain their permanent value, as a lesson in the pathology of archives that no subsequent controversy has made less timely.

## Grading the parts separately: the verdict

The verdict of this article is delivered in parts because the evidence arrives in parts, and a summary sentence that averaged them would misdescribe every one. The first part concerns the journalism of 1853. Its description of Indian society was wrong, derived from the archive of the power it criticised, and its constructive claim, that British rule belonged to history's progressive side, depended on that description and falls with it; its moral condemnation of the conquest's conduct was fierce, accurate, and insufficient to save the argument built beside it. Grade: a failure of evidence and of framework, on the record, in print, never retracted. The second part concerns the private language. The correspondence contains racist and antisemitic epithets used as instruments of contempt against a man's birth and ancestry; the characterisation has been given in full above with its citation; no context of period or privacy converts it into anything else, and it is the one part of the record for which no development can be pleaded, since the idiom of the letters does not improve as the analysis does. Grade: indefensible, and a permanent entry in the assessment of the man. The third part concerns the development. From 1857 the public analysis changes direction and keeps changing: agency is restored to the colonised, the stage scheme is dismantled by its author, communal forms are studied rather than sentenced, settler conduct is condemned from the victims' side. The development is documented in texts anyone may read, it is substantial rather than cosmetic, and it was achieved against the grain of his most famous formulations. Grade: a genuine intellectual achievement, unfinished and unsystematised, which the summary literature ignored for a century at the cost of falsifying its subject. The fourth part concerns the Ireland letters, graded not as evidence of their author's virtue but as contributions to analysis: the identification of national antagonism as a serviced mechanism of working class division, and of the oppressed nation's movement as the potential lever of the oppressor nation's emancipation, remains a live instrument, taken up wherever labour movements confront the politics of a divided workforce. Grade: the most consequential writing in the whole record, and the standard against which the rest is measured.

Two boundary rulings close the assessment, and both are matters of scope rather than of judgement. The first is that this article has assessed a man and his writings, not a doctrine and its career. Whether the colonial record of Marx discredits Marxism is a question with its own article in this series, and it cannot be answered from this evidence alone, since it involves what later movements made of texts he never controlled, in countries he never studied, under conditions he never imagined; the most this article's evidence establishes is that the corpus handed to those movements contained both the 1853 articles and the 1870 letters, the stage scheme and its repudiation, and that movements chose, as movements do, which inheritance to draw. The theory of imperialism proper, the doctrine the next generation erected on foundations examined in these pages, belongs to the [marxism and imperialism guide](/2017/02/01/marxism-and-imperialism-guide/) and is deliberately not summarised here. The second ruling concerns the present tense, which this article has avoided throughout by design. No living politician, party, campaign, or controversy has been named or graded here, and none will be: the record under assessment closed in 1883, the scholarly debate over it is a debate about texts and evidence, and the reader who wishes to draw lines from the Ireland letters to the labour politics of any later century has been given the mechanism, accurately sourced, with which to draw them. What a dead man's reversal teaches is a method: periodise the position, check the premises against the evidence of the people described, state the ugly parts in the same tone as the admirable ones, and never let a single text, however quotable, stand for a life's work. That method, applied to its own author, is the closest thing to justice this difficult record admits.

## For students and exam candidates

Your examination questions on this topic are won or lost on periodisation, so build the habit this article models: never quote a text without its year, and never let the 1853 articles answer for a man who wrote for thirty more years. The structure that scores is the graded separation: the articles and their failed premises, the private language stated without flinching, the development from 1857, and the Ireland reversal as the analytical summit. Candidates who reproduce the double mission as Marx's view of colonialism, full stop, are repeating the commonest error in the literature, and examiners know it. Work through the [marxism imperialism exam answers](/2017/06/15/marxism-imperialism-exam-answers/) for the question forms this material takes under time pressure, and keep your own notes in order with the [VaultBook study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), using the position over time table as the spine of a revision card: one row, one date, one change of premise. When a question asks whether the colonial writings discredit their author, answer in parts, concede the letter without being asked, and let the Irish letters carry your conclusion.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The classroom gift in this record is the Irish correspondence, and it should be taught before the famous articles rather than after them. The Irish letters are short, fierce, and immediately intelligible to students who arrive expecting the double mission and its discontents: set the Kugelmann and Meyer and Vogt letters beside the 1853 articles and let the seminar discover the reversal as an event in a mind, with the Fenian context and the General Council fight supplying the pressure that produced it. The 1862 letter requires a decision in advance, and this article's policy is recommended as the classroom's: inform students precisely what the letter contains and where it is printed, characterise it without softening, and spend the seminar's energy on what the language means for the assessment rather than on the words themselves. Said against Ahmad remains the most efficient pairing for the scholarly hour, with Anderson's notebook evidence held in reserve as the demonstration that archives, patiently extended, can move a debate that polemic had frozen.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this record is documentary, and it remains surprisingly open. The ethnological notebooks are still being absorbed into the standard accounts, the Kovalevsky and Maine marginalia reward closer publication, and the reception of the colonial writings in the colonised world's own historiographies is a field where the [indian marxist historians](/2018/03/01/indian-marxist-historians/) are indispensable guides to a literature that corrected the founder from inside his own method. Journalists covering the periodic flare-ups of the was-Marx-a-racist controversy should treat the tabulated sequence as a checklist before filing: a story that cannot say where 1870 and 1881 fall in its narrative is not ready. The scholarly exchange between Said and Ahmad, and the notebook scholarship that followed it, is reconstructed in the sections above with both sides at strength; researchers entering the debate are advised to begin from Anderson's chronology and to state their weighing rules for genre and date explicitly, since the controversy's remaining disagreements are, on inspection, disagreements about weights rather than about evidence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Did Marx support colonialism?

He supported, in 1853, a verdict about its effects: British rule in India, whose motives and conduct he condemned as criminal, was in his judgement dissolving a stagnant order and laying foundations from which Indians would eventually emancipate themselves. That is neither endorsement of the conquest nor opposition to it in the modern sense. From the late 1850s the verdict disappears from his writing, replaced by support for colonial peoples in revolt and, on Ireland, by the doctrine that the colony's freedom is the metropolis's precondition. The accurate answer is periodised.

### Q: What did Marx write about British rule in India?

Two long articles published in an American newspaper in 1853, while Parliament debated the East India Company's charter, followed by reporting on the rebellion of 1857 and on the settlement imposed in 1858 and 1859. The first pair argue the double mission from the parliamentary evidence and the colonial archive. The later reporting treats the rising as a national revolt, itemises the fiscal tortures and annexations of the restored regime, and quietly abandons the earlier framework. His Indian journalism is therefore two bodies of work in tension, not one position.

### Q: What is the double mission argument?

The claim, from the article of August 1853, that England in India had two historical tasks to fulfil at once: a destructive one, the annihilation of the old Asiatic society of self-sufficient villages, and a regenerating one, the laying of material foundations, in railways and telegraph lines, a single administration, and an Indian army trained by its conquerors, from which a new society could arise. The argument expressly separated the conqueror's despicable motives from the process's alleged direction. Its weakness is its premise: the stagnant India it required did not exist.

### Q: Did Marx use racist language?

Yes. The clearest instance is the much cited letter he sent Engels in the summer of 1862, after Lassalle's visit to London. In it he sneers at his rival by way of Lassalle's Jewishness and of physical features he chose to read as African, using for both purposes the ugliest vocabulary his language offered and treating the supposed descent as itself a stain. The letter is published in the standard collected edition of the correspondence, in the volume covering the early eighteen sixties, where the characterisation given here can be checked word for word. Private, unpublished in his lifetime, unremarkable in its century: none of it alters what the language is.

### Q: Did Marx change his mind about colonialism?

Substantially, and on the record. The 1853 articles treat colonial peoples as the material of a history made in Europe; the reporting of 1857 treats Indians as political actors in a national revolt; the American war journalism applies the same sympathy and analysis to the enslaved; the Irish letters of 1869 and 1870 reverse his earlier order of emancipation outright; and the Russian drafts of 1881 abandon the universal sequence of stages his 1853 framework, and the 1859 Preface, had assumed. The change was never announced as a recantation, which is why it is so often missed.

### Q: What did Marx say about Ireland?

He argued that the Union made Ireland the material base of the landed aristocracy of England, whose income and power flowed from Irish rents, so that English emancipation ran through Irish emancipation and not after it. His two correspondences, with Kugelmann and with Meyer and Vogt, add the psychological mechanism: English workers, taught by press and pulpit to despise the Irish whose cheap labour undercut them, felt themselves members of a ruling nation and so policed their own subjection. Until Ireland's freedom broke that antagonism, he concluded, the best organised working class in Europe would accomplish nothing.

### Q: Is Marx Eurocentric?

In the framework of his early work, yes, in the precise sense this article has documented: the Europe of 1853 supplies the stages, the dynamism, and the future, while Asia is assigned stasis and defined by what it lacks. The charge weakens as the record proceeds, because the later work abandons the single staircase, studies communal formations in their specificity, and relocates historical agency in the colonised peoples themselves. A one-word verdict on a thirty-year trajectory is a refusal to read it; the evidence supports a periodised answer, framework Eurocentric, development against the framework.

### Q: What did Edward Said argue about Marx?

In Orientalism of 1978, Said read the 1853 articles as exhibits of the discourse he was anatomising: Marx's India, he argued, was assembled from the Orientalist archive's stock, the Asiatic mode restored its founding assumption of a static East, and even Marx's sympathy was finally overborne by a redemptive determinism in which Asia entered history only through European transformation. His conclusion was that the discourse captured even its most formidable dissenter, which made Marx its most instructive exhibit rather than its crudest one. Aijaz Ahmad's reply of 1992 charged the reading with selective quotation and with freezing a developing thinker at his weakest text.

### Q: Was Marx's account of Indian society accurate?

No. The self-sufficient village, unchanging beneath its conquerors, and the despotism resting upon it, derived from the reports of the colonial administration and from a European tradition running through Bernier, Mill, and Hegel, and the account has been dismantled by subsequent historiography, much of it written by Indian Marxist historians such as Kosambi and Habib, who documented pre-colonial commerce, export manufacture, credit, and competing states from Indian evidence. The failure is located in the premises, and it is fatal to the conclusion built on them: a society in motion did not require a foreign solvent.

### Q: Does Marx's colonial writing discredit Marxism?

That question is treated separately elsewhere in this series; this article rules only on the evidence it examined. What the record establishes is narrower: the inheritance handed to later movements was divided against itself, the early articles and the stage scheme on one side, the Irish letters and the scheme's repudiation by their own author on the other. Whether a doctrine falls with its founder's record involves what movements made of those texts in countries and centuries he never saw, and cannot be settled from this file alone.

### Q: How does the Ireland reversal grade the early articles?

Harshly, because it withdraws their operative assumption. The articles of 1853 assigned the colonised the role of material in a history directed from Europe; the letters assign a colonised people the decisive role in the metropolis's own emancipation, and analyse its oppressor's working class as the mechanism's victim. A thinker's later work is not automatically his best, but where the later work argues against the earlier on fuller evidence, the earlier is graded as superseded by its author: of interest as the framework he escaped, not as the position he held.

### Q: What should a student separate between the early articles' premises and their conclusions?

Three things. The description of Indian society, which came from the colonial archive and has been falsified. The moral judgement of the conquest's conduct, which was fierce and has worn well. And the developmental conclusion, which depended entirely on the description: once the frozen India is removed, the progressive verdict has nothing to stand on, however often it is quoted. Students who separate these layers can explain both why the articles persuaded contemporaries and why they fail now, which is the difference between an examination answer and a recitation.

### Q: Why did Ireland reverse the argument?

Because Ireland united, in one case, every element the earlier framework had kept apart. It was a colony inside the same state and labour market as the metropolis, so its effects on English class politics could be observed directly. Its land system anchored the English aristocracy materially. Its people's migration divided the English working class measurably, to the ruling class's visible profit. And its national movement, in the Fenian years, forced the question into the International's agenda, where Marx had to argue it against English trade unionists rather than merely write it. Evidence, proximity, and organisational combat together reversed the sequence.

### Q: How does the position over time table order the evidence?

By date and by premise. Each row gives a period, what was written, the text it appeared in, the factual premises relied on, and the difference from the row before, so that the object of assessment is the trajectory rather than any extract. The arrangement prevents the two standard misreadings at once: the 1853 row cannot pose as the whole man while the rows of 1870 and 1881 stand beneath it, and the late rows cannot absolve the sequence while the row of 1862 stands in its proper place in the middle of it.

### Q: What separates a characterisation of the language from a reproduction of it?

The reader's need. A characterisation tells you what kind of language a document contains, against whom it is directed, on what occasion, and where the document is printed so that you may verify every word of the description. A reproduction adds only the epithets themselves to the page, where they circulate detached from the analysis, available for purposes the analysis does not serve. This article's policy, which it recommends as the series standard, is characterisation with full citation and no softening: the letter of 1862 is described as racist and antisemitic because it is, and its words are left in the edition where they belong.
