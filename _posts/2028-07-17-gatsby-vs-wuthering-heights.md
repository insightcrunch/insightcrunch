Two men rise from nothing, acquire money nobody can account for, buy their way back into the presence of a woman who married someone richer, and destroy a good deal of the surrounding world in the attempt. One does it on a Yorkshire moor in the 1780s. The other does it on a Long Island shore in 1922. A Gatsby vs Wuthering Heights comparison is worth running at length because the resemblance is not atmospheric or vague; it is a matching set of decisions about how obsession works, what class does to love, and why a story of consuming passion is best delivered by somebody who was not consumed by it.

The pairing is also useful because it corrects a reading habit. Readers who arrive at Fitzgerald's novel through the film adaptations and the party imagery tend to file Jay Gatsby under romance and Heathcliff under horror, as though one were a hopeful lover and the other a monster. Read the two books against each other and that separation collapses. Both men are dangerous. Both loves are total, and totality is the problem in each case rather than the virtue. What differs is the register the two novelists chose, and the register has fooled a great many readers into thinking the substance differs too.

## What the Gatsby vs Wuthering Heights Comparison Sets Side by Side

Emily Bronte's only novel appeared in December 1847 under the name Ellis Bell, in three volumes, bound with her sister Anne's Agnes Grey. It covers roughly thirty years and two generations. Mr Lockwood, a tenant newly arrived at Thrushcross Grange, is snowed in one night at the neighbouring farmhouse and afterward asks his housekeeper, Nelly Dean, to explain the household he has stumbled into. She tells him the story over several evenings, and he writes it down. That story concerns a foundling brought back from Liverpool by the elder Earnshaw and raised alongside his own children; the boy's bond with the daughter, Catherine; her decision to marry the wealthy neighbour, Edgar Linton; the boy's disappearance and return three years later with an unexplained fortune; and his systematic ruin of two families across the following two decades. He dies at the end, and the surviving children of the two households are left to repair what he broke.

Fitzgerald's novel appeared in April 1925, runs to roughly fifty thousand words, and covers one summer with a five-year backstory. Nick Carraway, a bond salesman from the Midwest, rents a cottage next to a mansion whose owner throws parties for strangers. The owner is Jay Gatsby, born James Gatz in North Dakota, who acquired a fortune through channels the novel never fully itemises and who bought the house because it faces Daisy Buchanan's dock across the bay. He loved her in 1917 when he was a penniless officer. She married Tom Buchanan instead. Gatsby uses Nick to engineer a reunion, the affair is exposed at the Plaza, Daisy kills Tom's mistress with Gatsby's car, and George Wilson kills Gatsby. Nick buries him and writes the account.

The comparison this article defends operates on four shared decisions. Both novelists build a central man whose desire is not one appetite among several but the entire content of his personality. Both make that desire cross a class line and make the class line the mechanism of the ruin. Both give the man a fortune whose origin is deliberately obscured and morally compromised. And both refuse to narrate the passion from inside it, handing the story instead to a witness who observes without feeling.

### Are Jay Gatsby and Heathcliff the same kind of lover?

Structurally, yes. Each man's desire is total rather than partial, each is barred by class before he is barred by circumstance, each acquires unexplained money as the instrument of return, and each destroys other people while pursuing a woman already married to a richer man. The registers differ; the anatomy does not.

Everything else in the two books diverges, and the divergences are large. Bronte gives her lovers reciprocity and a second generation that survives. Fitzgerald gives his man a woman who does not love him back with anything like equal force and an ending with no future in it at all. Those differences are where the comparison earns its keep, and they arrive later in this article.

## Point of Contact One: The Obsession That Becomes the Self

The strongest thing the pairing reveals is that neither novel treats obsession as an emotion. In both books it is an identity, and once it has become an identity there is nothing underneath it to fall back on when it fails.

Bronte states this with a directness that has shocked readers for a hundred and seventy years. Catherine tells Nelly that her love for Edgar resembles foliage, which changes with the seasons, while her feeling for the foundling resembles the rock beneath, which gives little visible pleasure and is necessary. She goes further and identifies herself with him outright, collapsing the distinction between loving a person and being a person. Bronte's man, for his part, describes his own life as ended when she dies, using the language of losing a soul rather than losing a companion. The novel's Gothic apparatus, the ghost at the casement, the opened coffin, the refusal to be buried apart, exists to dramatise a claim the prose has already made in plain statement: these two are not separable entities who love each other but a single entity distributed across two bodies.

Fitzgerald never says anything so metaphysical, and his refusal is precisely what makes the parallel interesting. His man's obsession is presented as a technical problem in engineering. Gatsby has an object, a plan, and a schedule. He bought a specific house for a specific sightline. He throws parties on the calculated chance that one guest will be the right guest. He asks Nick to invite Daisy to tea and supplies the flowers. Where Bronte reaches for the language of soul, Fitzgerald reaches for the language of project management, and the effect is not to reduce the obsession but to make it eerier. A man who has organised five years of his life around one afternoon has emptied himself just as completely as a man who says he cannot live without his soul.

The novel makes the emptying explicit. Nick tells us that after the reunion, when Gatsby is showing Daisy the house, he "revalued everything in his house according to the measure of response it drew from her" eyes, which is to say the mansion has no value of its own and never did. The famous shirt scene works the same way. Gatsby throws imported shirts onto a table until Daisy weeps into them, and readers who find the moment absurd are correct: the shirts are not the point and never were, they are simply the most expensive proof available that the five years were spent correctly.

Then Fitzgerald delivers the sentence that makes the whole pattern visible. Watching the green light after the reunion, Nick reflects that "his count of enchanted objects had diminished by one." The light had been a symbol; now it is a bulb on a dock. Possession destroys the object of obsession because the obsession was never about the object.

### Does obsession function as identity in both novels?

In both, yes. Bronte's lovers describe themselves as a single being distributed across two bodies, so bereavement reads as amputation rather than grief. Fitzgerald empties his man differently, showing a life organised entirely around one afternoon, so that when the afternoon arrives there is nothing left underneath it.

Bronte's man survives his loss by eighteen years and spends them on revenge, which is what a person does when the identity has been destroyed but the body persists. Fitzgerald's man survives his by a matter of weeks and spends them refusing to accept that the loss occurred. Nick reports the last conversation between Gatsby and Daisy's decision, and Gatsby's answer is that he is going to fix everything the way it was before, that she will see. Two ways of failing to die at the right moment.

The series treats this territory in its own right in the article on [obsession and idealization in The Great Gatsby](/2024/06/10/obsession-idealization-gatsby/), which stays inside the novel; the present comparison uses Bronte to make the anatomy visible from outside.

## Point of Contact Two: Love Across the Class Line

The second shared decision is the one most often missed, and it is the one that turns two love stories into two social novels.

Neither obsession is thwarted by temperament, distance, or misunderstanding. Both are thwarted by money, and both novelists make the mechanism explicit rather than atmospheric.

Bronte's is the more brutal because she puts it in the mouth of the woman herself. Catherine tells Nelly that marrying the foundling would degrade her, and she says so while insisting that she loves him more than she has ever loved anything. The sentence is one of the most quoted in English fiction because it refuses to let the reader separate the feeling from the economics. She is not choosing Edgar over the man she loves; she is choosing a house, an income, and a position, and she believes she can have both. The novel then spends five hundred pages demonstrating what that belief costs.

Fitzgerald reproduces the structure and moves the emphasis. Nick's account of the Louisville courtship, delivered in the eighth chapter, states plainly that the young officer had no money, no family, and no right to be in that house, that he was a penniless young man without a past who could at any moment feel "the invisible cloak of his uniform" slip from his shoulders, and that he "had certainly taken her under false pretences." He had not intended to; he had simply let her assume. Fitzgerald then gives the class barrier its most exact image. Daisy's house is described as gleaming with the bought luxury of star-shine, and the young man perceives beautiful houses of that kind as safe and proud above the hot struggles of the poor. Class is not an obstacle in the plot. It is the medium the whole desire is dissolved in.

The five-year gap is the crucial difference in tactics. Bronte's man vanishes for three years and returns rich; Fitzgerald's vanishes for five and returns rich. Both returns are attempts to buy the qualification that was missing the first time. Both fail, and they fail for the same structural reason: the class line is not primarily about the quantity of money but about its age and its manners. Bronte signals this by having her returning man remain unmistakably other to the Lintons no matter how much land he acquires. Fitzgerald signals it through the pink suit, the phrase "old sport," the shirts, the parties nobody was invited to, and Tom Buchanan's flat certainty that a man with that much recent money must be a bootlegger. Tom happens to be correct, but his method is pure class instinct, and the novel lets both facts stand at once.

### How does class-crossing love destroy in both novels?

Neither book lets love fail for emotional reasons. Bronte's Catherine chooses an income and a position while insisting she loves elsewhere. Fitzgerald's young officer courts under a false impression created by a uniform. In both, the returning fortune buys quantity but cannot buy the age and manner that admit a man to the class.

The consequence in both books is that the obsession curdles into a project of social acquisition, and the acquisition is the thing that destroys other people. Bronte's man does not merely become rich; he takes the mortgage on one house, marries into the other to secure the inheritance, and degrades the next generation of both families as a matter of policy. Fitzgerald's man does not do anything comparable, and this is a real difference, but the novel is careful to show that his acquisition also has victims: the fortune comes from bootlegging and from a bond fraud Wolfsheim's network was running, and Tom's exposure of it at the Plaza is accurate even though Tom's motives are contemptible.

## Point of Contact Three: The Fortune Nobody Explains

Both novelists make a deliberate and identical choice about their central man's money: the reader is told that it exists and denied a clear account of where it came from.

Bronte's is total. Her man leaves as a labourer without shoes and returns three years later with the bearing and the resources of a gentleman, and the novel never explains how. Nelly speculates. Lockwood wonders. No answer arrives. Readers have proposed the army, the Americas, the West Indies trade, and gambling, and the text supports none of them, because the absence is the point. A fortune with no visible origin is a fortune that cannot be socially placed, and a man who cannot be socially placed is a threat.

Fitzgerald's is partial, which is the more sophisticated version. We are told a good deal: the drugstores that sold grain alcohol over the counter, the association with Meyer Wolfsheim, the telephone calls from Chicago and Philadelphia, the man on the phone in the last chapter talking about a fellow named Parke picked up in a small town. We are never told the whole. The novel supplies enough to establish criminality and withholds enough to preserve mystery, and it lets the rumour economy do the rest. At the first party the girls in yellow say their host is a bootlegger. Later a guest reports that he killed a man. Tom asks whether he is some big bootlegger and then admits, when pressed, that he did not hear it anywhere and simply imagined it.

The comparative point is that both novelists understood something about money and narrative that most imitators miss. Explaining the fortune fully would convert the central man from a force into a case study. Withholding it entirely would make him a fairy tale. Bronte chooses the fairy-tale end of that spectrum and gets a figure of nearly supernatural menace. Fitzgerald chooses the middle and gets a figure who is simultaneously pitiable and criminal, which is a harder effect and one the novel could not achieve if the audit were complete.

There is a further shared implication. In both books the mysterious fortune is what allows the man to re-enter the story, and in both books it is useless for the purpose he acquired it for. The moor man's land does not make Catherine alive again. The Long Island man's mansion does not make 1917 return. The money is a key that opens the wrong door, and both novels arrange their plots so the reader watches the key turn and the door refuse.

## Point of Contact Four: Narrated at a Remove

The fourth shared decision is formal, and it is the one that most repays close attention in an essay, because it is the least likely to occur to a reader who has not been prompted.

Neither novel narrates its central passion from inside. Both hand the story to somebody constitutionally incapable of feeling it.

Bronte's arrangement is double and slightly comic. Lockwood is a shallow, self-regarding town gentleman who has rented a moorland property to cure himself of society, who congratulates himself on his own reserve, and who understands almost nothing he sees. He is snowed in, has a nightmare about a child's ghost at the casement, and afterward asks the housekeeper for an explanation. Nelly Dean supplies it. She was present for most of the events, she has her own loyalties and her own errors of judgement, and she narrates with the confidence of a servant who has decided what everything meant. So the reader receives the most violent love story in English fiction through a fool who did not witness it and a servant who did and who blames most of the participants.

Fitzgerald's arrangement is single and internalised, and it produces the same effect by different means. Nick is not a fool, but he is temperamentally the wrong person to narrate a consuming passion. He announces early that he is inclined to reserve all judgements and that "reserving judgements is a matter of infinite hope." He tells us that "life is much more successfully looked at from a single window." He conducts his own romance with Jordan Baker at a low simmer and ends it by telling her he is thirty and "five years too old to lie to myself and call it honour." A man who says that about his own affair is not going to understand a man who reorganised half a decade around a green light, and Fitzgerald knew it.

### How do both novels narrate the passion at a remove?

Bronte doubles the distance: a shallow tenant records a servant's account of events he never saw. Fitzgerald internalises it, giving the story to a cautious narrator who ends his own romance with a shrug. In both, the passion reaches the reader through someone temperamentally incapable of it.

Why does either writer accept such a handicap? Because a total passion narrated from inside is unreadable. It has no perspective, no scale, and no exit. Filtered through a bystander it acquires all three. The bystander supplies the ordinary world against which the obsession can be measured, and the measuring is the reading experience. Nelly's exasperation is the reason the moor lovers register as enormous. Nick's caution is the reason the parties register as absurd and the man throwing them registers as worth something anyway.

The series treats Fitzgerald's version of this machinery in [frame narrative and retrospection in Gatsby](/2026/02/02/gatsby-frame-narrative/), which covers the mechanics; the Bronte pairing is the fastest way to see why the mechanics were chosen.

## Point of Contact Five: The House as Argument

A fifth resemblance is architectural in the literal sense. Both novels organise their moral geography around two houses, and in both the distance between the houses is the distance between the classes.

Bronte's pair is famous enough to have entered the language: a working farmhouse exposed on the heights, and a sheltered park property in the valley below with carpets, chandeliers, and a lapdog the children fight over. The daughter of the farmhouse spends five weeks at the Grange and returns transformed, and the transformation is the hinge of the entire plot. The two buildings represent two futures, and every major character is defined by which one they belong to and which one they want.

Fitzgerald's pair is the two Eggs, separated by a courtesy bay. He tells us in the first chapter that they are identical in contour and dissimilar in every other particular, and he places his narrator in a small eyesore squeezed between mansions on the wrong one. The green light burns on the dock of the right one. The whole plot is a series of crossings: Nick drives east to dinner, Gatsby swims the social distance by way of a tea party, Daisy comes west once and is appalled by the guests, and the final movement of the book is Nick giving up on the East entirely and going home.

The comparative payoff is that in both books the man who crosses is destroyed and the houses are not. Bronte's foundling acquires both properties and dies in the room he was first brought to as a child. Fitzgerald's man buys the mansion, fills it, empties it, and is found in the pool while the Buchanans pack. Neither novel permits an architectural solution to a class problem, and both make the point through property rather than through speeches.

## Point of Contact Six: The Woman Who Cannot Be Possessed

A sixth resemblance sits in what the two novels do with the women at the centre, and it is the resemblance most likely to earn credit in an examination because it is the one least often noticed.

In both books the woman is presented almost entirely through the desire she generates, and both novelists are conscious of the distortion this produces. Bronte's Catherine is vivid, wilful, and articulate in the first half, and then dies at the midpoint, after which she exists only as an absence that organises everyone else's behaviour for eighteen years. The second half of the novel is populated by people acting in relation to a woman who is not there. Fitzgerald performs the same disappearance without needing a death. Daisy is present for the whole book and is never once given an interior. She is a voice, a white dress, a hand held over a mouth, a promise that she had done gay, exciting things just a while since. Nick tells us that Gatsby had thrown himself into the reunion with a creative passion, adding to it all the time, decking it out with every bright feather that drifted his way, and that "no amount of fire or freshness can challenge what a man can store up in his ghostly heart." The sentence is about Gatsby and it is also an admission about the book: the reader has been given the storage, not the woman.

The critical consequence is identical in both novels. Neither woman can possibly satisfy the desire aimed at her, because the desire is aimed at a construction. Bronte makes this explicit by having her heroine die and the obsession continue unabated, which proves that the living woman was never the requirement. Fitzgerald makes it explicit by having Daisy survive and the obsession fail anyway. Nick puts it plainly when he says there must have been moments even that afternoon when she tumbled short of his dreams, not through her own fault but through the colossal vitality of his illusion.

Read together, the two books make a claim that neither makes alone: total desire is not a relation between two people at all. It is a relation between one person and an object he has manufactured, and the manufacture is the reason the desire cannot be satisfied and the reason the woman at the centre of both novels is so oddly difficult to describe.

There is a further wrinkle worth noticing. Bronte's woman speaks for herself at length before she is removed, and what she says is damning and self-aware: she names her own calculation and predicts its consequences. Fitzgerald never gives Daisy an equivalent speech, and readers have argued for a century about whether that silence is a failure of characterisation or the novel's sharpest point about how such women are seen. The comparison strengthens the second reading, because Bronte demonstrates what a novel gains by letting the woman explain herself, and Fitzgerald's refusal then looks like a decision rather than an oversight.

## The Two Returns Read Closely

The single most productive close-reading exercise available in this pairing is to set the two returns beside each other, because both novelists spend enormous care on the moment when the enriched man walks back into the world that excluded him.

Bronte handles hers as a shock delivered in a doorway. Three years have passed. A servant announces a visitor. The man who enters is upright, dressed, and self-possessed, and the woman who receives him has been married for months. The scene is written so that every witness registers the transformation and nobody can account for it, and Bronte then lets the discomfort sit. Her returning man is polite for exactly as long as politeness serves him. Within a chapter he is lodging at the farmhouse, gambling with his foster brother, and beginning the acquisition that will take the rest of the novel.

Fitzgerald splits his return into two halves separated by two chapters, and the split is the more artful arrangement. The first half is the party. The man has been back for some time, has bought the house, has assembled the guests, and has done nothing whatever with any of it except wait, because the reunion he wants cannot be engineered directly. The second half is the tea at Nick's cottage in the fifth chapter, and it is one of the most physically awkward scenes in American fiction: the flowers sent ahead, the rain, the white flannel suit and silver shirt and gold tie, the clock nearly knocked off the mantel, the half-hour of silence, and the man leaning against the mantelpiece in a strained counterfeit of perfect ease.

The comparison illuminates both. Bronte's return works because it is instantaneous and unexplained; the reader feels the same vertigo as the characters. Fitzgerald's works because it is protracted and humiliating; the reader watches a man who has spent five years preparing discover that preparation does not help. And the two together demonstrate a general principle about the returning-outsider plot: the return is always an anticlimax, because what the man acquired was money and what he needed was time.

Both novelists then follow the return with an inventory. Bronte's man surveys the two properties and begins calculating. Fitzgerald's man walks Daisy through his house, room by room, and the narration records that he "revalued everything in his house according to the measure of response it drew from her" eyes. Two men who have accumulated for years, both discovering in the same gesture that the accumulation was only ever a bid.

## Recurring Misreadings Worth Avoiding

Three misreadings recur in student writing on this pairing, and naming them is often worth more than another paragraph of analysis.

The first is the assumption that a Gothic novel and a modernist novel cannot share a structure. Students trained to sort by period and movement will sometimes resist the comparison on the grounds that the books belong to different worlds, and then produce an essay that spends its length on context rather than on text. The resistance is misplaced. Structures migrate across periods more easily than styles do, and the returning-outsider plot with an unexplained fortune is one of the most durable structures in the language. Naming the migration is a stronger move than denying it.

The second is treating the second generation as a flaw in Bronte rather than as a finding about Fitzgerald. Readers often report that the second half of the Yorkshire novel is a falling-off, that the children are less interesting than the parents, and that the reconciliation is a concession. Whatever the merits of that judgement, it is the wrong thing to do with it in a comparative essay. The correct use is to observe that Bronte thought a future was necessary and Fitzgerald did not, and to ask what each choice says about the two societies the books describe. One novel believes damage can be repaired across a generation. The other believes the damage is the society.

The third is over-reading the water. Because the green light sits across a bay and the Yorkshire novel has a moor, essays sometimes construct an elaborate symmetry in which both landscapes represent the unattainable. They do not. Bronte's moor is shared ground, the place where the bond was formed, and its meaning is union rather than separation; the barrier in her novel is a drawing room, not a landscape. Fitzgerald's water is a measured distance and functions as an obstacle throughout. Getting this right is worth doing, because it is exactly the kind of discrimination that separates an essay which has read both books from one that has read summaries of them.

A fourth habit is worth flagging even though it is less common. Some essays treat Nelly Dean as a neutral reporter and Nick Carraway as an unreliable one, as though the two narrators sat at opposite ends of a scale. Both are partial. Nelly makes decisions that shape the plot, withholds letters, and delivers judgements on everyone with total confidence, and a good deal of modern criticism reads her as considerably less trustworthy than Lockwood assumes. Treating her as a window while treating Nick as a lens misrepresents both novels.

## The Decisive Differences

Five differences are large enough to change the reading, and an essay that names them will beat one that only lists resemblances.

The first and largest is reciprocity. Bronte's love is mutual and stated. Her woman declares her identification with the man to a third party and never retracts it; his grief at her death is presented as the destruction of a self rather than the loss of a partner. Fitzgerald's love is not mutual, and the novel says so at the Plaza. Asked to declare that she never loved Tom, Daisy cannot manage it. She says she loved Gatsby too, which is precisely the answer his whole project cannot survive. Nick's narration afterward is unsparing: "only the dead dream fought on as the afternoon slipped away, trying to touch what was no longer tangible." Bronte's tragedy is that two people who were one thing were separated by money. Fitzgerald's is that one man built a cathedral around a woman who liked him.

The second is the presence of revenge. Bronte's second half is a sustained campaign: mortgages called in, an heiress married and mistreated for her inheritance, a boy deliberately kept illiterate, a dying son used as an instrument to secure a property. Nothing remotely comparable exists in Fitzgerald's novel. Gatsby wants a sentence spoken; he does not want the Buchanans ruined, and when Daisy runs Myrtle down he stands outside her house all night watching over what he calls nothing. Readers who describe Gatsby as vengeful have imported the Gothic.

The third is the second generation. Bronte's novel resolves. The surviving children of the two houses learn to read together, the farmhouse is repaired, and the final image is of three graves and a quiet moor. There is a future. Fitzgerald's novel has no second generation to speak of; Pammy Buchanan appears once, is displayed to guests, and is removed. The last movement is a man alone on a beach imagining sailors who arrived three hundred years earlier. There is a past and there is a current, and there is nothing in between.

The fourth is mode. Bronte writes Gothic romance with supernatural machinery she never disowns: a ghost at a window, a sleepwalking servant's testimony, a man who claims to be haunted for eighteen years and asks to have a coffin wall removed. Fitzgerald writes a compressed realist novel with one symbolic light and a pair of advertising eyes, and every uncanny effect in it is explicitly a matter of perception rather than of fact. Owl Eyes is a drunk with spectacles, not an oracle. The doctor's billboard is a billboard, and the novel says so through a character who mistakes it for God and is treated as pitiable for doing so.

The fifth is scale in time. Bronte covers thirty years and two generations across roughly the length of two Fitzgerald novels. Fitzgerald covers a summer with flashbacks, in fifty thousand words, and the compression is the source of his effects. A reader who moves from one to the other will feel the difference immediately as a difference in patience: Bronte accumulates; Fitzgerald detonates.

### Is Gatsby's love gentler than Heathcliff's?

Softer in manner, not in kind. Bronte's man ruins two households deliberately across eighteen years; Fitzgerald's ruins nobody on purpose. But both desires are total, both are pursued with criminal money, and both leave bodies. Politeness is a register, not a moral difference.

That distinction is the single most useful correction the pairing offers, because it dismantles the sentimental reading of Fitzgerald's novel more effectively than any amount of internal argument. Set the polite obsessive beside the violent one and the politeness stops looking like innocence.

## Obsession, Class, and Frame: A Comparison Table

The findable artifact for this article is a paired reading along the axes that carry the comparison. Call it the ruinous-love table.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Central man | A foundling brought from Liverpool, raised as a servant after the elder Earnshaw dies | James Gatz of North Dakota, renamed at seventeen after seeing Dan Cody's yacht |
| Barrier | A marriage chosen for house, income, and position | A uniform that concealed the absence of family and money |

| Reciprocity | Mutual and declared, described as a single self in two bodies | One-sided; at the Plaza she will not say she never loved her husband |
| Revenge | A sustained eighteen-year campaign against two families | Absent; the aim is a sentence spoken, not a household ruined |
| Narration | Doubled: a shallow tenant records a servant's account | Single and internalised: a cautious bond salesman writing a year later |
| Narrator's own love life | Lockwood retreats from a seaside flirtation before it begins | Nick ends an affair by declaring himself too old to lie about honour |
| Geography | Two houses, farmhouse above and park below | Two Eggs, identical in contour and dissimilar in everything else |
| Supernatural | Present and never disowned: a ghost at the casement, an opened coffin | Absent; the uncanny is always someone's misperception |
| Second generation | Survives, reconciles, repairs the farmhouse | Appears once, is displayed to guests, and is removed |
| Time span | Roughly thirty years across two generations | One summer with a five-year flashback |
| Ending | Three graves and a quiet moor | A pool, an unattended funeral, and a man alone on a beach |

Read the rows downward and the argument of this article appears without commentary: the top third is almost interchangeable, the middle diverges on reciprocity and revenge, and the bottom third shows two writers using an identical anatomy of obsession to opposite ends.

There is one more axis worth adding informally, because it does not fit a table row. The two novels differ in what they think money is for. Bronte's man treats a fortune as an instrument of power: he uses it to acquire deeds, control marriages, and place himself above the people who once humiliated him, and the acquisition works exactly as intended. Fitzgerald's man treats a fortune as an instrument of display: he uses it to be seen, to be talked about, and to generate the one social event that might bring a particular guest across a lawn. The first strategy succeeds and delivers nothing. The second fails and delivers nothing. Both novels arrive at the same verdict on money by testing opposite theories of it, which is a more rigorous demonstration than either book could manage alone.

## What Each Book Illuminates in the Other

Bronte clarifies Fitzgerald's man by removing the manners. Strip away the pink suit, the courtesy, the "old sport," the flowers sent ahead of the tea party, and what remains is a person who has organised a decade around possessing someone else's wife and who has funded the attempt with crime. The moor novel performs that stripping for us, because it presents the identical structure with none of the charm applied, and a reader who has just finished it will find the Long Island version harder to sentimentalise. The novel's own hardest sentences confirm the reading. Nick tells us the man "took what he could get, ravenously and unscrupulously," which is not a description of a romantic hero, and it sits in the same chapter as the grail language.

Fitzgerald clarifies Bronte by supplying the thing she withholds, which is an origin. Her man arrives from Liverpool as a mystery and departs as one, and the novel gains enormous power from the vacancy. Fitzgerald fills the vacancy and demonstrates what is gained by doing so. The sixth chapter tells us the boy's real name, the age at which he changed it, the yacht that occasioned it, and the fact that he "sprang from his Platonic conception of himself." Knowing all of that makes the American figure sadder rather than smaller, and the comparison lets a reader see that disclosure and mystery are two techniques rather than a right answer and a wrong one.

A third illumination runs through the narrators. Nelly Dean's exasperation and Nick Carraway's caution perform the same office, and reading them together exposes how much of each novel's tone is a property of the teller rather than of the events. Try the thought experiment: narrate the moor story through Nick and it becomes an elegy; narrate the Long Island story through Nelly and it becomes a cautionary tale about a criminal who got what was coming to him. The events do not change. The books do.

## The Witness as Arranger

One further resemblance deserves its own treatment, because it converts a formal observation into an ethical one.

Neither narrator merely watches. Both intervene, and in both books the intervention is what makes the catastrophe possible.

Nelly Dean is not a bystander. She carries messages and declines to carry others. She decides which letters reach which hands. She is present at the deathbed, the elopement, and the forced marriage, and at several of the novel's hinges she chooses silence over disclosure on the grounds that speaking would cause trouble. Criticism has spent decades arguing about how much of the disaster she could have prevented, and the argument exists because Bronte deliberately left the question open. Her housekeeper narrates with the assurance of someone who has settled her own account, which is exactly the tone of a person who has not examined it.

Nick Carraway is even more directly implicated, and the novel is candid about it. He is asked to invite his cousin to tea so that a man she has not seen for five years can walk in unannounced, and he agrees. He buys the flowers, or rather receives a greenhouse full of them. He leaves the two of them alone in his own house and walks out into the rain to stand under a tree. Every subsequent event in the novel, the afternoons at the mansion, the exposure at the Plaza, the drive back in the wrong car, the death in the road, the death in the pool, follows from an introduction he arranged. He tells us at the end that he was thirty and that he had been within and without, simultaneously enchanted and repelled. That formulation is elegant and it is also an evasion, because a man who arranges the meeting is not standing without anything.

The comparison sharpens both figures. Nelly's complicity is easy to miss because her tone is so certain; Nick's is easy to miss because his tone is so rueful. Set them together and the shared design becomes visible: both novelists deliberately chose a narrator whose account is compromised by participation, and both refused to have the narrator acknowledge the compromise fully. That refusal is the source of a great deal of the interpretive energy both books continue to generate.

There is a practical essay point here that is worth taking. Any argument about narration in either novel is stronger if it moves from what the narrator says to what the narrator does. Nelly says she wanted peace in the house; she also withheld a letter. Nick says he disapproved of Gatsby from beginning to end; he also organised the reunion, told nobody about the car, attended the funeral, and wrote the book. Conduct is better evidence than testimony, and both novelists supplied plenty of it.

The final touch is that both narrators end their books with an act of curation. Lockwood chooses to close on the quiet graves rather than on the children's reports of two figures walking the moor, and the choice is his. Nick chooses to close on the sailors and the current rather than on the empty mansion, the unpaid butcher, or the man who telephoned about a fellow named Parke. Both endings are arrangements, and noticing that they are arrangements is the last thing the comparison gives a reader.

## The Two Endings, Compared Line by Line

Endings are where comparative essays are won, and these two are unusually instructive because they arrive at the same conclusion by opposite routes.

Bronte ends in a graveyard on a benign evening. Lockwood, back in the district after an absence, walks past three headstones set into the slope by the moor, watches moths among the heath and harebells, listens to soft wind through the grass, and wonders how anyone could imagine unquiet sleepers in that earth. It is a gentle final image, and it is placed immediately after the reader has been told that local children refuse to walk that way after dark because they claim to see two figures on the moor. Bronte gives the last word to peace and lets the rumour stand behind it unrefuted. The effect is a settlement that the novel declines to guarantee.

Fitzgerald ends on a beach at night with nobody in it. Nick, having sold nothing and buried the only person he admired, sprawls on the sand in front of the abandoned mansion and imagines the island as Dutch sailors first met it, "a fresh, green breast of the new world," noting that "Its vanished trees, the trees that had made way for Gatsby's house," had once flattered the last and greatest of human dreams. The book then makes its two most famous moves in six lines. It shifts into the future tense to describe a receding object and a hope that will always be one year out of reach, and it shifts into the first person plural: "tomorrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms further," and then the boats and the current and the past.

Put the two closing gestures side by side and the difference in scope is immediate. Bronte narrows. She takes a story about two houses and two generations and ends it with one man looking at three graves, and the narrowing is what produces the calm. Fitzgerald widens. He takes a story about one summer on one bay and ends it with three centuries and a continent, and the widening is what produces the ache.

Yet the finding underneath is the same, and this is the payoff of the pairing. Bronte's peaceful graveyard is preceded by eighteen years of ruin that nothing in the final paragraph undoes. Fitzgerald's continental sweep is preceded by an unattended funeral at which the only mourner besides Nick and the father is a man in owl-eyed spectacles who delivers a four-word verdict on the dead. Both novels close by placing an enormous private wreck inside a frame large enough to make it look small, and both refuse to say that the smallness is a comfort.

An essay that reads the two endings together should resist the temptation to declare one superior. The useful observation is that Bronte's ending belongs to a novel that believes in continuity, since somebody is still there to walk past the graves and somebody else is repairing the farmhouse, while Fitzgerald's belongs to a novel that does not, since the house is empty, the guests have stopped coming, and the narrator is leaving for the Midwest in the morning.

## Placing the Two Books in Their Periods

Two sentences of context are usually enough in an essay, but the two sentences should be accurate.

Bronte's novel arrived in December 1847 under a masculine pseudonym, in an England where the Gothic tradition was still active, where regional fiction was gaining ground, and where the reviewing establishment found the book brutal enough that her sister Charlotte felt obliged to write a defensive preface for the posthumous 1850 edition. Its violence was a scandal and its structure was widely thought clumsy, and the reputation it now enjoys was built slowly across the following century.

Fitzgerald's novel arrived in April 1925 into a very different situation: a compressed, experimental American fiction reacting to a war, a boom, and a legal prohibition on alcohol that had converted an ordinary trade into an underworld. It sold modestly, was overshadowed by his earlier work, and reached its present standing only after his death, when wartime paperback distribution put it into hundreds of thousands of hands.

The parallel worth noting, and it is a genuine one, is that both novels were undervalued on arrival for the same reason: each was doing something structurally unusual that its first reviewers read as a defect. Bronte's doubled narration looked like disorder. Fitzgerald's compression and his refusal to let the narrator settle into consistent judgement looked like thinness. In both cases the supposed defect turned out to be the achievement, which is a useful thing for a student to be able to say in a conclusion.

## The Verdict, and the Factor That Decides It

The verdict this article defends: the Gatsby vs Wuthering Heights comparison is legitimate and productive on obsession, class, unexplained money, and framed narration, and it fails on reciprocity, revenge, and mode. The deciding factor is whether the desire is answered.

That criterion is chosen because everything else follows from it. Bronte's love is mutual, and mutual love separated by money produces rage, revenge, and a haunting that lasts eighteen years, because there is a wrong to be avenged and a partner who agreed. Fitzgerald's love is not answered in kind, and unanswered love separated by money produces performance, accumulation, and a green light, because there is nothing to avenge and nobody to avenge it with. Once a reader fixes on reciprocity, the whole divergence between the two second halves becomes predictable rather than mysterious.

The similarity, meanwhile, all traces to a single prior decision that both novelists made independently: to write a man whose desire is his entire content. Once that decision is taken, the class barrier follows, because an ordinary appetite can be redirected and a total one cannot, so the novel needs an obstacle that cannot be argued with. The unexplained fortune follows, because the man must be able to return and must not be socially placeable when he does. The framing narrator follows, because a total passion narrated from inside has no scale. Four resemblances, one decision.

## How to Write the Comparative Essay

Four mistakes recur in student work on this pairing, and each one is a mark-losing habit rather than a knowledge gap.

The first is the gentleness assumption. The essay treats Fitzgerald's man as a romantic and Bronte's as a villain, then finds itself unable to explain why both books end in a pile of bodies. The fix is to separate register from substance in the introduction and to hold the separation throughout. Manner differs; anatomy does not.

The second is losing the class argument. Because both books are so obviously about love, essays drift into discussing feeling and forget that neither obsession is thwarted by feeling. The fix is to make the class line a structural claim rather than a background detail: name the specific mechanism in each book, the marriage chosen for position in one and the uniform that concealed poverty in the other, and return to those mechanisms whenever the argument threatens to become atmospheric.

The third is mode confusion. The essay imports Gothic vocabulary into the American novel and starts describing the green light as spectral or the billboard as supernatural. Fitzgerald's text does not support it. Every uncanny effect in the novel is somebody's misperception, and the novel is explicit about this when George Wilson mistakes an optician's advertisement for God. The fix is to name the mode difference as a finding rather than to let it leak.

The fourth is unequal weighting. On a course centred on the American novel, a fifty fifty split produces two half-essays. Sixty forty, with Bronte used as the instrument that reveals Fitzgerald, is almost always the stronger allocation, and it also solves the plot-summary problem, since a subordinate text needs only enough orientation to make the comparison legible.

A workable thesis might run as follows. Both novels build a man whose desire constitutes his entire identity, place a class barrier in front of that desire, fund his return with money the reader is not permitted to audit, and deliver the passion through a narrator incapable of feeling it; but the identical anatomy produces opposite second halves, because Bronte's love is answered and Fitzgerald's is not, so one novel turns to revenge and the other to performance. That claim is specific, testable, and contains its own counter-argument, which is what examiners reward.

For evidence, the highest-yield Fitzgerald passages are the Louisville courtship in the eighth chapter, the description of the bought luxury of star-shine, the reunion and the shirts in the fifth, the diminished count of enchanted objects, the Plaza confrontation, and the final paragraph. Six passages, closely read, will always beat twelve mentioned in passing. On the Bronte side, the essential material is Catherine's declaration to Nelly, the five weeks at the Grange, the return with the fortune, and the reconciliation of the second generation.

One further tactical note. Because the two novels sit nearly eighty years apart and in different national traditions, examiners will expect some acknowledgement of context. Two sentences are sufficient: one placing Bronte in the 1840s Gothic and regional tradition and one placing Fitzgerald in the compressed modernist realism of the 1920s. Anything longer becomes a history essay wearing a literature essay's clothes.

## Closing Verdict

Obsession that consumes across the class line. That is the shape both books make, and naming it is what the comparison is for.

Emily Bronte found the shape in 1847 and gave it every advantage her mode allowed: mutual love, a ghost, thirty years, two generations, and a revenge campaign conducted with the patience of a man who has nothing else left. F. Scott Fitzgerald found it again in 1925 and stripped it of all of them. No ghost, no reciprocity, no revenge, and no time; one summer, one bay, one light on one dock, and a man who thought a sufficiently large house would repair 1917.

What survives the stripping is the anatomy, and the anatomy is the finding. A desire that becomes an identity cannot be satisfied, because satisfaction would require the desire to end, and the desire is the person. A class line cannot be crossed with new money, because the line is made of age and manner rather than of quantity. And a passion of that size cannot be narrated from inside, because from inside it has no edges.

Two novelists working eighty years apart, in different countries and opposite modes, arrived at the same three conclusions and built the same machine to demonstrate them. That convergence is the reason the pairing keeps appearing on syllabuses, and it is worth more than any list of surface resemblances a comparison could produce.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does The Great Gatsby compare to Wuthering Heights?

The two novels share an anatomy rather than a plot. Each builds a central man whose desire is not one appetite among many but his entire identity; each places a class barrier in front of that desire and makes the barrier the engine of the ruin; each gives the man an absence followed by a return with money whose origin is obscured; and each refuses to narrate the passion from inside, handing the story to a witness who cannot feel it. The differences are equally large. Bronte's love is mutual, spends eighteen years on revenge, and resolves with a second generation. Fitzgerald's is one-sided, contains no revenge at all, and ends with a man alone on a beach.

### Q: How are Gatsby and Heathcliff alike in obsession?

Both men are defined entirely by one desire, which is why neither survives its failure intact. Bronte states the identification directly through Catherine, who describes her feeling as the rock beneath rather than the foliage above, and through the man's own account of bereavement as the loss of a soul. Fitzgerald reaches the same place through logistics rather than metaphysics: a house bought for a sightline, parties thrown on the chance of one guest, five years organised around a single afternoon. When the afternoon arrives, Nick observes that the count of enchanted objects had diminished by one. Different vocabularies, identical emptiness underneath.

### Q: How does class-crossing love destroy in both novels?

Neither book allows love to fail for emotional reasons, which is what makes them social novels rather than romances. Bronte's Catherine chooses a marriage that brings a house, an income, and a position, while insisting she loves elsewhere, and the novel then spends five hundred pages costing that choice. Fitzgerald's young officer courts Daisy under an impression created by a uniform, having no family and no money behind him, and the narration says outright that he had taken her under false pretences. In both, the returning fortune buys quantity and fails to buy the age and manner that admit a man to the class, which is why both returns end badly.

### Q: How do both novels narrate the passion at a remove?

Bronte doubles the distance. A shallow, self-satisfied tenant named Lockwood arrives at a rented property, is snowed in overnight at the neighbouring farmhouse, and afterward asks his housekeeper to explain what he has seen. Nelly Dean, a servant with her own loyalties and her own errors, supplies the account, and Lockwood writes it down. Fitzgerald internalises the same move. Nick Carraway announces a habit of reserving judgement, observes that life is more successfully looked at from a single window, and ends his own romance by declaring himself too old to lie about honour. In both books the passion reaches the reader through someone constitutionally incapable of it, and the incapacity supplies the scale.

### Q: Is Gatsby's love gentler than Heathcliff's?

It is politer, which is not the same thing. Bronte's man conducts a deliberate campaign across eighteen years: he calls in mortgages, marries an heiress in order to mistreat her and secure an inheritance, keeps a boy illiterate as a matter of policy, and uses his dying son as an instrument. Fitzgerald's man does nothing comparable and ruins nobody on purpose. But both desires are total rather than partial, both are funded with criminal money, and both leave corpses on the page. The narration is blunt about the American figure when it says he took what he could get, ravenously and unscrupulously. Manner is a register, not a moral category.

### Q: How do the American and Gothic modes differ?

Bronte writes Gothic romance and never disowns her supernatural machinery: a child's ghost at a casement in the third chapter, a man who claims to be haunted for eighteen years, a request to have a coffin wall removed. The uncanny is presented as possibly real, and the novel refuses to adjudicate. Fitzgerald writes compressed realism in which every uncanny effect turns out to be somebody's misperception. The oculist's billboard is a billboard, and the novel demonstrates this by having a grief-maddened George Wilson mistake it for God, a mistake the surrounding prose treats as pitiable. Importing Gothic vocabulary into the American novel is one of the most common errors in comparative essays on this pairing.

### Q: Do Lockwood and the Long Island bond man serve the same purpose?

They perform the same function with different levels of competence. Both are outsiders who rent property, both are temperamentally cautious about attachment, and both exist so that the central passion can be measured against ordinary feeling. Lockwood is the more comic instrument: he understands almost nothing, retreats from a seaside flirtation before it begins, and mistakes a household of the walking wounded for a picturesque rural curiosity. Nick is sharper and more implicated, since he actively arranges the reunion that destroys everyone. The shared point is structural. Neither novel could survive being narrated by someone who felt what the central man feels, because a total passion described from inside has no edges.

### Q: Which woman is more culpable, Daisy or Catherine Earnshaw?

Both novels resist the question, and an essay that answers it too confidently will look reductive. Catherine chooses her marriage knowingly, states her reasons, and believes she can keep both men, which is a calculation rather than an accident, and the novel holds her responsible for the wreckage that follows. Daisy's culpability is different in kind: she is passive at the Plaza, unable to say the sentence Gatsby needs, and afterward she leaves a man dead in a pool and does not send flowers. Bronte's woman destroys through decision; Fitzgerald's destroys through carelessness, and the novel names carelessness as the defining vice of her class rather than as a personal failing.

### Q: Does either man earn his fortune by means he can name?

Neither, and the two novelists withhold to different degrees on purpose. Bronte discloses nothing at all. Her man leaves as a labourer and returns three years later with the bearing and resources of a gentleman, and no explanation is offered by any narrator or character. Readers have proposed the army, the Americas, and gambling; the text supports none of them. Fitzgerald discloses partially: drugstores selling grain alcohol over the counter, the association with Meyer Wolfsheim, a bond fraud, telephone calls from Chicago and Philadelphia. The partial disclosure is the more sophisticated technique, because it establishes criminality while preserving enough vagueness for the rumour economy to keep working.

### Q: How long does each lover wait, and what does the waiting cost?

Bronte's man is absent three years and then waits eighteen more after Catherine's death, which he spends on a revenge campaign against two families and on the conviction that he is haunted. The cost is two ruined households and a generation of children raised in cruelty. Fitzgerald's man waits five years, spends them assembling a fortune and a house positioned for a sightline, and gets one reunion, one afternoon at the Plaza, and a few weeks of certainty before he is shot. The cost is Myrtle Wilson, George Wilson, and himself. The comparison is instructive because the longer wait produces more damage and the shorter one produces a more concentrated grief.

### Q: Why does the Yorkshire moor function differently from a Long Island bay?

The moor is shared ground and the bay is a barrier. Bronte's landscape belongs to both her lovers equally; they run on it as children, it is where their bond is formed, and its openness is the physical expression of a relationship that predates and outlasts every social arrangement imposed on it. Fitzgerald's water divides. It sits between two peninsulas that the first chapter describes as identical in contour and dissimilar in everything else, it is the distance the green light has to cross, and it is what the whole fortune was assembled to bridge. One landscape unites the lovers against society; the other is the exact measure of what keeps them apart.

### Q: Do the two books punish social climbing in the same way?

Both punish it, and neither punishes it simply. Bronte's man acquires both properties, becomes the landlord of everyone who once despised him, and finds the victory worthless; he stops eating, stops sleeping, and dies in the room he was first brought to as a child. Fitzgerald's man acquires the mansion, the shirts, the hydroplane, and the parties, and the novel arranges for him to die in the pool while the people he wanted to impress are packing to leave. The shared verdict is that acquisition cannot buy admission. The difference is that Bronte lets her climber win the property first, which makes the emptiness of the win her subject.

### Q: What happens to the second generation in each book?

This is the largest structural divergence and the best single point for a strong essay. Bronte devotes her entire second half to the children: a boy kept deliberately illiterate, a sickly son used as a legal instrument, and a daughter forced into a marriage, all of whom are eventually released when the man loses interest in his own campaign. The novel closes with two of them learning to read together and the farmhouse being repaired. Fitzgerald has no second generation at all. Pammy Buchanan is brought in once, displayed to guests, and taken away. There is no repair, no inheritance, and no future, only a beach and a memory of sailors.

### Q: Is revenge a motive on West Egg the way it is at Thrushcross Grange?

No, and confusing the two is the fastest way to misread Fitzgerald. Bronte's second half is a sustained programme of retribution conducted over eighteen years with mortgages, marriages, and children as instruments. Gatsby has no such programme. What he wants is a sentence spoken by one person: that she never loved her husband. He does not want the Buchanans ruined, he does not pursue Tom after the Plaza, and after Myrtle's death he stands outside Daisy's house all night watching over what he himself calls nothing. Readers who describe him as vengeful have imported the Gothic novel's machinery into a book that does not contain it.

### Q: Which passages best pair for a comparative close reading?

Four pairings do most of the work. Set Catherine's declaration to Nelly, in which she distinguishes the foliage from the rock beneath, against the Louisville courtship in the eighth chapter, where the narration states that the young officer had taken Daisy under false pretences. Set the return of the enriched foundling against the description of Gatsby's mansion and the rumour economy of the first party. Set the five weeks at the Grange, which transform a farm girl into a lady, against the passage on the bought luxury of star-shine. And set the reconciliation of the second generation against the final paragraph on the beach, where no future exists at all.

### Q: Does the ghost at the window have any equivalent across the water?

Not a literal one, and this is a real difference rather than a gap to be papered over. Bronte's third chapter delivers a child's ghost at a casement, a hand gripping a sleeper's wrist, and a man afterward flinging the window open and calling into the snow, and the novel never tells the reader that any of it was a dream. Fitzgerald's nearest equivalents are all explicitly perceptual: a figure on a lawn stretching his arms toward a single green light minute and far away, and a pair of enormous painted eyes that a grieving man mistakes for God. The first is a man looking at a dock. The second is an advertisement. Neither is permitted to be supernatural.

### Q: How should a student write a comparative essay on these two books?

Organise around one generating decision rather than a list of resemblances. Open by naming the shared choice both novelists made, which is to build a man whose desire constitutes his whole identity. Take the consequences in order: the class barrier, the unexplained fortune, the framed narration, the two-house geography. Then devote substantial space to the divergences, above all reciprocity and revenge, since an essay that finds only similarity has not compared anything. Weight the allocation toward whichever novel the course centres, usually sixty forty. Close with a verdict that names the deciding factor and defends it in a single sentence.

### Q: What does each ending say about whether obsession can be satisfied?

Both answer no, and they answer it in opposite registers. Bronte's man finally owns everything he wanted, both houses and both estates, and the acquisition renders him indifferent; he stops eating, keeps a window open in the rain, and dies looking at nothing. Possession arrives and turns out to be irrelevant. Fitzgerald's man never possesses at all, but the novel supplies the same finding in miniature at the reunion, when Nick observes that the count of enchanted objects had diminished by one. The green light stops being a symbol the moment it becomes reachable. One book proves the point by granting the wish; the other proves it by granting a single afternoon.

### Q: Are the two title figures outsiders in the same sense?

They are outsiders by different mechanisms, which produces one of the sharper contrasts in the pairing. Bronte's man is othered from the moment he arrives: brought from Liverpool as a child of unknown parentage, described in terms that mark him as visibly different from the family that takes him in, and degraded to servant status by his foster brother. His exclusion is inherited and permanent. Fitzgerald's man is a native-born American farm boy who excludes himself by choice, changing his name at seventeen and inventing a biography complete with Oxford and a war record. One outsider cannot escape his origin; the other discards his and is destroyed by the discarding.
