Gnomeo and Juliet performs the boldest single edit in the play's four centuries on screen and stage: it deletes the ending. Kelly Asbury's animated film of 2011 moves the feud from Verona to two adjoining gardens in Stratford-upon-Avon, staffs the Montagues and Capulets with painted ceramic gnomes, scores the quarrel with the Elton John catalogue, and then, at the point where the tragedy demands two bodies in a tomb, lets its lovers climb out of the rubble and marry. The critical reflex is to call that a betrayal, a tragedy with its tragedy removed. This article argues the opposite verdict. The film is a structural translation, not a vandalism: it keeps the play's mechanism entire, the groundless feud, the love across the line, the escalation through injury and mistaken death, the reconciliation purchased by loss, and changes only the currency in which the loss is paid. Where the play spends lives, the film spends things, a mower, a hat, a wisteria, a garden, things a gnome can lose and a child can watch being lost. The one scene that settles the argument is the film's own invention, in which Gnomeo climbs to a statue of William Shakespeare, hears the original ending recited by its author, and rejects it to his face. A film that stages its disagreement with the playwright, and wins the argument on screen, has earned its happy ending rather than stolen it.

## The Verdict in Brief: What This Children's Version Keeps and What It Refuses

The film keeps the skeleton. Two households, red-hatted Capulet gnomes under Lord Redbrick and blue-hatted Montague gnomes under Lady Bluebury, inherit a feud whose origin nobody states, exactly as the play's Prologue announces "two households, both alike in dignity" breaking "from ancient grudge" to "new mutiny" without ever naming the grudge's cause (Prologue, lines 1 to 4, in the Oxford Shakespeare edition). The lovers meet across the line, marry their fortunes in secret, and are separated by an escalation neither controls. A kinsman's fall drives the hero into exile and apparent death; the heroine is immobilised by her father; a machine of revenge built by a friend brings both gardens to ruin; the elders reconcile over what they believe is the lovers' destruction. Every load-bearing joint of the plot is present. What the film refuses is the play's bookkeeping of death: nobody stays dead, the single fatality is glued back together, and the reconciliation that the play buys with corpses is bought instead with the wreckage of the two gardens themselves.

That refusal was the production's founding problem, stated by its director. Asbury joined the project in 2006 and, with producer Steve Hamilton Shaw, rewrote the screenplay from scratch; he later described the central challenge as finding how to differentiate the ending from the play's in order, in his documented phrase, "to keep daggers and poison and suicide out." The sentence deserves attention because it names the method. Daggers, poison and suicide are the tragedy's instruments, not its meaning; the film's whole design follows from separating the two, and from asking what instruments a garden can supply instead. A lawnmower replaces the sword, glue replaces the potion, and a shattered teapot replaces the corpse. The sections that follow take the substitutions one at a time, and the verdict accumulates: the substitutions are systematic, they preserve function while changing substance, and the few places where the film merely decorates, chiefly its gag density and its song montages, are faults of texture rather than of structure. One measure of the film's nerve is its certificate. The play it adapts contains a double suicide, three further killings and a heroine of thirteen; the film carries a G rating in the United States, suitable for the youngest audience a cinema admits, and a U in Britain. No earlier mainstream screen version had attempted to hold the story at that age level, and the attempt is the article's subject: not whether the tragedy can be made safe for children, since safety is the entry fee, but whether anything recognisable survives the making.

For its primary audience, though, the film is not an adaptation at all, and the verdict should record the consequence plainly. A child of six does not experience Gnomeo and Juliet as a version of a prior text; the gnomes are simply the story, and the play, when it arrives years later in a classroom, will be experienced as the strange, fatal expansion of a tale already owned. The film understands its own priority and builds the understanding in: the opening Chorus jokes presume an adult who knows the story, while every structural choice flatters a viewer for whom the story is new, which is why the statue scene matters beyond its wit. That scene is the footnote the film appends to itself for its youngest viewers, the disclosure, made inside the fiction, that another ending exists elsewhere and was declined on their behalf. Adaptation studies usually treats the copy as the dependent term and the source as the fixed one; in the only audience that cannot read the source, the dependency runs the other way, and the film's happy ending is the original from which the tragedy will later deviate. Judged as that original, the film is a complete, argued and good-humoured account of how feuds end when the people inside them refuse the script, and judged as an adaptation, it is something rarer: a copy that cross-examines its model, in public, and is acquitted.

## The Children's Shakespeare Tradition: What the Lambs Kept and the Gnomes Changed

Children have been given this playwright for two centuries, and the terms of the gift were set in 1807, when Charles and Mary Lamb published their Tales from Shakespeare, prose retellings addressed to young readers whose sisters, in the preface's famous arrangement, might read them the stories before the boys were allowed the plays themselves. The Lambs' Romeo and Juliet is the control case for every later children's version. The Lambs softened the language into kindness, compressed the bawdy, thinned the subplots, and at the end they killed the lovers without hesitation: their Juliet wakes in the vault, their Romeo lies poisoned beside her, and the story closes over the bodies exactly where the play closes. The tradition's founding assumption was that a child could be trusted with the ending provided the sentences behaved. Two hundred years of picture books, school editions and stage abridgements for the young broadly kept that settlement, adjusting vocabulary and length while treating the tomb as untouchable.

The same year, 1807, produced the settlement's rival: Thomas Bowdler's Family Shakespeare began the long career of the verb that bears his name, cutting whatever a parent might blush to read aloud while advertising the result as the play improved. Bowdlerism and the Lambs between them defined the children's Shakespeare question as a question about content: which words, which scenes, which jokes may pass? Gnomeo and Juliet is the first widely seen version to relocate the question from content to outcome. Its language is clean by any standard, cleaner than the Lambs dared, yet its heresy is far larger than any Bowdler ever attempted, because it changes not what the audience hears but what the story does. And where Bowdler concealed his edits behind a claim of improvement, the gnome film exhibits its edit in open court, sending its hero to consult the author about the ending it intends to refuse. The tradition's arc therefore runs from protecting children from the play's words, through trusting children with the play's deaths, to a film that protects children from the deaths by arguing with the playwright in front of them.

The age arithmetic sharpens the point. The play's Juliet is thirteen, a fact the text states with unusual precision, and the play is conventionally taught to readers of fourteen and upward, an audience just older than its heroine. The gnome film addresses an audience younger than its heroine, children of five, six and seven, for whom Juliet's predicament must be translated out of marriage, sexuality and suicide altogether. What survives that translation is instructive about the play itself: strip away everything that belongs to adolescence and death, and a complete story remains standing, feud, love, escalation, exile, false report, catastrophe, reconciliation. The film is in that sense an experiment the scholarship never ran, a determination of the tragedy's minimum viable form, and its box office is the experiment's result: the minimum viable form found the largest child audience any version of the play had ever reached.

## From Verona to the Garden Fence: The World the Film Builds

The film opens by announcing its method. A lone gnome climbs onto a stage to deliver the Prologue, begins the paraphrase, complains at the dullness of the exercise, and is dropped through a trapdoor before he can finish. The joke is the thesis in miniature: the Chorus is honoured, quoted, and then dispensed with, because the audience has been promised a story it already knows and does not need read aloud. The story is then set not in Verona but in Shakespeare's own town, Stratford-upon-Avon, where two elderly human neighbours, Miss Montague and Mr. Capulet, despise each other across a fence. The humans are on screen for seconds. Their feud is the weather of the film, origin unexplained and motive unstated, and when they leave for the day their garden ornaments wake and continue the quarrel by other means: red hats against blue hats, the Montague blues of the house numbered 2B and the Capulet reds of the house beside it, numbered with a slash through the same characters, Not 2B. The address gag is the film in one image: the whole of the play's metaphysics of fortune reduced to a house number and a negation.

Beneath the humans, the gnome world is a complete feudal order in miniature. Lord Redbrick, voiced by Michael Caine, rules the reds as a widower and an anxious father; Lady Bluebury, voiced by Maggie Smith, rules the blues as a widow; each house has therefore lost the parent the play pairs against them, and the film's Verona is a town of single parents guarding ornaments. The supporting order maps the play's cast with economy. Benny, voiced by Matt Lucas, fuses Benvolio and Mercutio into a single best friend, peacemaker and firebrand in one body. Nanette, a plastic pond frog voiced by Ashley Jensen, takes the Nurse's office of confidante and go-between. Paris survives as a harmless garden bore voiced by Stephen Merchant. Tybalt, voiced by Jason Statham, keeps his name, his temper and his function. Even the servants persist, miniaturised: the director himself voices the Red Goon Gnomes, the film's Gregory and Sampson. What is missing is as deliberate as what is kept. There is no Prince Escalus, no civic authority above the gardens except the absent humans, and there is no Rosaline: Gnomeo's heart has no first tenant, so the film's love story is also its hero's first, which purifies the plot at the cost of the play's portrait of a lover in love with love before he is in love with Juliet.

The census repays a slower count, because the margins of the gnome world carry the film's jokes about scale. Tybalt's best friend is Fawn, a miniature deer ornament voiced by Ozzy Osbourne, a statue's statue, decoration at one remove from life. The gardens keep pets and portents: Gnomeo is attended by Shroom, a small blue mushroom who functions as messenger and confidant, the film's Balthasar reduced to a fungus with perfect loyalty. Two red gnomes arrive conjoined at the base, a sight gag about manufacture that also states the film's quiet premise, that these citizens were poured and painted into their factions before they could choose anything. Casting itself followed the premise. Asbury conducted the voice auditions blind, listening to readings without knowing which actor was speaking until the voice fitted the figure, a method that produced the film's strangest and most effective piece of miscasting-as-insight: Jason Statham, the screen's specialist in controlled menace, compressed into a garden bully whose entire empire is an alley and a lawnmower. Before McAvoy and Blunt were cast, the leads had been promised to Ewan McGregor and Kate Winslet, a pairing that would have made the film a romance between film stars; the voices chosen instead make it a romance between neighbours, which is the scale the story needs.

The setting completes the design. Stratford-upon-Avon is not Verona with the names filed off; it is the author's own ground, the town where the playwright was born, buried and statued, so the film's Verona is a heritage site before it is a neighbourhood. The gardens are its suburbs, the feud a dispute between two semis, and the whole arrangement flatters the adult audience with a literary postcode while telling the child audience nothing it needs explained. A garden in the town of Shakespeare is simply where gnomes would live.

The film's first five minutes perform its whole method in miniature, and they repay the same attention critics give the play's first scene. A lone gnome climbs onto a bare stage, clears his throat and begins the contract: the story about to be seen has been told before. The trapdoor takes him mid-sentence. The film then opens its world in a single unbroken survey, the two houses on Verona Drive, the colour-coded mailboxes, the owners departing in a fury of near-collisions, the weather vanes signalling the all-clear, and the gardens erupting into life. The feud's first hostile act of the film follows within moments, and it is a masterpiece of scaling: a blue gnome flings a snail across the fence into the open mouth of a snoozing red. Shakespeare's first scene opens with servants biting thumbs and proceeds within pages to drawn swords and a princely death sentence; the film opens with a mollusc delivered orally to a sleeping enemy, an insult that is also a pratfall, and proceeds to a lawnmower race. The descent in lethality is the thesis, announced before a single line of plot has been spoken: everything in this film will be the play with the voltage lowered and the structure intact, aggression preserved as choreography. Even the weather vanes work the theme. Verona's citizens take their cues from princes; the gnomes take theirs from roof ornaments, the highest authorities their world contains, and the film's hierarchy of statues, ornaments all the way up, prepares the audience for the moment its hero consults the tallest statue of all.

## A Property in Limbo: From Boardroom to Back Garden

The film's own biography rehearses its theme, a valuable thing passed between feuding institutions while it waited for someone to treat it as alive. The idea arrived from the writers Rob Sprackling and John Smith, whose pitch, a Romeo and Juliet populated by the warring ornaments of two English gardens, landed first at Disney's feature animation division, which declined it, and then at Miramax, where the studio chief Dick Cook saw the family property his label lacked and greenlit the film in 2006. Kelly Asbury, who had co-directed Shrek 2, came aboard the same year and has described the screenplay he inherited as material he and Steve Hamilton Shaw effectively rebuilt from the ground up; the finished film's seven screenplay credits and six story credits record the sediment of those years, committee authorship of exactly the kind the statue scene pretends to resolve in a single voice. The corporate ground then shifted in the manner of the gardens. Disney sold Miramax in 2010 while retaining the gnome film, and released it in February 2011 through Touchstone Pictures, its label for grown-up fare, giving the picture two statistical distinctions that sum up its strangeness: the first animated release under the Touchstone name since Tim Burton's The Nightmare Before Christmas in 1993, and the first and only G-rated film in that label's history. A children's film issued by the adult imprint, made by a studio that had passed on it, for a label that had been sold from under it: the production's orphans-and-adoptions history is the closest thing the film industry offers to the play's own transmission, a text surviving by being useful to successive owners.

The making itself, by the Toronto studio Starz Animation, turned the budget into an aesthetic. Thirty-six million dollars is a small purse for a computer-animated feature, and the film spends it where it shows: on surfaces. Its gnomes are glazed, chipped, weather-stained ceramic, rendered with the highlights and imperfections of fired clay, and their painted faces carry the slight crudity of the mass-produced originals, a texture more expensive to fake than photoreal fur and far more eloquent, since the audience can feel the object's cheapness and fragility in every close-up. The world is correspondingly small: two gardens, an alley, a greenhouse, a park, a derelict plot, a street. A Verona of six locations is a producer's economy and a dramatist's discipline at once, and it enforces the play's own chamber scale, in which everything that matters happens within earshot of a wall. The gnomes' rigidity, the animator's usual enemy, is here the subject: these are beings whose bodies are their constraint, and the film's best physical comedy is built from the negotiation between a living will and a ceramic chassis, lovers who cannot bend, fighters who cannot bleed, a heroine who can be glued to her own display stand by a father who treats her, in the film's central visual rhyme, as the ornament she is.

### How does the film's opening treat the Prologue it inherits?

The opening honours the Prologue by staging it, then ejecting its speaker through a trapdoor. The gesture tells the audience two things at once: the story that follows is the famous one, and the film will not be lecturing anybody. The Chorus survives as a joke about choruses, respect disguised as impatience.

The production history reinforces the design. The project began as an original idea by screenwriters Rob Sprackling and John Smith, sold to Disney through Rocket Pictures, the company of Elton John and David Furnish. Disney's chairman Dick Cook greenlit the film at Miramax in 2006 after the studio's own animation arm had passed on it; when Miramax was later sold, Disney kept the film, sat on it because it came from outside its animation units, and finally released it through the Touchstone Pictures banner. The finished credits list seven screenplay writers and six story writers, a crowd that usually spells trouble, and yet the film's structure is unusually clean, because the rewrite by Asbury and Hamilton Shaw subordinated everything to one question: how does each element of the tragedy behave when its materials are ceramic, plastic and grass?

## The Feud Without a Cause: Hatred Sorted by Hat Colour

The play never explains its feud, and generations of readers have treated that silence as the point: the hatred is ancestral, mechanical, a machine that runs because it has always run. The analysis at [the feud between the houses and what actually drives it](/2022/04/01/capulet-montague-feud-explained/) sets out how the text withholds a cause so that the feud can stand for every inherited quarrel. The film radicalises the withholding. Its gnomes hate by hat colour, a difference painted on at the factory, arbitrary, visible at fifty paces, and meaningless in every other respect, since a gnome's colour is assigned by manufacture rather than by birth. The satire is precise and quietly serious: the film gives its child audience the feud as pure form, allegiance without content, and trusts even a young viewer to see that two groups who are identical except for a coat of paint have nothing to fight about. Where the play's blank origin invites the audience to supply causes, the film's blank origin instructs the audience to notice that no cause is required.

### Why does the film sort its gnomes by the colour of their hats?

The colours make the feud visible to a child at a glance and expose it as arbitrary at once: red and blue are painted on, assigned at the factory, and change nothing about the gnome underneath. The line between the houses is a coat of paint, and the quarrel is inherited, never argued.

The escalation ladder is built from property. The feud's first theatre is an alley lawnmower race, in which Tybalt wrecks Gnomeo's machine; the blues retaliate by night sabotage; the reds answer by destroying the blues' prized wisteria; Gnomeo prepares a counterstroke against the reds' tulips with weedkiller and is caught in the act by Juliet, which produces the lovers' first quarrel and their first honesty. Every rung of the ladder is an injury to a possession rather than a person, so a child can follow the arithmetic of revenge without meeting a body. Yet the arithmetic is the play's own. The Prince's warning in the play prices street violence in lives: "If ever you disturb our streets again, / Your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace" (1.1.93-94). The film has no Prince to issue the warning, so the forfeit is collected by events instead: each act of sabotage raises the price of the next, until the price is the gardens themselves. The structure teaches what the Prince's speech states.

The gardens also run an economy of honour, and the feud's battles are fought over its currency: plants. Each house keeps a prize specimen whose condition is the family's public credit, the blues' wisteria, the reds' tulips, and the film's sabotage war is a campaign against those assets, horticultural heraldry attacked in the dark. The arrangement is shrewder than it looks. Honour in the play is an abstraction that men kill to defend and cannot define; honour in the film is a flower bed, visible, tended, and capable of being murdered in a night with weedkiller, which is why a child can grasp both the stake and the sacrilege when the wisteria is destroyed. The one plant that belongs to neither house is the Cupid's Arrow orchid in the derelict garden between the territories, the feud's only piece of unclaimed value, and the plot quite properly sends both lovers to compete for it. The gardens' geography is the play's politics drawn to scale: two cultivated orders, a wilderness between them where the law of the houses lapses, and in the wilderness the only honest conversations in the film.

The elders who preside over this economy are drawn with a sympathy the play does not extend to its own. Lord Redbrick is a widower raising his daughter alone; Lady Bluebury is a widow; and the film quietly understands the feud as the surviving structure of two bereaved households, the ritual that fills the houses' empty rooms. Redbrick's tyranny over Juliet, the promoted match, the watchfulness, the glue, is a widower's terror of a second loss wearing the feud's uniform, and Lady Bluebury's pugnacity has the same root: each garden is a grief with a fence around it. This is why the reconciliation, when it comes, does not require the elders to be argued out of an ideology. They have no ideology. They have a habit that has kept them company, and the catastrophe gives them a stronger feeling to organise around, the discovery that the habit has nearly killed the only family each has left. The play's Capulet and Montague clasp hands over bodies; the film's elders clasp hands over a shared mistake, which is the same gesture performed one scene earlier, in time.

The feud is also, crucially, public, and the film takes care to give it the audience the play's brawls always have. Verona's street fights are civic events, watched by citizens, reported to the Prince, priced by the whole city's peace; the gardens reproduce the arrangement exactly. The alley between the houses is the street, neutral ground where the races run before both populations; the sabotage raids are matters of instant general knowledge; and when the Terrafirminator detonates, the noise draws every inhabitant of both gardens to witness the consequence together, the full citizenry assembled over the rubble at the moment of reconciliation. The film needs that crowd for its ending to work as politics rather than as sentiment. A private apology between two elders would settle a quarrel; a reconciliation performed before the assembled houses, over the believed bodies of the heirs, settles a feud, because the witnesses are the feud's real substance, the neighbours whose participation kept it warm. The play knows this, which is why its final scene packs the tomb with the Prince, the parents, the watch and Paris's page, a whole city crowded into a vault to hear the story told whole. The film crowds its gardens the same way, in daylight, with the story's principals alive to hear it too.

## A Lawnmower for a Sword: Violence Translated into Property

The translation of violence is the film's most disciplined piece of work. Shakespeare's Verona runs on blades: the opening brawl, the duel that kills Mercutio, the duel that kills Tybalt, the duel that kills Paris. A children's film rated G, the first and only G rating in Touchstone's history, cannot run on blades, so the film runs on collisions. The street brawl becomes a lawnmower race down an alley, choreographed with the full grammar of a joust, heralded by a race announcer voiced by Dolly Parton and scored to "Saturday Night's Alright for Fighting," so that the audience hears the fight the picture cannot show. Sabotage replaces ambush. The film's one true killing is displaced twice over: Tybalt, racing his mower in the confrontation after he has shattered Benny's hat, runs into a ditch, is thrown against a wall, and breaks. The death is real within the film's physics, since a shattered gnome is a dead gnome, and the film lets the consequence land: grief, blame and a revenge plot follow, exactly as the play's consequences follow from Tybalt's fall in the street.

Benny's hat is the subtlest substitution of all. In the play, Mercutio's wound is the hinge of the tragedy, the injury that turns comedy toward death and draws his curse: "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1). The film gives the Mercutio half of Benny that hinge as a maiming of his silhouette instead of his body: Tybalt smashes the point of Benny's hat, a visible, permanent-looking damage that a child reads instantly as injury, and Benny's thirst for revenge afterward is Mercutio's curse translated into motive. When Benny later buys the weapon of the climax online, through the human owner's computer, the film completes the translation: in a world without swords, escalation is a purchase, and the arms race arrives by courier with a sales pitch voiced by Hulk Hogan.

The middle of the film is a sabotage war conducted with a quartermaster's logic, and its set pieces deserve notice because they show the translation working at scene level rather than merely at plot level. The blues' night raid on the red garden, intended to disable Tybalt's mower, collapses into farce when Benny, assigned to lookout duty, paints a wishing well instead and triggers the security lights, so that the raid's only casualty is the raiders' dignity and its only product is the lovers' meeting in the flight that follows. In the play, the equivalent hinge is a death: Romeo's banishment follows a killing. In the film, the hinge is an embarrassment that throws the hero into the neutral garden and into Juliet's path, and the machinery is characteristic, since the film repeatedly manufactures its turns from property, paint and machinery rather than from blood. The mower itself is the film's class object, each house fielding a champion machine whose honour is the rider's, maintained, sabotaged and raced like a destrier. When Tybalt cheats the race by wrecking Gnomeo's mower, the offence registers in this economy as a wound, and the audience has been taught to feel it as one before any gnome is harmed at all.

Two pieces of the gardens' machinery complete the translation of violence. The first is light. The human houses are fitted with security lights that flood the gardens when movement trips them, and the gnomes conduct their war in the knowledge that illumination is arrest: to be caught in the light is to be frozen mid-gesture before the eyes of the only power in the film that cannot be charmed, fought or outrun. The Prince's watch has become a sensor. Where Shakespeare's Verona polices its streets with a prince who arrives after the bloodshed to pronounce sentence, the film's gardens police themselves with a device that prevents the deed by making concealment impossible, and the sabotage war's comedy of alarms and near-discoveries is the sound of that system working. The second machine is animal. A bulldog patrols the alley between the gardens, and to the gnomes it is not a pet but a climate, a predator whose appearances reorganise every plan the way weather reorganises a battle. The play's violence is human and answerable: men choose to draw. The film's dangers increasingly are not: lights, dogs, traffic, gravity and machinery, a world whose threats are environmental rather than moral, which is precisely the moral physics of childhood as children experience it. The feud's folly, translated into this physics, reads exactly as the film intends: the only enemies the gnomes choose are the neighbours; everything else that can kill them is merely the world, and it is quite dangerous enough without help.

## The Pond and the Orchid: A Meeting Rebuilt, a Juliet Rebuilt

The play's lovers meet at a masked ball and exchange a sonnet; the film's meet in disguise in a derelict garden, in pursuit of the same prize. Juliet has climbed out at night to seize a rare flower, the Cupid's Arrow orchid, blooming in the abandoned greenhouse between the territories, and Gnomeo blunders into the same errand. Their courtship begins as competition and turns, in a fall into the pond, into recognition: the water washes away their disguises and their colours, and the scene restates the play's most famous question in visual grammar. "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet" (2.2) argues that identity survives relabelling; the pond argues that identity survives the loss of the label itself, since each lover has already chosen the other before the paint washes off. The orchid does the balcony's work too. Where the play raises Juliet above Romeo in architecture, the film raises her in competence: she is the one who scales the greenhouse, and the film's Juliet, voiced by Emily Blunt, is written as the more capable of the pair, an adventurer under guard rather than a daughter at a window.

That rewriting of Juliet is the film's second argument. The play's Juliet is thirteen, managed by a nurse, bargained to Paris, and driven to a friar's desperate pharmacology; her agency is real but exercised in secret and at mortal risk. The film keeps the management and drops the helplessness. Lord Redbrick promotes the match with Paris openly; Juliet deflects the suitor onto Nanette with a tactician's ease; her secret is not a marriage but a friendship with the enemy that she conducts on her own expeditions. When her father finally immobilises her, he does it with glue, fastening her feet to her pedestal, the film's most quoted image and its most considered one. The glue replaces the potion point for point: a father-imposed stillness that mimics death, removes the heroine from the action at the crisis, and must be broken before the ending can move. Shakespeare's Juliet simulates death to escape a marriage; the film's Juliet is fixed in place to prevent an escape, and the inversion preserves the function while reversing the agent, a change that tells a child audience the truth about the original scene, that it was always about a girl being stopped.

### What does Juliet's glued pedestal replace in the play's design?

The glue replaces the Friar's potion as the device that takes Juliet out of the plot at the crisis: she is rendered still, silent and unreachable while others decide the ending around her. The film reverses the agent, since the play's Juliet chooses her stillness and the film's has it imposed by her father, yet keeps the function intact.

The secret meetings themselves are held in the abandoned garden, neutral ground, chaperoned by the film's largest act of invention, to which the next section turns. Their courtship is brief, musical and interrupted; the film grants the lovers no wedding in secret, reserving marriage for the public ending, because a secret wedding is a plot engine a children's film does not need once it has a simpler one: in this version, everyone finds out.

Two further features of the meeting repay attention. The first is its structure. The play's lovers exchange a perfect shared sonnet at their meeting, fourteen lines passed between strangers like a dance step learned in advance, and the scene's argument is that form itself ratifies them: two voices completing one poem are already married in the only register the play finally trusts. The film cannot quote the sonnet, so it rebuilds the exchange as action: a competition for the orchid conducted in matched feats of climbing and daring, each lover answering the other's move, until the contest tips them into the pond and the rivalry dissolves in the same instant as the disguises. The grammar is the sonnet's grammar, call and response resolving into unison, translated into the only verse form an animated film commands, choreography. The second feature is Juliet's presentation before the meeting. The film first shows her displayed on her pedestal in her father's garden, an ornament among ornaments, positioned and admired, which is the visual truth about the play's Juliet as her world intends her: a marriageable asset on show. Her night expedition to the greenhouse is thus not a charming extra but the character's founding act, the moment the asset removes herself from display and goes to steal something for her own reasons. The play gives Juliet that self-removal in language, in the balcony's soliloquy spoken to an empty garden; the film, having no soliloquies, gives it to her in conduct, and the conduct is legible to a viewer of six.

The pedestal deserves a final comparison, because it is the film's anti-balcony and the two structures measure the two heroines. The play's balcony is an instrument of speech: elevated, private, architecturally useless except as a place from which a girl may think aloud at night to a garden she believes empty, and the scene built on it gives Juliet more consecutive self-expression than any other figure in the play's first half. The film's pedestal is an instrument of display: elevated, public, and designed for a girl to be looked at, and Juliet's story is the progressive refusal of it, first by climbing down, then by being glued to it in punishment for climbing down, finally by descending from the ruins of it on her own terms to be married in the open. A balcony gives its heroine a voice and a pedestal gives its heroine a silhouette; the film's Juliet spends eighty-three minutes converting the second into the first, which is the play's Juliet's journey performed without a single line of the play's poetry. Beside her, the film runs Nanette as the confidante she actually uses. The play's Juliet deploys the Nurse as messenger, alibi and finally broken instrument; the film's Juliet deploys Nanette once, decisively, feeding her to Paris as a substitute bride with the briskness of a general feeding a decoy to a battery, and the joke carries a real judgement: this Juliet wastes no scenes, keeps her counsel in a pond frog, and solves the Paris problem in an afternoon that costs the play's Juliet three acts of evasion.

## Featherstone in the Friar's Place: The Flamingo Who Smuggled Loss Back In

Every substitution in the film has a function except one, and that one has a wound instead. The Friar Laurence of this Verona is Featherstone, a plastic pink flamingo voiced by Jim Cummings, named in compliment to Donald Featherstone, the sculptor who created the plastic flamingo lawn ornament in 1957. The Friar's cell, his garden of herbs, his learning and his plot machinery are all stripped away. Featherstone owns no potion, arranges no marriage, sends no letter and fails at nothing, because the film has deleted the entire apparatus of adult mediation through which the play's disaster flows. What he carries instead is the one genuinely adult experience the film permits itself: he was locked in a shed for twenty years after the human couple who owned his garden divorced and divided their ornaments between two households, taking his mate away and leaving him behind a closed door. His counsel to the lovers is not strategy but testimony. He encourages the match because he is the only figure in either garden who knows what separation costs.

The placement is exact. In the play, the Friar warns the lovers on their wedding morning that "these violent delights have violent ends" (2.6.9), a prophecy the plot proceeds to honour. Featherstone delivers the anti-prophecy from the same structural chair: delight is not the danger; division is. His story arrives at the lovers' first quarrel, when Gnomeo has been caught preparing to poison the red tulips and Juliet has caught him at it, and it reconciles them by exhibit rather than by argument. The flamingo's twenty years in the shed are the film's tomb scene, displaced to the middle of the film and survived: a figure who was buried by other people's arrangements and came out of the dark with his affections intact. When critics charge the film with evacuating the tragedy's feeling, Featherstone is the standing counterexample, the point at which the children's version admits irreversible loss through the one door its rating leaves open, the backstory of a minor character, where the loss can be total without being fatal to anybody the plot still needs.

### How does Featherstone's twenty years in the shed work on the lovers?

The story works as testimony rather than advice: Featherstone does not warn the lovers about their conduct but shows them the end state of a divided garden, twenty years alone behind a shed door after a divorce split him from his mate. The lovers reconcile because the film previews its feared fate in one minor character: separation that lasts.

Nanette completes the pattern. The Nurse in the play is Juliet's conduit to the world, bawdy, warm and finally treacherous in counsel when she advises the Paris marriage. The film's frog is warmer and simpler, and her great deviation is the film's neatest solution to the Paris problem: the unwanted bridegroom is not killed in a tomb but redirected to the confidante, falling for Nanette while Juliet looks on with relief. The play must park Paris's body outside the Capulet vault; the film parks his heart in a happier match, and the audience is asked to notice that a comedy can dispose of a rival by pleasing him, where a tragedy can dispose of him only by killing him. Paris, in the film, is the one character whose fate is kinder than his original's in every particular, and he never knows it.

The finale pays Featherstone's story off with an exactness that confirms its structural place. When the gardens merge and the feuding households become one company, the flamingo's lost mate is restored to him, the film's last reconciliation reserved not for the elders or the lovers but for the minor character whose loss financed the audience's understanding of what division costs. The colour design underlines the promotion. Featherstone is pink, a colour that belongs to neither faction, red diluted toward white, and he stands in the gardens' palette as the first hint of the film's closing argument, completed when the lovers depart on the purple mower: the feud's colours are not exhaustive, and the characters who matter most to the ending are the ones the binary cannot file. Nanette benefits from the same dispensation. The play's Nurse ends in diminishment, her counsel rejected and her mistress dead; the film's frog ends with a suitor of her own, promoted from go-between to bride, as if the film were distributing happy endings downward through the cast list in deliberate correction of a source that distributes graves.

The substitution has a textual warrant that the film never advertises. The play's Friar is introduced, in his first scene, as a gardener: Laurence enters at dawn with a basket, gathering herbs, and delivers the play's most famous piece of natural philosophy over his plants, "For naught so vile that on the earth doth live / But to the earth some special good doth give" (2.3), the doctrine that every growing thing carries a virtue if it is rightly used, poison and medicine in the same root. Featherstone is that speech with the theology removed and the gardening kept: a creature of the garden preaching that what grows together belongs together, his evidence not scripture but his own biography, his sacrament not a potion but a story told in a greenhouse at night. The film's deepest piece of fidelity may be this one, that it understood the Friar's scene is a garden scene, and rebuilt its moral counsellor accordingly, replacing the man who gathers plants with the ornament who lives among them.

One further displacement completes the flamingo's design, and it is the film's subtlest. The tomb experience, the play's defining horror, belongs in this version not to the heroine but to her counsellor. Featherstone has lived it: entombed alive in the shed when the household broke, sealed in the dark with the garden growing wild outside the door, for twenty years. Juliet's forty-two hours in the Capel vault are the play's measure of extremity; the film gives its Friar-figure two decades of the same condition, survived. That is why his counsel persuades where a mere optimist's would not, and why the lovers believe a plastic bird: he is the only character in the film who has already lived through the ending the statue predicts, separation perfected and made permanent, and come out of the shed still preaching gardens. The play's Friar compounds a potion to simulate the tomb and the simulation kills; the film's Friar-substitute has undergone the reality and it has made him gentle. Between those two preparations for the office, the adaptation's whole moral preference is stated: experience over scheme, testimony over plot.

## The Statue's Verdict: Shakespeare States the Ending and Loses the Argument

The film's centre, and the reason it belongs in a series on this play at all, comes after Gnomeo's apparent death. Pushed into the road during the red counterattack, he is believed shattered; what broke on the tarmac was a blue teapot from the truck that nearly killed him, and the gardens mourn over kitchenware. Gnomeo, stranded across town, pours out his story in a public park to the one listener who recognises it: a statue of William Shakespeare, voiced by Patrick Stewart with the gravity of a man reading a verdict he has delivered before. The statue knows this plot. He tells the hero how it ends: the lovers die. Gnomeo protests that the ending is horrible. The author, unbothered, stands by his text, and the scene closes with the playwright dropping his petitioner, who is caught and carried back toward the crisis by Featherstone.

Nothing else in the children's Shakespeare tradition attempts this. Adaptations for the young normally handle the ending by silence, cutting away before the tomb; by soft focus, treating the deaths as sleep; or by substitution, grafting on a rescue the text never contemplated. This film puts the substitution on trial in the presence of the author and lets the defendant argue. The statue's case is the tragedy's own logic, that a story built from this feud, this secrecy and this haste can end in only one room. Gnomeo's rebuttal is not an argument but a refusal: the ending is horrible, therefore it will not be his. The film then spends its climax proving the rebuttal performable, constructing a catastrophe large enough to do the tomb's narrative work, the reconciliation of the houses over the lovers' supposed bodies, while quietly ensuring the bodies are pottery-free and breathing. The gag that seals the scene is the statue's reappearance at the moment of the explosion, surveying the wreckage with the satisfaction of a critic whose prediction has come in: "Told him so." He is wrong within the minute, since the lovers rise from the rubble, and the film's final word on authorship is a playwright corrected by his own characters on his own plinth.

Set against the text, the scene is a precise inversion of the play's most famous act of defiance. Romeo in Mantua, hearing of Juliet's death, cries "I defy you, stars!" (5.1.24), an assertion of will against a heaven that has already finished deciding. Gnomeo defies not the stars but the author, and unlike Romeo he wins, because the film's cosmology has an author who can be argued with and a heaven made of gardeners. The defiance is the point at which the adaptation stops adapting and starts answering, and it converts the film's softening from an evasion into a position: the tragic ending is presented, weighed, and declined in open court, with the playwright himself delivering the prosecution's speech. A child who has watched the statue scene has been taught the one thing bowdlerised versions usually conceal, that the original ending exists, that it is terrible, and that changing it is a decision somebody made.

The scene's placement is as considered as its content. It falls in the hero's exile, in the park across town that serves the film for Mantua, and the statue discharges, in a single conversation, the offices of two of the play's messengers. He is Balthasar, the bearer of the report that governs the hero's next move, except that his report is not a fact about Juliet but a fact about the story; and he is Friar John, the letter-carrier whose failure in the play seals the tomb, except that his message arrives perfectly and is rejected on its merits. The play's catastrophe turns on a communication that never lands; the film's rescue turns on a communication that lands and loses. Between those two arrangements lies the whole distance between the tragedy's world, in which intention is helpless against accident, and the children's film's world, in which a sufficiently stubborn hero may appeal against the text itself. Stewart's casting loads the scene further. His voice arrives with a lifetime of the classical stage inside it, the Royal Shakespeare Company veteran as park statuary, so that the authority Gnomeo defies is not a cartoon of a playwright but the theatrical establishment in person, robed, bronzed and entirely confident of its ending. The film needs the statue to be right about everything except the one thing the scene is about, and it casts accordingly.

Read as rhetoric, the scene is a miniature of the transaction literary criticism usually conducts in prose. The statue argues from precedent: the story has run before, under the same conditions, to the same end, and precedent in his mouth is destiny, the accumulated authority of four centuries of performances all ending in the tomb. Gnomeo argues from standing: the ending is unacceptable to the person who would have to die in it, and his objection is ethical before it is literary, a defendant's plea rather than a scholar's footnote. The film then does what criticism cannot, it reruns the experiment. The climax is the statue's precedent staged under the new management, feud, secrecy, weapon, apparent deaths, and for one held breath the precedent seems vindicated, which is why the statue's "Told him so" lands as more than a gag: it is the voice of every teacher who ever assured a class that the story could not end otherwise. The lovers' emergence is the counterexample, produced not by argument but by event, and the scene's pedagogical generosity is that it leaves both speeches standing. A child is not told the statue lied; a child is shown that precedent, even the author's own, is a prediction, and that predictions about people, even ceramic ones, can be beaten by the people themselves.

## Elton John's Catalogue as Chorus: Music, Money and the Rocket Pictures Wager

The film exists because Elton John wanted it to. Rocket Pictures, the production company John formed with David Furnish, originated the project from the Sprackling and Smith idea, carried it through the Miramax greenlight and the years of Disney hesitation, and delivered it as a vehicle in which John's back catalogue does the work of a chorus. The score proper is by James Newton Howard, a former keyboard player and string arranger for John, working with Chris Bacon; the songs are the catalogue, plus two new compositions. "Hello Hello," performed in the film as a duet between John and Lady Gaga, carries the courtship, and the new ballad "Love Builds a Garden" gives Featherstone's creed its musical form. Around them the film arranges the standards as commentary: "Saturday Night's Alright for Fighting" over the mower race, "Your Song" and "Tiny Dancer" inside the love plot, and a cover of "Crocodile Rock" by Nelly Furtado, who appears on the soundtrack beside Kiki Dee, John's partner on "Don't Go Breaking My Heart," to play the film out.

The device is older than it looks. The play's own first audiences heard a chorus promise the story in advance; the film's audience hears a catalogue it already owns promise the feelings in advance, each song arriving with its thirty years of radio memory attached. For adults the music is the bridge of recognition that carries them over the silliness; for children it is simply the sound of the film being pleased with itself. The commercial record shows which audience the wager was placed on. Made for a reported budget of 36 million dollars, the film opened in February 2011 to 25.4 million dollars in its first North American weekend, in third place, and then refused to fall: it became the highest-grossing animated film ever released in the winter months at that date, a record it held until The Lego Movie in 2014, and finished with 193.9 million dollars worldwide, roughly 100 million of it in North America. It outgrossed Disney's own far more expensive Mars Needs Moms, released a month later by the same studio that had kept the gnome film on a shelf. "Hello Hello" was nominated for the Golden Globe, Satellite and Critics' Choice awards for best original song, and the film collected four Annie Award nominations. A sleeper hit built out of a shelved script and a songbook: the catalogue was not decoration on the production; it was the production's insurance, and it paid out.

The album itself, released by Buena Vista Records on 1 February 2011 ahead of the film, preserves a small oddity that testifies to the production's layered authorship: "Hello Hello" plays in the film as a duet between John and Lady Gaga, recorded for the screen, while the soundtrack album carries John's solo rendition, the duet circulating separately afterward. A film built by committee leaves such seams, and the honest response is that this committee's seams mostly show in the credits rather than in the viewing. The film also travelled on its songs in a second sense. John and Asbury presented ten minutes of footage at the Cannes Film Festival during the production's long gestation, using the music as the unfinished film's ambassador, and the finished picture's February window, traditionally a quiet corridor for family releases, was chosen in the confidence that a catalogue this familiar could carry a marketing campaign on radio alone. The wager is legible in the release record: the film's winter opening set the seasonal benchmark for animation and its legs, rather than its launch, did the work, the grosses accumulating weekend over weekend in the manner of a record gathering plays rather than a blockbuster gathering crowds.

The two new songs mark the film's own contributions the way cairns mark a path. The catalogue is assigned to the inherited beats, feud, race, courtship colour, the feelings the audience already owns; the new material is reserved for the two things the film itself invented. "Hello Hello" plays over the meeting in the derelict garden, the collision the play staged as a sonnet, as if the film were confessing that here, at its most original piece of staging, borrowed music would not do and the moment had to be scored by a song written for these two figures in this pond. "Love Builds a Garden" accompanies Featherstone, the character with no counterpart in the play at all, and states the creed the play never states, that cultivation is the alternative to feud. The pattern is a small map of the adaptation's conscience: what is Shakespeare's is sung by the record collection, what is the film's had to be composed. Underneath both, Howard and Bacon's orchestral score does the unglamorous work of continuity, and the film's musical architecture therefore mirrors its dramatic one at every level, an inherited frame, a borrowed chorus, and new work placed precisely where the adaptation departs from its source.

The release completed the argument the production had been making all along. The film opened on 11 February 2011 in both Britain and America, on the Valentine's Day weekend, a placement that only makes sense as a declaration: the year's most famous love story, offered to families as the season's romance, its tragedy converted to courtship in the marketing exactly as in the telling. February is the corridor the industry reserves for pictures it cannot place in the summer or the holidays, and the gnome film's performance there, third on its first weekend and then simply refusing to leave, became the trade's favourite kind of story, the sleeper. Its premiere at the El Capitan Theatre in Hollywood in January had set the tone of respectable eccentricity, a Disney palace hosting a Touchstone gnome picture made in Toronto for a label that no longer existed in its original form. The film's nationality performed the same doubleness as everything else about it: a British subject, a British songwriter, a largely British voice cast and the playwright's own town for a setting, financed through an American studio's vanished boutique label and animated in Canada, an object lesson in the modern circulation Lanier describes, the play belonging to whichever garden will have it.

## The Second Audience: Allusion, Freezing and the Double Address

Every family film addresses two audiences at once, and this one builds the arrangement into its physics. The gnomes live under the rule of the ornaments' world, codified for screen children by the Toy Story films: when a human approaches, motion stops. The film's purest statement of the rule comes at the false death, when the fleeing Gnomeo is caught in the street by an approaching jogger, freezes as a gnome must, and is knocked into the road by the startled woman, so that the tragedy's hinge is produced by the etiquette of being an ornament. The humans themselves are kept faceless, filmed from behind or at a distance, faces withheld, because a faced human would become a character and the film needs them as weather. The gnomes' whole civilisation is what happens when the weather clears, signalled by the weather vanes on the two roofs, and the child's pleasure in the arrangement is the oldest pleasure the film offers: the suspicion, confirmed for eighty-three minutes, that the objects of the house conduct a secret life conducted entirely for the audience's benefit.

Over that plot, legible to any six-year-old, the film spreads a second text addressed to the adults who paid for the tickets, and its density is the honest core of the quarrel between the film's defenders and its detractors. The houses stand on Verona Drive, numbered 2B and Not 2B, the second figure slashed through on the red mailbox. The removal firm that carried away Featherstone's mate in the film's saddest flashback is Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Movers, so that the tragedy's most expendable pair performs the one removal that matters. A rival firm trades as As U Like-It-Hauled; a truck advertises Tempest Teapots; the glue with which Redbrick fixes his daughter to her pedestal is branded The Taming of the Glue, a joke that converts the play's most coercive father into a hardware purchase. Nanette, the frog, carries two of the canon's most famous speeches as small talk, delivering "Parting is such sweet sorrow" as a pleasantry and garbling Hamlet's farewell into pigeons and sparrows. None of this is decoration in the economic sense. It is the adult ticket's compensation, recognition paid out at the rate of a joke a minute, and it explains both poles of the reception: Robinson's complaint that the film is a machine for eye-rolling gags is the complaint of a viewer who kept reading the second text and found it thinner than the first, while the defence's warmth is the warmth of viewers who accepted the exchange. The child, reading only the plot, gets the better film. That inversion is the family's bargain stated plainly, and the statue scene is its summit, the one sequence in which the two texts become a single argument that both audiences can follow at their own level: for the child, a statue that talks and a hero who talks back; for the adult, the author of the tragedy cross-examined by his own character and found, for once, refutable.

## The Terrafirminator and the Tomb: Catastrophe Without Corpses

The complication the brief demands, a happy ending for a tragedy, deserves the article's closest structural attention, because the ending is where the film either honours or abandons the play. The play ends in a vault: Paris dead at the door, Romeo dead by poison, Juliet dead by the dagger, and the fathers reconciled over the bodies with statues promised in gold. The reconciliation is genuine and it is worthless to its purchasers, which is the tragedy's final irony. The film ends in two ruined gardens: Benny's Terrafirminator, the super-mower bought in revenge for his hat, tears both plots apart, aims itself at the tower where Juliet stands glued, and explodes with the reunited lovers at the centre of the blast. The elders, arriving at the wreckage, do what Capulet and Montague do in the vault. They end the feud on the spot, over what they believe are the bodies of their children, and Lord Redbrick speaks the reconciliation before he knows whether anyone has survived to hear it. Then the rubble shifts, the lovers climb out intact, and the film collects its dividend: the reconciliation the play could only stage as remorse, this version gets to stage as relief.

The difference is not that the film flinches from the tomb's price but that it reassigns who pays it. In the play, the price of peace is the children's lives, and the golden statues are the receipt. In the film, the price is the gardens, the whole cultivated order the feud was supposedly fought over, and the receipt is the closing image: the married couple departing on a purple lawnmower, red blended with blue into a third colour that belongs to neither house. A child can read that image without help, and it states the film's thesis as plainly as the Prince's closing couplet states the play's, "For never was a story of more woe / Than this of Juliet and her Romeo" (5.3.309-310). Where the couplet seals the story as a warning, the purple mower seals it as a settlement: the feud's real victim was always the ground it was fought over. Tybalt, glued back together for the final celebration, completes the bookkeeping. The play's one death that the feud directly manufactures in its opening movement is unmade with adhesive, as if to say that in a garden, even the worst damage is a repair job away from a dance.

What, precisely, is lost? Three things, and the article should not pretend otherwise. The play's irony of timing, the letter that fails, the waking that comes minutes late, is gone, because the film's catastrophe is mechanical rather than temporal: the danger is a machine, not a misunderstanding. The play's moral discomfort, its refusal to let the audience decide whether the lovers were noble or foolish, is gone with it, replaced by a clean allocation of blame to the feud and its hardware. And the play's language, the thing itself, is gone almost entire: the film quotes nearly nothing, paraphrases the Prologue as a joke, and lets a statue carry the whole burden of the text. The losses are real, and the verdict of this article is that they are also correctly priced. What a child takes from the film is not the play's poetry or its irony but its civic shape: a quarrel nobody can explain, a love that ignores the line, a bill that comes due. Those are the parts of the tragedy that survive translation into any medium, and they are the parts this series has watched survive every other remaking.

The film's catastrophes are made of misread evidence, and the device deserves isolating because it is the precise point at which the translation touches the tragedy's engine room. The play's final act runs on a letter that never arrives: the Friar's explanation is quarantined with Friar John, and Romeo acts on the true report of a false death, since Juliet is sleeping, not dead. The gnome film distributes that single failure across its whole design. The blue teapot shard on the road is the letter inverted, a false report manufactured by accident, the wrong object shattered in the right place, and every faction acts on it in good faith. The statue's prophecy is evidence of a higher order misread in the opposite direction, the true ending applied to the wrong story. Even the Terrafirminator is bought on a sales pitch, evidence of the weakest kind, an advertisement. Where the play's world executes its characters for one failed delivery, the film's world brings its characters to the brink for a run of honest misreadings and then lets them compare notes over the rubble. The epistemology survives intact: nobody in either work has the whole picture, the feud's function is to prevent the comparing of notes, and the ending, tragic or happy, is simply the moment the notes are finally compared. The difference is the price of the delay.

The deepest of the film's substitutions is visible only against the play's last page. Shakespeare's fathers close the tragedy by promising each other statues: Montague will raise Juliet in pure gold, Capulet answers that Romeo's shall lie as rich beside her, and the lovers' immortality is to be commemorative, cast metal in a public place, erected by the men whose feud required the originals' removal. The gnome film runs the image backward. Its lovers begin as statues, cast goods in a public garden, and the ending's whole labour is to get them treated as alive, married, mobile, riding out of the commemorative world on a purple mower. The play turns children into monuments; the film turns monuments into children, and the same object, the garden statue, carries both meanings at once, which is why the conceit sustains an entire feature rather than a sketch. A gnome is a statue that a story has decided to animate, and the film's happy ending is nothing more than the completion of that decision, applied to the two figures the source text left in bronze.

The wedding itself is staged as a treaty with the colours left in force, and the detail is the film's political signature. The houses do not merge into a single colour, surrender their hats, or pretend the feud was a misunderstanding; red and blue stand in the same garden at the end, distinct, adjacent and at peace, the elders reconciled and the young couple married across the line rather than at its expense. The play's settlement is absorption by death, the houses united only in grief and gold; the film's is coexistence, the houses united in a calendar of shared occasions, beginning with a wedding. The lovers' purple mower, neither house's colour and compounded of both, is the treaty's heraldry: not a third faction but a household of two, founded in the open, on the road out of both gardens. And the treaty's most radical clause is a guest. Tybalt attends the peace he did everything to prevent, glued together and present, forgiven by a film that can afford to forgive him because his worst deed in this telling was breakage. The play cannot seat Tybalt at any wedding: his death is the tragedy's point of no return, the act that converts brawl to destiny. The film, having spent its whole length keeping every injury reparable, cashes the policy at the feast, and the sight of the repaired enemy among the guests is its final definition of a happy ending, not the absence of enemies but their restoration.

It matters that the film's instrument of repair is glue, because glue is the film's whole theology in a tube. Nothing in Gnomeo and Juliet is resurrected; the film grants no miracles, raises no one from any tomb, and works no magic beyond the founding premise that ornaments live. What it does, at every level, is repair. Tybalt is glued. The gardens are rebuilt. Featherstone's sundered household is reassembled by return. The feud itself is repaired, by apology and marriage, and the film's brashest gag, the glue branded The Taming of the Glue, turns out to be its thesis in disguise: the same adhesive that imprisons Juliet on her pedestal in the second act is the substance that restores Tybalt in the third, the instrument of coercion and of mercy differing only in the hands that apply it, which is the play's own doctrine about potions, letters and swords restated in hardware. The tragedy's world contains exactly one act of repair, the fathers' golden statues, and it is memorial rather than restoration, valuable metal standing where children stood. The children's film inverts the economy: nothing is memorialised because nothing is lost beyond mending, and the craft of mending, patient, unglamorous and available to anyone, is offered to its audience as the moral technology that feud societies lack. A child cannot imitate a resurrection. A child can imitate glue.

### Why does Benny's shattered hat matter more than a wound would?

The hat is the film's unit of injury: a smashed hat point is damage a child can see and grieve, and it gives Benny a Mercutio-sized grievance without a corpse in the second act. The revenge he buys with that grievance drives the third act's catastrophe, so the small breakage carries the causal weight of Mercutio's wound.

## The Critics, the Scholars and the Gnome: Adjudicating the Reception

The reviews split along a line the film itself draws, between those who judged it as a Shakespeare adaptation and those who judged it as a children's entertainment, and the split is the most instructive thing in its reception. The defence was led by senior trade and newspaper voices. Justin Chang in Variety called it "a welcome dose of honest silliness at a time when most family-oriented toons settle for smart-alecky," praising exactly the quality the prosecution would indict. Kenneth Turan of the Los Angeles Times found it "playful, inventive and endearing" and smart enough not to overstay its welcome. Joe Morgenstern of the Wall Street Journal called it "really entertaining," and Ty Burr of the Boston Globe wrote that it had "its own bizarre charms" and rendered itself "the very definition of a simple pleasure." The prosecution was equally credentialed. Stephen Holden of the New York Times called the film "a discombobulated grab bag of jokes peopled with characters who have little emotional resonance," conceding that it improved as it went. Tasha Robinson of the A.V. Club objected that far too much of the film was "devoted to eye-rolling pop-culture gags and long montages set to recycled Elton John songs." Rotten Tomatoes settled the argument at 55 percent approval from 126 critics, with a consensus that praised moments of inspiration and taxed the film with being too self-referential for its own good; Metacritic scored it 53, while audiences polled by CinemaScore graded it B plus.

The scholarly frameworks adjudicate what the reviews leave tied. Kenneth S. Rothwell's A History of Shakespeare on Screen mapped the screen afterlife of the plays as a field in which loose derivations and spin-offs vastly outnumber faithful stagings, and treated that proliferation as the medium's natural history rather than its decline. Douglas Lanier, in Shakespeare and Modern Popular Culture, gave the proliferation a model: the playwright's work circulates as a rhizome, each new node, however commercial, reactivating the inherited material for an audience the authorised versions cannot reach. Against those frames, the family-film literature supplies the missing discipline. Noel Brown's study of the Hollywood family film describes the genre's double address, a surface built for children carrying a second text of references priced for the adults who buy the tickets, and its habit of sanitising prestigious source material into moral shapes a family audience can use. Gnomeo and Juliet is an almost diagrammatic case of all three accounts at once: a rhizomatic node of the play, a double-addressed family film, and a spin-off that openly debates its source on screen.

The prosecution had reinforcements beyond the broadsheets. Lou Lumenick of the New York Post crossed the floor entirely, calling the picture a delightfully silly family entertainment, while Christy Lemire of the Associated Press filed the dissent that stings most, on the ground that a worthwhile family film should send its audience out arguing about it and this one, in her judgement, would not. The disagreement is itself data: the film divided reviewers not by quality of execution, on which the notices broadly agree, but by the standard each brought to a children's Shakespeare, the prosecution judging it against the play and the defence against the February release schedule.

The numbers kept the same divided counsel. The aggregators' scores, 55 per cent from 126 critics at Rotten Tomatoes and 53 from 28 reviews at Metacritic, sit in the band that records a profession unable to convict or acquit, while the audience's own instrument, the CinemaScore poll of opening crowds, returned a B+, the grade of a crowd that had been pleased and would say so at school gates and office doors. The production's identity compounded the puzzle for its judges: a British-American film, premiered at Hollywood's El Capitan Theatre in January 2011 and sold on a British songwriter's catalogue, retelling an Italian story by an English playwright in the playwright's own town, it belonged fully to no national cinema and was reviewed, in each country, faintly as an import. Time has been kinder than the opening weekend's third place suggested. The picture's standing as the winter season's animated benchmark lasted three years, its songs returned John's catalogue to family radio, and its commercial file was strong enough that a sequel was commissioned from an entirely different studio system, the surest institutional verdict Hollywood delivers.

So the adjudication runs as follows. Holden and Robinson win on texture: the gag rate is high, the montages are long, and the statue scene's wit throws the surrounding joke-writing into unflattering relief. Chang and Turan win on contract: at eighty-three minutes, with its catastrophe honestly built and its loss honestly felt in Featherstone, the film delivers the entertainment it advertised, and the charge of emptiness cannot survive the flamingo. The scholars win on significance, because the film's real claim on attention is the one the reviewers touched only in passing: it is the version of the play that argues with the play. Researchers assembling the file on a film like this one, its reviews, production interviews, box-office reports and award citations, do that work fastest in a single personal research archive such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), where the whole paper trail of an adaptation can be kept beside the notes that interpret it. In the wider field, the film also anchors the screen tradition surveyed in [the history of the play in animation](/2029/02/23/romeo-and-juliet-in-animation/), feeds the lineage traced in [the modern teen-film descendants of the story](/2029/04/27/teen-film-descendants/), and takes its place in [the running argument over which screen version of the play is best](/2029/05/25/which-film-version-is-best/), where its claim rests not on beauty or fidelity but on nerve: it is the only version that cross-examines its author and wins.

The sequel is the control experiment the argument needed, and it deserves the article's last word because it isolates the original's active ingredient. Sherlock Gnomes, released in 2018 under director John Stevenson for Paramount Animation and Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer after Disney let the property go, reassembled the gardens, the voices and the gnome mechanics, cast Johnny Depp as its detective, spent some 59 million dollars against the original's 36, and removed exactly one element: the Shakespeare. Its worldwide gross settled somewhere between 90 and 98 million dollars depending on the tally, against the original's 193.9 million on a smaller budget, and its approval rating fell below 30 per cent where the original had held the mid-fifties. Every other variable moved in the sequel's favour, stars, spend, an established brand, and the result halved. The difference is the play. The garden gnomes were never the attraction in themselves; the attraction was the sight of garden ornaments carrying the most famous tragic structure in the language, and behaving, at the decisive moment, better than it. Remove the tragedy and the gnomes are merchandise. Keep it, and argue with it, and the merchandise becomes an adaptation.

A last scholarly question is taxonomic, and the film's own credits answer it. The picture presents itself as based on the play, a claim of derivation rather than of resemblance, and the taxonomy matters because it decides the standard of judgement. Filed as a spin-off, a gnome comedy with Shakespearean garnish, the film is a trifle that over-performs; filed as an adaptation, a telling of the play for a new audience under new constraints, it is an argument that can be graded, and on the evidence assembled here it grades high on structure and candour, lower on texture, and alone in its class on the question of the ending. The classroom afterlife follows from the filing. As the only feature-length screen telling of the story rated for the youngest viewers, the film is positioned to be the first Romeo and Juliet its audience meets and the standard against which the play is later measured, a responsibility its makers seem to have understood, since the statue scene reads in retrospect like a disclosure addressed to exactly those future students: a warning, delivered by the author himself, that the version they are about to meet in school ends differently, and that the difference was chosen, by somebody, for reasons they are invited to judge.

The transferable lesson, for every future adapter who inherits a story too violent, too sexual or too sad for the audience in front of them, is written across the film's whole design: a young audience does not require a story emptied, only a story re-materialised. Nothing in the gnome film's plot would puzzle a reader of the play; everything in its physics would. Swords become machines because children read property before they read wounds; the letter becomes a teapot because children read objects before they read documents; the Friar becomes a flamingo with a biography because children trust testimony before they trust doctrine; and the deaths become a wedding because the film understood, and proved in the market, that what the deaths were for could be had without them. The failures of children's adaptation are almost never failures of nerve about content. They are failures of translation, stories abridged when they should have been rebuilt, endings amputated when they should have been re-engineered, until nothing remains but a famous name and a moral. Gnomeo and Juliet rebuilt, re-engineered and kept the name honest, and the standard it sets is the one this article has applied throughout: judge a children's version not by what it dares to leave out, but by whether everything it leaves out has been given a working replacement, and whether it tells its audience, somewhere in the open, what the original did instead. By that standard the film stands, in this series' judgement, as the most instructive children's adaptation in the play's screen history: not the prettiest, not the wittiest line by line, but the only one that solved the ending problem in public, with the author present, and left the solution standing in two Stratford gardens, red and blue together, for any later adapter, teacher or parent to inspect.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Gnomeo note, and what does it record about the film's method?

The InsightCrunch Gnomeo note is a side-by-side record of how the film translates the play, setting each element of the tragedy against its garden equivalent and naming the effect of the change. Its rows track the feud becoming hat colours, the street brawl becoming a lawnmower race, the potion becoming glue, the tomb becoming an explosion, and the playwright himself becoming a talking statue. Read as a whole, the note demonstrates the article's verdict: the film proceeds by substitution rather than by deletion, preserving each element's function in the plot while changing the material it is made of, so that a child audience can follow the tragedy's machinery without meeting its corpses.

### Q: Who directed Gnomeo and Juliet, and which company made it?

Kelly Asbury directed the film, for Rocket Pictures, the production company formed by Elton John and David Furnish. The idea originated with screenwriters Rob Sprackling and John Smith; Disney's chairman Dick Cook greenlit the project at Miramax in 2006, and after Miramax's sale Disney retained the film and released it in February 2011 through its Touchstone Pictures banner, with Starz Animation producing the animation. Asbury, the co-director of Shrek 2, joined in 2006 and rewrote the screenplay with producer Steve Hamilton Shaw. The final credits carry seven screenplay writers and six story writers, while the producing team included Baker Bloodworth, Hamilton Shaw and Furnish alongside John.

### Q: Why did Elton John produce a film about garden gnomes?

Elton John produced the film because his company, Rocket Pictures, originated it and his catalogue is its score. John served as the film's executive producer and presiding musical spirit: rather than commission a new songbook, the production built the film around his existing hits, adding two new compositions, "Hello Hello" and "Love Builds a Garden." The catalogue performs the chorus's office, telling the audience in advance what each movement of the story feels like, and it gave a modestly budgeted animation the emotional scale of a much larger film. The wager succeeded commercially: the film grossed 193.9 million dollars worldwide on a budget of 36 million.

### Q: How does the film turn the Capulet and Montague feud into a garden rivalry?

The film turns the feud into a rivalry between two adjoining gardens in Stratford-upon-Avon, ruled by red-hatted Capulet gnomes under Lord Redbrick and blue-hatted Montague gnomes under Lady Bluebury. Above them, their human owners, Miss Montague and Mr. Capulet, despise each other across the same fence for reasons nobody states, mirroring the play's refusal to explain its ancient grudge. Below them, the feud is prosecuted through sport and property: a lawnmower race in an alley, night sabotage, a destroyed wisteria, threatened tulips. Hatred becomes visible as a coat of paint, and every escalation injures a possession rather than a person until the revenge plot finally endangers the lovers themselves.

### Q: Who voices Gnomeo and Juliet, and who else is in the cast?

James McAvoy voices Gnomeo and Emily Blunt voices Juliet. The cast around them maps the play's household with notable economy: Michael Caine is Lord Redbrick, Juliet's widowed father; Maggie Smith is Lady Bluebury, Gnomeo's widowed mother; Jason Statham is Tybalt; Matt Lucas is Benny, a fusion of Benvolio and Mercutio; Ashley Jensen is Nanette, the frog who fills the Nurse's office; Stephen Merchant is the harmless Paris; Jim Cummings is Featherstone, the flamingo in the Friar's place; and Patrick Stewart voices the statue of William Shakespeare. Julie Walters and Richard Wilson appear as the human owners, with Ozzy Osbourne, Dolly Parton and Hulk Hogan in ornamental cameos.

### Q: What does the statue of Shakespeare tell Gnomeo about the ending?

The statue tells Gnomeo that his story has been written before and that it ends with both lovers dead. Stranded in a public park after his apparent death, Gnomeo recounts his troubles to the statue, voiced by Patrick Stewart, and the author recognises the plot as his own. He recites its destination with a playwright's confidence; Gnomeo protests that the ending is horrible; the statue, unbothered, lets him fall. When the climax appears to fulfil the prophecy, the statue surveys the explosion with satisfaction and delivers the film's shortest verdict on itself: "Told him so." The lovers' emergence from the rubble refutes him within the minute.

### Q: Why does the film refuse the play's tragic ending?

The film refuses the tragic ending because its audience, its rating and its governing design all exclude the instruments the ending requires. Director Kelly Asbury described the production's central challenge as differentiating the ending from the play's in order "to keep daggers and poison and suicide out," and the finished film, the only G-rated release in Touchstone Pictures' history, solves the problem structurally rather than by evasion. The reconciliation of the houses is preserved and even quoted, but it is purchased with the destruction of both gardens instead of the deaths of both children, and the refusal is argued on screen by the statue scene, in which the original ending is stated by its author and rejected by its hero.

### Q: What happens to Tybalt in the film, and why is he repaired at the end?

Tybalt is shattered against a wall during the confrontation that follows his smashing of Benny's hat: racing his lawnmower, he runs into a ditch and is thrown into the masonry, and a broken gnome in this film's physics is a dead one. His fall does the play's work, triggering grief, blame, Gnomeo's exile and the revenge plot, so the film keeps the consequence of the killing while reserving the right to refund it. At the finale Tybalt reappears glued back together for the closing celebration. The repair is the film's bookkeeping in a single image: damage in a garden is real, grievous and, given adhesive and goodwill, reversible.

### Q: Which character replaces Friar Laurence, and what does he change?

Featherstone, a plastic pink flamingo voiced by Jim Cummings, replaces Friar Laurence, and he changes the office from plot machinery to moral testimony. The play's Friar schemes: he marries the lovers, brews the potion and sends the letter whose failure kills them. Featherstone schemes at nothing. Locked in a shed for twenty years after a human divorce divided his garden's ornaments and took his mate away, he counsels the lovers from experience, urging them not to repeat a separation he has already lived. Named for Donald Featherstone, the sculptor who created the plastic flamingo, he carries the film's one irreversible loss inside a minor character's backstory, where a children's film can afford it.

### Q: How does the film replace the poison and the dagger?

The film replaces the poison and the dagger with a system of breakages, substitutions and household materials. Juliet's potion, the stillness that removes her from the plot, becomes glue: her father fastens her feet to her pedestal, immobilising her without any pretence of death. Romeo's poison has no equivalent at all; Gnomeo's apparent death is faked by accident, when a blue teapot shatters in the road and is mistaken for his remains. The dagger's work, the heroine's self-destruction, is deleted outright and its place taken by the Terrafirminator explosion, a catastrophe engineered by machinery rather than despair. Death's instruments are thus reassigned from the lovers' hands to accidents, fathers and appliances.

### Q: What is the Terrafirminator, and what does it do to the ending?

The Terrafirminator is a monstrous super-lawnmower that Benny orders online, through the human owner's computer, to avenge his shattered hat, its television advertisement voiced by Hulk Hogan. Unleashed during the final battle, it escapes control within seconds and destroys both gardens impartially, before steering itself at the tower where Juliet stands glued and exploding with the reunited lovers in the blast. Its narrative office is the tomb's: it creates the catastrophe over which the elders reconcile, persuades both houses that the children are dead, and prices the peace. Unlike the tomb, it leaves the lovers alive inside the wreckage, so the reconciliation it purchases can be enjoyed rather than mourned.

### Q: Which Elton John songs does the film use, and which were written for it?

Two songs were written for the film: "Hello Hello," performed in the film as a duet between Elton John and Lady Gaga, and the ballad "Love Builds a Garden." Around them the film arranges John's back catalogue as commentary, including "Your Song," "Tiny Dancer," "Rocket Man," "Bennie and the Jets" and "Saturday Night's Alright for Fighting," which scores the lawnmower race. Nelly Furtado contributes a cover of "Crocodile Rock," and Kiki Dee appears through "Don't Go Breaking My Heart," her duet with John. The composed score is by James Newton Howard, John's former keyboard player and string arranger, working with Chris Bacon. "Hello Hello" was nominated for Golden Globe, Satellite and Critics' Choice song awards.

### Q: What did critics praise and fault in the film on release?

Critics split between those judging an entertainment and those judging an adaptation. Justin Chang in Variety praised "a welcome dose of honest silliness"; Kenneth Turan of the Los Angeles Times called the film playful, inventive and endearing; Joe Morgenstern of the Wall Street Journal found it really entertaining, and Ty Burr of the Boston Globe granted it bizarre charms. Stephen Holden of the New York Times dissented, calling it a discombobulated grab bag of jokes whose characters carried little emotional resonance, and Tasha Robinson of the A.V. Club faulted its pop-culture gags and song montages. Aggregators recorded the draw: 55 percent approval on Rotten Tomatoes, 53 on Metacritic, and a B plus from CinemaScore audiences.

### Q: How much money did Gnomeo and Juliet make?

The film grossed 193.9 million dollars worldwide against a reported production budget of 36 million dollars, with roughly 100 million of the total earned in North America. Released in February 2011, it opened to 25.4 million dollars in its first North American weekend, finishing third, and then held unusually well, becoming the highest-grossing animated film released in the winter months at that date, a record it kept until The Lego Movie surpassed it in 2014. It also outgrossed Disney's Mars Needs Moms, a far more expensive production released by the same studio a month later, completing the film's reputation as a sleeper hit from a shelved script.

### Q: What is the difference between the film's Juliet and the play's Juliet?

The film's Juliet keeps her original's situation and reverses its terms of competence. Both Juliets are guarded daughters of a feud house, promised to a Paris, and driven to secret meetings with the enemy's son. Shakespeare's Juliet, thirteen years old, exercises her agency in secret and at mortal risk, through a nurse, a friar and a feigned death. The film's Juliet, voiced by Emily Blunt, is an adventurer under guard: she climbs out at night to seize the Cupid's Arrow orchid herself, out-climbs and out-plans her suitor, deflects Paris onto her confidante, and must finally be immobilised by glue because no fence or lecture will hold her. The film trades the original's interior daring for visible capability.

### Q: How does the film open, and what happens to its prologue?

The film opens on a stage, with a lone gnome mounting a platform to deliver a formal prologue about the feud, in paraphrase of the play's Chorus. He begins the recital, complains at its dullness, and is prevented from finishing when a trapdoor opens beneath him and drops him out of the film. The gag performs the adaptation's whole method in under a minute: the famous frame is produced, acknowledged and discarded, because the audience is presumed to know the story and to have come for its retelling rather than its recitation. The action then repairs to Stratford-upon-Avon, where the human neighbours whose gardens supply the feud are introduced in a handful of seconds.

### Q: Why does the film give the feud to human neighbours as well as to the gnomes?

The film gives the feud to human neighbours so that the gnomes' hatred can be shown as inherited behaviour copied from a larger world. Miss Montague and Mr. Capulet, played by Julie Walters and Richard Wilson, despise each other across the garden fence in the film's first seconds and are never given a reason, exactly as the play never explains its ancient grudge. Their gnomes absorb the quarrel the way ornaments absorb weather, and continue it in their owners' absence, which lets the film argue its thesis about feuds generally: they are not argued into existence but handed down, practised by each generation in whatever materials it happens to be made of.

### Q: What is Sherlock Gnomes, and how did the sequel fare?

Sherlock Gnomes is the 2018 sequel, directed by John Stevenson, in which the garden cast, relocated to London, is joined by Johnny Depp's detective to investigate a wave of gnome disappearances. James McAvoy and Emily Blunt reprised their roles, and Elton John again presided over the production through Rocket Pictures, though the film passed from Disney to Paramount and MGM and abandoned the play entirely, taking its plot from Arthur Conan Doyle instead. It fared far worse than its predecessor: reported worldwide grosses run between 90 and 98 million dollars against a budget of 59 million, and its Rotten Tomatoes approval settled below 30 percent, confirming that the first film's success belonged to its argument with Shakespeare rather than to the gnomes alone.

### Q: Why are the gnomes given red and blue hats?

The hats make allegiance legible to the youngest viewer and expose it as arbitrary in the same gesture. A gnome's colour is painted on at manufacture: red marks a Capulet, blue a Montague, and the difference predicts nothing about the gnome underneath, which is why the pond scene, washing the disguises away, can stage the play's argument about names as a simple act of recognition. The device also serves the film's design, letting crowds read as factions at a glance in the race, the sabotage raids and the final battle, and it sets up the closing image, the married couple departing on a purple lawnmower, red and blue blended into a colour neither house owns.

An essay should use the InsightCrunch Gnomeo note as a map of substitutions, reading down its rows to show that each element of the play retains its function while changing its material: the feud survives as hat colour, the wound as a shattered hat, the death as a broken teapot, the tomb as an explosion, the reconciliation as a bargain struck over rubble.
