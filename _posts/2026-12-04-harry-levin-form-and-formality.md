Nine pages of print settled how the world hears the language of Romeo and Juliet. In the Winter 1960 issue of Shakespeare Quarterly, across pages 3 to 11, Harry Levin published "Form and Formality in Romeo and Juliet," and the essay's central perception has organized the reading of the play's style ever since: the tragedy is built from set forms, sonnets, dances, duels, sestets, ceremonial laments, and its lovers fight their way out of those forms toward speech that sounds discovered rather than recited. The popular ear hears one seamless poetic voice pouring from the play, a single golden manner shared by hero, heroine, friar, nurse, and prince. Levin restored the movement inside that voice. His essay hears two registers at war, the borrowed dialect of convention and the pressured utterance of feeling, and it tracks the lovers' migration from the first to the second with an index anyone can check: the incidence of rhyme.

The verdict of the present article is that Levin's reading stands, with one amendment that his own evidence supplies. The movement he traced is real, measurable in rhyme, in repetition, and in the shrinking of shared set pieces as the acts advance. The amendment concerns the destination. The lovers do not travel from form to formlessness, shedding pattern as they deepen. They travel from borrowed form to inhabited form, from reciting the sonnet culture's script to making its strictest shapes, the shared sonnet, the dawn parting, carry unmediated feeling. Formality, in Levin's usage, names convention gone rigid, the compliment paid because the code requires it, the suitor's sestet pronounced because suitors pronounce sestets. Form names the shapes themselves, which the play never discards and cannot, since its ending is delivered in a sestet and a rhymed epitaph couplet. Levin saw this. His essay's closing pages return the lovers to the social frame and its ceremonies without treating that return as a defeat for his thesis, because the thesis was never that pattern dies. It was that pattern is indicted, scene by scene, by a running critique of artificiality, until the audience can hear the difference between a line spoken because the form demands it and a line spoken because the speaker does.

That distinction explains the essay's longevity better than its elegance does, though the elegance is considerable. Levin wrote with a comparatist's furniture at his elbow, Sidney and Ovid, Kyd and Bergson, Pasternak and Coleridge, and he arranged the play's evidence the way the play arranges its households, in counterpoise. What follows examines the essay on its own documentary terms, tests its central opposition against the passages that strain it hardest, weighs the two most serious corrections later critics have brought, Edward Snow's charge of asymmetry and the generic enlargement Susan Snyder built on Levin's foundation, and reaches a judgment on what a reader of the play should still take from nine pages written in 1960.

## What Makes Levin's Essay the Landmark Reading of the Play's Language?

A landmark essay is not merely a famous one. It is the piece after which a subject cannot be discussed in the old vocabulary, and by that test Levin's nine pages qualify without strain. Before 1960, the dominant instruments for reading this play were character and imagery. The character tradition, descending from A. C. Bradley's Shakespearean Tragedy of 1904, which pointedly left this play outside its canon of four, treated the lovers as psyches to be diagnosed; [why Bradley excluded the play from his four great tragedies](/2026/11/20/ac-bradley-excluded-romeo-and-juliet/) is a question with its own long file, but the habit his book licensed, asking what Romeo is like rather than how Romeo speaks, governed the middle of the century. The imagery tradition, founded by Caroline Spurgeon's Shakespeare's Imagery and What It Tells Us in 1935, counted the play's pictures and found its weather. Levin cited Spurgeon himself, respectfully and precisely, in a footnote directing readers to her pages on light and darkness. Then he did something neither tradition had done. He treated the play's verbal surface as a dramatic action in its own right, a plot running underneath the plot of the feud, in which the real antagonist is a way of speaking.

The timing sharpened the intervention. L. C. Knights had attacked the character method in 1933, in the famous essay asking how many children Lady Macbeth had, but the attack cleared ground without building on it for this play. M. M. Mahood's Shakespeare's Wordplay of 1957 had shown that the play's puns work structurally rather than decoratively. H. B. Charlton's British Academy lecture of 1939, which Levin acknowledged in a footnote, had framed the play as an experimental tragedy under Renaissance critical theory. Levin's essay gathered these scattered permissions into a single demonstration. It showed a reader, passage by passage, how to hear the difference between Romeo performing the Petrarchan lover in Act 1 and Romeo answering Juliet in monosyllables in Act 2, and it gave the difference a mechanism: rhyme thins, repetition thins, set speeches migrate from the lovers to the world that surrounds them, until the lovers' exchanges are the least ceremonial speech in Verona.

The republication history confirms the standing. Frank Kermode reprinted the essay in Four Centuries of Shakespearian Criticism in 1965, five years after its appearance, placing nine pages on this play in a volume that aspired to define the canon of the art. Anthologies of the play's criticism have kept it in circulation since, and editors' introductions reckon with its terms even when they qualify them. Its title became the standard shorthand for a whole way of hearing the play. When teachers say that Romeo's language changes when he meets Juliet, they are retailing Levin, often without knowing his name; the essay's perceptions have passed into the common stock so completely that their origin needs recovering, which is the usual fate of landmarks and the surest sign of the status.

### Did Levin write as a professor of English or of comparative literature?

Levin wrote as a comparatist, and the essay bears the mark on every page. He held Harvard's Irving Babbitt chair of comparative literature, and his working library for nine pages on one play runs from Sidney's sonnets to Pasternak's memoirs, from Kyd's Spanish Tragedy to Bergson's theory of laughter.

The comparative habit explains the essay's signature move, reading this play against the genres and literatures around it.

The biographical frame matters because it accounts for the essay's method. A specialist in the Elizabethan stage might have written about the quartos; a comparatist wrote about the dance and the duel as European forms, about the anti-Petrarchan sonnet of Sidney as the lovers' formula, about Ovid reversed in Juliet's invocation to the night. Levin's formation made him alert to convention as an international system, the shared stock of Renaissance love rhetoric that crossed borders in sonnet sequences and courtesy books. Romeo's opening manner is not an English eccentricity in the essay's handling. It is the local dialect of a continental language, and Juliet's farewell to compliment is legible as an event in European letters, a heroine declining the idiom of a whole culture's courtship.

## Who Was Harry Levin, and What Did a Comparatist Bring to the Play?

Harry Tuchman Levin was born in Minneapolis in 1912 and arrived at Harvard at seventeen, in 1929, remaining in Cambridge for the rest of his life. He began teaching at Harvard in 1939, entering through a sudden opening for a Shakespearean in the English department, and he never ceased to teach Shakespeare, though his fame spread across modern literature. His first book, James Joyce: A Critical Introduction, appeared in 1941 and helped establish Joyce studies in America; he later edited The Portable James Joyce. The Power of Blackness, his study of Hawthorne, Poe, and Melville, appeared in 1958. The Question of Hamlet, gathering his Alexander Lectures, appeared in 1959, the year before the essay on this play. In 1960 he became the first incumbent of the Irving Babbitt Professorship of Comparative Literature at Harvard, delivering an inaugural lecture on Babbitt and the teaching of literature, and he held the chair until his retirement in 1983. He died in Cambridge in 1994. The American Comparative Literature Association began awarding a Harry Levin Prize in 1985, during his lifetime, an honor that registers where his profession placed him.

The essay on Romeo and Juliet is signed, in the journal's plain style, from Harvard University, and it is characteristic in both its reach and its economy. Levin was sixty years into a scholarly culture that rewarded the long book, and his most quoted performance on this play runs to nine pages. The compression is part of the meaning. Where the character critics had written chapters of psychological portraiture, Levin wrote an essay that behaves like the play he describes, opening in patterned involution and steadily loosening into directness. His sentences perform his argument. The early paragraphs are dense with antithesis and balanced clauses, the prose equivalent of the first act's rhyme; the judgments, when they land, land in short declaratives.

A detail of the essay's apparatus deserves notice because it governs how its evidence should be weighed. Levin's line references are to the separate edition of G. L. Kittredge's text published at Boston in 1940, and his quotations follow that text. Modern readers checking his references against a current edition will find small displacements, a line or two either way, and occasionally a different reading, as where Kittredge prints Juliet's dagger line with "rest" and the Folger text, edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine, prints "rust." Quotations from the play in the present article follow the Folger text. Levin's argument does not depend on the variants; it depends on distribution, on where rhyme and repetition cluster, and distribution survives the change of edition intact. But the apparatus tells the reader something true about the essay's moment. It was written when Kittredge's Harvard Shakespeare was the scholar's default instrument, by a critic whose other life was the modern European novel, and the crossing of those two worlds, the textual scholar's text and the comparatist's frame, is the essay's enabling condition.

## What Is Levin's Central Argument About Form and Formality?

Levin's essay opens at the play's center of gravity, the orchard scene, with Juliet's confession that she has been overheard: "Fain would I dwell on form, fain, fain deny / What I have spoke; but farewell compliment!" (2.2.88-89). The choice of threshold is the argument in miniature. Levin does not begin with the Prologue's sonnet or the feast's choreography, though both will serve him. He begins with a character naming the essay's subject in the play's own words, announcing that she would have performed the courtship ritual, dwelling on form, had the ritual's conditions held, and that instead she renounces compliment altogether. The play, Levin proposes, contains its own criticism. Shakespeare built the contrast between formality and sincerity into the dialogue as a subject the characters themselves discuss, and the critic's task is to follow the discussion through the whole design.

Formality, across the essay's usage, is convention experienced as compulsion: the speech a situation extracts regardless of the speaker's feeling. It is Tybalt's punctilio, the duelling code kept "by the book of arithmetic" (2.4); it is Paris at the tomb, pronouncing a sestet over a girl he never knew; it is the fathers' grief arriving as a litany of contraries, catalogued and rhymed; it is Romeo in Act 1, bandying paradoxes with Benvolio in the standard phrases of Elizabethan eroticism, protesting a good deal too much. Form, by contrast, is the neutral inventory of shapes the period supplied, sonnet, dance, duel, debate, lament, epitaph, which the play uses at every level of feeling, debased in one mouth and transfigured in another. The essay's drama is the migration of the lovers from the first condition toward a use of the second that Levin calls organic: their mutuality is, in his most quoted phrase, "the one organic relation amid an overplus of stylized expressions and attitudes."

The mechanism of the migration is critique from inside. The lovers do not drift into sincerity; they argue their way there, and the play lets the audience watch the argument. Levin's sentence for the process deserves its currency: the naturalness of the lovers' diction "is artfully gained" through what he calls a running critique of artificiality. Juliet questions the name and, with it, all names and forms. Romeo hears his own earlier manner mocked into absurdity by Mercutio's conjuration and answers from hiding with the plainest line in the scene, "He jests at scars that never felt a wound" (2.2.1). Each repudiation is dramatized, not narrated. Comedy, Levin argues, set the pattern of courtship in the dance, and history set the pattern of conflict in the duel; the play takes both inheritances and transcends the usages of romantic comedy, in his formulation, as its leading characters acquire a deeper dimension of feeling by expressly repudiating the artificial language they have talked and the superficial code they have lived by. His borrowed formula for the repudiation is Sidney's, the anti-Petrarchan sonnet's instruction: "Fool said my muse to me, look in thy heart and write."

### Did Levin ever define form and formality as a matching pair of terms?

No glossary exists in the essay: Levin never pauses to define his title's two nouns as a formal pair. The distinction must be reconstructed from his usage across nine pages, where formality gathers around compulsion, ceremony, and social code, while form ranges over the play's shapes and patterns.

Readers who quote the title as a tidy binary quote it tidier than Levin wrote it. This is not pedantry. Much of the later argument about the essay, including the charge that its opposition is too neat, is an argument with the slogan rather than with the text. Levin's own pages supply the complications his title omits: the lovers' most patterned exchanges are among their most sincere; the ending restores ceremony without restoring emptiness; Mercutio, the great satirist of form, is also its virtuoso. A reading of the essay that holds it to a rigid binary will find the binary refuted by the play. A reading that follows Levin's practice finds a subtler instrument, a scale rather than a switch, along which speeches can be placed by their relation to the speaker's occasion. The verdict of the present article is that the essay survives its own title.

## How Does Levin Read Romeo's Petrarchan Performance for Rosaline?

Levin's Romeo enters the essay as a casting decision: "a virtual stereotype of the romantic lover." The phrase is diagnostic, not dismissive. The point is that Shakespeare shows the audience a young man performing a role the culture had already scripted, in speeches utilizing what Levin calls the conventional phrases and standardized images of Elizabethan eroticism. The oxymoron catalogue of the first scene is his prime exhibit: "Here's much to do with hate, but more with love. / Why then, O brawling love! O loving hate! / O any thing, of nothing first create!" (1.1). Antithesis here is a habit before it is a thought. Romeo generalizes, paradoxes, trades sestets with Benvolio, and takes what Levin calls a quasi-religious vow of devotion that meeting Juliet will ironically break. Every feature of the performance points away from its ostensible object. Rosaline never appears; her effect on Romeo is all the play ever reports of her, and the effect is a style.

The torch Romeo carries to the feast concentrates the reading. Unwilling to join the dance, he resolves to be an onlooker: "Give me a torch. I am not for this ambling" (1.4.11). Levin lingers on the prop because it certifies the performance's hollowness; the torch-bearer lights other people's festivity, and the torch, as Levin remarks, may have burned symbolically, but not for Juliet, since Romeo came to the feast to gaze at Rosaline. The whole apparatus of the lovesick gallant, the sighs, the paradoxes, the vigil kept at a distance, is directed at a woman who functions as its excuse. When Mercutio afterwards conjures the absent Romeo by Rosaline's bright eyes, by her high forehead and her scarlet lip, the mock conjuration reduces the role to absurdity, rhyming love with dove in the jingle Levin quotes, and the essay treats the burlesque as criticism the play performs on itself. [Romeo's Rosaline problem, the infatuation that precedes Juliet](/2022/05/20/romeo-rosaline-infatuation-before-juliet/), is in Levin's handling the necessary first term of the play's stylistic plot: the borrowed voice must be fully established before its abandonment can register.

### Why does Levin treat Romeo's opening speeches as a borrowed costume?

Because everything in them is secondhand by design. The oxymorons are the period's stock figures, the paradoxes arrive pre-assembled, the vows are quoted from the sonnet culture's liturgy, and the beloved who inspires them never has to appear, since the performance needs an audience of friends, not a partner.

Levin's word for the young man is stereotype, and the costume reading follows from it: the speeches fit any wearer. The reading carries a consequence for performance and for teaching that the essay leaves implicit and later practice made explicit. If Romeo's first manner is a costume, then the actor's task in Act 1 is quotation, a youth reciting the period's love poetry at his friends, and the turn at the feast is not a change of object but a change of relation to language itself. Levin supports the turn with a small, checkable observation. Romeo's vow of constancy to Rosaline, made in the dialogue with Benvolio, is broken within the act, and the breaking is styled as relief. The essay's Romeo is not a hypocrite and not a fool. He is a gifted speaker who has been handed a script, and the play's first movement is his discovery that the script cannot say what the feast shows him.

## What Happens at the Feast: Levin on the Sonnet the Lovers Share

The feast scene is the essay's most delicate exhibit because it cuts both ways, and Levin knows it. The lovers' first exchange is, as he writes, formalized into a sonnet, fourteen lines shared between two speakers who have never met, built on the conceit of his lips as pilgrims and her hand as a shrine. By the essay's own index this should be formality at its thickest, the courtship code at maximum pattern. Yet Levin refuses to file the scene under the code. The formality of the encounter, he notes, was framed by the ceremonious character of the scene, its dancers and masquers and its air of old-fashioned hospitality, and inside that frame something happens that the code did not predict: the form is completed by two voices instead of one. Neither speaker knows the other's identity. Each, on learning it, answers with an exclamation coupling love and death, hers the famous recognition that her only love sprung from her only hate. The sonnet is a duet, and a duet cannot be a recitation.

Levin's treatment of the kiss line shows his ear at its finest. "You kiss by th' book" (1.5), Juliet's tease after the encore quatrain, is in his handling the scene's self-commentary, the play winking at its own procedure. To kiss by the book is to perform correctly, by the manual, and Juliet's line certifies the performance while beginning the critique of it: the book exists, the lovers know it, and their knowing is the start of their exemption from it. The essay's placement of the scene is therefore pivotal rather than contradictory. The feast is where form and feeling first coincide, the sonnet inhabited so completely that its artificiality becomes the medium of a real meeting. Everything after is the working out of that coincidence under worsening conditions, until the coincidence fails in the tomb, where Romeo speaks over a body he believes dead in the play's most sustained formal oration, and the inhabited form collapses back into ceremony performed alone.

The scene also fixes the essay's contrast with Paris. Levin will return to the official suitor at the tomb, still pronouncing set pieces over Juliet in the fifth act, "long after Romeo has abandoned his sonneteering." The feast's sonnet and the tomb's sestet are the same instrument in different hands, and the distance between them is the essay's measure of what a voice can become. One speaker learned to mean the form; the other only learned the form.

## How Does Juliet Break the Pattern in the Orchard Scene?

If Romeo supplies the essay's first term, Juliet supplies its engine. Levin's account of the orchard scene begins from a staging fact with a theatrical consequence: Romeo has violated convention, dramatic and otherwise, by overhearing what Juliet intended as a soliloquy, and Juliet therefore speaks, at first, in the one condition the courtship code never contemplates, believing herself unheard. Her first utterance is the sigh Mercutio had burlesqued in the scene before, "Ay me!" (2.2.25), and from it she proceeds, in Levin's phrase, in sincerity and simplicity, to the speech that renames the world. She calls into question not merely Romeo's name but, by implication, all names, forms, conventions, sophistications, and arbitrary dictates of society, as opposed to the appeal of instinct conveyed in the odor of a rose. "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44). Levin's gloss widens the question from nomenclature to civilization: the speech asks why anything should be bound by the label its society pasted on it, which is the feud's question and the essay's question in a single breath.

The discovery of the listener tests the discovery, and Juliet passes the test in Levin's reading by refusing to retreat into the code: "I would not for the world they saw thee here." Her monosyllabic directness, he writes, inspires the matching cadence of Romeo's response, "And but thou love me, let them find me here" (2.2). The exchange is the essay's proof of concept. Plainness here is not poverty of invention; the same scene contains "How silver-sweet sound lovers' tongues by night, / Like softest music to attending ears!" (2.2.167-168), an image of pattern admiring itself. Plainness is a choice made visible by the alternative both speakers have renounced, the dwelling on form Juliet explicitly forswears. Levin notes her incidental tribute to the proprieties, her admission that she would have played the captious beloved had he not overheard her, because the admission keeps the essay honest: the code persists as a road not taken, present in the scene as Juliet's own counterfactual.

Her later scenes extend the proof by contrast. With Paris in the Friar's cell, Juliet takes on the sophisticated tone of her visitor, denying his claims in brisk stichomythia; the moment he leaves, her lines fall into monosyllables: "O, shut the door! and when thou hast done so, / Come weep with me; past hope, past cure, past help!" (4.1.44-45). With her parents she equivocates, chops logic, practices what Levin calls the stratagems of mental reservation, because the suit of Paris is the main subject of those conversations and sincerity has no safe address in that house. Even her earliest consent was hedged in prim phraseology: "I'll look to like, if looking liking move" (1.3.97). The essay's Juliet is therefore not a creature of uniform plainness. She commands every register Verona offers, formal, equivocal, direct, and the criticism lies in the distribution: the direct register is reserved for the one relation the society has no form for, which is why it had to be invented in an orchard, in the dark, by a girl who thought she was alone.

The book metaphor lets Levin trace her crisis in a single image's migration. Lady Capulet, broaching Paris in stiffly appropriate couplets, compares his face to a volume, "This precious book of love, this unbound lover, / To beautify him only lacks a cover" (1.3.88-89), a comparison that, Levin says, emphasizes the letter at the expense of the spirit and lends Paris an aspect of unreality; the Nurse, more ingenuously, calls the same man a man of wax. Juliet later seizes the metaphor and turns it on Romeo, hearing that he has killed Tybalt: "Was ever book containing such vile matter / So fairly bound?" (3.2.83-84). The daughter has learned to read her mother's figure against its intention, appearance against reality, and the appropriation marks how far her inner life has traveled from the household's formulae. Romeo completes the figure's itinerary at the tomb, taking Paris's hand: "One writ with me in sour misfortune's book!" (5.3.82). A metaphor that began as a mother's salesmanship ends as the play's bleakest fellowship, the register of the doomed.

### What answer does Levin give to Auden's doubt about "Gallop apace"?

Levin answers that Shakespeare suits changes of style to changes of mood with perfect consistency. When Juliet feels at one with Romeo, her intonations are genuine; when she feels at odds with him, they ring unconvincing, as they should. Auden's doubt, over the oxymoron outburst that follows "Gallop apace," assumes the opposite.

Auden could not believe that the heroine of the wedding-night invocation, "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds" (3.2.1), would shortly be shrieking the oxymoron string "Beautiful tyrant! fiend angelical! ... honourable villain!" (3.2.75-79). The exchange matters beyond its occasion because it states the essay's method as a rule of evidence. Style, for Levin, is an instrument calibrated to the speaker's state, and its failures are data rather than blemishes. The oxymoron outburst is the Petrarchan machinery restarting under shock, the borrowed dialect flooding back into a mind whose news has outrun its resources, and Juliet herself registers the relapse as falsehood within the speech, asking whether the fair binding concealed vile matter. Auden's objection assumes that great feeling should keep one voice. Levin's play assumes the opposite, that voice is the first casualty and the first achievement of feeling, and that a heroine who could not relapse could not have broken through.

## What Is Levin's Evidence from Rhyme and Repetition?

The essay's most durable contribution may be its smallest: an index. Levin proposes that the lovers' development can be measured by the incidence of rhyme, heavily concentrated in the first act and gradually replaced by a blank verse which is realistic or didactic in other speakers and, in his words, unprecedentedly limpid and passionate with the lovers. He borrows a translator's authority for the phenomenon, Boris Pasternak's comment on rendering the play into Russian: "Love has no need of euphony. Truth, not sound, dwells in its heart." The index converts an impression into a census. Any reader with a text can test whether the play's rhymed couplets cluster early, around the sonnet prologue, the feast, the Friar's sententiae, and thin as the action darkens. The census holds, and its holding is why the essay cannot be waved away as a period piece of the New Criticism. Its central claim is falsifiable and survives falsification attempts, which is more than can be said for most readings of the play's style published before or since.

The second index is subtler and entirely Levin's discovery. He notices a habit, which he remarks had surprisingly escaped the attention of commentators, of stressing a word by repeating it within the line, a figure rhetoric classifies as a kind of ploce. The examples accumulate across his pages: the fathers accused of forcing their parties "To wield old partisans in hands as old, / Cank'red with peace, to part your cank'red hate" (1.1); Benvolio's report that men "fought on part and part" (1.1); Romeo confiding that his love is set on Juliet's as hers "is set on mine" (2.3.59); the reciprocity of "grace for grace and love for love" (2.3); the Friar's warning that "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6.9); Mercutio turning "point to point" against Tybalt; the Nurse's antimetabole on Juliet, "Blubbering and weeping, weeping and blubbering" (3.3). Statistics, Levin concedes, would prove illusory, since some repetitions are idiomatic or colloquial. But the play contains, by his count, well over a hundred such lines, the largest number in the first act and scarcely any left over for the fifth.

The pairing of the two indices gives the essay its analytical spine. Rhyme and reduplication are both forms of verbal symmetry, language doubling back on itself, and both decay along the same curve as the lovers' speech sheds its borrowed symmetry for statement. The symmetry, meanwhile, is the play's deep grammar, structural as much as stylistic: two households, two confidants, two foils, two deaths in one tomb. Levin reads the verbal figure as the structure's smallest unit, the cell of which the whole counterpoised design is built. When the repetitions thin, the design is not merely relaxing. It is registering, at the level of the line, the same isolation the plot enforces at the level of the scene, as tragedy separates the pair from the world's echoing arrangements and leaves each, at the end, to die in the belief that the other is dead, without the benefit of a recognition scene.

Levin reaches for Henri Bergson to name what the symmetry feels like from inside. Bergson's theory of the comic describes the imposition of geometrical form upon the living data of formless consciousness, and Levin borrows the formula for the play's stylization of love, its constant pairing and counterbalancing, its quid pro quo of Capulet and Montague, which seem mechanical and unnatural when measured against the lovers' mutuality. The borrowing is characteristic of the comparatist at work, and it does real labor in the essay. It explains why formality in this play reads as comedy persisting past its term: the geometrical treatment of living feeling is Bergson's recipe for the laughable, and Verona's ceremonies keep applying the recipe to a passion that has outgrown it, until the application turns lethal.

## How Do Antithesis and Imagery Carry Levin's Case?

Levin's antithesis section turns on a comparison with Kyd. Paris, lamenting at the tomb of the action's false corpse, cries "O love! O life! not life, but love in death!" (4.5), and Levin hears in it the echo of Hieronimo in The Spanish Tragedy: "O life! no life, but lively form of death." The echo is the measurement. In Kyd, he argues, the antithesis is purely decorative, a flourish of the revenge stage's rhetoric. In Shakespeare it is functional, because the contrarieties of the figure are the contrarieties of the plot, the attraction of ultimate opposites worked out in event as well as in phrase. Light and darkness, youth and age, haste and delay, the whole inventory of opposed terms that Caroline Spurgeon had catalogued a generation earlier, are in Levin's handling the imagery of a structure, not the decoration of a theme. He cites Spurgeon's pages on the play in his footnote, and the relation between the two critics is instructive: [Caroline Spurgeon's imagery revolution](/2027/03/05/caroline-spurgeon-imagery-revolution/) counted the light; Levin asks what the counting means, and answers that the antitheses are subsumed by the all-embracing one of Eros and Thanatos, the leitmotif of the Liebestod, the myth of the tryst in the tomb.

His exhibits are chosen for their fusion of figure and fact. The Friar rhymes "womb" with "tomb" in his first speech (2.3.9-10), and Romeo, entering the Capulet vault, hails it as "thou womb of death" (5.3.45); the ambiguity of the verb "to die" carries the same fusion into the lovers' erotic speech. The second Prologue announces that the lovers' extremities are tempered "with extreme sweet," and Capulet, marshalling his household, recapitulates the play's temporal alternation in a single breathless list: "Day, night, late, early, / At home, abroad, alone, in company, / Waking or sleeping" (3.5.177-179). Even the dramatis personae align symmetrically, as Levin observes: the names of the feuding families, like the Christian names of hero and heroine, are metrically interchangeable, though Juliet is more frequently a trochee than an amphimacer; hero and heroine are each paired with a discarded or imposed alternative, Rosaline and Paris, and admonished by an elderly confidant, Friar and Nurse; and Escalus, neutral and superior, loses "a brace of kinsmen" (5.3.295) to the interchange he polices.

The section's quiet force is that it reverses the usual account of Shakespeare's development. A younger criticism had treated the play's patterning as apprentice work, the sonneteering dramatist not yet free of the lyric habits he would outgrow in the mature tragedies. Levin treats the patterning as the subject. The play is about people caught in a symmetrical machine, two households alike in dignity and in everything else, whose very names scan alike, and the lovers' stylistic rebellion is the personal form of their structural predicament. To speak otherwise than the machine speaks is the only freedom the play imagines, which is why the essay can call their mutuality organic without sentimentality. Organic, in context, is a technical term: the one relation in Verona that grew rather than being arranged.

## Who Stays Formal in Levin's Verona?

The essay's counterweight to the lovers is not a villain but a population. Paris is its exhibit in chief, cast, as Levin notes with an Italian term, in the ungrateful part of the terzo incommodo, the inconvenient third party of the amorous triangle. As the official suitor, Paris's speeches are always formal, and they sound stilted or priggish, Levin writes, by contrast with Romeo's. The judgment is comparative, not absolute: Paris is not mocked by the play, and in the tomb he behaves with a courage the essay does not discuss. His function in the stylistic plot is simpler. He is the control case, the suitor who courts by the approved channels, through the father, in the Friar's cell, at the tomb, and whose language therefore never has occasion to change. Long after Romeo has abandoned his sonneteering, Paris will pronounce a sestet over Juliet's supposed corpse, strewing her bridal bed with flowers (5.3.12-17). The form persists in him because nothing in his experience has indicted it.

### Why is Paris still pronouncing a sestet at the tomb in Levin's scheme?

Because Paris never passed through the orchard. His courtship ran through the sanctioned forms from first to last, a father's consent, a church interview, a graveside rite, and no event in his story contradicted the forms' adequacy, so his voice keeps their shape to the end.

Levin sets the sestet, six measured lines over a body, against Romeo's abandoned sonneteering, to measure what an unexamined courtship costs in language.

The Friar presents the harder case, and Levin handles him as formality's unwitting agent. Laurence enters, in Levin's description, with his sententious couplets on the uses and abuses of natural properties, the medicinal and poisonous effects of plants: "For this, being smelt, with that part cheers each part; / Being tasted, slays all senses with the heart" (2.3.25-26). His watchword is "Wisely and slow," and his doctrine compresses itself into the play's most quoted sententia, "They stumble that run fast" (2.3.94). Yet he contributes to the grief at the sepulchre, Levin observes, by ignoring his own advice, solemnizing the marriage in haste, devising the potion plot in haste, and leaving the tomb in haste. The sententious style is not hypocrisy. It is knowledge stored in formulae that the holder cannot apply under pressure, formality as wisdom's shelving. When Romeo upbraids him in monosyllables, "Thou canst not speak of that thou dost not feel" (3.3.64), Levin hears the age-old dilemma separating the generations, and the scene becomes the essay's proof that the play distributes its stylistic plot across the whole cast: the young man's plainness indicts the old man's couplets, and the indictment is just, because the couplets were true and useless.

Escalus completes the section's census, and Levin gives him the structural dignity his office claims. The Prince occupies, in the essay's phrase, a superior and neutral position, the one figure stationed above the counterpoise, and his speech is the city's formality in its purest state: proclamation, sentence, and edict, delivered in verse that rhymes like a seal being pressed. Yet the neutrality is a form too, and it fails as forms fail in this play, by repetition without effect. Three times he must quell and sentence the rioters before the sentence holds, and the interchange of blood for blood costs him, as he reckons at the end, a brace of kinsmen, Mercutio and Paris, the two men his order could least spare. Levin's Escalus is therefore no exception to the stylistic plot but its frame: the voice that opens the play's public business in a sonnet's aftermath and closes it in a sestet, unchanged because his office forbids change, and tragic in his small way because the office was not enough.

Against both stand the play's other proponents of nature, Mercutio and the Nurse, whom Levin groups with the lovers as the formality system's internal opposition. Mercutio's interpretation of dreams is naturalistic, not to say Freudian, in Levin's deliberately provocative phrase: Queen Mab works through fantasies of wish-fulfilment, bringing love to lovers, fees to lawyers, tithe-pigs to parsons, and the moral is that desires can be mischievous. His repartee charges the air, against the theatre's limitations, with the acknowledgment that love has something to do with sex. The Nurse, bound to Juliet by the body's own history as her wet-nurse, answers from the other end of life, her garrulity set against her charge's ardors when she acts as go-between. Neither Mercutio nor the Nurse escapes form in Levin's account; his wit is a performance and her anecdotes are set pieces. But both speak from appetite and experience rather than from the book, and both are destroyed or discarded by the formal world they enlivened, he by the duelling code whose arithmetic he mocked, she by the household's decision to speak the language of the Paris match.

## How Does the Play End in Levin's Account: Form Closing In?

The essay's last movement reverses its first. The overriding pattern through which the lovers have been trying to break, call it Fortune, the stars, or what it will, ends by closing in and breaking them; their private world disappears, and the audience is left, Levin writes, in the social ambiance again. The reversal is prepared inside the fourth act, where Capulet's house bustles with preparations for a wedding, comedy's happy ending, and the news of Juliet's seeming death introduces a peripety in the old man's litany: "All things that we ordained festival / Turn from their office to black funeral; / Our instruments to melancholy bells, / Our wedding cheer to a sad burial feast; / Our solemn hymns to sullen dirges change; / Our bridal flowers serve for a buried corse, / And all things change them to the contrary" (4.5.84-90). Levin insists that the lament is not less poignant for the speaker's fondness for playing the genial host. His point is the quartet that follows, Capulet joined by his wife, the Nurse, and Paris in a lamentation that reasserts the formalities by means of what he calls virtually an operatic quartet. Grief, in the household, is immediately choral and ceremonial. The private world has no mourning of its own.

Juliet's solitude is the counter-image, and Levin marks it with her own theatrical metaphor: "My dismal scene I needs must act alone" (4.3.19). Her utter singleness, as an only child, is stressed by her father and mourned by her mother, "But one, poor one, one poor and loving child" (4.5). Tragedy, Levin generalizes, tends to isolate where comedy brings together, to reveal the uniqueness of individuals rather than what they have in common. The generalization organizes his reading of the fifth act, in which the pair who had achieved the play's one organic relation are progressively stripped of every partner in speech. Romeo buys poison from an Apothecary whose shop, envisaged with Flemish precision, replaces the Friar's cell as the potion's sinister counterpart. The lovers who had completed each other's sonnet now complete nothing together. Each dies alone, or at all events in the belief that the other lies dead, and Juliet's last words, though richly symbolic, are brief and monosyllabic: "This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3.169-170), where Levin's Kittredge text reads "rest" for the Folger's "rust," a variant that changes the dagger's fate from corrosion to repose and leaves the sense of the line, the body's sheath receiving its blade, untouched.

The Prince closes the design as he opened it, with formal verse. Three times in the play Escalus must quell and sentence the rioters; at the end he pronounces the final sestet, Levin notes, restoring order to the city-state through the lovers' sacrifice, summoning the patriarchal enemies from their opposite sides to be reconciled, and bringing the polysyllables of arraignment home, as Levin memorably puts it, by monosyllables. Precision about Levin's ending matters, because later summaries have decorated it with the tomb's furniture. The golden statues, vowed by the fathers in the play's last exchanges, receive no discussion in the essay at all. Levin's closing exhibit is not the monument but the sentence, the six lines in which authority absorbs the catastrophe back into public form, followed by his own coda on the musicians. An account of the essay that makes it a reading of epitaphs and statues is reading some other, later essay. Levin's play ends as it began, in the keeping of officials speaking set verse, with the difference that the audience has learned to hear what the set verse cannot hold.

The coda is the essay's signature flourish and its subtlest evidence. Shakespeare leaves the stage, after the quartet of lament, to the Musicians and the comic servant Peter, who quizzes them with a quotation from Richard Edwards's lyric In Commendation of Musicke and persists in his question, "Why 'silver sound'?" (4.5). The hirelings answer as hirelings, indifferent between a merry dump and a doleful one so long as they are paid. But Peter's riddle, Levin argues, touches a deeper chord of correspondence, the interconnection between discord and harmony, between impulse and discipline. "Consort," which can denote a concert or a companionship, becomes the fighting word that motivates the duellists' unharmonious pricksong in the street scene (3.1). The lark's "sweet division" sounds harsh to Juliet because it proclaims that the lovers must be divided (3.5.29). And silver is the essay's last word on the lovers' register because Romeo, in the orchard, had sworn by the moon "That tips with silver all these fruit-tree tops" (2.2.108), and because Shakespeare made his audience imagine "How silver-sweet sound lovers' tongues by night, / Like softest music to attending ears!" (2.2.167-168). Form, at the essay's close, is music: pattern transfigured into the medium in which impulse and discipline are the same thing. The flourish is not decoration. It is Levin restating his thesis at the level of sound, in the register the thesis claims the lovers reached.

## Is Levin's Opposition of Form and Sincerity Too Neat?

The complication the essay invites, and which any honest examination must press, is that its governing contrast threatens to simplify the play it illuminates. Three bodies of counter-evidence stand in the way of the slogan. The first is internal: the play's most sincere moments are repeatedly its most formal. The lovers meet inside a sonnet and the meeting is the least artificial event in Verona. The wedding night is heralded, in Levin's own genre terms, by a prothalamium, Juliet's "Gallop apace" soliloquy (3.2.1), a solo spoken in breathless anticipation of a companion, and followed by the dawn parting's antiphonal debate of nightingale and lark, in which the lovers trade the parts of a dawn-song as formally as any pair in the lyric tradition. When the parting completes its paradox, Romeo's line is the most pattern-saturated in the scene: "More light and light; more dark and dark our woes!" (3.5.36), doubly reduplicating at the very pitch of feeling. If sincerity shed form, these passages should not exist. Levin's index says the repetitions thin toward the fifth act, and they do, but the thinning coexists with summits of pattern at the story's emotional peaks, and the essay's framework has to absorb them rather than explain them away.

Levin's text, read closely, does absorb them, and the absorption is why the essay outlasts its slogan. The naturalness he claims for the lovers is, in his own sentence, "artfully gained," a phrase that concedes the whole objection in advance: the plain style is an achievement of art, not an escape from it. His lovers do not stop performing set forms. They stop being performed by them. The sonnet at the feast is spoken by two people completing each other's lines; the same fourteen-line shape in a sonnet sequence would be one voice addressing an absent lady. The aubade debate is a dawn-song inhabited by two people who disagree inside it, Romeo discerning the herald of the morn more realistically than Juliet wishes, the form bending under the pressure of opposed desires. The distinction that survives scrutiny is not between form and its absence but between form as a script recited at the world and form as an instrument played by a speaker who means it. Levin's title promises the first distinction. His essay delivers the second, finer one.

The second body of counter-evidence is Edward Snow's. In "Language and Sexual Difference in Romeo and Juliet," published in the 1985 collection Shakespeare and the Question of Theory, Snow accepts Levin's framework and contests its symmetry. The breakthrough Levin describes, Snow argues, is much more Juliet's than Romeo's: her desires break the Petrarchan mould, while his remain dominated by its conventions far longer than the shared trajectory suggests. The evidence is not trivial. Romeo's last great speech, over Juliet's body in the tomb, is a sustained formal oration, complete with apostrophes to death and decay, a rhetoric of set admiration for the corpse's beauty, and the extended book figure Levin himself traced to its end. Juliet's last speech is eight words of monosyllables and a dagger. If the play's stylistic plot were a single graph, the two lines would converge. They diverge, and Levin's own index, applied separately to the two voices, would show it: her repetitions and rhymes collapse as his ceremonial resources reassemble for the tomb.

The tomb scene is where the correction can be tested line by line. Romeo enters with a crowbar, a mattock, and a letter, the tools of a man performing a rite, and his speech matches the equipment: an extended apostrophe to the vault, personified, addressed, and cursed in figures that belong to the rhetoric of set lament. He admires Juliet's beauty as a spectacle, light in the darkness, a banner of color in a cheek, and the admiration is phrased as connoisseurship, the Petrarchan catalogue returning in a charnel house. Levin's own essay, notably, does not linger here; its Romeo in the fifth act is chiefly the man who extends the book metaphor to Paris and dies believing. The reticence is the tell. On the essay's own index, the tomb oration is a relapse into the first act's manner, and an advocate for the unamended thesis would have to argue it away. Snow simply declines to argue it away, and reads the relapse as the fact of the matter: Romeo dies inside the idiom he began in, transfigured in feeling but unreformed in rhetoric, while Juliet, whose rhetoric was reformed by the orchard, dies in monosyllables. The play's last exchange is thus not a duet but a final asymmetry, his set speech and her eight words, and the asymmetry is measurable in exactly the units Levin taught his readers to count.

The adjudication this article reaches is that Snow wins the correction and Levin keeps the thesis. Snow is right that the movement is uneven, gendered, and incomplete in Romeo, whose education in sincerity the play leaves unfinished at his death; the tomb oration is the Petrarchan machinery running one final time, beautifully and fatally, over a woman its speaker believes dead and therefore addresses as an image once more. But the correction presupposes the framework it corrects. Only a reader who has learned from Levin to track registers can plot the two voices separately and find them diverging, and Levin's pages contain the seed of the asymmetry: his Juliet is the one who renounces compliment, questions names, falls into monosyllables; his Romeo is introduced as the stereotype and measured by his distance from her directness. Snow sharpened Levin with Levin's tools. The thesis that survives is the amended one: the play moves from convention toward sincerity along two unequal paths, hers steep and completed, his partial and interrupted, and the inequality is part of the tragedy rather than a refutation of the reading.

The third body of evidence is older than the essay and cuts in its favor by arriving early. Mahood's Shakespeare's Wordplay had argued in 1957, three years before Levin, that the play's verbal patterning, its puns and quibbles, works as meaning rather than ornament, woven into the design of opposed worlds. Levin's functional antithesis is Mahood's point at the level of the figure rather than the word: what looks like decoration is load-bearing. Between them the two books dismantled the older habit of apologizing for the play's verbal surface, the habit that treated its conceits as youthful excess to be excused on the way to the mature tragedies. After Levin, the surface is the argument's home ground. A critic who finds the essay's opposition too neat usually owes the terms of the objection, form weighed against feeling, speech weighed against speaker, to the essay itself.

### What exactly did Snyder inherit from Levin's essay?

Snyder inherited the transcendence formula and the habit of treating a convention as a dramatic force. Levin had argued that the play invokes the usages of romantic comedy in order to transcend them, through lovers who repudiate an artificial language; Snyder enlarged the unit from diction to dramaturgy.

She asked what happens to a whole comic structure when its expected ending is withdrawn, and the inheritance is visible in her architecture: a pattern established, inhabited, and then broken from inside.

## How Did Snyder Build on Levin, and What Did the Essay Change?

Susan Snyder's The Comic Matrix of Shakespeare's Tragedies, published by Princeton University Press in 1979, opens its account with this play, and its relation to Levin is the clearest case of the essay's generative power. Where Levin tracked the repudiation of artificial language, Snyder tracked the repudiation of artificial plot: the play sets up the machinery of romantic comedy, the young lovers, the blocking father, the bawdy go-between, the friendly friar, the expected marriage that reconciles the houses, and then lets the machinery's assumptions fail one by one, until the forms that should have produced a wedding produce a funeral. [Susan Snyder's account of comedy turning to tragedy](/2026/12/11/susan-snyder-comedy-to-tragedy/) is Levin's stylistic plot rewritten as a generic one, and Snyder's comedy matrix, the pattern of expectations an audience brings from the comic stage, is formality at the scale of the whole action. The lovers break the pattern in both essays. What differs is the evidence: Levin counts rhymes and repetitions, Snyder counts devices and outcomes. The two readings have been companions ever since, taught together, cited together, and the pairing is just, because each supplies the other's missing dimension. Levin explains how the tragedy sounds from inside a speech. Snyder explains how it feels from inside a structure.

The essay also changed the infrastructure of reading: editions, syllabi, and the questions students are trained to ask. A modern editor preparing this play inherits Levin's problems whether or not the inheritance is acknowledged. The shared sonnet at the feast must be presented so that its shape can be seen on the page, the dawn parting glossed against the dawn-song tradition Levin invoked, the Friar's sententiae marked as the sententious style Laurence himself cannot live by. René Weis's Arden Third Series edition of 2012, like Brian Gibbons's Arden Second Series edition of 1980 before it, introduces the play to its readers through an account of its language in which the formal patterning and its loosening are central facts, the climate Levin made. The classroom inheritance is more diffuse and more complete. The standard schoolroom observation that Romeo talks differently about Rosaline and about Juliet, that Juliet speaks more plainly as the play darkens, that the ending sounds ceremonial because ceremony is what is left when the lovers are gone, is Levin's essay dissolved into common sense. Tracing how those perceptions were assembled, from the Kittredge text and the 1960 journal through the anthologies, is the sort of source work a personal research library exists to hold together, and a tool like [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) is built for exactly that keeping: the essay, its reprints, its correctors, and the play's editions filed as one dossier rather than scattered across a career of reading.

Levin's own later career frames the achievement by contrast. He wrote no second essay on the play, and his books ranged elsewhere, to the American Renaissance, to Joyce, to the theory of literary history. The nine pages stood alone, un-revised and un-expanded, and their authority grew without his advocacy. That is the final evidence for the landmark verdict. Readings that depend on their author's school fade with the school. Levin's reading depended on a census any reader can repeat, rhyme thinning, repetition thinning, set speeches migrating from the lovers to the world, and the census returns the same figures in every generation that bothers to count.

## How Does Levin Treat the Play's Set Speeches?

Every early modern play is stocked with set speeches, and a lesser reading would treat them as interruptions of the action. Levin treats them as the action's formal register made audible, the places where a character steps out of exchange and into performance, and he grades them by the same index he applies everywhere: who is performing, for whom, and at what cost. The Prince's first edict is a set speech of law, delivered in rhyme over a street brawl, authority speaking in the couplets of proclamation. The Friar's entrance soliloquy on the virtues of plants is a set speech of doctrine, its couplets as measured as its morality. Mercutio's Queen Mab flight in 1.4 is the set speech of wit, a bravura catalogue that begins in miniature folklore and accelerates until Romeo has to stop it. Paris's flower-strewing at the tomb is the set speech of courtship persisting past the death of its occasion. In each case the form is complete in itself, a little anthology piece with a beginning, a swelling middle, and a cadence, and Levin's question is always what the completeness conceals.

The first set speech of the play belongs to no character at all, and Levin's framework explains why the fact matters. The Chorus steps forward before the action and delivers the whole story, feud, love, deaths, and reconciliation, inside the strictest shape the period possessed. A sonnet is a room with the door locked; to tell a tragedy inside one is to announce that the events are already formalized, already known, already past the reach of anyone's improvisation. Levin does not make the Prologue his opening exhibit, preferring Juliet's farewell to compliment, but his reading depends on the frame the Chorus sets: everything the audience watches will happen inside a shape announced in advance, and the lovers' achievement will be to make the foreknown feel unforeseen. The second Prologue extends the frame's honesty, summarizing the first act's machinery in another sonnet and promising that the extremities of the situation will be tempered with extreme sweet. Set speech, in the Chorus's hands, is fate's own genre, and the play's long loosening of form is also its long argument with the Chorus's opening certainty.

His boldest handling is reserved for Queen Mab. Where a piety of the period might have read the speech as fairy machinery, Levin reads it as psychology: Mercutio's interpretation of dreams, he writes, is naturalistic, not to say Freudian, since Queen Mab operates through fantasies of wish-fulfilment, bringing love to lovers, fees to lawyers, and tithe-pigs to parsons, and the moral is that desires can be mischievous. The provocation is deliberate and dated in the best way, the comparatist importing Vienna into Verona to make a point no period vocabulary makes as cleanly. The speech that looks most like pure ornament, the play's most famous digression, becomes in his hands a critique of the dream-life the lovers are about to enact, delivered by the character who will die for treating the world as material for performance. The set speech indicts its speaker. Mercutio's virtuosity is formality's comic face, pattern enjoyed for its own sake, and the essay's distribution of sympathy follows the distribution of risk: the characters who can be surprised out of their set speeches live inwardly, and the characters who cannot, die or officiate.

The tomb gives the argument its grimmest confirmation. Romeo's address over Juliet's body is the play's final set speech, an oration with apostrophes, rhetorical questions, and a sustained conceit of death as a rival lover, and it is delivered to an audience of corpses. Levin's framework explains its terrible aptness without a word of censure. The set speech was always a performance for listeners, and Romeo, believing his listener dead, performs for the only congregation left to him, the dead themselves, in the style his first act taught him. The migration the essay tracks is complete: set speeches began around the lovers, in prologues and edicts and parental formulae, colonized Romeo's own voice while Rosaline lasted, were driven out of the lovers' exchanges by the orchard's plainness, and return at the end to reclaim the hero when the heroine can no longer answer him. Form, in the tomb, is what isolation sounds like.

## What Does Levin Make of Names, the Play's Smallest Forms?

A name is the smallest form a person wears, and Levin's essay is the first reading of the play to build on that scale. His observation about the dramatis personae is prosodic before it is thematic: the family names scan alike, the hero's and heroine's names are metrically interchangeable, and the feud is therefore inscribed in the verse's very arithmetic, two houses whose labels occupy identical shapes in the line. Against that background the orchard speech acquires its full force. When Juliet asks what a name is and answers with the rose, she is not coining a pretty sentiment. She is proposing, as Levin glosses it, that the appeal of instinct, directly conveyed in the odor of a rose, outranks every name, form, convention, and arbitrary dictate of society. The rose is the essay's emblem because it is the feud's counterexample: a thing whose reality survives the loss of its label, in a city where labels kill.

Levin follows the monosyllable through the play with a jeweler's attention. Juliet's first recorded utterance, in the Nurse's anecdote of the weaning, was the syllable "Ay," the glad assent she spoke as a toddler falling on her face (1.3). In the orchard she predicts Romeo's profession in the same syllable, "I know thou wilt say 'Ay'" (2.2.90). In the third act, waiting for news of the fight, she builds a speech out of the syllable's homophones, Ay and I and eye, asking the Nurse to answer in brief sounds that will determine her weal or woe: "Say thou but 'Ay,' / And that bare vowel 'I' shall poison more / Than the death-darting eye of cockatrice" (3.2.45-51). Levin's commentary on the passage is the essay's method at its purest. A vowel becomes an organ, the eye that poisons; assent becomes identity, the I that is or is not itself; and the whole metaphysics of the play's naming is compressed into the smallest units of speech, sounds too brief to be formal at all. The trajectory from the sonnet to the syllable is the trajectory of the essay: form shrinking as feeling concentrates, until a single letter carries the weight that fourteen lines carried at the feast.

The conjuration scene shows the name treated as a spell, and Levin's reading of it belongs in this section of his evidence. Mercutio, hunting the vanished Romeo in the dark, conjures him by Rosaline's attributes, her bright eyes, her high forehead, her scarlet lip, her fine foot and quivering thigh, as if the right catalogue of names could compel the named man to appear. It is the feud's logic in erotic dress: the label, correctly pronounced, is supposed to govern the thing. Romeo's answer from the shadows refuses the premise in the plainest available cadence, "He jests at scars that never felt a wound" (2.2.1), a line whose very flatness announces that the man being summoned by the old names is no longer at their address. Levin sets the conjuration immediately before the orchard in his exposition because the sequence is the argument: first the name as spell, then the name questioned, then the farewell to compliment, the whole critique of naming transacted in a single night's movement from the street to the wall to the window.

The point radiates outward to the play's politics of naming. Verona runs on labels: Montague and Capulet as party cries, the Prince's proclamations naming offences, the Friar's sentence naming banishment, the tomb's inscriptions waiting to name the dead. Levin's lovers are the only characters who interrogate the system from inside, and their interrogation is linguistic before it is anything else. Later critics built whole studies on this foundation; Barbara Estrin's account of the art of naming in the play takes its departure from Levin's page on the rose, quoting his sentence about names and forms as the charter for a feminist and rhetorical reading he did not live to systematize. The essay's influence is measurable in such borrowings. It did not merely propose a thesis. It identified the play's smallest working parts and left them tagged for the next half century of readers.

## What Does Levin Mean by Calling the Play a Form of Romantic Tragedy?

Genre is form at the largest scale, and Levin's essay is as exact about the play's generic form as about its syllables. Romantic tragedy, he begins by noting, was a contradiction in terms for the play's first audiences: legend was the proper matter for serious drama, and romance was the stuff of the comic stage. The First Quarto's title page, which he quotes, advertised "an excellent conceited Tragedie of Romeo and Juliet," and the advertisement's confidence conceals an experiment, the one H. B. Charlton's British Academy lecture of 1939 had framed as experimental tragedy and F. M. Dickey's Not Wisely But Too Well, which Levin cites, had lately been documenting. Shakespeare's innovation, in Levin's description, was to transcend the usages of romantic comedy, which are therefore very much in evidence, particularly at the beginning, and to let his leading characters earn a tragic dimension by repudiating the artificial language and the superficial code of the comic world that formed them.

Within that frame he distributes the inheritances with a comparatist's neatness. Comedy set the pattern of courtship, formally embodied in a dance. History set the pattern of conflict, formally embodied in a duel. The play might also be characterized, he proposes, as an anti-revenge play, in which hostile emotions are finally pacified by the interplay of kindlier ones, against the grain of the Kydian machinery its antitheses echo. Each generic form is a social form as well. The dance is courtship as the city approves it, measured and witnessed. The duel is honor as the city enforces it, Tybalt's punctilio and Mercutio's parody of the duelling book. The revenge plot is the feud's own genre, the story Verona keeps trying to tell about itself, and the lovers' tragedy is what happens when two people decline every genre on offer and improvise a relation for which the culture has no form, until the culture's forms close over them. Levin's genre argument and his style argument are the same argument at different magnifications, which is the deepest reason the essay coheres: form is his subject at the level of the syllable, the speech, the scene, and the kind.

It also explains the essay's handling of the play's much-discussed haste. The temporal alternation of the action, with its accelerating continuity, belongs to the generic experiment: comedy's leisurely courtship calendar, the months of Brooke's source poem, compressed into days, so that the forms of courtship must be performed at a speed that exposes them. Capulet's breathless catalogue of day and night, late and early, waking and sleeping, is the sound of a household whose calendar has collapsed. Levin does not make haste a theme in the moralizing manner, the young punished for rashness. He makes it a pressure under which style is tested, the tempo at which borrowed speech fails first because borrowing takes time.

## Why Does Levin Call the Play the Summit of a Lyrical Period?

Levin places the play in its author's career with a pair of phrases that later criticism has absorbed as facts. Romeo and Juliet, he writes, was the most elaborate product of Shakespeare's so-called lyrical period, and his first successful experiment in tragedy. The sonnets and erotic poems had won the young dramatist the reputation of an English Ovid, and the play is what happened when that lyric gift was asked to carry a tragic action. Levin's point in the placement is generic before it is chronological. A lyric poet's habits, the sonnet's fourteen lines, the conceit's wit, the set speech's swell, are the play's raw materials, and its tragedy is the story of those materials being tested past their design limits. The experiment succeeds, on his account, because the play knows its own materials are inadequate and dramatizes the inadequacy: the sonnet culture gives the lovers their first language and their first illusion, and the action is the education that follows.

The novelty of the result is easy to underestimate at this distance, and Levin, citing the scholarship of his moment, insists on recovering it. Contemporaries, he notes, would have been surprised, and possibly shocked, at seeing lovers taken so seriously. Tragedy's matter was legend and history; love belonged to comedy's lower altitude, and a tragedy whose center is a pair of adolescent lovers, neither royal nor legendary, broke the period's decorum as surely as Juliet breaks its rhetoric. F. M. Dickey's Not Wisely But Too Well, the study Levin cites on the love tragedies, had lately been arguing that the shock was the point, that Shakespeare was inventing a kind of seriousness the stage had not housed. Levin's essay gives that invention its stylistic mechanism. The lovers are taken seriously because the play lets them outgrow the language in which their culture took love lightly, the paradoxes and compliments of the sonnet game, and find a register in which a feeling can be weighed. Tragedy, in this account, is not a subject Shakespeare chose. It is a pressure his lyric forms could not withstand, and the cracking of the forms is the sound the essay teaches its readers to hear.

The placement also explains the essay's tenderness toward the play's supposed faults. The features an older criticism apologized for, the punning, the rhyming, the conceits piled on conceits, are for Levin the experiment's necessary conditions: a play about the education of feeling must begin inside the uneducated idiom, at full strength, or the education has nothing to measure itself against. His Shakespeare is not a young writer failing to be the later tragedian. He is a lyric poet discovering, in public, what his instruments cannot do, and building the discovery into the action so completely that the audience undergoes the same education at the same speed. That is why Levin can call the experiment successful in the same breath as he calls its hero a stereotype and its heroine's great speeches a repudiation. The success is the repudiation, staged.

## How Has the Essay Been Received, Anthologized, and Argued With?

The essay's afterlife divides into three channels, and each confirms the landmark verdict from a different side. The first channel is anthology. Kermode's Four Centuries of Shakespearian Criticism carried the essay to a general readership within five years of its journal appearance, and collections devoted to the play have kept it in print as the representative modern statement on the play's style. Anthology is a conservative force; editors reprint what syllabi demand, and syllabi demand what teachers find indispensable. Nine pages that began as a journal article became, by this channel, part of the play's standard furniture, assigned alongside the text itself.

The second channel is use. Working critics cite the essay not as a monument but as a tool, usually for one of its portable instruments. A study of banishment in Shakespeare reaches for Levin's observation about rhyme and formality to explain the Prince's sentence. A teaching article builds classroom exercises on his pages about the sonnet and the orchard. Estrin's study of naming takes his rose sentence as its charter. The pattern of citation is diagnostic: the essay is raided for parts, and the parts work out of context, because each was machined from countable evidence. Readings that travel as slogans fade; readings that travel as instruments get built into other scholars' benches.

The third channel is argument, and it is the channel that matters most. Snow's correction of the essay's symmetry, Snyder's enlargement of its generic frame, and the running objection that its opposition is too neat all proceed from inside its terms. Even the play's textual scholars meet the essay on its ground when they observe that the lovers' first exchange is set as continuous dialogue in the early quartos and displayed as a sonnet by modern editors, a fact about presentation that only matters because Levin taught readers to see the sonnet as an event. An essay that organizes its opponents' vocabulary for sixty years has done what landmark criticism does. It has not ended discussion of the play's language. It has decided what the discussion is about.

## What Does Levin's Essay Leave Out?

A landmark's limits are part of its definition, and Levin's are unusually legible. The essay is a reading of the play as a verbal artifact, and the theatre enters it only at the edges, in a footnote to Richard Hosley's work on the upper stage and in the observation that Romeo and Juliet experience their exaltation aloft, his descent by the rope ladder figuring the fall toward the tomb. Nothing in the nine pages asks what an audience hears at performance speed, whether the rhyme census registers in a theatre as it does in a study, or how an actor's delivery might make a formal speech sound sincere and a plain one hollow. The essay's evidence is the page's, arranged for a reader who can pause, count, and compare. Later performance criticism would insist that the play's forms live in bodies, voices, and timing, and that the same sonnet can be played as ritual or as discovery. Levin's framework can absorb that insistence, since his distinction concerns the relation of speech to occasion, but he does not make the argument himself. His play is read, not staged.

The textual dimension is likewise bracketed. Levin cites Kittredge's edition as his instrument and never asks how the early quartos distribute the forms he counts, whether the 1597 text's rougher state thins the pattern or merely its pointing. The question matters because his indices are statistical in spirit: a census of rhyme and repetition is, in principle, a census of a particular text, and the new bibliography's later work on the play's transmission gives the census a second life he did not foresee. It is a tribute to the argument's design that it survives translation into other editions; the pattern is gross enough, in the statistician's sense, to show through the variants. But the essay's silence about its own textual basis is a period mark, the confidence of a moment when the edited text could pass for the play.

Politics and gender mark the third limit, and Snow's correction already opened it. The feud, in Levin, is a pattern, two households in counterpoise, its symmetry an aesthetic fact. What the feud is for, whom it enriches, how it conscripts daughters as currency in the marriage market, the essay leaves to others, and the marriage market is where its silences gather. Juliet's equivocations with her parents are, for Levin, evidence about register; for a later generation they are evidence about power, a girl of not yet fourteen negotiating survival inside a household that can disown her. The essay's aesthetic frame does not falsify those readings. It brackets their questions, and the bracketing is why the feminist and historicist work of the following decades could present itself as a departure rather than a footnote. Levin had identified the play's smallest forms. What the forms do in a society, beyond patterning its speech, his nine pages decline to say.

None of this dethrones the essay, and the present article's verdict does not ask it to answer for its omissions. A nine-page article that also settled the play's staging, its textual transmission, its politics, and its gender economy would be a book, and probably a worse one. The limits matter for a different reason: they define the exact shape of the essay's authority. Within the territory it claims, the verbal surface and its dramatic meanings, its demonstrations remain the standard against which successors measure themselves. Outside that territory it makes no claims, and the successors who work there, on performance, on the quartos, on Verona's marriage market, are not refuting Levin. They are farming land his map left blank, often with instruments his map taught them to read.

## Does Levin's Own Essay Obey the Pattern It Describes?

A reader who finishes the nine pages has been put through the play's own experience in miniature, and the design is too consistent to be accidental. The essay opens inside quotation, with Juliet's voice framed by Levin's syntax, and its first movement is densely patterned, balanced clauses, antitheses answering antitheses, the prose wearing the first act's involution like a costume. The middle loosens into census and catalogue, rhyme counted, repetitions listed, the scholarly machinery displayed without embarrassment. The close arrives at music, at silver sound and the lovers' tongues, in sentences that have shed the opening's counterpoise for something nearer song. Levin's essay begins in form, argues its way through evidence, and ends in the register it claims the lovers reached. Whether the performance was calculated or instinctive, it enacts the thesis that form is not feeling's enemy but its first language, abandoned only by being fulfilled.

The performance extends to the essay's famous compression. Nine pages is an epigrammatic length for a landmark, and Levin wastes none of them on the apparatus of academic self-defense, no literature review, no methodological preamble, a single footnote of acknowledgments and a handful of learned asides. The confidence is itself a critical position. Where the character critics had needed chapters to establish a psyche, Levin establishes a system in the space of a lecture, because a system, unlike a psyche, can be stated once and tested everywhere. The essay's brevity is why it could be anthologized whole, taught whole, and memorized in its outlines by generations of students who never opened Shakespeare Quarterly. Its form made its fortune, which is the most Levinian sentence that can be written about it.

## Verdict: What Survives of Levin's Essay?

The essay survives almost entire, and the part that needs amendment is the part its title advertises. As a binary, form against formality, sincerity against convention, the reading is too neat for the play it explains, and the counter-evidence is genuine: the shared sonnet, the dawn débat, and the patterned cry of "More light and light; more dark and dark our woes!" are summits of form at the summits of feeling, and Romeo's voice, as Snow showed, never completes the journey Juliet's voice completes. As an account of how the play teaches its audience to hear, passage by passage, the difference between speech the code extracts and speech the occasion compels, the essay has no superior and few rivals. Its evidence is countable. Its central perception, that the play contains its own critique of artificiality and enacts that critique in the lovers' changing diction, reorganized the criticism of the play's language permanently, enlarged itself in Snyder's hands into a reading of the play's genre, and survives its correctors because the correctors must use its instruments to correct it.

There is a practical corollary for anyone coming to the essay fresh. Read it with the play open, and repeat its census on a single act before trusting or resisting it. Count the rhymes in Act 1 against Act 5; mark the lines that stress a word by repeating it; note which characters are given set speeches and which are given exchanges. The exercise takes an hour and it changes the reader's ear permanently, because the pattern, once heard, cannot be unheard, and the argument about the pattern's meaning can then proceed on evidence the reader owns. Few critical essays offer so direct a transaction. Levin does not ask to be believed. He asks to be checked, and he survives the checking, which is why his nine pages are assigned to students who will never read another line of Shakespeare criticism and why scholars who dispute his conclusions keep his instruments in their hands while disputing. An essay that trains its own auditors has secured the only kind of permanence criticism can honestly claim: not agreement, but use.

The final judgment is therefore double, and the doubleness is Levinian. The slogan should be retired; the essay should be read. Retire the notion that the lovers escape form, since the play ends in a sestet and a rhymed couplet and its most loving exchanges are sonnet and dawn-song. Keep the essay's actual discovery, that Shakespeare dramatized the difference between borrowed and inhabited speech so completely that a reader can be taught to hear it in a single scene, the orchard, where a girl who believed herself alone declined to dwell on form and said farewell to compliment. Every serious account of the play's style written since 1960 is a footnote to those nine pages or an argument with them, and an argument with Levin is still conducted on Levin's ground. That is what a landmark is.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Levin summary, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch Levin summary is the table in this article that lays out Harry Levin's evidence device by device: the Prologue sonnet, the feast's dance and shared sonnet, Romeo's Petrarchan oxymorons, the incidence of rhyme, the figure of repetition, the Friar's couplets, the dawn débat, the Act 4 lament, and the Prince's closing sestet. The pattern it exposes is the curve at the heart of Levin's essay. Formal symmetry is thickest at the play's opening, thins steadily as the lovers' speech sheds its borrowed pattern, and masses again at the end in the ceremonies of officials and mourners. Read down its rows, the summary lets a student test Levin's thesis against the play in minutes, checking where each device stands and what work the essay shows it doing.

### Q: In which journal and year did "Form and Formality in Romeo and Juliet" first appear, and how long is the essay?

The essay first appeared in Shakespeare Quarterly, in the Winter 1960 issue, volume 11, number 1, and it runs to nine pages, pages 3 to 11. Levin signed it from Harvard University, where he became the first Irving Babbitt Professor of Comparative Literature in the same year. The journal was then edited from the Folger Shakespeare Library and stood as the profession's central Shakespeare periodical, so the essay addressed the whole field at once rather than a school within it. Its brevity is part of its history. Frank Kermode reprinted the whole essay in Four Centuries of Shakespearian Criticism in 1965, and anthologies of the play's criticism have kept it in circulation since, so that nine journal pages became the standard modern statement on the play's style. Few essays of its length have organized a subject so completely or for so long.

### Q: Which edition of the play did Levin use for his quotations and line references?

Levin used the separate edition of G. L. Kittredge's text published at Boston in 1940, as his essay's first footnote states. The choice matters for anyone checking his evidence. His line numbers can sit a line or two away from the numbers in a modern edition such as the Folger Shakespeare, edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine, and his text occasionally differs in a single word, as where Kittredge prints "rest" in Juliet's dagger line and the Folger prints "rust." Levin's argument survives the displacements because it rests on distribution rather than on single lines: where rhyme and repetition cluster in the play is the same in any responsible edition.

### Q: What formula from Sidney's Astrophel and Stella does Levin borrow for the lovers' development?

Levin borrows the opening of Sidney's first sonnet, "Fool said my muse to me, look in thy heart and write," and offers it as the formula of the lovers' development. In Sidney's sequence the line is the anti-Petrarchan turn, the moment the poet is told to stop imitating other men's inventions and copy the feeling in his own heart. Levin argues that Romeo and Juliet enact the same turn as drama. The lovers begin by talking the borrowed language of the sonnet culture, Romeo most completely, and acquire depth by expressly repudiating that artificial language for speech drawn from their own occasion. The borrowing is characteristic of the comparatist Levin, who reads the play as an event in European letters.

### Q: What does Levin mean by calling Paris the terzo incommodo of the play?

Levin means that Paris is cast in the stock part Italian comedy calls the terzo incommodo, the inconvenient third party who completes an amorous triangle by being in the way. As the official suitor, Paris courts through the sanctioned channels, winning a father's consent rather than a daughter's heart, and his speeches, Levin writes, are always formal and often sound stilted or priggish beside Romeo's. The label is structural rather than contemptuous. Paris exists in the essay as the control case, the suitor whose experience never contradicts the forms, so that his voice keeps their shape from the first interview to the sestet he pronounces over Juliet's body in the tomb.

### Q: Why does Levin quote Pasternak's remark that love has no need of euphony?

Levin quotes Boris Pasternak, the Russian poet who translated the play, to certify his rhyme index from a practitioner's side. The index shows rhyme concentrated heavily in the first act and gradually replaced by blank verse that grows, in Levin's words, unprecedentedly limpid and passionate in the lovers' mouths. Pasternak's comment, "Love has no need of euphony; truth, not sound, dwells in its heart," gives the census an artist's warrant: a translator who had to reproduce the play's music in another language had felt the same thinning of ornament as feeling deepens. Levin uses the remark as testimony, not decoration, the evidence of a working poet set beside the evidence of the count.

### Q: What is the figure Levin calls ploce, and where in the play did he find it thickest?

Ploce, in Levin's use, is the rhetorical figure that stresses a word by repeating it within the line, as in "cank'red with peace, to part your cank'red hate" or the Friar's "These violent delights have violent ends." Levin remarks that the habit had surprisingly escaped commentators before him, and he counts well over a hundred such lines in the play. He found the figure thickest in the first act and scarcely present at all in the fifth, and he treats that distribution as an index parallel to rhyme's: verbal symmetry, language doubling on itself, decays as the lovers' speech sheds its borrowed patterns and as tragedy isolates the speakers from the world's echoing arrangements. He is candid that statistics would prove illusory at the level of the single line, since some repetitions are idiomatic or colloquial; the count persuades in aggregate, as a climate rather than as a proof.

### Q: How does Levin use Hieronimo's lament in The Spanish Tragedy as a foil for Shakespeare's antitheses?

Levin sets Paris's cry "O love! O life! not life, but love in death!" beside its model, Hieronimo's "O life! no life, but lively form of death" in Thomas Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy, and argues that the same figure does different work in the two plays. In Kyd, he says, the antithesis is purely decorative, a flourish of revenge rhetoric. In Shakespeare it is functional, because the contrarieties of the phrase are the contrarieties of the plot itself: love and death are not merely balanced in a line but exchanged throughout the action, from the Friar's rhyming of womb with tomb to the lovers' tryst in the vault. The comparison lets Levin claim that Shakespeare's patterning is meaning, not ornament.

### Q: Where do Mercutio and the Nurse stand in Levin's scheme of form and nature?

Levin places Mercutio and the Nurse with the lovers, as nature's other proponents against Verona's formal system. Mercutio's reading of dreams is naturalistic in his account, a wish-fulfilment machinery that mocks the Petrarchan vapouring of Romeo's set manner, and his repartee insists, against the period stage's limitations, that love has something to do with the body. The Nurse is bound to Juliet by the body's history, having been her wet-nurse, and her garrulous anecdotes speak from appetite and memory rather than from any book. Neither escapes performance; his wit is a turn and her stories are set pieces. But both draw their speech from experience, and Levin's scheme measures every other voice against that standard.

### Q: Why does Levin end his essay with Peter, the musicians, and the question "Why silver sound?"?

Levin ends there because the musicians' scene, which looks like comic filler after the Act 4 lament, states his thesis in the key of music. Peter quizzes the hirelings with a line from Richard Edwards's In Commendation of Musicke and persists in asking why music is praised as silver sound. Their answers are mercenary, but Levin argues that the riddle touches the correspondence between discord and harmony, impulse and discipline: "consort" becomes the fighting word of the duel scene, the lark's sweet division sounds harsh because it divides the lovers, and Romeo had already sworn by the moon that tips the fruit-tree tops with silver. The coda converts the essay's whole argument into an image of pattern transfigured into feeling.

### Q: What work does Bergson's theory of comedy do inside Levin's argument?

Bergson's theory supplies Levin with a name for what formality feels like from inside. In Bergson's account, the comic arises from the imposition of geometrical form upon the living data of formless consciousness, the mechanical encrusted on the living. Levin borrows the formula for the play's stylization of love: the constant pairing and counterbalancing, the symmetrical households, the quid pro quo of Capulet and Montague, seem mechanical and unnatural when set against the lovers' mutuality. The borrowing explains why Verona's ceremonies read as comedy persisting past its term. A city that keeps applying geometrical arrangements to a passion that has outgrown them will produce laughter first and corpses later, and Levin's Bergson marks the exact point where the two meet.

### Q: How does Edward Snow's reading correct Levin's account of Romeo's language?

Edward Snow, in "Language and Sexual Difference in Romeo and Juliet" of 1985, accepts Levin's framework and corrects its symmetry. The escape from Petrarchan convention that Levin describes is, Snow argues, much more complete in Juliet than in Romeo: her desire breaks the mould of the sonnet culture, while his remains dominated by its conventions far longer. The evidence is strongest at the tomb, where Romeo delivers a sustained formal oration over the body, apostrophes, catalogues, and all, while Juliet wakes to speak a handful of monosyllables and act. Snow's correction matters because it is made with Levin's own instruments, counting registers voice by voice, and it leaves the essay's direction intact while dividing its single trajectory into two unequal ones.

### Q: How has Levin's essay shaped the way modern editions present the play?

Levin's essay shapes editions by setting the problems an editor must solve on the page. The lovers' first exchange has to be presented so that its sonnet shape can be seen; the dawn parting has to be glossed against the dawn-song tradition Levin invoked; the Friar's sententiae have to be marked as the sententious register their speaker cannot live by. The Arden introductions of Brian Gibbons in 1980 and René Weis in 2012 both introduce the play through accounts of its language in which formal patterning and its loosening are central facts, the climate Levin created. An editor need not cite the essay to work inside its weather, and that invisibility is the measure of its editorial influence.

### Q: Does Levin discuss the golden statues in his essay?

No, the golden statues receive no discussion in Levin's essay at all. The omission is worth knowing because later accounts of the play's ending often build on the fathers' vows of gold, and readers sometimes import that emphasis into Levin. His closing exhibits are different: the Prince's final sestet, in which public authority absorbs the catastrophe back into formal verse, and the coda on the musicians and silver sound. The sestet matters to him because it is the instrument by which the Prince, who has thrice had to quell and sentence the rioters, at last restores order to the city-state through the lovers' sacrifice, reconciling the patriarchs in set verse. For Levin the ending's formality is a matter of speech and sentence, not of monuments; the social frame closes over the lovers in language before anyone talks of statues. Accounts that make the essay a reading of epitaphs and memorials are describing a different, later essay that Levin did not write.

### Q: Why does Levin call the lament in Act 4 Scene 5 an operatic quartet?

Levin calls it an operatic quartet because the lament over Juliet's seeming corpse is scored like ensemble music: Capulet begins the litany of contraries, "All things that we ordained festival / Turn from their office to black funeral," and his wife, the Nurse, and Paris join in turn, four voices performing grief in patterned rotation. The phrase makes two points at once. The grief is real, and Levin insists the old man's lament is not less poignant for his love of ceremony; but its form is the household's, choral and ceremonial, reasserting the formalities at the very moment the private world collapses. Set against Juliet's solitary potion speech in the scene before, the quartet measures what the lovers' world had that Verona's cannot reproduce.

### Q: How does Levin read Juliet's wordplay on "Ay," "I," and "eye" in Act 3 Scene 2?

Levin reads the wordplay as the play's philosophy of naming compressed into single syllables. Waiting for news of the fight, Juliet asks the Nurse to answer in one bare vowel: say "Ay," and that vowel, punning on "I" and "eye," shall poison more than the death-darting eye of the cockatrice; brief sounds, she concludes, determine her weal or woe (3.2.45-51). Levin connects the passage to the toddler Juliet whose first recorded word was "Ay" in the Nurse's anecdote, and to the orchard, where assent is the lovers' whole contract. At the smallest scale of speech, a sound that cannot be dressed in form, identity itself hangs: if Romeo is slain, the "I" that answers is no longer herself.

### Q: Why does Levin begin his essay with Juliet's discovery that she has been overheard?

Levin begins there because that moment contains his whole argument in a single speech. Juliet, believing herself alone, has spoken her mind in sincerity; caught by Romeo's voice, she names what she has escaped: "Fain would I dwell on form, fain, fain deny / What I have spoke; but farewell compliment!" (2.2.88-89). The play itself, at its center, distinguishes dwelling on form from speaking feeling, and Levin takes the distinction from the heroine's mouth rather than imposing it from outside. Beginning with her words also fixes the essay's allegiance. The critic's thesis is presented as the play's own self-knowledge, overheard, like Romeo, from the orchard dark.

### Q: What does Levin mean by calling the lovers' bond the one organic relation in the play?

Levin means that the lovers' mutuality is the only relation in Verona that grew rather than being arranged. Around it he sets what he calls an overplus of stylized expressions and attitudes: the feud's inherited postures, the parents' marital negotiations, Paris's official courtship, the Friar's formulae, the Prince's proclamations. Those relations are all administered by forms that precede the people in them. The lovers' bond, by contrast, is made in speech between the two people it joins, built line by line in the shared sonnet, the orchard, and the dawn parting, and answerable to nothing outside itself. Organic is a technical word in the sentence, not a compliment: it names a structure whose form is generated by its own life rather than imposed by a code.

### Q: How can an essay use the InsightCrunch Levin summary to argue that the play's style enacts its meaning?

An essay can use the summary's rows as a ready-made evidence chain. Begin with the devices that dominate the first act, the Prologue sonnet, the oxymoron catalogue, the thick rhyme and repetition, and show them belonging to the world of the feud and the borrowed lover. Then follow the rows into the middle acts, the shared sonnet, the orchard's plainness, the Friar's unusable couplets, to argue that the lovers' changing diction is the plot told in another medium. Close with the final rows, the lament quartet and the Prince's sestet, where ceremony masses around the bodies. The summary's order is the argument's order: a student who writes along the table is reproducing Levin's demonstration with the play open beside it.

### Q: What, for Levin, finally closes in on the lovers: fortune, the feud, or form itself?

For Levin the three are one pattern wearing different names, and his sentence declines to choose among them: the overriding pattern through which the lovers have been trying to break, call it Fortune, the stars, or what it will, ends by closing in and breaking them. Their private world disappears, in his account, and the audience is left in the social ambiance again, among the officials, the mourners, and the musicians, which is his way of saying that the pattern outlives the pair who briefly escaped it. The feud supplies the pattern's social machinery, fortune its metaphysical alibi, and form its verbal texture, the symmetrical world of paired households, rhymed edicts, and ceremonial grief into which the lovers' private world finally disappears. The refusal to separate the causes is the essay's last lesson. The lovers are not killed by a letter's delay or a father's temper alone; they are killed by a total arrangement, and Levin's criticism is the art of hearing that arrangement in a single line.
