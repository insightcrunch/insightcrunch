Almost every account of historical materialism and Hegelian idealism ends at the same place: Hegel had the dialectic standing on its head, and Marx turned it the right way up. The image comes from Marx himself and it is not wrong. It is also not an explanation. It tells a reader that something was reversed without saying what, leaves them unable to say which parts of Hegel survived the operation, and gives an examiner nothing to reward. A student who writes it has written the sentence every other candidate wrote.

The two moves that actually constitute the difference can be stated in one line each, and everything else in this comparison is a specification of them. Marx kept Hegel's account of history as a process driven by contradiction, in which conflict within a form of life generates the next form rather than merely destroying the present one. Marx rejected Hegel's self-moving subject, the account in which the process is the development of thought, spirit, or reason coming to know itself, and replaced it with the development of human productive activity and the social relations it generates. Keep the contradiction, reject the subject. That is the inheritance and the break, and any accurate statement of the difference is a specification of those two moves rather than a repetition of the inversion image.

This article sets out both positions as their own defenders would state them, works through the seven points on which they genuinely differ, separates those from the differences that are only vocabulary, gives the verdict with the deciding consideration named, and deals honestly with the strongest objection to the whole distinction, which is that Marx never escaped the teleology he claimed to have abandoned. The theory on the materialist side is set out in full in the [complete guide to historical materialism](/2010/01/15/historical-materialism-complete-guide/); the method the two share and dispute belongs to the [complete guide to dialectical materialism](/2012/07/01/dialectical-materialism-complete-guide/); and the narrower comparison of the two dialectics is owned by the [comparison of Hegel's dialectic with Marx's](/2012/08/15/hegel-dialectic-vs-marx-dialectic/). This article owns the comparison of the two conceptions of history.

## What idealism actually means

The most common error on this topic is not a subtle one. A great many students arrive believing that idealism means optimism, or that it means having ideals, and write essays in which Hegel is contrasted with Marx as a hopeful thinker against a hard-headed one. The word has nothing to do with either.

Idealism in philosophy is a claim about what is fundamental in reality. Its family includes several positions that should not be run together. Subjective idealism, associated with George Berkeley, holds that what exists is minds and their contents, so that material objects have no existence independent of being perceived. Transcendental idealism, Kant's position, holds that the world as we experience it is structured by the categories of the understanding, so that we know appearances rather than things as they are in themselves. Objective idealism, which is Hegel's, holds neither of these.

Hegel's position is that reality has a rational structure, that this structure is not imposed on the world by human minds but belongs to the world, and that thought and being share a form which makes knowledge possible. He is not saying that only minds exist and he is not denying that there are stones. He is saying that the intelligible order which philosophy discovers in the world is really there, that it develops, and that the development is best understood on the model of thought working out its own implications rather than on the model of billiard balls striking one another.

This matters for the comparison because a great deal of criticism of Hegel from a materialist direction attacks the subjective position rather than the one he held. Pointing out that the world existed before there was anyone to think about it does not touch objective idealism, which has no difficulty granting it. The materialist objection has to be aimed at the claim that the structure of historical development is rational and thought-like, and at the account of what drives it, and those are more demanding targets.

### Is materialism the opposite of idealism?

In the philosophical sense, yes: materialism holds that matter and its organisation are fundamental and that mind is a product of material processes, while idealism holds that the rational or mental structure is fundamental. The opposite of idealism is not realism, and it is certainly not pessimism, which is the error most often carried into essays on this topic.

## Hegel's conception of history, stated as its defenders would state it

A comparison is worthless if one side is a straw figure, so the Hegelian account deserves a full statement before any criticism.

History, on Hegel's account, is not a sequence of events but a process with an intelligible structure, and the task of philosophy is to grasp that structure. The process is the development of spirit, which is his term for the collective, objective life of a people expressed in its institutions, laws, art, religion, and self-understanding. Spirit is not a ghost hovering above human affairs; it exists only in and through the activity of human beings, and its development is the development of what human communities are able to understand about themselves and to realise in their institutions.

The direction of the development is towards freedom, and Hegel gives this a precise sense. Freedom is not the absence of constraint but the condition in which a person's obligations and institutions are recognisably their own, in which what they must do and what they will do coincide because the institutional order expresses a rationality they can recognise. On this measure, history is the progress of the consciousness of freedom: in the ancient East one was free, in the Greek and Roman world some were free, and in the modern European world the principle that all are free has been articulated, though its institutional realisation is incomplete.

The mechanism of development is contradiction. A given form of life embodies a principle, works that principle out through its institutions, and in doing so runs into the limits of the principle, generating conflicts it cannot resolve on its own terms. The resolution is a new form that preserves what was achieved while cancelling what could not be sustained, which is what Hegel's term Aufhebung names and which English renders variously as sublation, supersession, or abolition. The three renderings carry different implications and none captures all three senses of the German, so an argument that turns on this word should name it.

The most striking feature of the account, and the one that bears directly on Marx, is the cunning of reason. Historical actors pursue their own particular interests, ambitions, and passions, and in doing so bring about results none of them intended. Caesar wanted power and produced the conditions for a universal empire. The individual's purpose is consumed in the process and the larger development is served. Hegel is explicit that nothing great is accomplished without passion, and equally explicit that the passion serves an end it does not know.

Two further features are worth stating because they are routinely misdescribed. Hegel did not use thesis, antithesis, and synthesis as the form of his dialectic; that schema comes from a contemporary expositor and became attached to his name afterwards, and Marx ridiculed it in print. And Hegel's claim that the rational and the actual coincide, the doppelsatz of the Philosophy of Right, is not the claim that whatever exists is justified; actual in his technical vocabulary means what fully expresses its concept, which a great deal of existing arrangement does not. The [treatment of the thesis, antithesis, synthesis formula](/2012/07/15/thesis-antithesis-synthesis-myth/) sets out the provenance of that error in detail.

## Historical materialism, stated as its defenders would state it

The materialist account can be set out with the same structure, which makes the comparison sharper than a narrative treatment would.

History is the development of human productive activity and of the social relations through which that activity is organised. The starting point is not a concept of humanity but the fact that human beings must produce their means of subsistence, that the way they do so requires them to enter into determinate relations with one another, and that these relations are not chosen individually but encountered as a given framework. What people are, on this account, coincides with what they produce and with how they produce it.

The direction of development is towards greater productive capacity, and the mechanism is contradiction, though contradiction of a specific kind. The productive forces develop within a given set of relations of production; at a certain stage the relations that fostered the development become obstacles to it; the resulting conflict is fought out through the political and ideological forms of the society and issues in a transformation of the relations. Classes are the agents of this process, since the relations of production distribute people into groups with opposed interests in maintaining or transforming them.

Freedom appears in this account too, and its appearance is one of the clearest continuities with Hegel. The development of productive capacity is valued because it reduces the portion of human activity that must be devoted to necessity and expands the portion available for self-determined activity. The passage in the third volume of Capital distinguishing the realm of necessity from the realm of freedom makes this explicit, and it is a Hegelian thought in materialist clothing.

The cunning of reason has a materialist counterpart as well. Individuals pursue their particular interests within a set of relations and produce aggregate outcomes none of them intended and none of them controls, and the systematic character of those outcomes is precisely what a theory of history is meant to explain. The difference is that Marx locates the mechanism in the structure of the relations rather than in the self-development of reason, which is the point at which the two accounts part.

A biographical note belongs here because it is often assumed away. Hegel died in Berlin in 1831, when Marx was thirteen and still at school in Trier, so the two never met and never corresponded. Marx entered the University of Berlin five years after Hegel's death, into a faculty and a student culture still organised around him and already splitting into the conservative and radical wings later called the right and left Hegelians. His friends and teachers in that period had known Hegel personally, which is why the transmission was direct in the sense that mattered. The point is worth stating because popular accounts sometimes imply a dispute between contemporaries. Marx was engaging with a completed system whose author could not reply, and with a set of followers who had already begun turning it against its own political conclusions.

## The genealogy, and why Feuerbach cannot be skipped

The move from Hegel to Marx did not happen directly, and accounts that omit the intermediate step make the inversion story look like a magic trick.

Marx's formation ran through the Young Hegelians, the group of radical philosophers who took Hegel's system and turned its critical resources against religion and the Prussian state. Their common assumption was that criticism of consciousness was the effective form of criticism, since a society's institutions rest on what its members believe.

Ludwig Feuerbach supplied the decisive move, and it is the hinge on which the whole transition turns. His account of religion, developed in his book on the essence of Christianity, is that the attributes ascribed to God, infinite love, infinite knowledge, infinite power, are human attributes abstracted from actual human beings, magnified, projected outwards, and then worshipped as an alien being. Religion is therefore true as anthropology and false as theology: it tells us about human capacities in an inverted form. The method that follows is what he called transformative criticism, and it consists in reversing the subject and the predicate. Where speculative philosophy says that thought thinks in man, transformative criticism says that man thinks.

Marx applied the same operation to Hegel's political philosophy in 1843. Hegel's account of the state makes the state the actuality of the ethical idea and treats civil society and the family as moments within it, so that the concrete institutions appear as the working out of an idea. Marx's criticism, written that year and left unpublished in his lifetime, is that this inverts the real relation: actual families and actual civil society are the subjects, and the state is their product, but Hegel has made the idea the subject and the real conditions its predicates. The charge is precise and it is Feuerbach's charge with a different target.

This is why the inversion metaphor makes sense at all. It names an operation that Marx had learned from Feuerbach and applied to Hegel before he had a materialist theory of history, and it means something quite specific: taking a relation in which an abstraction appears as the active subject and the concrete as its expression, and reversing which of the two is doing the explaining.

What Marx then did, and what distinguishes him from Feuerbach, was to turn the same criticism against Feuerbach. The manuscripts of 1845 and 1846 charge him with a materialism that observes rather than acts, that treats the sensible world as given rather than as produced by accumulated human activity, and that therefore cannot explain change. The eleven notes now known as the Theses on Feuerbach make the charge aphoristically, the sixth by defining the human essence as the ensemble of social relations rather than an abstraction inhering in each individual, and the eleventh by observing that "the philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways". The two criticisms are complementary: Hegel is criticised for making the idea the subject, Feuerbach for making the subject passive. Historical materialism is what results from correcting both.

### What role did Feuerbach play between Hegel and Marx?

He supplied the method of transformative criticism, which reverses subject and predicate so that the concrete explains the abstraction rather than the reverse, and he applied it to religion. Marx applied it to Hegel's political philosophy, then criticised Feuerbach for a materialism that only contemplates the world instead of grasping it as the product of human activity.

## The inversion metaphor and what it conceals

The image comes from the afterword Marx wrote for the second German edition of the first volume of Capital, where he says his dialectical method is the direct opposite of Hegel's, that with Hegel it is standing on its head, and that it must be inverted to discover "the rational kernel within the mystical shell". Three things about this passage are worth knowing before it is used.

The first is what it is doing. Marx wrote it at a moment when Hegel was being treated in Germany as a dead dog, and the passage is partly a defence of his own indebtedness against readers who thought the association discreditable. It is a statement about method in a preface, not a worked-out account of the relation between two philosophies.

The second is that a metaphor of inversion carries an assumption worth examining, namely that the structure survives the operation and only the orientation changes. If you turn a person the right way up you have the same person. This is exactly the assumption Louis Althusser attacked, and his objection is the most serious criticism the inversion story has received. If Hegel's dialectic is idealist in its structure, in the way it relates contradictions to a whole, in its treatment of every conflict as an expression of a single underlying principle, then inverting it produces a materialism with an idealist structure: the economy simply replaces spirit as the essence of which everything else is an expression. What is needed, on this view, is not an inversion but a different structure, which is what his account of overdetermination, in which historical events are produced by contradictions at several levels rather than expressing one, was designed to supply. His treatment of the point sits in the essays collected under the title For Marx, and the argument is developed further in his reading of Capital.

The third is that Marx's practice is more complicated than his metaphor. The presentational order of Capital, moving from the commodity through money to capital, from simple to complex determinations, is recognisably Hegelian in its architecture, and Marx acknowledged as much when he described himself as having coquetted with Hegel's modes of expression. Readers who take the inversion image as a full account of the relation are unable to explain why the mature economic work is structured the way it is.

## The seven-point comparison

The table below is the instrument for the comparison, and it is organised so that each row states a genuine difference rather than a difference of vocabulary. Reading down the last column gives the fastest available account of what separates the two conceptions.

| Point of comparison | Hegelian idealism | Historical materialism | What the difference amounts to |
|---|---|---|---|
| The subject of history | Spirit, the collective self-understanding of a people, developing towards knowledge of itself | Human beings producing their means of life and entering relations to do so | Whether the process has a thought-like subject working itself out or is constituted by material activity with no subject beyond the people involved |
| The motor of change | Contradiction internal to a form of life, resolved by a higher form that preserves what was achieved | Contradiction between developing productive forces and existing relations of production, fought out by classes | Shared form, different content: both locate change in internal conflict rather than external shock, but one places the conflict in the concept and the other in the organisation of production |
| The status of contradiction | Real and constitutive; a form of life genuinely embodies incompatible commitments | Real and constitutive; relations genuinely become fetters on the forces they organised | The strongest continuity between the two, and the reason Marx would not describe his method as the opposite of Hegel's in every respect |
| Direction and end point | Towards the actualisation of freedom, with the modern state as the form in which the principle is articulated | Towards greater productive capacity and, on the strong reading, towards a society without class antagonism | Both accounts are directional; the question is whether the direction is guaranteed by the nature of the process or produced by specified mechanisms that could fail |
| The role of ideas | Constitutive; the institutions of a period express its principle, and philosophy grasps what has developed | Conditioned by the organisation of production; effective as the medium in which conflicts are fought out rather than as the source of change | Whether ideas explain the institutional order or require explanation by reference to it |
| Method of inquiry | Reconstruction of the concept, working out what a principle requires and where it fails | Investigation of how a society produces and reproduces itself, with the concepts developed from the material | Whether the philosopher reconstructs a rational order or the investigator establishes how a specific mode of production works |
| What counts as evidence | Coherence of the reconstruction with the historical record as interpreted through the concept | The organisation of production, the distribution of surplus, class composition, and the historical record of conflict | Whether an account is tested by intelligibility or by the material record, which is where the two are least compatible |

The row that carries the most weight is the first. Everything else follows from whether history has a subject whose development the process expresses. The row that shows the deepest continuity is the third, and any account of the difference which claims Marx rejected the dialectic has misread it. Both those findings are the substance of the inheritance-and-break rule stated at the start.

## The differences that are only vocabulary

Four apparent contrasts dissolve on inspection, and identifying them prevents a comparison from becoming a list of oppositions that do not oppose anything.

The first is the supposed contrast between a philosophy of ideas and a philosophy of matter. Neither thinker is describing physics. Hegel's spirit is realised in institutions, practices, and works, and has no existence apart from them; Marx's material conditions include skills, techniques, and forms of cooperation that are as much products of thought as any doctrine. The contrast between the ideal and the material, stated at that level of abstraction, does not separate them, and essays built on it produce two positions neither man held.

The second is the contrast between abstraction and concreteness. Both writers use highly abstract categories, and Marx's are in places more abstract than Hegel's. The category of value in the first volume of Capital is not more concrete than the category of spirit; it is differently grounded. What differs is what the abstractions are abstractions from, and where the analysis is meant to end up.

The third is the contrast between a conservative thinker and a revolutionary one. This is a claim about political conclusions, not about the conceptions of history, and it is not even reliable at that level: Hegel's position on the Prussian state has been read as an accommodation and as a critical constitutionalism, and the debate is genuine. Using a political judgment to do the work of a philosophical comparison is a common way of avoiding the comparison.

The fourth is the contrast between necessity and freedom. Both accounts hold that freedom is achieved rather than given, that it consists in a certain relation between what one must do and what one wills, and that it is realised in institutions rather than in isolation. This is one of the deepest continuities and it is repeatedly presented as a contrast by writers who assume that a materialist must be a determinist and an idealist must be a voluntarist. Neither implication holds, and the [debate over economic determinism](/2010/03/15/economic-determinism-debate/) sets out why the materialist side of that assumption fails.

## The verdict and the deciding factor

The verdict is that the two conceptions differ decisively on one thing and share a great deal else, and that the decisive difference is the status of the subject.

For Hegel the historical process is the development of something that is, in the relevant sense, a subject: it has a direction internal to what it is, its stages are stages in its self-understanding, and the whole is intelligible because it is the working out of a rational structure. For Marx there is no such subject. There are human beings producing, the relations they enter into, and the aggregate consequences of their activity, and the direction such consequences exhibit is the outcome of specified mechanisms rather than the unfolding of a nature. That is why Marx can say that people make their own history under circumstances they do not choose, a formulation with no Hegelian equivalent, and why his account of unintended outcomes locates the cunning in the structure of the relations rather than in reason.

The deciding factor, then, is not materialism against idealism in the abstract. It is whether the direction of history is guaranteed by what the process is or produced by mechanisms that could operate otherwise. On that question the two are genuinely opposed, and everything else in the comparison can be derived from it.

What would overturn this verdict is a demonstration that Marx's account does contain a guarantee, which is exactly what the strongest objection alleges.

## The strongest objection: that Marx never escaped the teleology

The objection deserves the fullest statement this article can give it, because it is the one serious reader of both thinkers should worry about.

The charge is that Marx replaced the subject of history without abandoning the structure that required one. In Hegel, the process moves towards a condition in which spirit knows itself, and each stage is intelligible as a step towards that condition. In Marx, the process moves towards a society without class antagonism, in which human beings collectively control the conditions of their own existence, and the stages are intelligible as steps towards it. The end point is different; the role the end point plays in the explanation is the same. The proletariat, on this reading, performs the function that spirit performed: a universal class whose particular emancipation is at the same time the emancipation of humanity, whose self-recognition is the recognition of a truth about the whole, and whose action completes a process that was under way before it appeared. The vocabulary of the early writings, in which the proletariat is described in terms that a Hegelian would recognise immediately, is evidence for the charge rather than against it.

The objection is not a hostile invention. It has been pressed by philosophers sympathetic to Marx and by historians of philosophy with no political stake, and it is the reason the relation between Marx and Hegel has never been settled.

The reply has to distinguish two things that the objection runs together. A tendency generated by specified mechanisms is not a telos. If a theory says that competition compels firms to accumulate, that accumulation concentrates capital, that concentration socialises production while ownership remains private, and that the resulting tension produces recurrent crises, then it has described a direction without invoking an end that draws the process towards it. The direction is a consequence of the mechanisms, and if the mechanisms are blocked or countervailed the direction fails to appear. Marx's economic work is full of exactly this structure, most clearly in the treatment of the tendency of the rate of profit alongside its counteracting influences.

A telos, by contrast, is an end that explains the stages leading to it. A theory containing one says that the earlier stages occurred because they were necessary for the later, and that is a form of explanation Marx's economic analysis does not use.

The reply then has to make a concession, and the concession is substantial. A great deal of later Marxism did reintroduce the teleology, and did so explicitly. The stage theories of the Second International and of Soviet orthodoxy, in which societies pass through a fixed sequence culminating in socialism, are teleological in the strict sense, and they were presented as Marx's own view. Passages in Marx's own writing, particularly the more rhetorical ones, lend themselves to that reading. The honest position is that the mature economic work supports the mechanisms reading, that the popular and rhetorical writings often support the teleological one, and that Marx did not systematically distinguish them, which is why his followers could take either.

The further concession worth making is that the assignment of a world-historical role to the proletariat is not derived from the economic mechanisms and cannot be. That a class is exploited, that it is concentrated by the development of industry, and that it has an interest in transforming the relations that exploit it are all conclusions the analysis supports. That its emancipation is universal, that it cannot free itself without freeing everyone, is a different kind of claim with a recognisably Hegelian shape, and the argument for it in the early writings is philosophical rather than economic. Whether that claim can be reconstructed on other grounds is one of the tradition's genuinely open problems.

### Did Marx turn Hegel upside down?

He said so, in the afterword to the second German edition of Capital, and the image describes a real operation he had learned from Feuerbach: reversing which term explains the other. It is not a full account of the relation, since it implies the structure survives unchanged, and Althusser's objection that an inverted idealism would remain idealist in structure is the strongest reason to distrust it.

## What Marx kept from Hegel, item by item

The inheritance is more extensive than the standard account allows, and naming the items is what converts a slogan into an argument.

He kept contradiction as a form of explanation. The idea that a stable arrangement can contain incompatible requirements, that the working out of those requirements generates conflict, and that the conflict is internal to the arrangement rather than imposed on it, is Hegel's and Marx uses it throughout. The analysis of the commodity as a unity of use value and value that becomes an external opposition in the money form is a Hegelian move in economic material.

He kept the category of totality, the requirement that a phenomenon be understood by its place in a whole rather than in isolation. This is the methodological commitment that separates his political economy from the classical economists he studied, and it is the point Georg Lukacs made central to his reading in the 1920s.

He kept Aufhebung, the notion that a form can be at once cancelled and preserved at a higher level, which is why he can describe communism as abolishing private property while presupposing the productive achievements that private property brought into being. Nothing in a simple materialism would supply that structure, and the term's three English renderings, sublation, supersession, and abolition, each capture one aspect and lose the others.

He kept the account of alienation, at least in the early writings, and its structure is straightforwardly Hegelian: a subject externalises itself in a product, the product acquires an independent existence, and the subject confronts its own activity as an alien power. Hegel described this for consciousness and for spirit; Feuerbach applied it to religion; Marx applied it to labour. The [complete guide to Marx on alienation](/2011/04/01/marx-on-alienation-complete-guide/) works through what changed when the account was transposed.

He kept the movement from the abstract to the concrete as a method of presentation, in which analysis begins from simple determinations and reconstructs the complex whole by adding determinations in order. The methodological introduction Marx drafted in 1857 states this explicitly, and the architecture of Capital follows it.

He kept the historicising of categories, the insistence that concepts which appear timeless are the products of particular social forms. Hegel historicised the categories of political philosophy; Marx historicised the categories of political economy, arguing that labour in general, capital, and value are not eternal features of production but the categories of a specific mode of it.

## What Marx rejected, item by item

The rejections are fewer and each is load-bearing.

He rejected the self-moving subject. Nothing develops itself in Marx's account; people develop productive capacities under conditions they inherit, and the aggregate pattern is a consequence rather than a self-realisation.

He rejected the guarantee. However directional the process, its direction is a product of mechanisms, and mechanisms can be countervailed. This is the rejection that the teleology objection contests and it is the most important of the four.

He rejected the priority of the concept in inquiry. For Hegel the philosopher grasps the rational structure of what has developed; for Marx the investigator establishes how a particular society produces and reproduces itself and develops categories adequate to that, and the difference shows in what each treats as evidence.

He rejected the state as the culmination. For Hegel the modern constitutional state is the institutional form in which freedom is articulated and particular interests are reconciled with the universal. For Marx the state is precisely where the reconciliation is illusory, a form in which the common interest appears as separate from real individuals while resting on a determinate class basis. The 1843 criticism of Hegel's philosophy of right is built around this and it is the earliest point at which Marx's position becomes distinctively his own.

### What did Marx keep from Hegel?

Contradiction as a form of explanation, the requirement to understand phenomena through their place in a totality, the structure of Aufhebung in which a form is cancelled and preserved, the account of alienation, the method of moving from abstract to concrete, and the historicising of categories that appear timeless. What he rejected was the self-moving subject and the guarantee of the outcome.

## How idealism explains historical change

A comparison should be fair to the framework it finds less persuasive, and the idealist account of change is more resourceful than materialist summaries usually allow.

On the Hegelian account, a form of life is organised around a principle that its members recognise and act on, and the institutions of the period embody that principle. Change occurs because the principle, worked out consistently, generates demands the existing institutions cannot meet. The Greek city-state embodied a substantial ethical life in which the individual's identity was constituted by membership, and the emergence of individual conscience within it, dramatised in tragedy and articulated by philosophy, could not be accommodated by the form itself. The Roman world universalised legal personality and lost the substantial community. The Reformation established the principle of individual conscience in religion and generated the demand that political institutions reflect it. Each transition is intelligible as the working out of a demand the previous form had generated and could not satisfy.

Two strengths of this account should be conceded. It explains why change occurs at particular points rather than continuously, since a principle can be embodied stably until its implications are pressed. And it handles the intelligibility of historical transitions in a way that purely external explanations cannot: the people involved understood themselves to be pursuing something, and their self-understanding is part of what has to be explained.

The materialist objection is not that this is unintelligible but that it is unanchored. It cannot say why a principle gets pressed at one moment and not another, why some demands generate institutional change and others are contained for centuries, or why the transitions have the particular economic character they do. Supplying an answer to those questions is what the materialist account is for, and whether it succeeds is a question about evidence rather than about philosophical preference.

The strongest form of the idealist reply, made by writers working in Hegel's wake rather than by Hegel himself, is that the materialist account cannot explain why productive capacity should develop at all without smuggling in a commitment to human rationality and self-development that is itself an idealist premise. That reply has force against the versions of historical materialism that treat the development of the productive forces as a basic assumption, and it is one of the reasons the primacy thesis has been so hard to defend without a mechanism.

## Which philosophers besides Hegel shaped the materialist conception

Presenting Marx as Hegel's inversion makes it look as though one philosophical relationship produced his position, and it did not.

The classical political economists supplied the economic content. Adam Smith's account of the division of labour and of the sources of value, and David Ricardo's development of the labour theory of value and of distribution as a conflict between classes, are the material Marx worked on, and his conception of history is inseparable from the conclusion that the categories of political economy are historical rather than natural.

The French socialists and the historians of the Restoration supplied the concept of class struggle, which Marx explicitly disclaimed having discovered. Historians writing on the French Revolution had already explained political conflict by the conflict of classes, and Marx said as much in a letter, adding that his own contribution was to connect class struggle to particular phases of production and to the transformation of society rather than to have identified it.

Feuerbach supplied the transformative method, as set out above.

The materialist tradition of antiquity mattered more than is usually recognised. Marx's doctoral dissertation was on the difference between the natural philosophy of Democritus and that of Epicurus, and what interested him in Epicurus was the swerve, the element of indeterminacy that leaves room for something other than mechanical necessity. A reader who thinks Marx arrived at materialism by simply inverting Hegel has to explain why his first sustained piece of work was an argument about which ancient materialism was preferable and why.

The English and Scottish historians and the Enlightenment accounts of social stages also contributed, particularly the idea that societies pass through forms distinguished by their mode of subsistence, which was in circulation long before Marx and which he transformed by making the relations of production rather than the means of subsistence the differentiating feature.

## The passage on religion and why it belongs here

The introduction Marx wrote in 1844 for his unpublished criticism of Hegel's philosophy of right contains the most quoted and most mangled sentences he ever produced, and the mangling is directly relevant to this comparison.

The passage describes religion as the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world, and the spirit of spiritless conditions, before adding that it is "the opium of the people". Almost every quotation of that phrase omits everything before it, which reverses the sense. In the surrounding sentences religion is a protest against real suffering as well as an expression of it, and the medical connotation of opium in the 1840s, when it was the principal available analgesic, is closer to painkiller than to intoxicant. The argument concludes that abolishing religion as illusory happiness is a demand for real happiness, and that criticism of religion is therefore the premise of all criticism.

The passage belongs in this comparison for two reasons. It shows the Feuerbachian method still fully in operation, with religion treated as an inverted expression of a real condition. And it shows Marx already moving beyond Feuerbach, because the conclusion is not that people should stop believing but that the conditions which make the belief necessary should be changed. Feuerbach's criticism ends in enlightenment; Marx's ends in a demand for transformation, which is the difference between a philosophy of consciousness and a philosophy of practice.

A writer quoting this passage should quote it whole or not at all. Journalists working quickly are the most frequent offenders, and the truncated version has become one of the standard misattributions of the subject, which is why it appears in the verification section below.

## Five misreadings this comparison has to correct

Each of these appears in student writing and in published commentary, and each has a short correction.

Marx did not simply reverse Hegel and keep everything else. The inversion metaphor implies exactly that, and Althusser's objection to it is the most serious criticism the image has received.

Hegel was not an idealist in the sense of denying that matter exists. Objective idealism is a claim about the rational structure of reality, not a denial of stones, and a materialist objection aimed at subjective idealism misses him entirely.

Marx did not reject the dialectic. He rejected an idealist account of what the dialectic is a dialectic of, and described his own method as the direct opposite of Hegel's while insisting on his debt in the same paragraph.

Feuerbach cannot be skipped. Without transformative criticism the inversion story has no content, and accounts that omit him make the transition look like a personal decision rather than a method.

Idealism does not mean optimism. This is the single most common error in undergraduate work on the topic and it produces essays that are unsalvageable, since every subsequent sentence rests on a definition of the key term that is simply wrong.

## Which framework should a reader use

A comparison article that reaches a verdict owes its readers a recommendation, and the honest one is that the choice depends on what is being explained.

For explaining how a society reproduces itself, how surplus is produced and appropriated, why particular institutional arrangements persist, and what makes them vulnerable, the materialist framework is the more useful instrument, because it specifies mechanisms and identifies evidence. Questions about the persistence of an economic order, about the interests served by a legal regime, or about why a technically superior arrangement failed to spread are questions this framework was built for.

For explaining the internal development of a body of thought, a legal tradition, a religious doctrine, or an artistic form, the Hegelian framework has resources the materialist one lacks, because it takes seriously the idea that working out the implications of a commitment generates problems that force change. A history of doctrine written on strict materialist principles tends to be thin, and the best Marxist work in these areas, from Lukacs on the novel to Thompson on customary law, borrows heavily from the Hegelian side without saying so.

For explaining a large historical transition, neither framework is sufficient alone, and the best historical writing on such transitions uses both: the material conditions that made an outcome possible, and the self-understandings that made it thinkable to the people involved.

The recommendation for a reader who has to choose one is therefore conditional rather than absolute, and stating it that way is more useful than a verdict for one side. What is not conditional is the requirement to state which framework is being used and what it treats as needing explanation, since every framework has something it declines to explain, and identifying that is the first move in evaluating any of them.

## Answering the examination question on this topic

This comparison is an examination staple across philosophy, sociology, and political theory papers, and the question is nearly always some version of asking how Marx's conception of history differs from Hegel's, or what Marx meant by turning Hegel on his head.

What the assessment rewards is precision about the two moves. An answer that states the inversion image and then illustrates it with examples of material conditions mattering will earn a modest mark, because it has restated the question. An answer that identifies what was kept, contradiction as a form of explanation and totality as a methodological requirement, and what was rejected, the self-moving subject and the guarantee of the outcome, has answered it. The single distinction that earns marks is between the inheritance and the break, stated in those terms and supported with one example of each.

The standard trap is idealism understood as optimism, and it is fatal because it corrupts everything downstream. The second trap is asserting that Marx rejected the dialectic, which is contradicted by the passage the candidate is usually quoting. The third is treating Feuerbach as an optional detail, which leaves the answer unable to explain what the inversion consists in.

Two short pieces of evidence are worth having exactly: the rational kernel and mystical shell formulation from the afterword to the second German edition of Capital, and the eleventh of the Theses on Feuerbach. Both are under fifteen words, both are instantly recognisable, and both can be deployed in a sentence. Everything else should be paraphrased. The answer structures into which this material fits are set out in the [exam answer guide for historical materialism](/2010/05/01/historical-materialism-exam-answers/), and candidates should build the evaluation paragraph from the teleology objection above, since that is the point at which an answer moves from description to argument.

A final tactical note. Examiners on this topic frequently reward candidates who show they know that Hegel did not use thesis, antithesis, and synthesis, because it is a reliable signal that the candidate has read something beyond a revision guide. It costs one sentence.

## Teaching the comparison

The misconception that dominates a class on this topic is the meaning of idealism, and it must be dealt with in the first ten minutes or the rest of the session is lost. Students arrive with the everyday sense of the word, in which an idealist is someone with high principles, and they will map that onto Hegel and produce a contrast between a hopeful philosopher and a hard-headed one that no amount of later correction dislodges.

The question that surfaces it quickly is to ask the class whether an idealist could be a pessimist, and then whether a materialist could believe in progress. Both answers are yes, and discovering that dissolves the everyday sense in a way that a definition on a slide does not.

The extract that resolves the substantive comparison best is a paired reading: the passage on the cunning of reason from Hegel's lectures on the philosophy of history, set beside the opening paragraphs of the Eighteenth Brumaire. Both describe historical actors producing outcomes they did not intend. The class can be asked to say what accounts for the gap between intention and outcome in each, and the answers, reason working through passion in one case and inherited circumstances and class position in the other, are the whole comparison in miniature.

For a politically mixed class this topic is safe ground, since the disagreement is about the structure of historical explanation rather than about political conclusions, and students who are hostile to Marx's politics can engage with the question of whether an inverted idealism remains idealist without any commitment at all.

## What a researcher should verify

Three checks apply to any writing on this comparison.

Check the translation of the afterword before quoting it. The passage about the mystical shell and the rational kernel exists in several English renderings that differ in wording, and an argument that leans on the exact phrasing should name the translation. The same discipline applies to Aufhebung wherever it appears; naming the German and the rendering costs a clause and prevents an avoidable objection.

Check the status of the texts on the Marx side. The 1843 criticism of Hegel's philosophy of right was unpublished in his lifetime, the Theses on Feuerbach are notebook entries published by Engels in an edited version, and the manuscripts of 1845 and 1846 were abandoned and assembled posthumously. Only the afterword to Capital is a published statement by Marx about his relation to Hegel, which gives it a different evidential weight from everything else and makes it the citation of choice. The [reader's guide to The German Ideology](/2010/03/01/german-ideology-readers-guide/) sets out the manuscript problem in detail.

Check the attribution of the triad. Thesis, antithesis, and synthesis is not Hegel's formula, and repeating it as his is the fastest way to lose a reader who knows the field. The unsafe claim most likely to be repeated by a writer working quickly is the truncated opium quotation, which reverses the sense of the passage it comes from; quote the surrounding sentences or paraphrase. Keeping each quotation on a card with its work, its translation, its status, and its full context attached is the practical defence, and you can [save your notes and build a citation-linked reading list free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.org/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) to keep the provenance travelling with the quotation.

## What is settled and what is not

Settled: Marx retained contradiction, totality, and the structure of sublation from Hegel, and rejected the self-moving subject and the priority of the concept in inquiry. The inversion image is Marx's own and describes an operation he learned from Feuerbach. Objective idealism is not the denial that matter exists, and the alternative to idealism is not optimism or realism.

Unsettled: whether the resulting position escapes the teleological structure it inherited, or whether the role assigned to the proletariat performs the function spirit performed in the original. Whether the architecture of Capital is Hegelian in substance or only in presentation. Whether Althusser's charge, that an inverted idealism retains an idealist structure, requires abandoning the dialectic or reconstructing it. Those three questions are live in the scholarship and a reader who can state them is in possession of the topic rather than of a summary of it.

## Two worked comparisons

Abstract statements of the difference are easy to agree with and hard to use. Applying both frameworks to the same historical material shows what each actually does.

### The French Revolution

For Hegel, writing within living memory of it, the Revolution is the moment at which the principle of freedom, developed in thought through the Reformation and the Enlightenment, demanded institutional realisation. His account in the lectures on the philosophy of history treats it as the attempt to build a state on the principle of the will, and treats the Terror as the consequence of an abstract conception of freedom that recognised no determinate institutions, so that any particular arrangement appeared as a limitation to be destroyed. The explanation is internal to the principle: the failure follows from what the principle was, and the resolution comes when freedom is given determinate institutional content.

For Marx, the Revolution is the political form of a transformation in property relations, in which a class whose economic position had developed within the old order removed the legal and political obstacles to its further development. The universal language of the Revolution is not dismissed as insincere; it is explained as the form any rising class must adopt, since it has to present its particular interest as the general interest to secure the support of other classes. The Terror and the subsequent settlement are explained by the composition of the classes and factions involved and by the limits of what each could concede.

Notice what each account does well. Hegel explains why the revolutionaries said what they said and why the language of universal right had the force it did, which a crude materialist account reduces to hypocrisy. Marx explains why the settlement took the property form it did and why the universal language coexisted with a franchise restricted by wealth, which the Hegelian account has difficulty with. A good historian uses both, and the best Marxist writing on the Revolution has always taken the ideological language seriously as a real historical force rather than as decoration.

### The modern state

For Hegel, the modern constitutional state is where the comparison reaches its sharpest point. His account makes the state the institution in which particular interests, pursued in civil society, are reconciled with the universal interest, through a constitution, an impartial administration, and a system of representation. The state is not an instrument of any group; it is the form in which a community becomes rationally self-governing.

For Marx, that reconciliation is precisely what does not occur. The state presents itself as the general interest while resting on a determinate distribution of property, and its universality is real as an appearance and false as a description. His earliest distinctive argument, in the criticism of 1843, is that Hegel has inverted the relation, treating the state as the actuality of an idea of which the family and civil society are moments, when the real relation runs the other way.

The row is instructive because Hegel's position is not naive. He knew that civil society generates poverty and that the accumulation of wealth at one pole and destitution at the other is a problem his system does not solve, and he says so in the Philosophy of Right in a passage that has been much discussed. What he lacks is an account of why the state should be capable of solving it. Marx supplies an account of why it is not, and the cost of his account is that it has difficulty explaining the cases where states have acted against the immediate interests of dominant classes, which is the standing problem for every Marxist theory of the state since.

## How the inversion story became standard

The comparison as most readers meet it was fixed by a small number of texts, and knowing which ones explains why the standard account has the shape it does.

Engels's book on Feuerbach and the end of classical German philosophy, published in 1888, is the most influential single source. Written for a general readership, it presents the relation as a succession, with Hegel's system containing a revolutionary method inside a conservative system, Feuerbach breaking the system, and Marx recovering the method on a materialist basis. That narrative is clear, memorable, and does real explanatory work, and it is also considerably tidier than the record. Almost every textbook account descends from it.

Plekhanov's expositions carried the same narrative into the Russian movement and added a systematic vocabulary. Lenin's notebooks on Hegel's Logic, written during the war and published posthumously, pulled in the other direction, with their observation that Capital cannot be fully understood without the Logic, and they became the licence for a more philosophically serious engagement in the Soviet period than the orthodoxy otherwise permitted.

The revival of the Hegelian reading in the twentieth century came from outside the official movement. Lukacs's essays of the early 1920s reconstructed Marx as a philosopher of totality and of the subject-object relation, and did so before the early manuscripts were available, which made his reading look prophetic when they appeared. The Paris lectures on the Phenomenology given in France in the 1930s produced a generation of readers for whom the master and servant passage was the key to Marx. The result is that the twentieth century contains two Marxes, one continuous with Hegel and one defined against him, and both have textual support.

The strongest dissenting position deserves mention because it is usually omitted. Lucio Colletti argued that the attempt to hold onto the dialectic while rejecting idealism is incoherent, since dialectical contradiction is a relation between concepts rather than between things, and that a materialist should accept real opposition without contradiction and abandon the Hegelian apparatus altogether. He drew the conclusion that this would leave much less of Marxism intact than its defenders assume. Whether he was right is one of the open questions listed above, and the [comparison of Hegel's dialectic with Marx's](/2012/08/15/hegel-dialectic-vs-marx-dialectic/) engages the technical argument this article only names.

## The master and servant passage and its afterlife

No single stretch of Hegel has done more work inside Marxism than the section of the Phenomenology of Spirit usually rendered as lordship and bondage, or master and servant, and its reception is a case study in how the two frameworks interact.

The passage describes two self-consciousnesses that confront one another and require recognition from each other in order to be what they take themselves to be. The confrontation produces a struggle, the struggle produces a relation in which one commands and the other serves, and the relation then undermines itself. The master obtains recognition from someone whose recognition he has already discounted as that of a mere instrument, so the recognition is worthless to him. The servant, working on the world under compulsion, forms and shapes objects, and in the formed object sees his own activity made durable and external, which is the beginning of a self-consciousness the master cannot reach. Formative activity, in short, produces a subject; consumption does not.

The attraction of this for Marxism is obvious and the risk is equally so. It appears to supply a philosophical warrant for the claim that the class which labours is the class which will come to consciousness of itself, and it does so in the vocabulary of work, objectification, and dependence that Marx was already using in 1844. Alexandre Kojeve's lectures in Paris in the 1930s made this passage the centre of the Phenomenology and read it as an anticipation of the class struggle, and a generation of French readers came to Marx through it.

### Does the master and servant passage prove Marx was Hegelian?

It shows a shared structure and it does not prove a derivation. Hegel is describing a movement of self-consciousness in general, not a historical class relation, and the servant is not a proletarian. Marx's account of labour producing a class capable of transforming society rests on economic mechanisms Hegel does not supply.

Two cautions apply and both are frequently ignored. The passage is a moment in an argument about the development of self-consciousness, not a chapter about slavery or wage labour, and treating it as a covert social theory imports content it does not have. And the reading that makes it the key to the whole Phenomenology is a twentieth-century interpretative decision with a specific history, not an obvious feature of the text. What can be said safely is that Hegel identified a structure in which the party that works acquires something the party that commands does not, that Marx's early writing on labour uses that structure, and that the mature economic analysis of how a class is formed by the development of industry does not depend on it.

## What each side gets wrong about the other

Both traditions have accumulated a stock of objections to their rival that miss.

Materialist critics of Hegel routinely attack a position he did not hold. The charge that he believed history is made by ideas floating free of material life is answered by his own account, in which spirit exists only in institutions, practices, and works, and in which the passions and interests of actual individuals are the sole means by which anything happens. The charge that he was indifferent to poverty is answered by the passage in the Philosophy of Right where he acknowledges that civil society produces a class of the impoverished as a systematic consequence of its own operation, a problem he states clearly and does not solve. The serious materialist objection is not that Hegel ignored material life but that he had no account of why the development of a principle should track anything measurable, and that his transitions are intelligible after the event and predict nothing before it.

### Why is idealism so often confused with optimism?

Because the everyday English sense of an idealist is someone who holds high principles, and students meet that sense long before the philosophical one. The philosophical term concerns what is fundamental in reality, not a disposition. An idealist can be thoroughly pessimistic, and a materialist can hold that history improves.

Idealist critics of materialism make a mirror error. The charge that historical materialism reduces human beings to economic units is answered by the account of species activity, of the development of human powers, and of the realm of freedom, all of which treat the expansion of human capacity as the point of the exercise. The charge that it cannot take ideas seriously is answered by the analysis of ideology as the medium in which conflicts are fought out. The serious idealist objection is different and sharper: that a theory which explains the development of productive capacity by material circumstances has to assume the very human rationality and drive to self-development that it claims to explain, and that this assumption is an idealist premise smuggled in at the foundation.

Both serious objections are about foundations rather than about caricatures, and any comparison that spends its time on the caricatures has missed the argument.

## The comparison in the wider history of philosophy

Placing this dispute in a longer line prevents the impression that it is peculiar to two German writers.

The question of whether historical development has a structure that thought can grasp, and if so what drives it, was live long before either. Vico proposed a science of the common nature of nations in which societies pass through recurring stages of gods, heroes, and men, and argued that human beings can know history in a way they cannot know nature, because they made it. Herder rejected a single line of development in favour of the incommensurable value of distinct national cultures, each unfolding an inner principle. Kant proposed that nature might realise its purposes through the antagonism of human beings in society, a formulation that anticipates the cunning of reason directly. Every one of these positions supplies part of the background Hegel was writing into.

On the other side, the tradition of explaining social forms by the mode of subsistence runs through Montesquieu, through the Scottish writers on the stages of society, and through the political arithmetic of the eighteenth century, and it reached Marx as a going concern rather than as an invention of his own.

The consequence for a reader is a useful deflation. Neither the claim that history has an intelligible direction nor the claim that its direction is set by how people produce is the property of one thinker. What Hegel contributed was a rigorous account of development through internal contradiction. What Marx contributed was the specification of the mechanism in terms of production relations and class conflict, and the historicising of the categories of political economy. The comparison is between two contributions to a long argument, and treating it as a duel between two systems flattens both.

## Inside Marx's criticism of Hegel's political philosophy

The text where Marx first becomes distinguishable from his teachers repays a closer look, because it is the moment the comparison acquires content and because it is almost never read.

The manuscript works through the sections of Hegel's Philosophy of Right dealing with the state, paragraph by paragraph, and its method is consistent throughout. Hegel's exposition presents the family and civil society as moments of the idea, which divides itself and gives itself finite actuality in them, so that the actual institutions appear as the medium through which the idea realises itself. Marx's response is to ask what is doing the explaining. Real families exist and real civil society exists; the idea does not act, divide itself, or give itself anything. Hegel has taken a relation between actual things and described it as a relation between the idea and its expressions, which converts the empirical into the manifestation of something else.

The consequences Marx draws are political rather than merely logical. If the state is the actuality of the ethical idea, then its bureaucracy is the universal class, its officials serve the general interest, and the conflict between particular interests in civil society is genuinely resolved at the political level. If, instead, the state is a product of civil society, then the bureaucracy is a particular interest presenting itself as universal, the political constitution registers rather than resolves the conflicts beneath it, and the appearance of universality is the thing requiring explanation. Every later Marxist account of the state descends from this argument.

Two features of the text are worth noticing for the comparison. Marx is not yet a materialist in the developed sense: he has no theory of production, and the alternative he proposes to Hegel's idea is democracy understood as the constitution in which the people are the actual subject. And the criticism is entirely internal, using Hegel's own standards against his conclusions, which is why the relation between the two thinkers cannot be described as rejection. The move that separates them is a move made from inside.

## A third case: secularisation

Applying both frameworks to the decline of religious authority in Europe shows their characteristic strengths without either winning outright, which is the fairest test available.

The idealist account traces an internal development. The Reformation established the principle of individual conscience against institutional mediation; the working out of that principle in theology and philosophy produced criticism of the authority of scripture and of the church; the criticism generated its own further implications, culminating in a position from which religious claims could be assessed by standards internal to reason. The development is intelligible from within, and it explains why the criticism took the particular form it did rather than some other.

The materialist account traces a change in conditions. Urbanisation removed populations from parish structures that had organised social life; the extension of wage labour and of state administration displaced institutions of religious welfare and record-keeping; the growth of literacy and of a commercial press created channels for the circulation of ideas that clerical institutions did not control; and the interests that had been served by an established church changed as the composition of property changed. The development is intelligible from outside, and it explains why the criticism had effects at one moment rather than another.

Neither account is complete. The idealist account cannot explain the timing or the geography, since the arguments were available long before they were widely acted on, and were acted on differently in societies with similar theological traditions. The materialist account cannot explain the content, since it says why authority weakened without saying why it weakened in the direction of these particular doctrines rather than others. A history using both is better than either, and the case is worth having available because it is one where a reader is unlikely to have a prior commitment that decides the outcome in advance.

## How the two frameworks read the same evidence

The clearest way to see the difference in practice is to watch what each does with a single document, and a constitution, a law code, or a party programme will serve.

The idealist reading asks what principle the document articulates, how coherently it articulates it, what commitments follow from it that its authors may not have seen, and where it fails by its own standards. The document is treated as an attempt to state something, and the analysis proceeds by working out what the attempt requires. This is not a naive procedure; it is what legal and constitutional scholarship largely consists of, and it produces knowledge that no external account supplies, because a body of doctrine really does constrain what can be argued next within it.

The materialist reading asks who drafted the document and under what pressures, whose claims it recognises and whose it leaves unrecognised, what it makes enforceable and what it leaves to be settled by the balance of forces, and how its provisions distribute the costs and benefits of the arrangements it establishes. The document is treated as an artefact of a conflict, and the analysis proceeds by reconstructing the conflict.

The two readings are not rivals for the same job. They become rivals only when each is offered as the complete account, which is when the characteristic failures appear: an internal reading that cannot explain why a doctrine won when a rival doctrine was equally coherent, and an external reading that cannot explain why the winning doctrine took the specific form it did rather than any other formulation that would have served the same interests.

The most useful discipline for a writer is therefore to say which reading is being performed and to name what it is leaving to the other. That is a modest conclusion for a comparison of two philosophical systems, and it is the conclusion the practice of good historical work actually supports. The strongest writing in this area, on either side, tends to be the writing that knows what it is not explaining.

### Which framework should a student use in an essay?

Use whichever the question demands and say so explicitly. A question about the development of a doctrine wants the internal reading; a question about why an arrangement persisted wants the material one. Answers that apply one framework to a question calling for the other lose marks even when the analysis is competent.

One further observation about this pairing, offered as a caution rather than a conclusion. The habit of treating the two frameworks as exhaustive alternatives is itself a legacy of the nineteenth-century German argument, in which materialism and idealism were the available positions and choosing one meant refusing the other. Nothing compels a modern reader to accept that framing. Accounts of historical change built on institutional analysis, on evolutionary mechanisms of selection and retention, on network structures, or on the study of practices and their transmission are not obviously reducible to either camp, and several of them explain things both camps struggle with. A comparison of two frameworks is worth doing carefully, and it does not establish that one of them must be right. What it establishes is what each of them is for, what each treats as given, and what it costs to adopt one without the other. That is a smaller result than a verdict for one side, and it is the result the evidence supports, which is the only reason to prefer it.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between historical materialism and idealism?

Historical materialism explains the development of societies by the way they produce their means of life and the social relations that organise production, treating legal, political, and intellectual arrangements as conditioned by that organisation. Hegelian idealism explains the same development as the working out of a rational structure, the progressive realisation of freedom in the institutions and self-understanding of a people. The difference is not that one accepts and the other denies the existence of matter, since objective idealism has no difficulty granting that the world exists independently of minds. It is that one treats history as having a thought-like subject whose development the process expresses, and the other treats it as constituted by human productive activity with no subject beyond the people involved. Everything else follows from that, including what each treats as evidence and what each regards as needing explanation.

### Q: Did Marx turn Hegel upside down?

He used the image himself, in the afterword to the second German edition of the first volume of Capital, where he said that with Hegel the dialectic is standing on its head and must be inverted to find the rational kernel within the mystical shell. The image describes a real operation that Marx had learned from Feuerbach and applied to Hegel's political philosophy in 1843: reversing which term explains the other, so that actual social relations explain the idea rather than the idea explaining them. What the image conceals is that an inversion leaves the structure intact, and Louis Althusser's objection is that a dialectic which is idealist in structure remains so after being inverted, with the economy simply replacing spirit as the essence everything expresses. That objection is the strongest reason to state the difference as two specific moves rather than as a reversal.

### Q: What did Marx keep from Hegel?

Six things, and naming them is what turns a slogan into an argument. Contradiction as a form of explanation, so that a stable arrangement can contain incompatible requirements whose working out generates conflict from within. The category of totality, requiring that any phenomenon be understood through its place in a whole. The structure of Aufhebung, in which a form is cancelled and preserved at once, which is why communism can be described as abolishing private property while presupposing what private property produced. The account of alienation, in which a subject externalises itself in a product that then confronts it as an alien power. The method of moving from abstract to concrete in presentation, which shapes the architecture of Capital. And the historicising of categories that appear timeless, applied by Hegel to political philosophy and by Marx to political economy.

### Q: What do the rational kernel and mystical shell refer to?

They are Marx's terms, from the afterword to the second German edition of Capital, for the two components he distinguished in Hegel's dialectic. The rational kernel is the account of development through internal contradiction, in which a form generates the conflicts that transform it, which Marx regarded as a genuine discovery and retained. The mystical shell is the idealist framing in which that development is the self-movement of spirit or of the concept, which he rejected. The metaphor is doing double work: it presents Hegel as containing something valuable that its author had misdescribed, and it defends Marx's own indebtedness at a moment when German opinion had turned against Hegel entirely. It is worth remembering that the passage is a paragraph in a preface, not a worked-out philosophical account, and readers who treat it as a complete statement of the relation cannot explain why Capital is structured as it is.

### Q: Was Marx ever a Hegelian?

Yes, and he said so. He belonged to the Young Hegelian circles in Berlin and Cologne in his early twenties, his doctoral work was written within a broadly Hegelian framework, and his first sustained political criticism, of Hegel's philosophy of right in 1843, is conducted with Hegelian tools against Hegelian conclusions. He described himself as a pupil of that mighty thinker and as having openly avowed himself so at a time when it was unfashionable. What changed was not an abandonment of the apparatus but a change in what the apparatus was applied to and in what was treated as fundamental. Whether the change amounts to a break or a development is a genuine scholarly dispute, and the answer depends on which texts are weighted: the early criticism looks like continuity, the manuscripts of 1845 look like rupture, and the architecture of Capital looks like continuity again.

### Q: How does idealism explain historical change?

A form of life is organised around a principle that its members recognise and act on, and its institutions embody that principle. Change occurs because the principle, worked out consistently, generates demands the existing institutions cannot satisfy. The emergence of individual conscience within the Greek city-state, which the substantial ethical life of the polis could not accommodate, is Hegel's paradigm case; the Reformation's establishment of individual conscience in religion and the subsequent demand that political institutions reflect it is another. Two strengths should be conceded: the account explains why change occurs at particular moments rather than continuously, and it takes seriously the fact that historical actors understood themselves to be pursuing something, which any adequate explanation has to accommodate. Its weakness is that it cannot say why a principle gets pressed at one moment rather than another, or why the transitions have the particular economic character they do.

### Q: Which philosophers influenced Marx besides Hegel?

Ludwig Feuerbach supplied the method of transformative criticism, without which the inversion story has no content. Adam Smith and David Ricardo supplied the economic material, including the labour theory of value and the treatment of distribution as a conflict between classes. The French socialists and the French historians of the Restoration supplied the concept of class struggle, which Marx explicitly said he had not discovered, claiming instead to have connected it to particular phases of production. Epicurus mattered more than is usually recognised: Marx's doctoral dissertation compared his natural philosophy with that of Democritus, and what interested him was the swerve, the element that leaves room for something other than mechanical necessity. The Scottish Enlightenment accounts of societies passing through stages distinguished by their mode of subsistence were also in circulation, and Marx transformed them by making relations of production rather than means of subsistence the differentiating feature.

### Q: Is materialism the opposite of idealism?

In the philosophical sense, yes. Materialism holds that matter and its organisation are fundamental and that mind is a product of material processes; idealism holds that a rational or mental structure is fundamental. But two cautions matter. The opposite of idealism is not realism, since objective idealists are realists about the structure they describe, and the opposite of idealism is certainly not pessimism, which is the everyday sense of the word and the source of the most damaging error students make on this topic. Second, the opposition is less useful than it looks for comparing Hegel and Marx, because neither is describing physics. Hegel's spirit exists only in institutions and practices; Marx's material conditions include skills, techniques, and forms of cooperation that are products of thought. The real difference lies in what explains what, not in an inventory of what exists.

### Q: Why does the materialism and idealism distinction still matter?

Because the choice between them is a choice about what an explanation of a social phenomenon has to include, and that choice is made constantly by people who never use either word. An account of a legal reform that explains it by the arguments made for it, and stops there, has taken an idealist position on that question. An account that explains it by the interests it served, and never asks why those interests could be pressed in that form at that moment, has taken a materialist one. Both accounts are incomplete in identifiable ways. The distinction remains useful as a diagnostic: it tells you what a given explanation is treating as needing explanation and what it is treating as given. Every framework has something it declines to explain, and finding that thing is the first move in evaluating any of them.

### Q: What role did Feuerbach play between Hegel and Marx?

He supplied the operation that makes the inversion story intelligible. His account of religion treats the attributes ascribed to God as human attributes abstracted, magnified, projected outwards, and then worshipped as alien, so that religion is true as anthropology and false as theology. The method that follows, which he called transformative criticism, consists in reversing subject and predicate: where speculative philosophy makes thought the subject and human beings its expression, transformative criticism makes human beings the subject. Marx applied that operation to Hegel's political philosophy in 1843, arguing that Hegel had made the idea the subject and actual families and civil society its predicates. He then applied the criticism to Feuerbach in turn, charging him with a materialism that contemplates the world rather than grasping it as the product of accumulated human activity. Historical materialism is what results from making both corrections.

### Q: Is Hegel's philosophy of history compatible with Marx's?

Not as complete accounts, since they give incompatible answers to the question of what the historical process fundamentally is. They are more compatible as instruments than as doctrines. The Hegelian framework is better at explaining the internal development of a body of thought, a legal tradition, or a religious doctrine, because it takes seriously the way working out a commitment generates problems that force change. The materialist framework is better at explaining how a society reproduces itself, why particular institutional arrangements persist, and what makes them vulnerable, because it specifies mechanisms and identifies material evidence. The best historical writing on large transitions uses both, and the strongest Marxist work in cultural and legal history borrows heavily from the Hegelian side. Treating the choice as exclusive is a doctrinal habit rather than a methodological requirement.

### Q: Did Marx keep Hegel's idea that history has a direction?

He kept directionality and rejected the guarantee, and the distinction carries most of the weight in this comparison. A tendency generated by specified mechanisms is not a telos: if competition compels accumulation, accumulation concentrates capital, and concentration produces recurrent crises, the theory has described a direction without invoking an end that draws the process towards it, and blocking the mechanisms blocks the direction. A telos is an end that explains the stages leading to it, so that earlier stages occurred because they were necessary for the later. Marx's economic analysis uses the first structure. The honest concession is that his more rhetorical writing often reads teleologically, that he never systematically distinguished the two, and that a great deal of later Marxism, especially the stage theories of the Second International and Soviet orthodoxy, reintroduced the teleology explicitly.

### Q: How do you answer an exam question comparing Marx and Hegel?

State the two moves and support each with one example. Marx kept contradiction as a form of explanation and totality as a methodological requirement; he rejected the self-moving subject and the guarantee of the outcome. An answer built on that structure has said something, while an answer built on the inversion image has restated the question. Add Feuerbach, since without transformative criticism the inversion has no content, and the omission is what most weak answers have in common. Include one evaluative move: the objection that the proletariat performs the role spirit performed, and the reply distinguishing a tendency produced by mechanisms from an end that explains its own stages. Two short quotations are worth having exactly, the rational kernel formulation and the eleventh thesis on Feuerbach. Avoid the three traps: idealism as optimism, the claim that Marx rejected the dialectic, and attributing thesis, antithesis, and synthesis to Hegel.

### Q: Why do critics say Marx's materialism is still idealist?

The objection is structural rather than political and was pressed hardest by Louis Althusser. An inversion, by its nature, preserves the structure of what it inverts and changes only which end is uppermost. If Hegel's dialectic is idealist in the way it relates parts to a whole, treating every conflict in a society as an expression of a single underlying principle, then inverting it yields a materialism with the same architecture, in which the economy takes the place of spirit as the essence that everything else expresses. The result would be an economic reductionism that is idealist in form while materialist in content. Althusser's alternative was to abandon the expressive model altogether, replacing it with a complex whole whose levels have their own effectivity and whose events are overdetermined by contradictions at several levels. Whether that is a repair of Marx or a departure from him is contested.

### Q: Does Hegel say that whatever exists is rational?

No, though the sentence from the preface to the Philosophy of Right is constantly read that way. His technical vocabulary distinguishes existence from actuality: something is actual when it fully expresses its concept, and a great deal that exists does not. A corrupt institution exists without being actual in his sense, and nothing in the formula commits him to endorsing it. The reading that turns the sentence into a defence of the Prussian state was current in his own lifetime and he addressed it, and Engels made the point in his book on Feuerbach, arguing that the formula is revolutionary as much as conservative since it implies that what has ceased to be rational has ceased to be actual and is due to perish. Whether Hegel's politics were accommodating or critically constitutionalist remains a real scholarly dispute, but it cannot be settled by this sentence.

### Q: What should a teacher watch out for when comparing Marx and Hegel?

The meaning of idealism, and it must be handled in the first ten minutes or the session is lost. Students arrive with the everyday sense, in which an idealist has high principles, and they will map it onto Hegel and produce a contrast between a hopeful thinker and a hard-headed one that later correction never dislodges. The question that clears it fastest is whether an idealist could be a pessimist and whether a materialist could believe in progress; both answers are yes, and discovering that does what a definition on a slide does not. The second thing to preempt is the claim that Marx rejected the dialectic, which is contradicted by the very passage students quote. The most productive extract is a paired reading of Hegel on the cunning of reason and the opening of the Eighteenth Brumaire, since both describe actors producing outcomes they did not intend.

### Q: Did Marx read Hegel closely or only in summary?

Closely, and repeatedly, over decades. His engagement began in Berlin in his early twenties, produced a detailed section-by-section criticism of the Philosophy of Right in 1843, and continued into the period of his economic work: he wrote to Engels in 1858 that he had been looking through Hegel's Logic again and that it had been useful for his method of treatment. The architecture of Capital, moving from simple to complex determinations, reflects that reading. Lenin's notebooks on the Logic, written during the First World War, took the connection seriously enough to claim that Capital cannot be fully understood without it, a judgment that opened space for philosophical work in the Soviet period. What is disputed is not whether Marx read Hegel but how much of the Hegelian apparatus survives in the mature economics, and readings range from the substantially Hegelian to the emphatically anti-Hegelian.

### Q: Is the cunning of reason the same as the invisible hand?

They are structurally similar and philosophically different, and the comparison is worth making carefully. Both describe individuals pursuing private purposes and producing an aggregate outcome that none of them intended, and in both cases the outcome is presented as beneficial. Adam Smith's version is a claim about a mechanism, namely competition in a market, which converts self-interested conduct into an efficient allocation, and it carries no commitment to a direction of history. Hegel's version is a claim about the structure of the historical process, in which the passions of individuals serve the development of spirit, and it is inseparable from a direction. Marx's account of unintended aggregate outcomes borrows the form from both while locating the mechanism in the relations of production, which is why his analysis of competition compelling firms to accumulate has a recognisably Smithian shape inside a Hegelian architecture.
