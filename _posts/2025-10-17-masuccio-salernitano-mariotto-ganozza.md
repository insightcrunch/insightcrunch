The Romeo and Juliet story does not begin in Verona, and it does not begin with a feud. It begins in Siena, in a fifteenth-century Italian tale about a young man named Mariotto Mignanelli and a young woman named Ganozza, and the plot it assembles there is recognizably the plot the whole world knows: a secret marriage performed by a friar, a killing that drives the husband into exile, a sleeping potion that counterfeits death, a message that never arrives, a tomb opened at night, and two lovers dead because the machinery of rescue failed at the final joint. Masuccio Salernitano published that tale in his collection Il Novellino in 1476, roughly half a century before Luigi da Porto gave the lovers their Verona, their feuding houses, and their famous names. This article reads Masuccio's novella whole, as a work in its own right, and reaches a verdict on the question source study keeps circling: how much of the familiar story is already here, and how much had to wait for later hands.

The verdict can be stated at the outset, because everything that follows is its evidence. Masuccio's tale is the root of the tradition in the strong sense: it is the earliest surviving text in which the story exists as an assembled whole rather than as scattered motifs, and the ordered sequence of its inventions, secret friar-marriage, homicide and exile, feigned death by powder, miscarried message, tomb, catastrophe, is too specific and too sequential to have been assembled twice by coincidence. Yet a root is not a blueprint. The tale has no feud, no Verona, no balcony, no Mercutio, no double suicide, and no reconciliation over the bodies. Its hero is beheaded by the state and its heroine dies of grief behind convent walls. What Masuccio supplies is the skeleton and, above all, the failure mechanism that makes the skeleton tragic; what he does not supply is nearly everything the later tradition is loved for. Holding those two truths together, without inflating either, is the discipline this article observes throughout.

## Mariotto and Ganozza: Why the Earliest Full Telling Rewrites the Story's History

The familiar account of the play's ancestry starts the story late. It begins with Da Porto's Giulietta e Romeo in the 1520s or 1530s, passes through Bandello and Boaistuau, crosses the Channel with Arthur Brooke's poem of 1562 and William Painter's prose of 1566, and arrives at Shakespeare's stage in the mid-1590s. That chain is real, and the delivered studies in this series have traced it link by link. But the chain has a root beneath its first famous link, and the root changes what the chain means. When Da Porto set his lovers in Verona and gave them the names Romeo and Giulietta, he was not assembling the story from fragments. He was rewriting a story that already existed in finished form, with its catastrophe fully engineered, in a tale printed when his grandparents' generation was young.

The evidence for that priority is the tale's contents, and they deserve stating plainly before any argument is built on them. In Masuccio's thirty-third novella, Mariotto and Ganozza love each other in Siena and, because an open marriage is impossible, are married in secret by a friar who becomes their confidant and agent. Mariotto then kills a man in a street quarrel, is condemned, and flees Italy for Alexandria, where he lives in exile. Ganozza, pressed by her family toward another marriage, takes from the same friar a powder that lays her in a deathlike sleep for three days; she is mourned and buried as dead. The friar's letter explaining the stratagem never reaches Alexandria. Mariotto hears instead that his wife has died, returns in secret, opens her tomb by night, and is seized there by the watch; confessing everything, he is executed. Ganozza wakes, learns what has happened, shuts herself in a convent, and dies of grief. Every load-bearing joint of the later story is present: the clandestine rite, the killing, the exile, the drug, the failed letter, the tomb, the double death. What source study calls the Romeo and Juliet plot is, in its architecture, a Sienese plot of the 1470s.

That is why the standard scholarly instruments treat Masuccio as more than a curiosity. Geoffrey Bullough, in the first volume of Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, prints the Mariotto and Ganozza tale, in translation, among the documents a student of the play's ancestry must read, the earliest complete form of the story his collection assembles. Olin H. Moore, in his origins research on the legend, went further and argued that Da Porto took the plot from Masuccio directly. Later editors have been more guarded, and the guardedness matters; a later section of this article adjudicates the dispute in full. But the floor of agreement is high: no earlier text tells this story whole, and every later text tells it with Masuccio's joints in Masuccio's order or in an order derived from them. The habit of beginning the story with Da Porto's star-crossed Veronese is a compression of the record, and restoring Masuccio is the correction.

### Where does the Mariotto tale sit inside Masuccio's collection?

It sits thirty-third among the fifty tales of Il Novellino, the collection Tommaso Guardati published under the name Masuccio Salernitano at Naples in 1476. The position matters because the surrounding tales are largely comic and satirical, which makes the novella's tragic architecture stand out as the collection's most consequential experiment.

## Il Novellino and the Naples of the 1470s: The Book the Tale Lives In

Understanding what kind of root Masuccio is requires understanding what kind of book Il Novellino is, because the tale's character follows from the collection's character. The novella tradition Masuccio inherited was Boccaccio's: the Decameron, a century and a quarter old by 1476, had fixed the form of the framed tale collection, prose stories told for an audience, mixing the comic, the pathetic, and the scandalous. Masuccio alters the inheritance in a distinctive way. Where Boccaccio disperses narration among ten young tellers in a garden, Masuccio speaks in his own voice throughout: each of his fifty tales opens with a prose argument announcing its matter and its dedicatee, and closes with the author's own commentary, a section in which Masuccio steps forward, draws the lesson, jokes, moralizes, and not rarely settles scores. The collection is dedicated, tale by tale, to nobles and courtiers of Aragonese Naples, and its register is worldly, satirical, and sharply anticlerical in places, full of friars who scheme, husbands who are fooled, and women who outwit both.

That frame matters for the Mariotto tale in two ways. First, it explains the tale's tone at its joints: Masuccio is a moralizing narrator in his own person, and his commentary voice treats the lovers' fate as a warning about the governance of desire and the folly of trusting fortune, not as the sacrament later centuries would make of it. The play's Chorus, announcing "a pair of star-cross'd lovers" who take their life (Prologue), inherits that posture of public summary and judgment, though the playwright fills it with a pity Masuccio largely withholds. Second, the frame explains the tale's survival route. Il Novellino was a printed book, one of the early products of the Neapolitan press, produced within a few years of printing's arrival in the city, and Masuccio died in or about 1475, just before his collection appeared. A story carried in a printed collection at Naples in 1476 was positioned to travel: into the hands of the Venetian and Lombard storytellers of the next generation, among them the soldier from Vicenza, Luigi da Porto, and the Dominican friar Matteo Bandello, who between them would carry the tale from Siena to Verona and from prose anecdote to European myth.

The collection's date has a further consequence that readers of the play rarely register. When Shakespeare's contemporaries treated Romeo and Juliet as an old Italian story, the oldness they invoked was greater than they knew. The play reached the London stage around 1595; its plot, in assembled form, had already been in print for about a hundred and twenty years. Brooke's title page could advertise the story's Italian currency, and Brooke's preface could treat it as a tale of unfortunate lovers already moralized by others, because the moralizing had begun with Masuccio's own commentary voice in 1476. The tale arrived in England with a century of interpretation already attached, and Masuccio is where that interpretation starts.

Masuccio himself is worth a paragraph of biography, kept within what the record supports. Tommaso Guardati came from Salerno, south of Naples, and spent his career in the orbit of the Aragonese court, serving in secretarial and household roles under the kings of Naples; his surname in the documents is Guardati, and "Masuccio" is the familiar diminutive under which he wrote and was known. His collection shows a man writing for a court that enjoyed being scandalized within limits: the tales praise named patrons, attack clerical hypocrisy with relish, and return obsessively to the theme of fortune's reversals, the wheel that lifts servants and drops gentlemen. That courtly, fortune-haunted, anticlerical book is an unlikely cradle for the most famous love story in the language, which is precisely why its evidence is so valuable. Nothing in Masuccio's posture is romantic in the later sense. His lovers are not celebrated as martyrs to a transcendent passion; they are exhibits in an argument about desire, secrecy, and chance. The story's machinery was built by a skeptic, and it has outlasted every more sentimental machine built to replace it.

## The Tale Told Whole: Love and a Secret Marriage in Siena

Masuccio sets his tale in Siena, the Tuscan hill city whose factional violence was proverbial in fifteenth-century Italy, and he is careful, in the manner of the novella tradition, to present the events as a true story of named people rather than an invented fable. Mariotto bears a family name, Mignanelli, belonging to the Sienese world the tale addresses, and the narrative proceeds with the specificity of local report: these streets, this family, this friar. The posture of fact is a convention of the genre, and no modern reader is obliged to believe that a historical Mariotto died as the tale describes. But the posture matters for the tradition, because every reteller after Masuccio repeats it. Da Porto will claim that an archer named Peregrino told him a true Veronese story from the days of Bartolomeo della Scala; Brooke will present the tale as recent Italian fact; the play's Chorus will swear to "the two hours' traffic of our stage" as the record of a real catastrophe. The claim of truth enters the tradition with Masuccio, and it enters as a Siena claim before it is ever a Verona claim.

The love itself begins in the conventional grammar of the Italian novella: Mariotto sees Ganozza, is struck, and pursues the affair through the channels such stories allow, glances, messages, intermediaries, until the two arrive at the obstacle that organizes everything after. Ganozza's family stands between the lovers and any open marriage. The tale's economy of opposition is worth pausing over, because it differs from the later story's in a way that changes the tragedy's meaning. In Masuccio there is no feud. No second house hates the Mignanelli; no prince has banned street brawling on pain of death; no cousin waits with a rapier and a grievance. The obstacle is the ordinary machinery of fifteenth-century marriage politics: a daughter is an asset of her family's strategy, her wishes are not the deciding voice, and a match arranged without the family's consent is a theft of that asset. The lovers are not rebels against a civic curse. They are two young people trying to steal, from an indifferent system, the one thing the system reserves the right to dispose of.

Their instrument is the friar. Masuccio's friar is an Augustinian, a confessor figure with access to both households and a conscience flexible enough for the work, and he performs the act on which the whole tradition turns: he marries the lovers in secret. The sacrament is real, and that reality is the tale's tragic engine, because a secret marriage in the fifteenth century was not a pretend marriage. Before the Council of Trent tightened the rules of publicity a century later, consent exchanged before witnesses made a binding union in canon law, however clandestine and however furious the families. Masuccio's lovers are therefore not eloping fantasists; they are husband and wife in the eyes of the Church, armed with a sacrament and surrounded by a society that does not know it exists. Every later version keeps this structure exactly. The play's Friar Laurence states the same legal and pastoral reality when he agrees to marry Romeo and Juliet in his cell, hoping that "this alliance may so happy prove" to reconcile their houses (2.3), a hope the Sienese friar cannot even entertain, since no feud exists for his marriage to heal. In Masuccio the rite cures nothing. It only creates the secret that the rest of the tale will punish.

### How are Mariotto and Ganozza married, and who performs the rite?

The lovers are married in secret by an Augustinian friar who serves as their confessor, go-between, and sole witness of standing. The rite is clandestine but canonically binding: consent exchanged before the friar makes Mariotto and Ganozza husband and wife in the Church's eyes, even though Ganozza's family knows nothing of it and would never have consented.

The marriage's secrecy dictates the marriage's conduct. The lovers meet as they can, under the friar's cover, and the tale gives their union a brief season of stolen happiness before the second engine of the plot starts. That engine is violence, and Masuccio introduces it with the economy of a man who knows his city's habits. Mariotto, in a quarrel, kills a man, a fellow citizen of standing. The novella does not build the killing into a duel of honor with seconds and rapiers; it is street violence of the kind Siena produced constantly, a brawl's fatal outcome. But its legal consequence is absolute. Homicide in the commune's courts means a capital sentence, and the sentence, pronounced against the absent Mariotto, converts him into a dead man if he is taken within the city's reach. The parallel with the play is structural and exact, though the identities have shifted: Romeo kills Tybalt and receives banishment from the Prince, "exile is death misterm'd" in the Friar's rebuke (3.3), while Mariotto kills a citizen and receives a death sentence softened only by distance. Shakespeare will reduce the penalty and add the feud to motivate it; Masuccio needs no feud, because in his world the commune's law is penalty enough.

## The Killing and the Road to Alexandria: How the Lovers Are Parted

Mariotto flees. The geography of his exile is one of the tale's most distinctive choices and one of the strongest fingerprints it leaves on the tradition's prehistory. He does not withdraw to a neighboring town, as Romeo withdraws to Mantua, twenty miles from Juliet and close enough for a servant to ride with news in a morning. He leaves Italy altogether and settles in Alexandria, in Egypt, on the far shore of the Mediterranean, in the great trading city where Italian merchants maintained their fondaci and where a fugitive Italian could disappear into the Levant trade. The distance is the point. Alexandria is far enough that news travels slowly, by ship and by merchant letter, at the mercy of season and plague and war; far enough that a husband can be alive, faithful, and waiting while being, for every practical purpose, unreachable. Masuccio builds the catastrophe's fuse out of that distance. A message between Siena and Verona can fail once, by quarantine, as Friar John's letter fails in the play (5.2); a message between Siena and Alexandria can fail simply because the Mediterranean is wide and the fifteenth century's post is a rumor with a sail.

The exile section of the tale also fixes the lovers' roles in the shape the tradition will keep. Mariotto in Alexandria is the waiting husband: passive, faithful, suspended, his whole existence organized around letters and the hope of recall or reunion. Ganozza in Siena is the besieged wife: enclosed in her family's house, her secret marriage unknown, her hand an asset her kin are determined to spend. The pressure on her is the pressure of the marriage market resuming its ordinary course. A daughter of marriageable age, unmarried in the world's eyes, cannot simply refuse the world forever; suitors are entertained, negotiations advance, and the family's will hardens toward a match. Masuccio does not name the crisis after a cousin's death or a father's raging ultimatum, as the play will do when Capulet fixes Juliet's wedding to Paris on pain of disowning her (3.5). His crisis is quieter and, in its way, colder: the system simply continues, and the secret wife is about to be married, publicly and sacramentally, to another man, an outcome that would make her a bigamist in conscience and destroy her in fact.

It is at exactly this joint that the tale's third engine starts, and it is the engine the whole world remembers. Ganozza turns to the friar, the one man who knows she is already a wife, and the friar produces the stratagem: a powder, compounded of his art, that will lay her in a sleep indistinguishable from death for three days, long enough for her to be mourned, buried, and then recovered from the tomb by the friar and conveyed to safety, while word goes to Alexandria to prepare Mariotto for the truth. Every element of the play's potion plot is present in embryo: the clerical pharmacist, the measured duration, the counterfeit corpse, the tomb as a waiting room rather than an ending, and the letter on which everything depends. What Masuccio invents here, and what no earlier surviving tale assembles in this form, is the conversion of a love story into a logistics problem. From the moment the powder is proposed, the tragedy is no longer about whether the lovers love enough. It is about whether information can travel faster than error. That conversion is the deepest thing the play inherits, deeper than any name or place: Shakespeare's catastrophe, like Masuccio's, is an information catastrophe, a rescue that fails because a message and a rumor arrive in the wrong order.

## The Sleeping Powder and the Letter That Never Arrives: The Catastrophe Assembled

The feigned death is the tale's most famous bequest, and it deserves a precision the tradition's summaries do not always give it. Ganozza takes the friar's powder and falls into a sleep so complete that her family, her physicians, and her city accept her as dead. She is lamented with the full apparatus of a great household's grief and carried to burial in a tomb, in the manner of Italian interment inside a church, while the friar waits for the three days to run and the sleeper to wake into his keeping. The play preserves the design with its specifications tightened: the Friar's vial will keep Juliet in "borrowed likeness of shrunk death" for "two and forty hours" (4.1), a duration chosen, like the tale's three days, to cover the funeral and the tomb and nothing more. In both texts the drug is a technology of timing, and its horror is that it works perfectly. Nothing in either catastrophe is caused by the potion failing. The potion succeeds; everything else fails. That distinction, which weaker tellings blur by making the drug unreliable or the sleeper wake too soon, is Masuccio's gift to the tradition: a machine that performs exactly as designed, inside a world whose other machines, letters, ships, rumors, guards, do not.

### How long does Ganozza's feigned death last, and how is it produced?

Ganozza's feigned death lasts three days, produced by a powder the friar compounds and gives her to take in secret. The sleep counterfeits death closely enough to deceive her family and the whole city: she is mourned as a corpse, carried to the tomb, and left there to wake under the friar's care once the three days have run.

The letter is where the design meets the world. The friar writes to Mariotto in Alexandria, explaining that Ganozza's death is a stratagem, that she lives, and that she will be recovered and kept until they can be reunited. The letter never arrives. The accounts of the tale agree on the failure and on its consequence, and the consequence is the tradition's founding irony: what travels across the Mediterranean successfully is not the truth but the error. Travelers and merchants carry to Alexandria the news that Ganozza of Siena has died and been buried with public mourning, because a beautiful young woman's funeral is news, and a secret letter is only paper. Mariotto receives the rumor in its most lethal form, stripped of the one fact that would have disarmed it. The play keeps this structure with an almost documentary fidelity, merely changing the failure's mechanism: Friar John's letter is stopped by plague quarantine, "the searchers of the town, suspecting that we both were in a house where the infectious pestilence did reign, sealed up the doors, and would not let us forth" (5.2), while Balthasar's eyewitness error rides through unimpeded. Quarantine in the play, distance and chance in the tale: the variable is the obstacle, and the constant is the asymmetry. Truth in this story always travels slowly and in single copy; error travels fast and in many mouths. Masuccio saw that asymmetry first, and built the ending on it.

Mariotto's response to the news is the act that seals the tale. He does not wait, inquire, or write; grief annihilates procedure. He travels back to Italy and enters Siena in secret, a condemned man returning to the one jurisdiction where his life is forfeit, because the place where Ganozza lies is the only place he can imagine being. By night he goes to the tomb, opens it, and takes the body of his wife in his arms, weeping over the woman he believes dead, in the tale's most harrowing tableau: the husband embracing the sleeping wife in her grave clothes, neither knowing that a few hours divide them from a waking that would have saved them both. The play will transpose this tableau into Romeo's speech over Juliet's body in the Capulet vault, "for here lies Juliet, and her beauty makes this vault a feasting presence full of light" (5.3), and will give its hero poison and a dagger's sequel. Masuccio gives his hero something grimmer than poison: witnesses. The noise, the light, the opened tomb attract the attention of the watch, and Mariotto is taken in the tomb itself, in the act of embracing a corpse, in a city where he is already under sentence of death.

## The Tomb, the Scaffold, and the Convent: An Ending Without Suicide

The ending of Masuccio's tale is the point at which the tradition's debt and the tradition's originality can be measured most exactly, because the ending is where later tellers did their boldest rewriting. Mariotto, arrested at the tomb, confesses the whole story: the secret marriage, the flight, the reason for his return. The confession does him no good. The commune's justice is neither corrupt nor cruel in the tale's telling; it is simply itself, a legal machine processing a condemned homicide found within its walls. Mariotto is executed, beheaded, and the tale moves past the scaffold with the speed of a chronicle. The husband does not die by his own hand, does not choose his death, does not speak a famous last line over his wife's body. He is processed. The difference from the play could hardly be greater, and it is the difference between two theories of tragedy. In Masuccio, the world kills the lovers: law kills the husband, grief kills the wife, and the friar's stratagem stands exposed as a cleverness that underestimated both. In the play, the lovers kill themselves, and the world, in the persons of the Prince and the fathers, arrives too late with its gold statues and its "glooming peace" (5.3). Shakespeare privatizes the catastrophe, moving it from the scaffold to the tomb and from the executioner's hands to the lovers' own; Masuccio leaves it public, civic, and juridical, and there is a bleakness in his version that the play's more beautiful ending does not attempt.

Ganozza's death completes the asymmetry. She wakes from the three days' sleep into the friar's care and learns, in the tale's cruellest ordering, that the stratagem worked and that it killed her husband anyway: Mariotto heard of her death, returned, was taken at her tomb, and has already been executed for the return her feigned death provoked. Her response is the tale's last invention, and the one the tradition discarded most completely. She does not kill herself. She withdraws from the world, entering a convent, a monastery of religious women, and there, in a short span of time, she dies of grief, of the sheer refusal of a life that has been emptied. The convent death matters because it is a social death before it is a physical one: Ganozza removes herself from the marriage market, from her family's strategies, from the city that buried and mourned her, and dies as a kind of living reproach inside an institution. Later tellers found this ending unusable, and their reasons are instructive. Da Porto gives Giulietta a death at the tomb, holding her breath and then, in the developed tradition, the dagger enters: the play's Juliet, waking to find Romeo dead, takes his weapon with "O happy dagger, this is thy sheath" (5.3). The active, self-willed heroine's death is a later creation. Masuccio's Ganozza is destroyed by grief as Mariotto is destroyed by law, and the tale's final note is not transcendence but waste.

Two rows deserve prose emphasis because a table flattens them. The first is the message row. In both tellings the catastrophe is not caused by hatred, fate, or character in the grand senses the criticism of the play often prefers; it is caused by a communications failure between a friar and an exile, with rumor outperforming truth on the road. Masuccio's version is the harsher of the two because his failure has no single dramatic agent: no plague house, no sealed doors, only the ordinary unreliability of distance. Shakespeare, the dramatist, needed a scene, and gave the failure a face in Friar John; Masuccio, the chronicler of a court, needed only the world as it was. The second is the closing row. Masuccio's tale ends without a reconciling tableau because his Siena has nothing to reconcile: there are no two houses, only one family, one commune, one convent. The famous ending of the play, the fathers' hands joined over the corpses and the Prince's summary that "all are punished" (5.3), depends entirely on the feud machinery that Masuccio never built. The tradition's most quoted consolation is thus, strictly, an addition to the tradition's root, invented when the story acquired the civic frame it did not start with.

### What is the biggest difference between Masuccio's tale and the play?

The biggest difference is the feud, because everything else follows from its absence. Without two warring houses there is no divided city to heal, no cousin to kill, no banishment speech, and no reconciliation over the bodies; Masuccio's obstacle is one family's marriage strategy and a commune's homicide law, which produce a colder, more juridical tragedy.

## No Feud, No Verona, No Balcony: What the Tradition Had Not Yet Invented

The absences in Masuccio's tale are not gaps in a draft; they are a map of the later tradition's creativity, and cataloguing them precisely is the surest way to distribute credit honestly. Begin with the feud. The hatred of Montague and Capulet, "an ancient grudge" breaking "to new mutiny" in the Prologue's words, is the play's first fact and its closing problem, and it does not exist in Siena. Masuccio's lovers face opposition, but opposition of the ordinary, unheroic kind: parental strategy, social expectation, the law of homicide. There is no Tybalt, because there is no house honor for a Tybalt to defend; the man Mariotto kills is a citizen in a quarrel, not a cousin in a vendetta, and his death creates a legal problem rather than a blood debt. There is no Mercutio, no Benvolio, no Nurse as the tradition knows her, no County Paris pressing a father's timetable; the dramatis personae of the play beyond the lovers and the friar are, nearly entire, later inventions. Even the heroine's family behaves differently: Ganozza's kin press a marriage on her as families did, but Masuccio stages no scene of paternal rage, no Capulet bellowing that his daughter will be dragged to church or disowned (3.5). The pressure is structural, not theatrical.

The famous scenes are absent for the same reason. There is no ball at which the lovers meet across a crowded room and exchange a sonnet, because Masuccio's courtship moves through the novella's ordinary channels of sight and message. There is no balcony scene, no orchard at night, no parting at dawn with the lark and the nightingale disputed between them; those intensities belong to the story's stage life, invented when dramatists needed the lovers' private world made audible. There is no Queen Mab, no apothecary starving in Mantua whose poverty Romeo buys with gold and a speech about poison and law (5.1); Mariotto needs no poison, since the state will kill him free of charge. And there is, above all, no double suicide and no shared tomb death at all: the lovers never meet again after the exile, not even over a body, because Mariotto is arrested with the living Ganozza in his arms and executed before she wakes. The tradition's iconic image, the two bodies together in the vault discovered by the watch and the fathers, is an image Masuccio's plot makes impossible. His lovers are separated by the catastrophe as they were separated by the exile, and that separation, death without a final meeting, is arguably the bleakest single fact in the tale.

The names tell the same story in miniature. Romeo and Giulietta, Montague and Capulet, Verona itself: none of it is Masuccio's. The family names descend, as the scholarship of the tradition has long established, from Dante's Purgatorio, where Montecchi and Cappelletti appear in a catalogue of Italy's disordered houses, and it was Da Porto who drew the names from Dante's well and fixed them to the tale. Masuccio's Siena has its own real families, and his hero carries one of their names, Mignanelli, but the mythic nomenclature of the later story is a sixteenth-century graft onto a fifteenth-century root. Even the lovers' Christian names change at the grafting: Mariotto, a diminutive with a Marian echo, becomes Romeo, the pilgrim to Rome, a name that lets the play build its pilgrimage imagery into its hero's very signature; Ganozza, in some transmission spelled Gianozza, becomes Giulietta, the little Julia, a name with a July birthday argument waiting inside it. Nothing about the change is cosmetic. Names in this tradition are programs, and the program was rewritten when the story moved north from Siena to Verona.

## From Siena to Verona: How Da Porto Rebuilt Masuccio's Material

Between Masuccio's printing in 1476 and Da Porto's composition of Giulietta e Romeo, usually dated to the 1520s and printed in 1530 or 1531, the tale changed hands, cities, and meanings. Da Porto's novella is the hinge of the whole tradition, the point at which the Sienese anecdote becomes the Verona myth, and the delivered study of [Luigi da Porto and the birth of 'Giulietta'](/2025/10/03/luigi-da-porto-giulietta-origin/) treats his version whole: the soldier author, the frame of the archer Peregrino, the setting under Bartolomeo della Scala, the invention of the feud's named houses. The present article's concern is narrower and prior: what Da Porto did to Masuccio's material, item by item, because his alterations define what a reworking is. He kept the architecture and replaced the world.

He kept the secret marriage by a friar, the killing that exiles the husband, the wife pressed toward a second marriage, the friar's sleeping draught, the failed message, the tomb, and the deaths. He changed the setting from Siena to Verona and grounded it in a claimed history two centuries old; he named the families from Dante and set them at feud, converting private opposition into civic tragedy; he invented the meeting at a great feast, with the lovers' eyes locking across a dance; he gave the husband a rival cousin to kill, Tebaldo, so that the killing becomes kin-murder and the exile becomes banishment to nearby Mantua rather than flight across the sea; he brought the heroine's death into the tomb and onto the husband's body, ending her life at the scene of his, where Masuccio had sent his heroine to a convent; and he closed the tale with the two houses reconciled in grief, where Masuccio had closed with a chronicle's waste. Read against Masuccio, Da Porto's novella is an act of systematic theatricalization: every alteration either concentrates the geography, embodies the opposition, or moves death from the law's hands into the lovers' presence. It is the strongest evidence in the whole tradition that the tellers knew exactly what they were doing. Da Porto did not stumble on Masuccio's plot and copy it; he diagnosed it, saw that its machinery was perfect and its staging was prose, and rebuilt it as a story that a stage could one day detonate.

Matteo Bandello, retelling the tale a generation later in his Novelle of 1554, worked from Da Porto's Verona version rather than from Masuccio's Siena, adding the expansiveness, the courtly frame, and the household detail that Pierre Boaistuau would carry into French and the English translators into England, as the study of [Matteo Bandello's novella and its great retelling](/2025/10/10/matteo-bandello-novella-1554/) sets out. Bandello's dependence on Da Porto matters to the Masuccio question for a structural reason: it means the Siena root reached Shakespeare through a double filter, each filter a confident rewriter. By the time Brooke versified the tale in 1562, the story had been rebuilt twice since Masuccio, and yet its joints held: secret rite, killing, exile, powder, failed letter, tomb. Traditions reveal their load-bearing members by what survives rebuilding, and by that test Masuccio's architecture is the tradition. Everything else, the feud, the ball, the balcony, the statues, is cladding, magnificent cladding, fitted by later hands to a frame of 1476.

## Root or Analogue: The Scholars' Dispute and Its Adjudication

Source study has a technical vocabulary for the question this article must now decide, and the vocabulary disciplines the verdict. A source, strictly, is a text the later writer demonstrably used; an analogue is a text that resembles the work without a proven line of use; and between the two lies the murkiest territory in literary history, the territory of probable debts and lost intermediaries. Where does Masuccio stand? The cleanest statement of the strong claim belongs to Olin H. Moore, whose research into the legend's origins in the early twentieth century argued that Da Porto's plot descends from Masuccio's tale directly: the Sienese novella is not merely similar to the Verona novella, it is its parent. Moore's case rests on the density and order of shared elements, and on the absence of any competing assembled version in the decades between 1476 and Da Porto's composition. On his reading, the scholarly work of Article 196 in this series, which treats Da Porto as the story's effective origin, needs a preface: Da Porto originates the Verona form, not the story.

Geoffrey Bullough, assembling the documentary record a generation after Moore, prints Masuccio's tale in full translation at the head of the tradition and treats Da Porto as the play's great Italian intermediary, the source of the source. Bullough's placement is itself an argument: an editor does not house a text among the narrative sources of Shakespeare in order to call it a coincidence. Yet Bullough's manner is the documentarian's, presenting the tale and letting descent be inferred from resemblance and chronology rather than asserted as proven fact. Kenneth Muir, in his studies of Shakespeare's sources, represents the caution of the discipline at its most principled: the novella tradition was a common reservoir, motifs of secret marriage, feigned death, and failed messages circulated widely in Italian and French storytelling, and a shared stock can generate resemblances that mimic descent. On Muir's caution, some of Masuccio's parallels might be the tradition's common property rather than its patented inventions. The editors of the play itself have absorbed the dispute into their introductions with varying emphasis: Jill L. Levenson's Oxford edition and René Weis's Arden Third Series both give Da Porto the working-source role for the line that reaches Shakespeare, with Masuccio standing behind him as the earliest teller, the depth of his influence on Da Porto stated as probability rather than proof.

### Did Luigi da Porto read Masuccio, or is the resemblance coincidence?

Da Porto's debt to Masuccio is best stated as probable descent, not proven reading. The cluster of shared elements, secret friar-marriage, homicide exile, sleeping powder, miscarried message, tomb, arrives in the same order in both tales, which coincidence explains badly; but no letter, library record, or verbal borrowing survives to convert probability into documentary certainty.

The adjudication this article defends distinguishes two claims that the dispute often tangles. The first claim is about the story's existence: that the assembled plot, the specific machine whose failure kills the lovers, exists in Masuccio whole and nowhere earlier. That claim is as solid as anything in source study, because it rests on surviving texts and dates: Il Novellino was printed in 1476, and the assembled machine is in it. The second claim is about the channel: that Da Porto took the machine from Masuccio's pages, whether directly or through retellings now lost. That claim is probable rather than proven, and Muir's caution properly governs its wording. But probability here is not a coin toss. Coincidence must explain why an exile should flee to Alexandria in one tale and the machinery of exile survive in the other; why a friar should both marry and drug in both; why a letter should fail in both while a rumor succeeds in both; and why these elements should stand in the same causal order, each producing the next. Common stock explains shared motifs; it strains to explain shared engineering. The verdict follows: Masuccio is a root in the genetic sense, the earliest surviving ancestor from which the Verona line descends, with the channel of descent honestly labeled probable. Analogue is too weak a word for a text that contains the whole machine two generations early, and source, in the strict documentary sense, is one degree stronger than the evidence allows. Root is the exact term, and this article uses it in that exact sense.

## Ancestors Older Than Masuccio: What Even the Root Inherited

Calling Masuccio the root obliges one clarification, because roots grow in soil. The assembled plot is his; its separate motifs are far older than he is, and the article's verdict survives the admission intact. The device of a deathlike sleep produced by a drug belongs to ancient romance: in the Greek Ephesiaca of Xenophon of Ephesus, the heroine Anthia drinks a potion that counterfeits death and wakes in a tomb, from which robbers steal her, a sequence written well over a thousand years before Il Novellino. Boccaccio's Decameron, Masuccio's great model, plays its own variations on feigned death and medicinal powders, proof that the motif was live Italian property in the novella tradition Masuccio inherited. And the lovers destroyed by a message's failure and a tomb's misreading descend in spirit from the oldest ancestor the series recognizes, the Babylonian lovers of [Ovid's Pyramus and Thisbe, the play's ancient analogue](/2025/11/07/ovid-pyramus-and-thisbe-source/), who die because a veil and a lion rewrite the evidence of a tryst. None of this demotes Masuccio. A motif is not a plot. Anthia's potion serves an adventure romance of separation and reunion with a happy ending; Pyramus dies of a misread sign in a tale without marriage, friar, or letter. What no earlier surviving text does is chain the motifs into this machine, in this order, aimed at this catastrophe: marry them secretly, exile him by a killing, drug her to escape a second marriage, fail the letter, open the tomb. The chain is the invention, and the chain is Masuccio's.

The placement can be seen at a glance in [the sources of Romeo and Juliet set out as a complete family tree](/2025/09/12/sources-of-romeo-and-juliet-family-tree/), where Masuccio's tale stands at the base of the Italian line, beneath Da Porto, Bandello, Boaistuau, Painter, and Brooke, with Ovid and the ancient analogues behind it as ancestry rather than descent. The tree's shape is the article's argument in diagram form: a motif-pool of great antiquity, one fifteenth-century act of assembly, and then a century of brilliant rebuilding along a single line. It also explains why the tradition's later glories do not threaten Masuccio's standing. A root is not diminished by the tree. The balcony scene is not less Shakespeare's for having no Sienese precedent, and the tomb scene is not less Masuccio's for having been rebuilt by Da Porto and transfigured by Shakespeare. Source study at its best is not a contest for credit but an account of how credit distributes, and on that account the distribution runs: assembly to Masuccio, world to Da Porto, amplitude to Bandello, passage to Boaistuau and the English translators, and transfiguration to the playwright who turned a prose catastrophe of failed logistics into the defining love tragedy of the language.

## The Verdict: A Root, Not a Blueprint

The evidence now permits the verdict to be restated with its qualifications earned rather than merely announced. Masuccio Salernitano's thirty-third novella is the earliest surviving telling of the Romeo and Juliet story as an assembled plot, printed at Naples in 1476, set in Siena, and built around Mariotto Mignanelli and Ganozza. It contains the secret friar-marriage, the killing and exile, the sleeping powder and the three-day counterfeit death, the miscarried letter and the triumphant rumor, the night opening of the tomb. It does not contain the feud, the named houses, Verona, the ball, the balcony, the wit-combatants, the double suicide, or the reconciliation, and its own ending, the husband beheaded by civic justice, the wife dead of grief in a convent, is bleaker and less consoling than the ending the world has chosen to remember. Da Porto's descent from it is probable to the edge of certainty without documentary proof, and the scholarly dispute between Moore's direct claim, Bullough's documentary placement, and Muir's common-stock caution resolves, on the evidence of shared engineering in shared order, in Moore's favor as to substance and in Muir's favor as to wording. The tale anticipates the play's machinery almost entire and its meaning hardly at all; the machinery is the older thing, and the meaning, the feud's civic wound, love as a counter-city, death as a lovers' chosen sacrament, is the sixteenth century's addition to a fifteenth-century machine.

That distribution is why restoring Masuccio matters beyond antiquarian satisfaction. The familiar story, told from Da Porto forward, flatters a romantic reading: two houses, two lovers, a fate written in stars. Told from Masuccio forward, the story shows a different face: a skeptic at the Aragonese court, writing satirical tales for courtiers, assembles a machine in which institutions, family strategy, homicide law, a friar's cleverness, a slow post, kill two married young people who did everything the machine required of them, and then remarks, in his own commentary voice, on the folly of trusting fortune. The play's deepest ironies are already latent in that design. Romeo and Juliet die, in the end, not because the stars are cruel but because a letter is late and a rumor is early, and the first writer to build a tragedy on that bleak, modern, administrative fact was not the playwright of the 1590s but the Salernitan of the 1470s. The cliché starts the story late, in Verona, with the feud. The root starts it in Siena, with a secret, a powder, and a post that failed. Between those two starts lies the whole history of how a Sienese cautionary tale became the measure of romantic love, and the root, restored, judges every retelling that grew from it.

## Ganozza Before Juliet: The Heroine Masuccio Built

The heroine of the root tale rewards close attention, because the tradition's most consequential rewriting was performed on her. Masuccio's Ganozza is not a sketch awaiting Juliet's depth; she is a complete figure built to a different design, and the design's logic is worth recovering. She loves, consents to a clandestine sacrament, keeps the secret under the daily pressure of a household that does not know she is a wife, refuses the marriage her family prepares, and chooses the powder: the feigned death is her decision, taken with the friar, at the point where every lawful door has closed. Within the limits of her world, that is a formidable arc of agency. The novella form gives her no soliloquies, because prose anecdote has no soliloquies to give; her inwardness is rendered in acts rather than speeches. But the acts are hers. When later readers praise Juliet's potion soliloquy, the terror catalogued alone at night before she drinks (4.3), they are praising Shakespeare's conversion of Ganozza's decision into consciousness. The decision itself, the willingness to be buried alive in reputation and in fact rather than be married to another man, is Masuccio's invention, and it is the moral center of his tale.

Her ending sharpens the portrait. The tradition's Ganozza wakes, learns of the execution, and chooses the convent: a refusal of the world conducted through the world's own institution, grief organized as a permanent withdrawal. It is easy, from the far side of the play, to read this as passivity beside Juliet's dagger. Read in its own terms, it is something else: the only form of protest available to a woman whose marriage, husband, and public identity have all been annihilated by institutions acting lawfully. The convent is Siena's recognized exit from the marriage economy, and Ganozza takes it as a judgment on that economy, then dies inside it quickly, as if to complete the judgment. Da Porto and Shakespeare needed a heroine who dies at the tomb, in the lover's presence, because the stage and the novella's later taste required the catastrophe to be visible and shared. Masuccio needed a heroine whose death indicted the city that survived her. Both needs are legitimate; only one of them is original to the root.

There is a further difference in how the two heroines are known. Juliet exists for audiences as a voice: the balcony's reasoning, the gallop-apace impatience, the potion speech's terror, the tomb's brisk resolution. Ganozza exists as a sequence of facts reported by a narrator who stands outside her. The Masuccio comparison therefore measures not only what the plot gained but what the heroine gained in transit: a mind rendered from inside. Nothing in the root tale predicts that gain, and everything in the root tale enables it, because the plot Masuccio built is precisely a machine for putting a young woman in situations where her consciousness, once a dramatist supplies it, has world enough to work on.

## The Friar in Masuccio: Confessor, Pharmacist, and Postmaster

Every version of this story stands or falls with its friar, and Masuccio's is the type specimen: an Augustinian confessor who performs three functions that later tellers will distribute, expand, and judge. He marries the lovers, breaching his church's discipline and the families' rights in a single rite. He compounds the powder, supplying the technology of counterfeit death from the friar's traditional stock of herbal and medical knowledge. And he writes the letter, assuming the role of postmaster to Alexandria on which the rescue depends. Priest, pharmacist, and courier in one habit: the tale concentrates in a single minor cleric all the mediating functions between the lovers and the world, and then lets the weakest of the three, the letter, fail. The concentration is the design. Masuccio, the anticlerical satirist whose collection is full of scheming friars, builds his tragedy around the one friar who schemes benevolently and competently, and shows the scheme destroyed not by the friar's vice but by the world's friction. The satire turns somber: even the good friar, in this author's hands, is an instrument too frail for the weight lovers load on him.

Shakespeare's Friar Laurence is this figure under dramatic magnification, and the correspondences are exact enough to serve as a fingerprint of the line of descent. Laurence marries the lovers in his cell with the same pastoral casuistry, hoping the rite will reconcile houses as Masuccio's friar hoped it would simply shelter a union (2.3). He supplies the drug with the same confidence in dosage and duration, forty-two hours against the tale's three days (4.1). And the rescue's failure is postal in both: where Masuccio's letter simply never reaches Egypt, Shakespeare dramatizes the failure in Friar John's quarantine narrative, giving the catastrophe a scene, a witness, and a mechanism (5.2). What the play adds is judgment rendered in action: Laurence flees the tomb at the crucial moment, "I dare no longer stay" (5.3), and stands condemned by his own confession before the Prince. Masuccio's friar receives no such staged reckoning; he recovers the waking Ganozza and survives inside his stratagem's ruins, a quieter and in some ways more damning outcome. The root tale does not punish its friar, because its subject is not his guilt. Its subject is the system that made a friar's letter the only bridge between two married people and the truth.

## Alexandria: The Geography of a Message That Failed

The tale's catastrophe can be stated as a ratio: the distance between Siena and Alexandria divided by the speed of fifteenth-century news. Alexandria was no figure of speech for Masuccio's readers. It was the Levantine entrepôt where Venetian and Catalan and Florentine merchants kept their warehouses, where Italian ships wintered and summered on schedules set by wind and war, and where letters moved as cargo, entrusted to captains and factors, copied, delayed, and lost in ways no sender could audit. A Sienese exile in Alexandria was not hiding; he was employed, housed, and known inside the Italian merchant nation abroad. Mariotto was reachable. He was simply reachable only by the slow channels, and the friar's single letter had to survive all of them while the rumor of the funeral needed to survive only one ship, one tavern, one letter from a merchant to his partner mentioning the notable Sienese news. Masuccio understands, with a modernity that startles, that information systems have failure modes independent of anyone's intentions. No villain intercepts the letter. No traitor betrays the plan. The Mediterranean is merely large, and grief is merely fast.

Da Porto's geography is the first great revision of this design, and it is a revision toward drama. He shrinks the exile from Alexandria to Mantua, a ride of hours from Verona, which destroys the ratio that powered Masuccio's failure and obliges him to invent a replacement failure: in the developed tradition the letter is entrusted to a friar who is quarantined, delayed, or otherwise stopped at close range, a concentrated accident substituting for Masuccio's distributed one. Shakespeare inherits the close-range version and perfects it in Friar John's speech, where the plague that seals the doors is reported with a bureaucrat's flatness (5.2). Judged as engineering, Masuccio's version is the more realistic and Shakespeare's the more stageable; judged as tragedy, the two failures produce different flavors of horror. In the tale, the lovers are killed by the ordinary scale of the world, and the moral is about the world. In the play, they are killed by a run of specific accidents at close quarters, and the moral gathers around timing, the haste and delay that the play's language of time counts in hours. Both flavors descend from the root's central insight, that in this story the deadliest force is neither hatred nor heaven but distance, however distance is measured in the telling at hand.

## Masuccio's Moral: The Commentary Voice and the Governance of Fortune

Il Novellino's frame gives each tale an argument before and a commentary after, and for the Mariotto novella that frame is the earliest interpretation of the Romeo and Juliet story that exists. It deserves reading as criticism, the first entry in a five-century ledger. Masuccio's interpretive posture, in this tale as across his collection, turns on fortune: the wheel that raises and ruins without regard to desert, and on the folly of lovers who build their safety on secrecy, clerical cleverness, and the hope that institutions will not notice them. His lovers are not star-crossed in the play's astrological sense; no horoscope is cast over Siena. They are fortune-crossed, which is a colder doctrine, because fortune in Masuccio's world is not a metaphysical enemy but the ordinary behavior of events when plans meet them. The commentary voice treats the catastrophe as an exemplum, a case from which conduct can be learned: desire ungoverned, sacraments stolen, letters trusted. Later tellers inherit the posture even as they soften the doctrine. Da Porto wraps his tale in a frame of narration and reflection; Brooke's preface of 1562 addresses the reader with an explicit moral warning against disobedient, friar-assisted love, a direct descendant of Masuccio's commentary at two removes; and the play's Chorus and Prince resume the summarizing, judging voice at the tragedy's opening and close, even as the action between those poles argues for the pity the frame withholds.

The comparison clarifies what Shakespeare changed at the deepest level, deeper than plot or names. He kept Masuccio's machine and reversed Masuccio's moral. In the root tale, the commentary's judgment stands unopposed: the lovers are a warning. In the play, the frame's judgments, the Chorus's fatalism, the Prince's "all are punished," are steadily out-argued by the enacted scenes, which build a counter-case for the lovers' worth that no character states and no commentary endorses. The tragedy of the play is partly this quarrel between an inherited moral apparatus and an enacted sympathy, and the apparatus is inherited, ultimately, from the Salernitan's own habit of arguing with his tale in public. Masuccio invented both halves of the tradition's long debate: the machine that kills the lovers and the voice that judges them for dying. His successors spent five centuries redistributing sympathy between the halves.

## From Boccaccio to Masuccio: The Novella Inheritance and the Assembly

Masuccio's originality comes into focus only against the shelf he worked from. Boccaccio's Decameron contains nearly every ingredient of the later machine in isolation. Its third day tells of Ferondo, a jealous husband dosed by an abbot with a powder that makes him seem dead, buried, and kept in a counterfeit purgatory while the abbot enjoys his wife: a feigned death by clerical powder, complete with tomb and waking, played entirely as farce. Elsewhere the Decameron trades in secret loves, go-between friars, family opposition, and letters that miscarry. The ingredients were, as Muir's caution insists, common property of the Italian novella. What no Boccaccian tale does is assemble them into a tragedy of marriage: Boccaccio's powders serve adultery and comedy, his secret unions are affairs rather than sacraments, and his catastrophes of misdirected news fall on Pyramus-style lovers without the institutional frame of a binding clandestine marriage. Masuccio's act of assembly is therefore specific and dateable. He takes the novella's comic toolkit, a friar's powder here, a secret rite there, a slow post everywhere, and bolts it onto the one frame that makes it tragic: a real marriage, canonically binding, socially invisible, whose exposure and defense drive every subsequent event. The machine kills because the marriage is real. In Boccaccio a discovered affair ends in laughter or leverage; in Masuccio a threatened marriage ends at the scaffold and the convent gate, because the wife cannot simply be unmarried by her family's next arrangement.

That is also why the feigned death in the root tale has a different moral weight from its farcical ancestors, a point the study of the potion's folktale ancestry elsewhere in this run takes up at the level of motif. In Masuccio the powder is not a trick played on a fool but a sacrament's bodyguard: the only device by which a wife can remain a wife. The tale's tragedy is that the bodyguard works and the world does not. Originality in source study is rarely the invention of parts; it is the discovery of a load-bearing arrangement among parts that others held loosely. By that standard the assembly of 1476 is one of the most consequential acts of arrangement in European storytelling, and its arranger's name deserves the currency the tradition has instead lavished on his revisers.

## The Tale's Transmission: Print at Naples and the Road North

A final piece of evidence concerns the tale's availability, because descent arguments live or die on whether the later writer could have met the earlier text. Il Novellino was printed at Naples in 1476, among the earliest dated products of the Neapolitan press, and print changes everything about a tale's prospects: where a manuscript anecdote travels by household and court, a printed collection travels by the trade, into the libraries of exactly the class of literate soldiers, friars, and secretaries who retold such stories. Masuccio's collection circulated in the Italian world Da Porto and Bandello inhabited; Bandello, a Dominican with a storyteller's magpie memory and a career spent in the courts of Lombardy, worked downstream of Da Porto's version, but Da Porto, composing in the Veneto in the 1520s, lived in a book-world where Il Novellino was a known classic of the previous century, the most substantial novella collection between Boccaccio and his own contemporaries. The channel cannot be documented link by link, as the adjudication above concedes; but availability, the necessary condition, is not in doubt. The tale was in print, in Italian, in the peninsula, two generations before Da Porto wrote, and no rival assembled version existed to compete with it. Geoffrey Bullough's decision to print the tale in translation in 1957, in the first volume of Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, ratified that availability for modern scholarship, placing Masuccio's novella on the desk of every subsequent student of the play's ancestry and making the root, at last, as readable as the branches.

## The Root Restored: What Changes When the Story Starts in Siena

Start the story in Siena in 1476 and several familiar judgments quietly reverse. The play's plot, so often praised as a young dramatist's daring construction, becomes an act of conservation: Shakespeare, the supposed inventor, is revealed as the tradition's most faithful engineer, keeping a fifteenth-century machine's joints in order while replacing its world. The feud, so often treated as the story's essence, becomes an addition, and a late one, a sixteenth-century civic frame fitted to a private catastrophe that had run for two generations without it. The lovers' deaths, so often read as the supreme romantic gesture, lose their root form entirely, since the root kills its husband by due process and its wife by grief in a convent, and the gesture turns out to be Da Porto's and Shakespeare's poetry laid over Masuccio's prose of institutions. And the friar, so often debated as the play's near-villain or near-saint, recovers his original lineaments: a competent, benevolent minor cleric whose three services, rite, powder, letter, map exactly onto the three ways the early modern world mediated between private desire and public order, sacrament, medicine, and post. The root does not shrink the play. It gives the play a past commensurate with its power, and it gives the credit for the story's enduring machine to the satirist of Salerno who built it first, in a tale of Mariotto and Ganozza that centuries of Romeo and Juliet have eclipsed.

## Mariotto Before Romeo: The Husband the Root Built

The tradition's hero also begins in Siena, and Mariotto Mignanelli is a more distinct creation than his status as "the first Romeo" suggests. He is, first, a named citizen of a named family, where Romeo will be a son defined almost entirely by his house's feud and his own heart. Mariotto's story is the story of a householder manqué: he gains a wife and loses a city, gains a sacrament and loses every public identity that would let him live inside it. In Alexandria he is neither merchant prince nor pilgrim; the tale keeps him in the suspended condition of the exile who lives by others' ventures and his own patience, waiting for a recall that the plot has no intention of granting. His heroism, when it comes, is of a single kind: he returns. Knowing the sentence, knowing the city, he re-enters Siena for one night and one tomb, and the tale grants him no speech for the ages, only the embrace that the watch interrupts. Shakespeare's Romeo is this figure with the volume of consciousness raised to the play's pitch: the same passivity in exile, "banish'd" tolling through his grief in the Friar's cell (3.3), the same annihilation of procedure when the false news arrives, the same resolve to die at the tomb. What the playwright adds is everything inward: the poetry of perception, the wit, the self-dramatizing velocity. What he does not need to add is the outline. Mariotto's arc, lover, husband, killer, exile, returner, dead man, is Romeo's arc entire, drawn in 1476 by a writer who had never heard of Verona's feud because, in the story's first form, there was nothing in Verona to hear of.

Even the hero's name encodes the difference between the root and the tree. Mariotto is a Tuscan diminutive, domestic and Marian, the name of a neighbor; Romeo is a pilgrim's title, the Rome-goer, and it lets the play weave its hero into the pilgrimage language of the first meeting, where Juliet is the saint and his lips are pilgrims (1.5). The change of names at Da Porto's hands is the tradition in miniature: a local anecdote's personnel, renamed for resonance. Source study that stops at Da Porto mistakes the renaming for the creation. The man was already there, in Siena, with a wife, a sentence, and a tomb to open.

## The Killing in Its Legal World: Commune Justice and the Capital Sentence

Masuccio's killing deserves a section of its own because its legal texture explains the tale's ending and measures Shakespeare's most underappreciated alteration. In the Italian communes of the fifteenth century, homicide sat inside a dense machinery of statutes, inquests, and contumacy proceedings: a killer who fled the territory was tried in absence, sentenced, and lived thereafter under the ban, liable to execution if taken, his property exposed, his return a capital act in itself. Mariotto's position follows that machinery exactly. His flight to Alexandria is not romantic wandering; it is the standard geometry of the banned man, who must live beyond the commune's reach and the reach of its allies. His arrest in the tomb therefore requires no villainy and no betrayal: a condemned contumacious homicide found inside the walls is a file the courts complete almost automatically, and the tale's refusal to stage a rescue, a pardon, or a princely hesitation is realism, not cruelty. Siena had no Escalus to commute the sentence for a wedding's sake.

Shakespeare's handling of the same joint shows the dramatist adjusting the law to the feud. Romeo's killing of Tybalt is processed within minutes and within the fiction's own emergency jurisprudence: the Prince, who has promised death for the next street brawler, delivers banishment instead, "and for that offence immediately we do exile him hence" (3.1), a sentence the play then treats as a death equivalent in the lovers' rhetoric while keeping its hero alive and reachable in Mantua. The alteration is usually praised as plotting, since a dead or Alexandrine Romeo ends the play in Act 3. It is equally a change of meaning. Masuccio's law is impersonal and final, the commune as fate; Shakespeare's law is personal, princely, and negotiable, fortune wearing a crown. The root tale's bleaker proposition, that institutions acting lawfully are sufficient to destroy the innocent and the guilty with the same stroke, survives in the play only as a shadow, in the quarantine that stops a letter and the watch that comes too late. Restoring Masuccio restores that proposition to the center of the story's prehistory, where it sat for the tale's first century of readers.

## Siena Itself: Why the First City Matters

A story's first city is never a neutral container, and Siena shapes Masuccio's tale as surely as Verona shapes the play. Fifteenth-century Siena was a republic of factions, its government a rotation of monti, its streets educated in the choreography of vendetta and exile; a tale of a young citizen killed in a quarrel, a husband banned, a great family's daughter disposed in marriage, required no invention of atmosphere for its first audience. The tale's Siena is the working city of the novella tradition, the same Siena of merchants and communes that fills Boccaccio's and Sercambi's pages, and its institutions, the friar's confessional, the family's council, the commune's court, the convent, are the tale's true cast. When Da Porto moved the story to Verona, he gained the della Scala past, a signorial city with a prince to judge and a ready-made fall from greatness in its history, and he gained Dante's two surnames waiting in the Purgatorio's catalogue of Italian disorders. The move was a poet's improvement and a historian's loss: Verona in the tale is a claimed past, two centuries old at Da Porto's writing, while Siena in Masuccio is a living present, reported at the distance of a generation. The play inherits the claimed past and intensifies it into myth. Source study that begins with Verona inherits the myth's geography; source study that begins with Siena recovers the anecdote's, and with it the recognition that the story was once told as news.

## Reading Masuccio in Translation: The Tale's Modern Life

Modern readers meet the tale almost entirely through mediation, and the mediations deserve naming so that the article's evidence base is honest. The standard scholarly doorway in English is the translation and summary tradition consolidated by Bullough's Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare, which prints the Mariotto and Ganozza novella for anglophone readers and remains the citation of first resort in the play's source scholarship. The heroine's name itself testifies to the mediated transmission: the tale's manuscripts and early prints, and the modern editions that follow them, vary between Ganozza and Gianozza, and the summaries that descend from Moore and Bullough do not all choose alike; this article uses Ganozza, the form in its title and spec, while recognizing the variant as a fact of the text's life rather than an error to be corrected. Editors of the play, Levenson for Oxford and Weis for the Arden Third Series among them, handle Masuccio at introduction length, in the genealogy paragraphs where the story's line is drawn from Dante's names through Da Porto to Brooke; in those paragraphs the Sienese tale stands as the line's earliest Italian station, credited with the plot's assembly and passed over quickly for the Verona texts that stand nearer the play. The compression is understandable and, this article has argued, distorting in aggregate: an ancestry summarized in a sentence becomes an ancestry forgotten. The thirty-third novella of Il Novellino, read whole, is neither a footnote nor a rough draft of something better. It is the first finished form of the most retold love story in the world, built by a satirist who trusted none of its machinery to be kind, and it rewards the same close reading the tradition reserves for its descendants.

## The Sleep of Three Days: The Powder as the Tale's Central Machine

The powder is the element every retelling keeps and every audience remembers, and Masuccio's handling of it is more deliberate than the summaries suggest. Its duration, three days, is calibrated to the funeral customs of its world: long enough for the death to be certified by the household, lamented by the city, and sealed by burial in the tomb; short enough for the sleeper to wake into the friar's custody before the grave's realities intervene. The play's forty-two hours (4.1) tightens the same calibration to a drama's clock, where the wedding to Paris has been moved forward and the margin for error must shrink to a single night and day. In both cases the number is the plot's confession that the stratagem is a race: the sleeper's body is a message that must be collected before it is read as something else. Masuccio's three days also give his catastrophe its distinctive timing of failure. The friar's design allows generous slack; the letter to Alexandria has, in principle, time to arrive. That it fails anyway, against a timetable with slack in it, is the tale's coldest judgment on its world: the machine did not need to be perfect, because the world's ordinary friction was sufficient.

The powder's own nature is left, characteristically, in the friar's professional mystery. Masuccio does not supply a recipe, a herb, or a classical pedigree; the drug belongs to the friar's art as the novella tradition understood such arts, a compound of the kind that Boccaccio's abbot had used to counterfeit Ferondo's death and that apothecaries and empirics traded in across the Italian cities. The tale's interest is functional, not pharmaceutical: the powder produces a state that every witness, family, city, and eventually the husband in the tomb, reads as death. Its one imperfection is theological rather than chemical, since the sleeper cannot speak for herself while the world disposes of her, and in that enforced silence the whole tragedy lives. Ganozza awake is a wife who could end the plot by declaration; Ganozza asleep is an object over which family, friar, husband, and watch dispose in sequence. The tradition kept the powder because the powder creates the story's purest dramatic condition, a heroine present, alive, and unable to act, while every other character acts on a false premise at full speed. That condition, the engine of the play's last two acts, is assembled complete in Masuccio, and the folktale ancestry of death-feigning drugs, which another study in this run pursues on its own ground, explains the part without explaining the assembly: the drug was old, but its employment as a wife's defense against a second marriage, inside a real sacrament, under a three-day clock, against a slow post, is the Sienese tale's own engineering.

## Told as True: The Claim of Fact from Masuccio to the Chorus

Every major telling of this story insists that it happened, and the insistence begins with Masuccio. The novella presents Mariotto and Ganozza as Sienese people of the recent past, in the reportorial manner that gives the Italian tale its documentary flavor: named family, named city, events of public record, a scaffold and a convent that readers could locate on a map. Whether any historical Mariotto Mignanelli died as the tale describes is unverifiable and, for the tradition, beside the point; what matters is that the posture of fact is itself inherited. Da Porto rebuilds the posture with a witness, the archer Peregrino, who tells the tale as Veronese history from the age of Bartolomeo della Scala, lending the story two centuries of borrowed age at a stroke. Bandello repeats the historical presentation, Brooke advertises the tale's Italian currency on his title page, and Shakespeare's Chorus completes the sequence by presenting the action as a chronicle the stage will exhibit, "the two hours' traffic of our stage" (Prologue), a record rather than an invention. A fiction that arrives claiming to be a fact acquires a peculiar authority, and the tradition exploits that authority at every stage: each teller is not creating but reporting, not inventing but restoring, and the lovers' deaths gather the weight of deaths that a city once mourned.

Masuccio's share in this pattern is foundational and easily missed. A tale printed in 1476 about events of living memory in a neighboring city made a different truth claim from a tale printed in 1530 about events of 1300; Masuccio's claim was closer to journalism's, and its decay into legend is the tradition's first transformation. By the time the story reaches London, the claim of fact has survived three rebuildings and two translations, and no reader in the Globe could have audited a single link. The play's power does not depend on the claim, but its reception history does: the centuries of readers who have treated Romeo and Juliet as the supreme instance of a true and universal pattern are responding, in part, to a rhetorical inheritance whose first surviving form is a Salernitan's insistence that in Siena, within memory, a friar married two young people in secret, and that everything the world now weeps over followed from a letter that never came.

## The Half Century Between the Tellings: Italy, Print, and the Road North

The fifty years between Il Novellino's printing and Da Porto's composition were the most violent half century in Italian memory, and the tale's migration north happened inside them. Masuccio's Naples of 1476 was an Aragonese court at the height of its cultivated confidence, its press new, its storytellers writing for patrons whose names head the tales. Then came 1494 and the French invasion, the Italian Wars, the peninsula converted for a generation into the battlefield of France, Spain, and the Empire; the Sack of Rome fell in 1527, inside the very years when Da Porto, a Vicentine soldier whose own body had been broken in those wars, was composing or had lately composed his Giulietta e Romeo. The soldier who rebuilt Masuccio's tale had seen Italy's cities treated as prizes and its old orders collapse; his decision to set the story two centuries back, under the della Scala, in a Verona of formed factions and a strong prince, reads against that experience as more than antiquarianism. He gave the tale a political anatomy, the feud, the banished brawlers, the ruler's failing edicts, that Masuccio's courtly anecdote had not needed, because the world that produced Da Porto had learned to think of private violence as a public disease.

Print carried the root through the same decades. The novella collection was among the early press's most reliable genres, and Il Novellino's tales entered the common stock of Italian storytelling in the form most dangerous to tidy source study: not always as a book on a desk, but as stories in circulation, retold by friars and soldiers and courtiers, their joints remembered, their names negotiable. That circulation is the honest channel to imagine between Masuccio and Da Porto, broader than a single act of reading and narrower than coincidence: a famous tale in the air of the peninsula, available to a storytelling soldier in its assembled form, its Siena specifics fading as its machinery proved unforgettable. Bandello, collecting his Novelle at mid-century from a lifetime of courts, received the tale in Da Porto's Verona form and amplified it with a professional's confidence, and Boaistuau's French Histoires tragiques of 1559 completed the northward road that print and war had built. The English arrivals, Brooke's poem of 1562 and Painter's prose of 1566, are thus the last stage of a migration that began in a Neapolitan print shop in 1476, when a dead Salernitan's book put a Sienese anecdote into the one medium from which stories do not easily die.

## The Marriage at the Center: Clandestine Consent and the Law of the Rite

The whole machine depends on a point of law that modern readers habitually miss, and Masuccio's first audience would have felt in their bones: the secret marriage was a real marriage. Medieval canon law, following the consent doctrine consolidated in the twelfth century, held that a man and a woman who exchanged present consent, freely and in words of the present tense, were married in the eyes of God and the Church, even if no priest blessed the union, no banns were read, and no parent knew. Clandestine marriages were sinful, punishable, and socially ruinous, but they were valid, and validity is what the tale needs. Ganozza is not a girl with a promise; she is a wife, and the second marriage her family prepares would be bigamy, a crime against a sacrament already accomplished. The friar's role, in strict law, is witness and blessing rather than maker of the bond, which is why his triple function in the tale cuts so deep: the man who certifies the marriage also supplies the powder and pens the letter, because in a clandestine union the certifier is the only institution the couple has. Masuccio builds his tragedy on the gap between two legal realities: the marriage exists in the forum of conscience and canon law, and does not exist in the forum of the family, the commune, and the marriage market. Every event after the rite is that gap closing on the lovers like a vice: the killing activates the commune's law, the family's plan activates the market's law, and the one law that knows the truth, the Church's, speaks only through a friar whose voice travels by a single letter.

The tradition's later handlers all work this same seam, in their own legal worlds. The Council of Trent's Tametsi decree of 1563, requiring the parish priest and witnesses for validity, would retrospectively annul the tale's premise in Catholic Europe, one reason the story's machinery belongs to its fifteenth and early sixteenth-century tellings; Da Porto and Bandello wrote on the near side of that change, when clandestine consent still bound. Brooke's England adds a Protestant inflection: his preface moralizes against the friar-enabled secret marriage precisely because his readers' church had inherited the validity question in a new confessional key, and the play's Friar Laurence, marrying the lovers offstage between scenes with a haste the text both blesses and questions, inherits the whole problem in miniature. Shakespeare's audience, for whom clandestine marriage remained valid in English law long after Trent, would have felt the rite's reality as Masuccio's audience did: Juliet is a wife before she is a corpse, and the catastrophe is not that two children pretended at marriage but that a true marriage had no public existence to protect it. The root tale's legal architecture is thus not background. It is the load-bearing wall, and it was standing, complete, in 1476.

### Q: Who was Masuccio Salernitano, and what was his real name?

Masuccio Salernitano was the pen name of Tommaso Guardati, a man of letters from Salerno who served in the orbit of the Aragonese court of Naples in the fifteenth century. Under that familiar diminutive he wrote Il Novellino, a collection of fifty tales printed at Naples in 1476, shortly after his death in or about 1475, each tale dedicated to a named patron and closed by the author's own commentary. His thirty-third tale, the story of Mariotto and Ganozza, became the earliest surviving full telling of the plot the world knows as Romeo and Juliet, a fame its author, a satirist of friars and fortune, could not have foreseen.

### Q: Which tale number is Mariotto and Ganozza inside Il Novellino?

Mariotto and Ganozza is the thirty-third tale of Il Novellino, the thirty-third of the fifty novelle Masuccio arranged in his collection. The number matters less than the company: the tale sits inside a book dominated by comic, satirical, and anticlerical stories, where it stands out for its tragic architecture and its civic bleakness. Like every tale in the collection, it is framed by Masuccio's own apparatus, an opening argument announcing the matter and a closing commentary in which the author steps forward to draw the lesson. That frame is the earliest interpretation of the Romeo and Juliet story in existence: before Da Porto's frame, before Brooke's moralizing preface, before the play's Chorus, Masuccio's commentary voice was already judging the lovers' folly and fortune's cruelty over their bodies.

### Q: Why must the marriage of Mariotto and Ganozza be kept secret from her family?

The marriage must be secret because Ganozza's family would never consent to it, and in the marriage politics of the fifteenth-century Italian city a daughter's hand was an asset disposed by her house, not by her heart. Mariotto, though a citizen of standing, is not the match her family intends, and an open suit would be refused and watched thereafter. Secrecy is therefore the lovers' only road to a valid union: by exchanging consent before the Augustinian friar, they become husband and wife in canon law while remaining, in the world's eyes, an unmarried daughter and a suitor with no rights. The tale's tragedy grows directly from that split. The marriage is real where God sees it and invisible where Siena sees it, and every institution that later acts, the court, the family, the watch, acts on the invisible version.

### Q: Whom does Mariotto kill, and what sentence drives him from Siena?

Mariotto kills a fellow citizen of standing in a street quarrel, a brawl's fatal outcome of the kind Siena's factional streets produced constantly, rather than a formal duel over house honor. The killing is the tale's second engine: under the commune's homicide law, Mariotto is tried, condemned to death, and placed beyond return, a contumacious condemned man whose life is forfeit if he is taken within the city's reach. The sentence, not any feud, drives him from Siena, first from the territory and then from Italy altogether. Shakespeare converts the capital sentence into banishment to nearby Mantua, keeping his hero reachable and the plot alive, while Masuccio's law is impersonal and final.

### Q: Where does Mariotto flee after the killing, and why does the distance decide the ending?

Mariotto flees to Alexandria in Egypt, across the Mediterranean, where he lives in exile among the Italian merchants of the Levant trade. The distance decides the ending because it governs the speed and reliability of every message the rescue depends on. Siena and Alexandria are joined only by ships, merchant letters, and travelers' talk, channels measured in weeks and months and exposed to season, war, and loss. The friar's single explanatory letter must survive all of them; the rumor of Ganozza's funeral needs to survive only one. Error therefore outruns truth across the sea, and Mariotto acts on the error with a condemned man's single remaining freedom, return. Da Porto's later shrinking of the exile to Mantua, a few hours from Verona, destroyed this ratio and forced the tradition to invent close-range accidents, quarantines and intercepted friars, to replace Masuccio's simple, sufficient geography.

### Q: Why does Ganozza choose the feigned death instead of flight or confession?

Ganozza chooses the feigned death because every other door is closed by the split between her real and her visible condition. Flight would disgrace her house and, without Mariotto present to receive her, deliver her to a world where an unaccompanied daughter has no standing; confession of the secret marriage would expose the friar, enrage her family, and still leave her husband a condemned exile in Alexandria. The friar's powder offers the one move that answers the immediate threat, the second marriage her family is preparing, by removing her from the marriage market entirely while preserving the first marriage for a future reunion. It is a rational choice, and the tale's horror is that rationality is not enough: the stratagem works in every particular and still kills her husband, because the letter explaining it travels slower than the news of her funeral.

### Q: Why does news of Ganozza's funeral reach Mariotto while the friar's letter does not?

The funeral news travels because it is public, repeatable, and interesting, while the letter fails because it is private, single, and addressed across the Mediterranean. Ganozza's death is Siena's spectacle, mourned by a great household and carried abroad by every merchant and traveler leaving Italy; the friar's explanation exists in one copy, entrusted to the slow channels between Italy and Alexandria, with no duplicate and no confirmation. Masuccio builds the catastrophe on that asymmetry, which the play preserves with a changed mechanism: Friar John's letter is stopped by plague quarantine at close range (5.2), while Balthasar's mistaken eyewitness account rides to Mantua unchecked. In both tellings the pattern is identical and is the tradition's founding irony. Truth moves slowly and in single copy; error moves fast and in many mouths, and the lovers die in the gap between the two speeds.

### Q: What happens when Mariotto opens Ganozza's tomb at night?

Mariotto, returned in secret to Siena after hearing that Ganozza has died, goes by night to the tomb where she was buried and opens it, taking the body of his sleeping wife in his arms and weeping over her in the belief that she is dead. The scene is the tale's harrowing center: the husband embraces the living woman a few hours before her waking, each ignorant of how near rescue stands. The opened tomb, the light, and the disturbance draw the attention of the watch, and Mariotto is seized in the tomb itself, in the act, a condemned homicide found within the walls of the city that sentenced him. The play transposes the tableau into Romeo's speeches over Juliet's body in the Capulet vault (5.3), but with a decisive change: no watch interrupts in time, and the husband dies by his own poison rather than by arrest at the grave.

### Q: How is Mariotto punished after his arrest, and how does that differ from Romeo's death?

Mariotto is executed by the commune: arrested at the tomb, he confesses the whole story, the secret marriage, the flight, the reason for his return, and Siena's justice, processing a condemned homicide found within its jurisdiction, beheads him. The confession earns no reprieve, and the tale treats the outcome as law rather than villainy. The difference from Romeo's death is the difference between two theories of tragedy. Romeo dies by his own hand, by poison, in the tomb, beside his wife, in a death he chooses and speaks into fame; Mariotto dies by the state's hand, on a scaffold, away from Ganozza, in a death chosen by a court. Shakespeare privatizes the catastrophe and gives it to the lovers; Masuccio leaves it public and juridical, a demonstration that institutions acting lawfully can kill as completely as any passion, a bleakness the later tradition declined to keep.

### Q: What does Ganozza do after she wakes and learns that Mariotto is dead?

Ganozza wakes from the three days' sleep into the friar's keeping and learns the tale's cruellest ordering of facts: the stratagem succeeded, Mariotto heard of her death, returned for her tomb, and has already been executed for the return. Her answer is withdrawal. She refuses the world that has disposed of her marriage, her husband, and her public identity, enters a convent, a monastery of religious women, and there dies of grief within a short span. The ending completes the tale's asymmetry: the state kills the husband by process, and grief kills the wife by refusal, with no meeting, no farewell, and no shared tomb between them. Later tellers replaced the convent with the dagger, giving the heroine an active death beside her husband's body; Masuccio's heroine dies as his tale's world arranged for her to die, enclosed, silenced, and unreconciled, which is why his ending reads as chronicle waste beside the play's consolation.

### Q: Why is there no family feud in Masuccio's tale?

There is no family feud in Masuccio's tale because the feud had not been invented yet: it enters the tradition with Da Porto, who drew the names Montecchi and Cappelletti from Dante's Purgatorio and set the two houses at war in Verona. Masuccio's opposition is the ordinary machinery of fifteenth-century life rather than a civic curse: one family's marriage strategy for a daughter, a commune's homicide law for a killer, and a secret sacrament that neither institution knows exists. No second house hates the Mignanelli; no cousin waits to avenge an ancient grudge; the citizen Mariotto kills is a brawl's victim, not a kinsman in a vendetta. The absence changes the tragedy's meaning. Shakespeare's lovers rebel against a divided city and die healing it; Masuccio's lovers steal a marriage from an indifferent system and are processed by it, and the root tale's catastrophe needs no hatred at all, only law, distance, and a slow post.

### Q: What part does Ganozza's family play in bringing on the crisis?

Ganozza's family brings on the crisis by resuming, after Mariotto's exile, the ordinary business of marrying a daughter. In the world's eyes Ganozza is unmarried and marriageable; her secret rite is unknown to her kin, and her refusals of suitors can only delay, not cancel, the family's strategy. As negotiations harden toward a match, the secret wife faces the prospect of a public, sacramental wedding to another man, which would make her a bigamist in conscience and ruin her in fact. The family acts without villainy, by the lights of its world, which is the tale's coldest point: no raging father stages an ultimatum, as Capulet will do when he fixes Juliet's wedding day (3.5); the system simply continues, and its continuation is the threat. The friar's powder is proposed at exactly this joint, as the only instrument capable of stopping a marriage market that cannot be argued with.

### Q: How does the tale treat the friar who marries the lovers and supplies the powder?

The tale treats its friar with a restraint remarkable in Masuccio, a satirist whose collection is crowded with scheming and lecherous clerics. This Augustinian confessor is benevolent and competent: he performs the secret rite that makes the lovers husband and wife, compounds the powder that counterfeits death to the hour, and writes the letter on which the rescue depends. No staged punishment overtakes him; he recovers the waking Ganozza and survives inside his stratagem's ruins. His failure is not vice or folly but scale: one minor cleric's three services, sacrament, medicine, and post, are the lovers' only bridge to the world, and the bridge's weakest span, the letter, gives way under the ordinary friction of distance. Shakespeare magnifies the figure into Friar Laurence, adds the flight from the tomb and the confession before the Prince (5.3), and submits him to judgment; Masuccio leaves his friar unjudged, because the tale's subject is the system that made such a bridge necessary.

### Q: Why do editions and readers spell the heroine's name both Ganozza and Gianozza?

The two spellings record the tale's transmission through manuscripts, early prints, and modern summaries that do not all follow the same witness. Masuccio's novella survives through an early printed tradition with the orthographic fluidity normal for fifteenth-century Italian, and the heroine's name appears across editions and scholarship in both the Ganozza and Gianozza forms; the anglophone source tradition descending from Moore's origins research and Bullough's collection has not standardized on a single one. The variant is a reminder that modern readers meet this tale through layers of mediation, translation, summary, and editorial choice, rather than in a single fixed text. This article uses Ganozza, the form in its title, while treating the variant as a fact of the text's life. Neither spelling affects the identity: the same Sienese heroine, secretly married, drugged, buried, and widowed by the scaffold, stands behind both.

### Q: How can an essay use the Masuccio comparison to argue that the assembled plot predates Da Porto?

An essay can rest the argument on the comparison's rows, because the table converts a claim about influence into a checkable inventory. The assembled plot is not a mood or a motif but a sequence: secret friar-marriage, killing and exile, a wife pressed toward a second marriage, a friar's timed sleeping drug, a failed explanatory letter, a rumor that outruns it, a tomb opened at night, and two deaths. Each element can be verified in Masuccio's thirty-third novella, printed in 1476, decades before Da Porto composed Giulietta e Romeo, and the elements stand there in the same causal order the later tradition preserves. Against the objection that such motifs were common novella property, the essay should press the engineering point: common stock explains shared parts, not a shared sequence in which every part produces the next. The comparison thus supports the precise verdict that Da Porto originated the Verona form, not the story.

### Q: What does Masuccio's version reveal about the age of the story's failed-message device?

Masuccio's version reveals that the failed message is not Shakespeare's refinement or even Da Porto's, but a founding device of the story's first assembled form, present in 1476 with its full tragic logic already worked out. In the tale the device is geographic: a friar's letter entrusted to the channels between Siena and Alexandria fails, while the public news of the funeral succeeds on the same routes. The asymmetry, truth traveling slowly in a single copy while error travels fast in many mouths, is stated by the plot itself rather than by any commentator. Later tellers preserve the logic and vary only the obstacle: Da Porto's line develops the close-range mishaps, and Shakespeare gives the failure a witness and a mechanism in Friar John's plague quarantine (5.2). The device's age matters because it relocates the tragedy's cause: from its first form, this story kills its lovers not by fate's decree but by information arriving in the wrong order.

### Q: Which elements of the familiar plot cannot be credited to Masuccio?

The elements that cannot be credited to Masuccio are the ones the later tradition added when it rebuilt his Sienese anecdote into the Verona myth. The feud between two houses, and with it the names Montecchi and Cappelletti drawn from Dante, belongs to Da Porto, as do Verona itself, the lovers' famous names, the meeting at a great feast, and the reconciliation of the houses over the bodies. The balcony and the dawn parting, Mercutio, Benvolio, the Nurse of the play, County Paris, the apothecary of Mantua, and the heroine's death by the dagger in the tomb are later endowments, chiefly of the story's sixteenth-century retellers and its dramatist. Even the double suicide is an addition: Masuccio's husband is executed and his wife dies of grief in a convent. The honest distribution runs assembly to Masuccio, world to Da Porto, amplitude to Bandello, and transfiguration to Shakespeare, who received a finished machine and gave it a soul.

### Q: Why does Masuccio frame his tales with arguments and closing commentaries?

Masuccio frames his tales with arguments and commentaries because Il Novellino is a courtly performance in the author's own voice, not an anthology of anonymous stories. Each novella opens with a prose argument announcing its matter and naming the patron to whom it is dedicated, and closes with a section in which Masuccio steps forward to gloss, joke, moralize, and draw the lesson, a structure that advertises authorship in a way Boccaccio's framed storytellers deliberately diffuse. For the Mariotto tale the frame has a special consequence: the commentary is the first interpretation of the Romeo and Juliet story, treating the lovers' end as an exemplum of desire, secrecy, and fortune's governance. Every later frame descends from that posture, Da Porto's narrating frame, Brooke's warning preface of 1562, and the play's Chorus and closing judgment by the Prince, each reteller inheriting not only Masuccio's plot but his habit of standing beside it to say what it means.

### Q: What is Mariotto's family name, and what does it tell readers about the tale's Sienese roots?

Mariotto's family name is Mignanelli, the name of a real Sienese house, and it tells readers that the tale presents itself as local report rather than fable. Masuccio sets his story in a Siena of named families, working courts, friars, and convents, the documentary texture of the Italian novella at its most civic, and his hero carries that texture in his very signature. The contrast with the later tradition is exact: Da Porto will discard the Sienese surname and name his houses from Dante's Purgatorio, Montecchi and Cappelletti, trading reportorial specificity for literary resonance and a feud the root tale never had. The Mignanelli name is thus a small fossil of the story's first form, evidence that before the lovers were Verona's myth they were Siena's neighbors, and that the tale's claim on its first audience was not that such things happen in storybooks, but that such a thing had happened, within memory, in a city they could walk to.
