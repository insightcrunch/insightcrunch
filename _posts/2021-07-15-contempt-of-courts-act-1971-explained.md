The Contempt of Courts Act 1971 (Act 70 of 1971) is the statute that defines contempt of court in India, lists the conduct that is not contempt, fixes the maximum punishment a court may impose, and sets the period within which a proceeding must begin. It is also a statute with an unusual constitutional position. The power it organises does not come from the statute itself. The Supreme Court and every High Court hold the power to punish for contempt because the Constitution makes each of them a court of record, and the Act operates inside that constitutional power, defining its categories, supplying its defences and confining its procedure. A reader who grasps that single point understands why the recurring proposal to abolish the offence of scandalising the court by amending this Act would not abolish the power at all, and why the Law Commission of India, when it examined that proposal in its Report No. 274 of 2018, recommended against the amendment. This article states the Act section by section, separates civil contempt from criminal contempt, works through the defences in the order the statute gives them, explains the change made in 2006 when truth became a possible defence, and then presents the abolition debate in the terms its two sides use, without adopting either.

## The constitutional setting: a power the statute did not create

Article 129 of the Constitution provides that the Supreme Court shall be a court of record and shall have all the powers of such a court including the power to punish for contempt of itself. Article 215 makes the identical provision for every High Court. A court of record is, in the classical understanding the Constituent Assembly carried into the text, a court whose acts and proceedings are enrolled for a permanent record and which possesses the power to punish summarily those who obstruct its proceedings or diminish its authority. Article 142(2) adds that the Supreme Court shall, subject to the provisions of any law made by Parliament, have power to make any order for the purpose of securing the attendance of any person, the discovery or production of any documents, or the investigation or punishment of any contempt of itself. The legislative entries complete the picture without displacing it. Entry 77 of the Union List empowers Parliament to legislate on the constitution, organisation, jurisdiction and powers of the Supreme Court, expressly including contempt of that court, and Entry 14 of the Concurrent List covers contempt of court other than contempt of the Supreme Court. Parliament therefore has undoubted competence to pass a contempt statute. What the constitutional text withholds is any suggestion that the superior courts hold their contempt power on parliamentary sufferance.

The consequence runs through the whole law on the subject. Because the power is constitutional, the Supreme Court held in Sukhdev Singh Sodhi v. Chief Justice and Judges of the PEPSU High Court, decided in 1954, that the contempt power of a court of record is inherent and cannot be taken away by legislation; a statute may regulate the power but cannot abrogate it. The same reasoning was applied to the present Act in S.K. Sarkar v. Vinay Chandra Misra in 1981, where the Court held that Section 15 of the Act, which prescribes how cognizance of criminal contempt is taken, does not curtail the power of a High Court under Article 215 to act on its own motion. The statute is therefore best read as a code of definitions, defences, procedure and limits laid over a power that exists independently of it. The working map of that constitutional architecture, court by court and tier by tier, is set out in [the guide to the laws governing the judicial system in India](/2021/04/01/judicial-system-laws-india-guide/), within which the contempt power sits as one incident of the courts of record. Section 22 of the Act acknowledges the relationship from the statutory side: the Act is in addition to, and not in derogation of, the provisions of any other law relating to contempt of courts. Section 9 adds the mirror image, providing that nothing in the Act shall be construed as implying that any act or publication is punishable as contempt which would not have been punishable apart from the Act. The statute, by its own terms, neither enlarges the offence nor displaces the constitutional power.

Article 19(2) places contempt of court in a second constitutional location. The freedom of speech and expression guaranteed by Article 19(1)(a) is subject to reasonable restrictions imposed by law in the interests of, among other grounds, contempt of court. Contempt is thus one of the enumerated heads under which speech may lawfully be restricted, a position it has held since the Constitution (First Amendment) Act of 1951 restated the clause. This means the contempt power is not an anomaly inside a free speech constitution; it is one of the restrictions the constitution itself contemplates. It also means that every exercise of the power is open to the discipline of the Article 19(2) inquiry, namely whether the restriction is reasonable and whether the law imposing it serves the ground it invokes. The definitions and defences in the Act are the statutory form of that discipline, and much of the case law on scandalising the court is, in substance, an argument about where reasonableness ends.

### Why does the contempt power not come from this Act?

The Supreme Court and the High Courts are courts of record under Articles 129 and 215, and the power to punish for contempt of themselves is an attribute of that status. The Act defines contempt, lists defences, fixes punishment and sets a limitation period, but the power it regulates would survive repeal of the Act.

The relationship between statute and Constitution also explains a feature of the Act that surprises many readers: its principal provisions are framed as safeguards. Sections 3 to 8 declare classes of conduct that are not contempt. Section 13 declares classes of contempt that are not punishable. Section 20 forbids the initiation of proceedings after one year. Section 12 caps the sentence. A statute whose purpose were simply to arm the courts would not be written this way. The drafting reflects the Sanyal Committee's conception, examined in the next section, that the legislative task in this field is to define and confine a power that exists anyway, so that a citizen can know in advance what is punishable and a court can be held to the boundaries Parliament has drawn. Whether the confinement is sufficient is the live question in the reform debate; that the statute confines rather than confers is not in dispute.

## How the Contempt of Courts Act 1971 came to be written

Statutory contempt law in India begins with the Contempt of Courts Act 1926, which empowered the superior courts to punish contempt of subordinate courts and dealt in outline with contempt of the superior courts themselves. Before 1926 there was no general contempt statute, and the chartered High Courts exercised the contempt jurisdiction they had inherited as courts of record. The Act of 1926 was replaced by the Contempt of Courts Act 1952, which extended the scheme and defined the powers of the courts in somewhat fuller terms. Neither statute attempted a definition of contempt. The offence lived in the case law, and a person accused of contempt could not consult a parliamentary text to learn what the offence consisted of, what defences existed, or what the maximum punishment was. Dissatisfaction with that position led the Central Government in April 1961 to appoint a committee under the chairmanship of H. N. Sanyal, then Additional Solicitor General of India, to examine the law of contempt and recommend changes. The Sanyal Committee reported in 1963. Its central recommendations were that contempt should be defined by statute, that the defences available to an accused person should be stated in the statute, that the punishment should be capped, and that procedure should be regularised. The Bill introduced to give effect to the report was examined by a Joint Committee of Parliament, and the result was the Contempt of Courts Act 1971, Act 70 of 1971, which came into force on 24 December 1971 and repealed the Act of 1952 by its Section 24, with savings for pending proceedings.

### What did the Sanyal Committee change in the law of contempt?

Before the Sanyal Committee reported in 1963, no Indian statute defined contempt, and defences and punishments rested on case law alone. The Committee recommended a defining statute, and the Contempt of Courts Act 1971 is built to that design: definitions of the two kinds of contempt, stated defences, a capped punishment and regularised procedure.

The Act as enacted contains twenty-four sections. Section 1 gives the short title and extent and excludes the State of Jammu and Kashmir as the text then stood, an exclusion overtaken by later constitutional change. Section 2 defines the operative terms. Sections 3 to 8 state what is not contempt. Section 9 denies any enlargement of the offence by implication. Sections 10 and 11 deal with the High Court's power over contempt of subordinate courts and over offences connected with places outside its ordinary jurisdiction. Section 12 fixes punishment, Section 13 states when contempt is not punishable, Sections 14 to 18 regulate procedure, Section 19 grants appeals, and Section 20 sets the limitation period. Sections 21 to 24 exclude Nyaya Panchayats, preserve other laws, empower rule making and repeal the 1952 Act. One amendment of substance has been made since enactment. The Contempt of Courts (Amendment) Act 2006, Act 6 of 2006, substituted Section 13, adding the substantial interference threshold and the possibility of justification by truth as a defence. That amendment is treated in its own section below, because it changed the character of the statute more than its brevity suggests. Everything else in the Act stands substantially in the form the Sanyal design produced, which is itself a fact of some importance in the reform debate: the definitions under challenge in that debate are not colonial survivals but the considered product of an Indian committee, a parliamentary Joint Committee and the legislature of 1971.

## The definitions: what Section 2 actually says

Section 2(a) provides that contempt of court means civil contempt or criminal contempt. The definition is exhaustive of the categories and empty of content until the two limbs are read. Section 2(b) defines civil contempt as wilful disobedience to any judgment, decree, direction, order, writ or other process of a court, or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court. Section 2(c) defines criminal contempt as the publication, whether by words spoken or written or by signs or by visible representations or otherwise, of any matter, or the doing of any other act whatsoever, which (i) scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower the authority of, any court; or (ii) prejudices, or interferes or tends to interfere with, the due course of any judicial proceeding; or (iii) interferes or tends to interfere with, or obstructs or tends to obstruct, the administration of justice in any other manner. Section 2(d) defines High Court for the purposes of the Act, and the Explanation to Section 2 addresses pending proceedings: a judicial proceeding is deemed pending, in a civil case, from the time the plaint or other initiating document is presented to the court until disposal, and in a criminal case, broadly, from the issue of process or the taking of cognizance until disposal, with specified rules for appeals and revisions.

Three features of the definitions repay attention. The first is the word wilful in the civil limb. Disobedience that is involuntary, accidental or the product of genuine inability is not civil contempt, and the burden lies on the person alleging contempt to establish wilfulness. An order that is ambiguous, that bears two reasonably possible interpretations, or that the alleged contemnor understood in a sense the court later rejects, occupies uncertain ground, and courts repeatedly state that a person should not be held in contempt for failing to comply with an order whose meaning was open to honest doubt. The second feature is the reach of the criminal limb. It covers publication and also any other act whatsoever, and each of its three sub-limbs is drafted in the language of tendency: conduct that tends to scandalise, tends to interfere or tends to obstruct is within the definition whether or not any actual interference occurred. The prosecution of criminal contempt therefore does not require proof that a proceeding was in fact prejudiced, though Section 13, since 2006, requires that the contempt be of a nature that substantially interferes or tends substantially to interfere with the due course of justice before sentence may be imposed. The third feature is what the definitions leave out. There is no definition of scandalise, of lowering the authority of a court, or of the administration of justice. The core evaluative terms of the criminal limb are undefined, and their content has been supplied by judgments. That gap is the centre of the abolitionist argument about vagueness and, equally, the centre of the retentionist answer that the defences and the threshold in Section 13 do the confining work the definitions omit.

Contempt of a court includes contempt of the Supreme Court and the High Courts themselves and, through Section 10, contempt of courts subordinate to a High Court, which the High Court may punish in the same manner as contempt of itself. The Act does not extend to every body called a tribunal. Tribunals possess contempt powers only where their own governing statute confers them, as several statutes do, and bodies without such a provision must work through the High Court or through the ordinary criminal law. The distinction matters in practice: a litigant who disobeys a tribunal that has no statutory contempt power has not, by that disobedience alone, committed contempt under this Act, and the enforcing party's remedy lies elsewhere. Conversely, disobedience to a subordinate civil or criminal court is squarely within the High Court's reach under Section 10, and the bulk of the contempt business of the High Courts consists of exactly that: orders of subordinate courts and of the High Courts themselves that have not been obeyed.

## Civil contempt: the enforcement engine of the civil courts

Civil contempt is the form of contempt that litigants actually meet. Its function is coercive and remedial rather than punitive in purpose: it exists to compel compliance with orders and to vindicate the right of the party in whose favour an order was made. A decree-holder who has won an injunction and watches the defendant continue the prohibited act, a spouse against whom a maintenance order is simply ignored, an authority that has been directed to decide an application within a stated period and does nothing, a party who has given an undertaking to the court and then breaks it: each of these is a candidate for a civil contempt petition. The petition is filed in the court whose order was disobeyed. The court inquires whether the order was clear, whether the alleged contemnor knew of it, and whether the disobedience was wilful. If contempt is made out, Section 12 supplies the punishment, but the characteristic outcome of civil contempt is not imprisonment; it is compliance, often extracted by the proceeding itself, supplemented where necessary by directions for implementation, attachment of property in execution, or a sentence held in reserve and activated only if the default continues.

The undertaking limb deserves separate statement because litigants underestimate it. An undertaking given to a court is not a private promise. It is recorded by the court, relied upon by the court in moulding its order, and treated for enforcement purposes as equivalent to an injunction. A party who assures the court that possession will be delivered by a date, that construction will stop, that a sum will be deposited, and then defaults, faces contempt on the same footing as a party who defies an express prohibitory order. Counsel who give undertakings on instructions therefore carry a professional responsibility of a distinctive kind, and the discipline of the legal profession, which operates through the Bar Councils under the Advocates Act 1961 alongside the court's own contempt jurisdiction, treats the casual giving and breaking of undertakings as a serious matter, a point developed in [the account of the Advocates Act 1961 and the regulation of the profession](/2021/04/15/advocates-act-1961-explained/). The two jurisdictions do different work. Professional discipline addresses the advocate's fitness to practise; contempt addresses the breach of the court's authority in the individual case; and the same default can attract both.

Civil contempt also carries limits that the case law states with some consistency. The proceeding is quasi-criminal in the standard of proof: contempt must be established beyond reasonable doubt, not on a preponderance of probabilities, because the consequence may be imprisonment. The order said to be disobeyed must be unambiguous and must have been communicated to the party, or the party's knowledge of it otherwise proved. A party cannot be committed for failing to do something the order did not in terms require, and courts decline to expand an order by interpretation in contempt proceedings beyond its fair reading. Where compliance has become impossible without fault, or where the alleged contemnor has substantially complied, courts ordinarily purge the contempt or decline to proceed. These limits explain why civil contempt, despite dealing in disobedience, attracts little controversy in the reform debate. The argument over contempt law is almost entirely an argument about the criminal limb, and specifically about its first sub-limb. Civil contempt is the machinery by which paper orders become real ones, and its critics in the abolition debate generally accept that some such machinery must exist in any legal system that expects its orders to be obeyed.

## Criminal contempt and the scandalising limb

The second and third sub-limbs of Section 2(c) address interference with proceedings and with the administration of justice: publishing material that prejudices a pending trial, attempting to influence a judge or a witness, obstructing an officer executing process, or disrupting a hearing. These limbs have analogues in every common law system and generate relatively little dispute. The first sub-limb is different in kind. It makes it criminal contempt to publish matter, or do any other act, which scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower the authority of, any court. Scandalising, in the sense the courts have given the term, means an attack on the court or on a judge in the judicial capacity that imputes dishonesty, corruption, bias or other improper motive, or that is otherwise calculated to undermine public confidence in the administration of justice. The offence protects not the personal dignity of the judge but the institution's stock of public confidence, on the reasoning that compliance with judgments in a system without a large enforcement apparatus depends on the belief that courts decide honestly and impartially, and that attacks designed to destroy that belief injure the administration of justice itself.

The earliest Supreme Court authority, Bathina Ramakrishna Reddy v. State of Madras, decided in 1952 under the predecessor statute, drew the boundary that later cases have refined: criticism of a judge that is mere personal vilification unconnected with the administration of justice may sound in defamation, but attacks that impute corruption or want of integrity in the discharge of judicial functions fall within contempt. In E.M.S. Namboodiripad v. T.N. Nambiar in 1970 the Court held that criticism of the judiciary as an institution, framed as political or sociological argument, does not by itself escape the Act merely because it is general in form, though the case is also authority for the proposition that the test is the effect of the words on the administration of justice and not the sensitivity of the judge criticised. The courts have repeatedly distanced the offence from wounded feelings. Lord Atkin's sentence in Ambard v. Attorney-General for Trinidad and Tobago, delivered for the Privy Council in 1936 and quoted in Indian judgments across the decades, states the retained half of the doctrine: justice is not a cloistered virtue and must be allowed to suffer the scrutiny and the respectful, even if outspoken, comments of ordinary men. The doctrinal structure is therefore a pair of propositions held together: fair, temperate and accurate criticism of a judicial act is protected, and imputation of improper motive to a court in its judicial capacity is not.

### Where does fair criticism of a judgment end and contempt begin?

Section 5 protects fair criticism of a judicial act after the case has been heard and finally decided, so reasoned disagreement with a judgment, however strong, is not contempt. The line is crossed when criticism imputes dishonesty, corruption, bias or improper motive to the judge in the judicial capacity, or pressures a pending proceeding.

Two practical distinctions organise most of the case law. The first is between criticism of a judgment and attribution of motive to the judge. To write that a judgment misreads a statute, ignores a binding precedent or produces an unjust result is criticism of the judicial act and falls within Section 5 once the case is decided. To write that the judge decided as he did because of caste, religion, political loyalty, personal interest or corruption is an imputation of motive; it is the classic scandalising case, and the truth of the imputation, since 2006, may be raised only through the gated defence in Section 13. The second distinction is between pending and decided proceedings. Comment on a pending proceeding attracts the second sub-limb of Section 2(c) and the law of prejudicial publication, a distinct body of doctrine concerned with trials in progress, while comment on a concluded case is governed by the fair criticism defence. Much confusion in public discussion of contempt comes from treating the two as one subject. A journalist may lawfully publish a severe critique of a delivered judgment on the day it is delivered; the same severity directed at a judge mid-trial, in terms calculated to pressure the outcome, is assessed under a different limb and a stricter standard.

## The defences: Sections 3 to 8

The Act's defences are structured as declarations that specified conduct is not contempt, and a person answering a contempt notice begins by locating the conduct within them. Section 3 protects innocent publication and distribution. A person who publishes matter in connection with a pending proceeding is not guilty of contempt if, at the time of publication, there were no reasonable grounds for believing the proceeding was pending, and a distributor is protected on the corresponding terms in relation to publications whose contemptuous character he had no reasonable grounds to know. Section 4 protects a fair and accurate report of a judicial proceeding, or of any stage of it, published contemporaneously or subsequently, subject to the qualification that the protection does not extend to proceedings the court has prohibited from being published or that are otherwise outside the section. Section 5 protects fair criticism of a judicial act after the case has been heard and finally decided. Section 6 protects a statement made in good faith concerning the presiding officer of a subordinate court, where the statement is made to another subordinate court, the District Judge or the High Court: the complaint channel is a defence precisely because the alternative, public accusation, is where the scandalising limb bites. Section 7 regulates the publication of information about proceedings held in chambers or in camera, permitting it in specified circumstances, including where the court sits in chambers or in camera for reasons of public policy or in exercise of a power vested in it, subject to exceptions including cases where publication is contrary to a statute or the court expressly prohibits it. Section 8 preserves any other defence available under law, so the statutory list is a floor and not a ceiling.

### Can a complaint against a subordinate court judge be contempt?

A complaint made in good faith to the proper authority is not contempt. Section 6 protects a statement concerning the presiding officer of a subordinate court made to another subordinate court, the District Judge or the High Court. The same allegations published generally are assessed instead under the scandalising limb of Section 2(c).

The defences repay reading as a set, because their combined shape is the statute's answer to the charge that it silences legitimate speech about courts. Reporting is protected if it is fair and accurate. Criticism is protected if it is fair, addresses the judicial act and follows the decision. Grievance is protected if it travels through the complaint channel in good faith. Publication about a pending case is protected where the publisher did not know the case was pending. What remains outside the defences is a defined residue: prejudicial publication made with knowledge, imputation of improper motive made in public, and disobedience of orders. Whether that residue is drawn in the right place is contestable and contested, but the architecture is intelligible, and arguments about the Act are more precise when they specify which defence is said to be too narrow, or which part of the residue is said to be unjustified, rather than addressing the statute as a single undifferentiated prohibition. The table below states the practical position for the six kinds of conduct that generate most questions.

| Conduct | Classification | Provision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reporting a judgment accurately | Protected, not contempt | Section 4, fair and accurate report |
| Criticising a decision after it is delivered | Protected if the criticism is fair | Section 5, fair criticism of a judicial act |
| Alleging bias against a named judge in public | Potentially criminal contempt | Section 2(c)(i), scandalising or lowering the authority of the court |
| Complaining about a judge to the appropriate authority | Protected if made in good faith | Section 6, complaint concerning a presiding officer of a subordinate court |
| Publishing about a pending case | Potentially criminal contempt; protected if the publisher had no reasonable grounds to know the case was pending | Section 2(c)(ii) and Section 3, innocent publication |
| Disobeying a court order | Civil contempt if the disobedience is wilful | Section 2(b) |

Section 16 completes the statutory scheme of liability in a way readers often miss. It provides that a judge, magistrate or other person acting judicially is liable for contempt of his own court or of any other court in the same manner as any other person, subject to a protection for statements and acts done in good faith in the situations the section specifies. The provision rejects the idea that the contempt jurisdiction exists for the protection of judges as a class by making its most senior potential users answerable under it, and it is the statutory footing for the rare proceedings in which judicial officers themselves have faced contempt for disregarding the orders of a superior court. Section 21 excludes Nyaya Panchayats from the Act, and Section 23 empowers the Supreme Court and the High Courts, with the approval the section prescribes, to make rules regulating contempt procedure, a power under which the courts have framed the rules that govern the filing, listing and hearing of contempt petitions in daily practice.

## Section 13 and the amendment of 2006: threshold and truth

As originally enacted, Section 13 provided only that no court should impose a sentence for contempt unless satisfied that the contempt was of such a nature that it substantially interfered, or tended substantially to interfere, with the due course of justice. The Contempt of Courts (Amendment) Act 2006, Act 6 of 2006, substituted the section and gave it two clauses. Section 13(a) re-enacts the threshold: notwithstanding anything contained in any law for the time being in force, no court shall impose a sentence in respect of a contempt of court unless it is satisfied that the contempt is of such a nature that it substantially interferes, or tends substantially to interfere, with the due course of justice. Section 13(b) adds the new defence: notwithstanding anything contained in any other law, the court may permit, in any proceeding for contempt of court, justification by truth as a valid defence if it is satisfied that it is in the public interest and the request for invoking the said defence is bona fide.

Each clause does specific work. The threshold clause converts what had been sentencing practice into a condition of sentence. Trivial, technical or marginal contempt, contempt that causes no substantial interference with the course of justice, is not punishable at all, however clearly it falls within the Section 2 definition. The clause is the statute's proportionality device, and courts invoking the contempt jurisdiction in speech cases are required to confront it. The truth clause changed a position of long standing. Before 2006, an accused person could not defend an imputation against a court by proving the imputation true; the older case law treated truth as no answer, and in some formulations as an aggravation, because a true imputation of corruption was thought to lower the authority of the court more effectively than a false one. Parliament replaced that position with a qualified permission. Truth may be permitted as a defence, but the conditions matter and are frequently misstated in popular accounts. The defence is not available as a matter of right. The court must permit it. The court must be satisfied that invoking it is in the public interest. The request to invoke it must be bona fide. A contemnor who satisfies the court on those conditions may then attempt to prove the truth of the imputation; one who cannot pass the gate cannot convert the contempt hearing into an inquiry into the judge's conduct. The design is a compromise between the free speech objection to punishing true statements about public institutions and the institutional objection to allowing every contempt proceeding to become a roving trial of the bench, and it is defended and criticised from both directions on exactly those terms.

## Procedure: cognizance, consent and the hearing

The Act separates the procedure for contempt in the face of the court from contempt committed elsewhere. Section 14 governs the first case. When the Supreme Court or a High Court is satisfied that a person has committed contempt in its presence or hearing, the court may cause the person to be detained in custody, inform him of the charge, afford him an opportunity to make his defence, take such evidence as may be necessary, and determine the matter, with the hearing structured to occur on the same day where practicable and with the safeguards the section specifies, including an intimation of the charge and an opportunity to be heard. Section 15 governs criminal contempt in other cases. The Supreme Court or a High Court may take action on its own motion, called suo motu cognizance, or on a motion made by the Advocate General, or on a motion made by any other person with the consent in writing of the Advocate General, or, in the case of a High Court for a Union territory, by a law officer the section specifies. For contempt of a subordinate court, Section 15(2) provides for a reference by the subordinate court or a motion by the Advocate General or the specified law officer. Every motion by a private person must specify the contempt of which the person is alleged to be guilty, and Section 17 requires notice to be served personally on the alleged contemnor, with the material relied upon, except where the court for reasons recorded dispenses with personal service. Section 18 requires every case of criminal contempt to be heard and determined by a bench of not less than two judges, save for the exceptions the section states for courts of Judicial Commissioners. Civil contempt procedure follows a parallel course under the courts' rules, with the petition, reply, hearing and order sequence those rules prescribe.

The consent requirement in Section 15 is a structural feature of the Indian scheme and not a formality. A private citizen who wishes to set the criminal contempt jurisdiction in motion must first persuade the Advocate General, the senior law officer of the State or, for the Supreme Court, the Attorney General's corresponding office as the practice operates, that the case warrants the jurisdiction. The Advocate General acts as a filter independent of the court, and refusals of consent are a real and recurring outcome. The filter answers one of the standing objections to the contempt jurisdiction, that it is set in motion too easily by offended litigants, by routing private grievance through a public law officer before it can reach the court. It does not govern the court's own motion. Suo motu cognizance requires no consent, and the proceedings that attract the widest public attention are commonly of that kind. The two routes therefore distribute the initiative differently: private initiative filtered by consent, judicial initiative unfiltered but exercised by the bench that must then also hear the matter, an arrangement whose critics point to the combination of complainant and adjudicator in the suo motu case and whose defenders point to the alternative, which is that courts would depend on law officers to defend the administration of justice. The debate section below returns to that exchange.

## Punishment, apology, limitation and appeals

Section 12(1) provides that, save as otherwise expressly provided in the Act or in any other law, a contempt of court may be punished with simple imprisonment for a term which may extend to six months, or with fine which may extend to two thousand rupees, or with both. Two features of this provision are persistently surprising to readers who encounter it against the background of public controversy. The first is that the maximum sentence of imprisonment is modest by the standards of the penal statutes: six months of simple imprisonment, not rigorous imprisonment. The second is that the maximum fine, two thousand rupees, stands in the statute book in the figure fixed in 1971, a sum whose real value has shrunk across five decades and which legislatures have left unrevised. Sentencing practice works within these bounds. Courts frequently accept apologies, impose nominal fines, sentence for short periods, or record a finding of contempt while imposing no sentence of imprisonment at all. The finding, the conviction for contempt of court, carries its own stigma and professional consequences, particularly for public servants and members of the legal profession, and is treated by courts as a substantial part of the sanction. Section 12 also addresses contempt by a company, making the company and persons in charge of its business liable subject to the defences the section states, a provision of practical importance in civil contempt arising from corporate disobedience of injunctions and disclosure orders.

Apology occupies a formal place in the scheme. The proviso to Section 12(1) provides that the accused may be discharged, or the punishment awarded remitted, on apology being made to the satisfaction of the court, and the Explanation adds that an apology shall not be rejected merely on the ground that it is qualified or conditional, if the accused makes it bona fide. The apology in contempt is therefore neither a ritual humiliation nor an automatic exit. A court assesses whether the apology is genuine, whether it is tendered at a stage that shows contrition rather than tactics, and whether it is offered before or only after the court has indicated its conclusion. An apology offered unconditionally at the first opportunity, before argument on the merits, carries a weight that an apology offered after conviction does not. Courts also distinguish the apology that acknowledges the wrongfulness of the conduct from the expression of regret that maintains the conduct was justified; the second, whatever its merits as speech, is not an apology within the proviso. Where the apology is accepted, the proceeding ordinarily ends, and the willingness of courts to accept bona fide apologies is one of the reasons the number of contempt convictions is far smaller than the number of notices issued.

Section 20 sets the limitation period: no court shall initiate any proceedings for contempt, either on its own motion or otherwise, after the expiry of a period of one year from the date on which the contempt is alleged to have been committed. Section 19 grants appeals. An appeal lies as of right from an order or decision of a High Court in the exercise of its jurisdiction to punish for contempt, to a bench of not less than two judges of the court where the order was made by a single judge, and to the Supreme Court where the order was made by a bench, with further appeals to the Supreme Court in the terms the section provides; the section also empowers the appellate court to suspend execution of the punishment and to regulate bail, and fixes limitation for appeals at thirty days for an appeal to a bench of the same court and sixty days for an appeal to the Supreme Court. The appellate structure matters because contempt orders are frequently interlocutory in appearance but final in effect, and the Supreme Court's appellate docket in contempt includes both punishment orders of High Courts and disputes about whether the High Court's jurisdiction was properly invoked at all.

## The statute and the constitutional power: how the two are reconciled

The decided cases reconcile the Act with Articles 129 and 215 through three propositions. The first is that legislation may define and regulate, but may not abrogate or unduly restrict, the contempt power of a court of record. Sukhdev Singh Sodhi established the inherence of the power; S.K. Sarkar applied it to hold that the modes of cognizance in Section 15 do not exhaust the High Court's power under Article 215, so that the consent and motion framework governs proceedings set in motion by others and does not disable the court from acting itself. The second proposition runs in the opposite direction: the courts accept and apply the statute's safeguards as the governing framework of their proceedings even though the power is constitutional. High Courts take cognizance through the Section 15 routes in the ordinary run of cases, apply the Section 12 ceiling, follow Section 17 notice, and constitute benches under Section 18. The third proposition completes the reconciliation and concerns limitation. In Pallav Sheth v. Custodian, decided in 2001, the Supreme Court held that Section 20 applies to proceedings initiated under the constitutional power as well as under the Act, so that a contempt proceeding begun more than one year after the contempt was committed is barred even where the court acts under Article 215, with the provision applied by reference to the date the applicant acquired knowledge in the circumstances the judgment examines. The pattern the cases produce is neither statutory supremacy nor constitutional immunity from the statute. It is a working settlement under which the power is the Constitution's and its regular exercise is the Act's, and under which the Act's defences, ceiling and limitation are treated as binding discipline on the power's use.

That settlement has one consequence that reform proposals must reckon with and frequently do not. If Parliament deleted the scandalising limb from Section 2(c), the conduct in question would not cease to be punishable. The Supreme Court and the High Courts would retain the power to punish attacks that scandalise them under Articles 129 and 215, a power whose content predates the statute and is stated in the pre-statute case law. What deletion would remove is the apparatus the Act wraps around the power: the definitions that locate the offence, the defences in Sections 3 to 8 that declare protected conduct, the substantial interference threshold, the gated truth defence, the two thousand rupee and six month ceiling in Section 12, the two judge bench requirement, the appellate structure in Section 19 and the one year limitation in Section 20. A court exercising an unmediated constitutional power would be free of the statutory ceiling and the statutory limitation unless it chose to observe them. The namable claim of this article is therefore that the amendment most often proposed as a liberalising reform of contempt law would, on the courts' own doctrine, liberalise nothing and would deregulate the power it targets. This is not an argument invented by defenders of the status quo after the event. It is the reasoning on which the Law Commission recommended retention, and it converts the abolition debate in India into a debate about a different question: not whether the power should exist, which the Constitution settles, but whether its statutory discipline should be tightened, loosened or left alone.

## The abolition debate, stated on both sides

The proposal examined by the Law Commission was confined in terms: whether the definition of criminal contempt should be amended to remove the scandalising limb, so that criminal contempt would cover only interference with proceedings and obstruction of the administration of justice. The Commission, in Report No. 274, titled Review of the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 (Limited to Section 2 of the Act), submitted in 2018, recommended that no amendment was necessary. Its reasoning had four strands. First, the constitutional point: the power of the Supreme Court and the High Courts to punish for contempt, including scandalising, flows from Articles 129 and 215 and is independent of the Act, so deleting the limb would have no impact on the power itself. Second, the clarity point: with no statutory definition, superior courts would develop the concept case by case, producing multiple definitions and less certainty than a single statutory text gives. Third, the safeguard point: the Act contains provisions declaring what is not contempt and what is not punishable, and those safeguards, including the defences and the threshold, would fall away with the definition they qualify. Fourth, the factual point: the volume of pending contempt business, overwhelmingly civil contempt but including a substantial body of criminal contempt across the High Courts, showed the jurisdiction in active use in Indian conditions, a record the Commission contrasted with the disuse of the scandalising offence in England before its abolition. The report was circulated with summaries and slide presentations for consultation and comment, the kind of material a reader works through page by page in [a PowerPoint viewer for presentation files opened in the browser](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) when comparing the Commission's text against the summaries made of it.

The case for abolition, as its proponents state it, answers each strand and adds grounds of its own. The offence of scandalising, proponents argue, is a survival from a period in which courts stood at a distance from public scrutiny and in which confidence in institutions was maintained by deference; a mature judiciary, in this argument, earns confidence by the quality of its judgments and cannot protect it by punishing detractors. The defining terms are said to be incurably subjective: scandalise and lowers the authority have no statutory definition, their application depends on the temperament of the bench seised of the matter, and two benches can and do assess comparable criticism differently, which is a failure of the certainty a penal provision owes the citizen. The structural objection follows: in a scandalising case the court is the party aggrieved, the prosecutor in substance and the judge, a combination that no other area of the criminal law tolerates and that sits uneasily with the principle that no one should judge his own cause. The comparative objection completes the case: England and Wales abolished the offence, and no collapse in respect for courts followed. Proponents answer the constitutional point directly. That the power would survive deletion is, in their submission, a reason to pair deletion with the discipline the Constitution permits, and several argue that courts exercising an undefined constitutional power in speech cases would in practice be driven by Article 19(2) reasonableness and by their own precedent to restraint, so the predicted deregulation is overstated. They also point out that the retention argument was considered and rejected in the jurisdiction that invented the offence.

The case for retention, as its proponents state it, begins from the enforcement reality of the Indian system. Judgments are obeyed, in the main, because the losing party and the officials who must implement the order accept the court's authority; the judiciary commands no police force or budget of its own, and its working capital is public confidence. Unfounded imputations of corruption and bias, made at scale and without answer, are said to erode that capital in a way that reasoned criticism does not, and the judiciary is the one institution that cannot reply in kind: judges do not hold press conferences, do not answer articles and do not campaign. The defences are presented as the operative answer to the free speech objection. Fair criticism is protected by Section 5; fair and accurate reporting by Section 4; good faith complaint through the proper channel by Section 6; truth, since 2006, is admissible with permission where the public interest and bona fides are shown; and Section 13 forbids sentence unless the contempt substantially interferes with the due course of justice. On this view the offence as confined is narrow, its sanctions are modest, its use against reasoned criticism is excluded by the text itself, and the cases that trouble public opinion are presented as failures of application rather than of the statute. The constitutional strand is then restated in its retentionist form: since deletion cannot remove the power, and would remove the defences, the threshold, the ceiling and the limitation, the practical choice is between a statutory offence with statutory safeguards and a constitutional power without them, and the second is the worse position for the speaker.

### What happened to scandalising the court in the United Kingdom?

Scandalising the court was abolished in England and Wales by Section 33 of the Crime and Courts Act 2013, following a Law Commission recommendation, after the offence had fallen into disuse, the last prosecution being in 1931. It was a common law offence there; in India the power behind it is constitutional.

Both cases are recorded here in their proponents' terms because the register of this article requires it and because the debate is genuinely undecided in the public sphere. The statutory position, which is the business of a statute pillar, is narrower than the debate. Scandalising remains within Section 2(c)(i). Section 13 confines punishment to contempt that substantially interferes with the due course of justice and admits truth by permission. No amendment has been enacted on the Commission's reference. The institutional criticism question runs wider than contempt: movements for reform of judicial appointments and accountability, including the litigation over the National Judicial Appointments Commission, have made criticism of the judiciary a standing feature of public life, and [the account of the NJAC Act and the collegium judgment](/2021/08/01/njac-act-collegium-judgment/) traces how the appointments question and the criticism question meet. The contempt statute operates inside that wider argument as the one provision that attaches a penal consequence to a particular kind of criticism, and every debate about it is in part a debate about the rest.

## Speech, transparency and the working boundaries

The free speech analysis of contempt begins with Article 19(2), which names contempt of court as a permissible ground of restriction, and proceeds to the reasonableness of the restriction as applied. The modern free speech jurisprudence of the Supreme Court, including the vagueness and overbreadth reasoning in [the Shreya Singhal judgment on free speech online](/2019/04/15/shreya-singhal-judgment-free-speech/), supplies the vocabulary in which challenges to contempt action in speech cases are framed: whether the words fall within the ground invoked, whether the connection between the words and the apprehended harm is proximate rather than remote, and whether the law as applied chills protected criticism along with punishable imputation. The contempt cases state their own version of the same discipline. Criticism that is fair comment on a judicial act is protected; the protection is lost where the comment imputes motive; and the substantial interference threshold requires the court to find a real risk to the course of justice, not a theoretical one. The resulting boundary is fact specific in every case, which is precisely what both sides of the reform debate seize upon: abolitionists treat the fact specificity as evidence of standardlessness, retentionists as the ordinary condition of a body of law organised around a standard rather than a rule.

Transparency cuts in both directions in this field and is part of the working boundary. Open reporting of proceedings is the premise of Section 4, and the access regime under [the Right to Information Act 2005 and its guide](/2011/09/01/right-to-information-act-2005-guide/) supplies much of the documentary material, judgments, orders, cause lists and administrative decisions, on which informed criticism of courts depends. Restrictions on publication are correspondingly specific: a court may postpone or restrain reporting of a proceeding where the administration of justice requires it, and reporting made in defiance of such an order is assessed under the second and third limbs of Section 2(c). The general principle remains that courts sit in public, their judgments are public documents, and criticism of those judgments is the ordinary traffic of a legal culture. The statute's interventions are at the margins of that principle, at the points of imputed motive, prejudiced trial and disobeyed order.

The suo motu criminal contempt proceedings of 2020, arising from statements published about the Supreme Court and its functioning, brought every strand of this article into a single public argument and are recorded here at headline level, without the facts of any individual statement beyond what the legal propositions require. The Supreme Court took cognizance on its own motion, held the statements in question to fall within the scandalising limb, rejected the defences advanced, and imposed a nominal fine. Supporters of the proceedings, including voices within the Bar, argued that imputations against the institution, made by a person of standing in the profession, required an answer from the court if public confidence was to be maintained, and that the sentence imposed was restrained. Critics, including former judges writing in public and sections of the Bar, argued that the proceedings illustrated the structural objection to the offence: that criticism of the court's functioning, however sharp, should be answered by the quality of adjudication and not by the penal jurisdiction, and that the spectacle of the court judging imputations against itself damaged the confidence it sought to protect. The judgments in the proceedings are the authoritative statement of the court's position; the criticism of them is part of the continuing debate this article has recorded in both its forms. Nothing in the episode changed the statute. Section 2(c) and Section 13 stand as the 2006 amendment left them, and the episode's legal significance is as an application of the existing text at its most contested point.

The working summary for the three readerships of this article is as follows. For the journalist and publisher, the operative protections are Section 4 for fair and accurate reports and Section 5 for fair criticism after decision, and the operative risks are prejudicial comment on a pending proceeding and imputation of motive to a named judge. For the litigant and counsel, the operative provision is Section 2(b): an order disobeyed wilfully is enforceable through contempt, an undertaking given to the court binds like an order, and the proceeding is decided on proof beyond reasonable doubt with ambiguity in the order resolving against commitment. For the researcher, the operative point is the constitutional one: the Act is a regulatory code over a power conferred by Articles 129 and 215, the Law Commission's Report No. 274 of 2018 recommended retention of the scandalising limb on that express ground, and the United Kingdom comparison, on which abolitionist argument relies, concerns a jurisdiction where the offence was a creature of common law that had been unused for eight decades. This article is legal information about a statute and its case law, not legal advice on any individual proceeding, and a person facing a contempt notice requires representation in that proceeding.


## Proof and hearing: the safeguards inside a contempt trial

A contempt proceeding is a criminal proceeding in its safeguards though it is not a trial under the penal code. Because the consequence may be imprisonment, the charge must be proved beyond reasonable doubt. That standard, stated across the case law for both civil and criminal contempt, disciplines every stage. The alleged contemnor must know the charge with precision: the petition or the court's own motion must identify the words, the publication or the disobedience relied on, and a vague allegation that the respondent has lowered the authority of the court, without particulars, will not sustain a conviction. Section 17 requires the notice to be accompanied by the material on which the charge rests, a copy of the motion or reference and of the affidavits or documents relied upon, and requires service to be personal unless the court, for reasons to be recorded, directs another mode. The respondent is entitled to file an affidavit in defence, to be heard, and to test the material relied on against him. Evidence in contempt proceedings is ordinarily taken on affidavit, and courts are cautious about converting the summary jurisdiction into a roving inquiry, which is one reason the gated truth defence of Section 13(b) matters: without the court's permission, a respondent cannot insist on leading evidence to prove the truth of an imputation as a justification, though material may always be placed to show that a publication was a fair and accurate report, that criticism was fair comment on a decided case, or that a statement travelled through the Section 6 channel.

The bench composition rules add a further safeguard in criminal contempt: Section 18 requires the case to be heard and determined by not less than two judges. The requirement embodies a judgment that the jurisdiction, in which the court is complainant in substance, should never be exercised by a single judge sitting alone in the class of cases where the alleged contempt is of the court itself or of the administration of justice generally. The exception the section preserves concerns courts of Judicial Commissioners, a category of historical interest in the territories those courts served. Civil contempt petitions, by contrast, are routinely heard by the single judge who made the order or to whom the roster assigns the matter, because the controversy there is between parties and the court is enforcing its own direction at a party's instance. The distinction tracks the theory of the two jurisdictions: where the moving party is a litigant seeking compliance, the proceeding is an extension of the suit; where the moving force is the affront to justice itself, the Act doubles the bench.

Two restraint doctrines, developed in the judgments rather than in the text, govern how readily courts should use the jurisdiction at all. In In re S. Mulgaokar, decided in 1978, a bench of the Supreme Court laid down working guidelines that are still quoted: the jurisdiction is to be used sparingly; courts should be generous in their assessment of criticism and slow to treat it as contempt; the court should act only where the attack is calculated to obstruct or destroy the administration of justice and not merely because criticism is strong, wounding or even unfair; and the court should bear in mind that it is judging a matter in which its own authority is said to be engaged. Those guidelines have not been displaced. They sit alongside the Section 13 threshold as the second proportionality control, the first being statutory and the second doctrinal, and a court proposing sentence for a speech-based contempt is expected to satisfy both. The decided cases also recognise a category of technical contempt: a breach that is real but venial, committed under a mistaken understanding of an order's scope or a bona fide interpretation of an ambiguous direction, where courts record the breach and decline punishment, sometimes with a direction for future compliance. Technical contempt is the doctrinal cousin of the ambiguity rule in civil contempt, and together they mark the distance between the definition of the offence and the circumstances in which the courts will in fact deploy it.

A further decided limit deserves record because it is frequently misstated. In Supreme Court Bar Association v. Union of India, decided in 1998, the Supreme Court held that its contempt power under Articles 129 and 142 does not extend to suspending or revoking an advocate's licence to practise, a punishment the Advocates Act 1961 reserves to the disciplinary authorities it creates. The judgment is a significant datum for the statute-Constitution relationship this article has traced: the inherent power of a court of record is wide, but it is a power to punish for contempt, and punishments that belong to another statutory scheme cannot be imported into it. The contempt sanction remains the one Section 12 states, imprisonment up to six months or fine up to two thousand rupees or both, and neither the constitutional source of the power nor the gravity of a particular affront enlarges the menu.

## Contempt of subordinate courts: the reference route in daily practice

Most contempt in the system is not committed against the Supreme Court or a High Court. It is committed against the orders of district and subordinate courts: injunctions ignored in property disputes, maintenance and custody directions defied, stays breached while appeals sleep, and processes obstructed at the point of service or execution. Section 10 routes that business to the High Court. Every High Court has the same jurisdiction, powers and authority in respect of contempt of courts subordinate to it as it has in respect of contempt of itself, and the Contempt of Courts Rules framed by the High Courts regulate the reference the subordinate court makes: a subordinate court that considers a contempt committed against it, or against a party's rights under its order, may either proceed through its own coercive machinery where a statute gives it one, or refer the matter to the High Court with a statement of the facts. The proviso to Section 10 marks the boundary with the ordinary criminal law: where the act alleged is an offence punishable under the Indian Penal Code, the High Court shall not take cognizance of it as contempt of the subordinate court. Insulting a judge in court, for example, is an offence under the Penal Code provision dealing with intentional insult or interruption to a public servant sitting in a judicial proceeding, and the code, not the contempt jurisdiction, is the route for that conduct at the subordinate level. The proviso prevents the summary contempt route from swallowing conduct Parliament has assigned to regular trial.

The subordinate court is not helpless in the interval before a reference is decided, and the practical law of civil contempt depends on understanding the division of labour. A civil court executing its own decree works through the Code of Civil Procedure: attachment and sale, arrest and detention in execution within the code's limits, and the appointment of commissioners and receivers. Contempt adds what execution cannot supply: a direct personal sanction for the disobedience itself, addressed to the authority of the order rather than to the property of the judgment debtor. Parties therefore commonly pursue both tracks, execution to recover the substance and a contempt reference or petition to answer the defiance. The distinction also explains the different emotional registers of the two proceedings. Execution is routine and unembarrassed. A contempt reference from a subordinate court is a deliberate escalation, a statement by the judicial officer that the order's authority, and not merely its subject matter, has been engaged, and High Courts treat references with corresponding care, not infrequently converting the occasion into directions that secure compliance without conviction.

Contempt by and against public authorities forms the largest single class of the subordinate and High Court civil contempt docket. Orders directing governments and their officers to decide representations, release admitted dues, restore possession, consider appointments or comply with service judgments generate petitions when the direction is not carried out. The courts' practice in this class is settled in outline. The petition is directed against the officer responsible for compliance, ordinarily by name, because contempt is personal and a department as such cannot be imprisoned. A change of incumbent does not defeat the proceeding: the successor who inherits the unimplemented order inherits the obligation, and courts implead the officer in position. Wilfulness remains the touchstone, and an authority that shows genuine processing of the direction, seeks clarification of an ambiguous order, or applies for extension before the deadline ordinarily escapes conviction, while silence, evasive affidavits and compliance produced only after notice are the pattern courts punish. The class matters to the theory of the Act because it is here that civil contempt performs its largest constitutional function, converting judgments against the State from declarations into conduct, in a system where the alternative enforcement machinery against government is limited. Critics of the jurisdiction in its speech applications rarely address this class; defenders of the jurisdiction rest much of their case on it, pointing out that the same Section 12 sanction that punishes the scandalising publication is the sanction that makes a ration shop licence, a pension or a plot of land materialise after the decree.

## Reporting, comment and pending trials: the media boundary

The second limb of Section 2(c) governs the space in which journalism and adjudication overlap. A publication prejudices a judicial proceeding when it creates a real risk that the proceeding will be influenced: by declaring a party guilty or innocent before the court has decided, by publishing material that would be inadmissible at trial as though it were proof, by conducting a parallel public trial of the issues, or by directing pressure at the judge, the witnesses or the parties. The doctrine was stated for India in In re P.C. Sen, decided in 1970, where a speech by a Chief Minister commenting on a matter pending before a court was held to be contempt; the test the Court applied was the tendency of the publication to interfere with the due course of justice, and not the intention of the speaker or the actual effect shown. Trial judges possess a connected power to postpone reporting. In Sahara India Real Estate Corporation v. Securities and Exchange Board of India, decided in 2012, a Constitution Bench of the Supreme Court recognised the power of a court to direct postponement of publication of reports of a proceeding, for a reasonable period, where publication would create a real and substantial risk to the fairness of a trial, and framed the power as a preventive measure of last resort, balanced against the open justice principle and the Article 19(1)(a) rights of the press. Postponement is not prohibition of criticism: it regulates the timing of reports of a live proceeding, and the judgment treats the fair and accurate report protected by Section 4 as the rule to which postponement is the narrow exception.

Against that, the protections for the press in the Act are structural and broad. Section 4 protects the fair and accurate contemporaneous or subsequent report of a judicial proceeding held in public, and the bulk of court journalism, including reporting that a judgment is wrong, that a sentence is lenient or that a line of authority is incoherent, never comes near the Act at all, because reporting is either protected by Section 4 or, after decision, criticism is protected by Section 5. Section 7 governs the exceptional proceedings held in chambers or in camera, permitting publication of the text of an order and specified particulars while protecting the substance of what was heard in private, and making publication in the teeth of an express prohibition, or of proceedings whose publication a statute forbids, punishable as contempt. The practical line for an editor is assembled from these provisions rather than from any single rule: report what was said in open court fairly and accurately; comment on decided cases on their merits; treat pending proceedings with the knowledge that verdict-like pronouncements, inadmissible material and imputations against the presiding judge are the three recurring triggers of proceedings; and expect the Advocate General's consent, or the court's own motion, to be the gate through which any complaint must pass. The line is demanding at its edges, and editors who work courts professionally organise their practice around anonymised caution in pending criminal trials, where the risk is highest, while treating decided cases as open ground.

## The criticism line in the decided cases

The boundary between protected criticism and scandalising has been drawn by accumulation of instances, and the leading instances state propositions a practitioner can use. C.K. Daphtary v. O.P. Gupta, decided in 1971, concerned a publication that imputed corruption and improper motives to judges of the Supreme Court; the Court held it contempt, and the case stands for the proposition that an attack on the integrity of judges in their judicial capacity is the core scandalising case, as distinct from criticism of any particular decision. Baradakanta Mishra v. Registrar, Orissa High Court, decided in 1974, marks the boundary from the other side: statements made in a judicial officer's appeal and representations to the higher authorities about his own superior, made through the channels provided for redress, were held not to be contempt, applying the principle that a communication addressed in good faith to an authority competent to act on it is not a public scandalising of the court, the same principle Section 6 codifies for complaints concerning subordinate court presiding officers. The Mulgaokar guidelines, noticed in the proof section of this article, supply the attitudinal rule: strong, even wounding, criticism is to be met with judicial restraint, and the jurisdiction is reserved for attacks that obstruct or are calculated to obstruct the course of justice. In re Arundhati Roy, decided in 2002, applied the scandalising limb to statements the Court found to impute motives to it in a public controversy about its own earlier proceedings, and the judgment restated the distinction between criticism of a judgment, which is permissible, and attribution of improper motive to the court, which is not, in terms that continue to be cited for both halves of the distinction.

Read together, the cases produce a workable if contestable map. Criticism survives where it is anchored to a decision and argues about its correctness, its reasoning or its consequences. It is at risk where it decouples from decisions and attaches to the person or the institution, imputing corruption, communal or political bias, subservience or dishonesty. It is at further risk where its manner is scurrilous rather than reasoned, since manner features in the courts' assessment of tendency, though the better view expressed in the restraint cases is that intemperance alone, without imputation of motive or interference with a proceeding, is a thin basis for the jurisdiction. Practitioners advise on that map with its uncertainties intact, because the map is the law: the alternative of a statutory definition of scandalising, with elements and exclusions stated seriatim, is one of the reform proposals in circulation, and its absence from the Act of 1971, which defined so much else, is a deliberate feature of the Sanyal design rather than an oversight. The committee chose a standard over a rule, and every subsequent debate about vagueness is a debate with that choice.

## The comparative map: scandalising outside India

The United Kingdom comparison dominates Indian argument because the Indian offence is a transplant of the English one, but the wider common law world shows a spectrum rather than a single destination. In the United States, the Supreme Court in Bridges v. California, decided in 1941, held that punishment for publications commenting on pending proceedings requires the substantive evil to be extremely serious and the degree of imminence extremely high, the American formulation of the clear and present danger test; Justice Hugo Black's opinion in that line of cases contains the sentence quoted in every Indian abolitionist brief, that the assumption that respect for the judiciary can be won by shielding judges from published criticism wrongly apprises the character of public opinion. The scandalising offence in its English form never took root in the United States, where criticism of judges, including intemperate criticism, is protected speech and contempt by publication is confined to obstruction in or near the court and to publications creating an imminent threat to a proceeding. Canada retains a scandalising jurisdiction in form but confines it by a test requiring a real, substantial and immediate danger to the administration of justice, and Canadian courts have described courts, in a phrase the Indian debate has also adopted, as not being fragile flowers that will wither in the heat of controversy. Australia retains the offence, and its courts apply it, though sparingly. New Zealand's Law Commission recommended abolition and replacement with targeted offences, and the offence's continued existence there in statutory form has been the subject of repeated review. The spectrum matters for the Indian debate because both sides select from it. The abolitionist points to England and the United States; the retentionist points out that the jurisdictions that abolished the offence either never constitutionalised the power behind it, or possess enforcement machinery and compliance cultures that do not depend on the contempt sanction to make orders effective against the State, and that several comparable systems retain the offence in substance under other names, including offences of insulting a court, perverting the course of justice and prejudicial publication, which continue to punish much of the conduct the Indian Section 2(c) covers.

The English history itself is more specific than the slogan of abolition suggests. The offence of scandalising the court was a common law misdemeanour, defined by the courts rather than by statute, directed at publications that vilified a judge in the judicial capacity. It fell into disuse in the nineteenth century, was revived in a small number of twentieth century cases, and was last prosecuted in 1931. When the Law Commission for England and Wales examined it in its 2012 report on scandalising the court, it recommended abolition on the grounds that the offence was unnecessary, uncertain in scope and liable to chill legitimate criticism, and Parliament enacted the recommendation as Section 33 of the Crime and Courts Act 2013. Two elements of that history do not transfer to India. The first is disuse: the English offence was abolished after eighty years of silence, while the Law Commission of India recorded in 2018 a substantial and continuing body of contempt business in the Indian courts, overwhelmingly civil but with criminal contempt a live category. The second is source: the English power was the courts' own common law creation, which Parliament could therefore remove without remainder, while the Indian power is conferred by the constitutional text on courts of record. The comparison is therefore evidence about the offence's necessity in one legal culture, not a demonstration of what an Indian amendment would achieve, and the Indian Law Commission's report is best read as an insistence on exactly that distinction.

## What the Act does not do

A statute pillar is incomplete without its negative space. The Act does not govern contempt of Parliament or of a State Legislature, whose privileges under Articles 105 and 194 proceed on their own footing and have generated their own line of authority about the boundary with the courts. The Act does not create a contempt jurisdiction in tribunals, commissions or information bodies, which hold such powers only where their own statutes confer them, a gap the series has noted wherever a regulatory body must enforce its orders through a High Court instead. The Act does not provide an appeal from an order of the Supreme Court itself: a person punished by the Supreme Court seeks review under the Constitution and the Court's rules, not under Section 19, which is addressed to orders of a High Court. The Act does not regulate the content of judgments about contempt: it fixes categories, defences, procedure, punishment and limitation, and leaves the evaluative terms, scandalise, lower the authority, administration of justice, to the case law, with the consequences for certainty that the reform debate examines. The Act does not make truth a defence of right, does not raise its fine ceiling, and does not index either figure, so the monetary sanction in the text is a survival from 1971 whose deterrent content is symbolic, leaving imprisonment and the fact of conviction to do the work. Finally, the Act does not apply to the conduct of proceedings in Nyaya Panchayats, by Section 21, and does not displace other laws relating to contempt, by Section 22, so that statutory contempt powers elsewhere in the statute book, including those of commissions of inquiry and specialised tribunals, operate in parallel.

The reform agenda that survives the Law Commission's report is correspondingly specific, and is better stated as a list of open questions than as a single proposal. Whether the fine in Section 12 should be revised to a figure with real content, as Parliament has done in other statutes of the period, is a question the abolition debate crowds out but does not answer. Whether scandalising should be defined with statutory elements, converting the Sanyal standard into something closer to a rule, is a middle proposal that neither abolitionists nor retentionists have fully owned, since it would confine the offence by drafting rather than by deletion. Whether the truth defence's permission gate should be replaced by a defence of right, leaving the court to test truth and public interest at the hearing rather than at the threshold, is a reform internal to Section 13(b) that would change the practical position of the respondent more than any other single amendment. And whether the Section 13(a) threshold, substantial interference with the due course of justice, should be extended from a condition of sentence to an element of the offence itself, so that a sub-threshold scandalising publication is not contempt at all, is the doctrinal completion of the 2006 design. Each of these questions is live in the commentary. None has been enacted. The article records them because a reader who can state them understands the Act's present shape better than a reader who knows only that abolition was proposed and declined.


## The remaining provisions: a walk through the rest of the Act

Several provisions not yet treated in their own sections complete the scheme and are the sections practitioners cite in the interstices of larger arguments. Section 9 provides that nothing contained in the Act shall be construed as implying that any act or publication is punishable as contempt of court which would not be so punishable apart from the Act. The provision is a rule against expansion by implication: the definitions state the offence, and a court is not to reason from the Act's scheme to new categories. Section 11 gives a High Court jurisdiction to inquire into or try a contempt of itself or of a court subordinate to it, whether the contempt is alleged to have been committed within or outside its local limits, where the person charged is within those limits or in a situation the section describes; it answers the practical problem of the contemnor, the publication and the offended court sitting in three different territories, a common pattern where a publication circulates across State boundaries. Section 12, beyond the punishment clause and the apology proviso already treated, contains the company provisions: where a contempt is committed by a company, every person who at the time was in charge of and responsible for the conduct of its business is liable, subject to the defence that the contempt was committed without his knowledge or that he exercised due diligence, and a director, manager, secretary or other officer is liable where the contempt is shown to have been committed with his consent or connivance or to be attributable to his neglect. The sentencing provisions of the section also preserve the position that a sentence under the Act is in addition to remedies by way of execution, so that punishment of the contemnor does not exhaust the decree-holder's ordinary rights.

Section 21 excludes Nyaya Panchayats from the Act, a survival of the period in which village level bodies exercised small judicial functions under State law and were thought to need protection from the summary jurisdiction of the High Courts being turned on their informal proceedings. Section 22, the non-derogation clause, keeps other contempt laws alive alongside the Act. Section 23 empowers the Supreme Court and every High Court to make rules, not inconsistent with the Act, regulating the procedure in contempt proceedings, subject in the High Court's case to the approval the section requires; the rules framed under it are the working procedure of contempt practice in each court, prescribing the form of the petition, the documents to accompany it, the issuance and service of notice, the filing of replies and the listing of the matter. Section 24 repealed the Contempt of Courts Act 1952 and saved proceedings pending under it, closing the statutory succession from 1926 through 1952 to 1971. Section 1, finally, states the extent of the Act and its commencement; the territorial exclusion in the original text concerning Jammu and Kashmir belongs to the constitutional arrangements of its time and has been overtaken by the reorganisation of 2019, after which the Act extends across the Union in the ordinary way.

## Orders, undertakings and service: the drafting side of civil contempt

Because civil contempt turns on wilful disobedience of an order, the law of contempt reaches back into how orders are made. Three working rules, stated in the cases with monotony because they decide petitions with monotony, govern the field. The order must impose a clear and unambiguous obligation: a direction to consider, to take steps, or to decide in accordance with law may support further proceedings in the original jurisdiction, but a contempt petition founded on it will ordinarily fail unless the order, fairly read, required a specific act by a specific date. The order must have been brought home to the person said to have disobeyed it: service of the order, or proof of knowledge by other means, is a constituent of the charge, and a party who first learns of an injunction from the contempt notice has not wilfully disobeyed it in the interval. And the disobedience must be of the order as made, not of the order as the petitioner wishes it had been made: courts deciding contempt do not clarify, extend or improve the underlying order, and a petitioner whose order is defective is sent back to seek clarification or modification in the suit. These rules are not technicalities. They are the procedural expression of the principle that a penal consequence, imprisonment, cannot rest on an obligation the subject could not read.

Undertakings occupy their own drafting discipline. An undertaking is extracted by the court, recorded in the order, and frequently given in order to obtain an indulgence: time to vacate, a stay of demolition, the release of attached property, the postponement of a coercive step. The cases treat the undertaking as the party's own chosen obligation, which is why its breach is regarded without indulgence: the contemnor is not the victim of an order imposed on him but of a promise he volunteered to obtain an advantage. Counsel's part in the transaction is correspondingly serious. An undertaking given by counsel on instructions binds the client in contempt, and the professional obligation to ensure that instructions to give an undertaking are real, and that the client understands its penal edge, is part of the conduct rules of the profession. Where an undertaking is broken, the court's options illustrate civil contempt's remedial character: it may purge the contempt on compliance and an apology, impose a sentence and suspend it against compliance by a date, or commit forthwith where the breach is contumacious. The sequence of those options in decided matters is so regular that practitioners describe civil contempt, accurately, as a jurisdiction whose punishments are characteristically conditional and whose object is the order's performance rather than the contemnor's suffering.

One further institution belongs to this section: the consent order. Parties compromise litigation constantly on terms recorded by the court, and the question whether breach of a consent term is contempt or merely breach of a compromise is answered by the form of the record. Where the compromise is recorded as an undertaking to the court, or the court directs performance in terms that import an obligation to the court, contempt lies. Where the terms are a contract recorded for convenience, the remedy is execution or a fresh suit, and contempt does not lie merely because the document sits in a court file. The drafting of compromise terms is therefore a contempt decision taken in advance, and experienced counsel draft with the distinction consciously in view, choosing the undertaking form where the client's performance needs the discipline of the court's authority and avoiding it where flexibility is worth more.

## The consent filter and the suo motu motion in practice

The Advocate General's consent under Section 15 operates as the scheme's main screening institution, and its practice is worth stating because the statutory sentence conceals most of it. A person who believes a publication or act amounts to criminal contempt applies to the Advocate General with the material relied on. The law officer considers whether the material, if proved, falls within Section 2(c), whether a defence under Sections 3 to 8 is plainly available, whether the Section 13 threshold could be met, and whether the public interest in the administration of justice is served by a proceeding, a consideration that includes the staleness of the publication and the prominence or obscurity of its circulation. Consent may be granted or refused, and the grant does not decide anything: it opens the court's door. A refusal is not ordinarily appealable, though the Supreme Court has entertained the position that an aggrieved person may place the material before the court and invite it to act on its own motion, a route that preserves the court's initiative while respecting the law officer's screen. The filter's critics observe that it screens only the private route and leaves the court's own motion untouched, so that the cases of greatest controversy bypass the institution entirely. Its defenders answer that a screen on judicial initiative would be a screen on the court itself, incompatible with the court of record's constitutional position, and that the consent requirement was designed for the mischief it addresses, which is the conversion of private offence into public prosecution at private instance.

Suo motu cognizance carries its own discipline in practice. The proceeding begins with a notice that must state the charge and the material, and the respondent is heard through counsel in the ordinary way; the bench that issued the notice ordinarily hears the matter, which is the feature the structural objection fastens on, though the practice is defended on the ground that the notice records an institutional act of the court rather than the personal grievance of the judge named in the publication, and that recusal conventions apply where the attack is personal to an individual judge in a manner that engages actual bias. The decided position distinguishes contempt of the court, an institution, from affront to the judge, a person: a publication aimed at a judge personally, in terms unrelated to the administration of justice, sounds in defamation or in the ordinary criminal law and not in contempt, and a judge who treats personal vilification as an affront to the institution commits the error the restraint cases warn against. That distinction, simple to state, is the point on which applications of the scandalising limb most often turn, and it is why the case law keeps returning to the question whether the imputation is of the judge in the judicial capacity. A charge that a judge's private conduct is disreputable is a matter for the mechanisms of judicial accountability, including the in-house procedure of the judiciary and, at the limit, removal by Parliament. A charge that the judge decided a case corruptly, or from bias, or under influence, is an imputation on the administration of justice and is the contempt case. The line is between the office and the person, and Section 2(c)(i), read with the restraint doctrine, is administered on it.

## Apology and purging: how contempt ends without punishment

The apology jurisdiction in the proviso to Section 12(1) is the point at which the Act's theory becomes most visible. Contempt, in the civil branch, exists to secure compliance; in the criminal branch, to protect the administration of justice against the corrosion of imputed dishonesty. In neither branch is punishment an end. An apology that is genuine performs the repair the jurisdiction exists to achieve: it withdraws the imputation or acknowledges the obligation, and it does so publicly, in the forum the contempt injured. The courts' insistence on bona fides, stated in the Explanation's protection of the qualified and conditional apology, is directed at the difference between repair and manoeuvre. An apology that withdraws the words while repeating their substance in the same breath, an apology accompanied by a public campaign asserting the truth of the charge, or an apology tendered for the first time after every defence has failed and sentence is about to be pronounced, is examined as tactics and commonly rejected. An apology tendered early, unconditionally and with the conduct it acknowledges discontinued, is commonly accepted even in serious cases, and the discharge that follows is not leniency but the jurisdiction achieving its object.

Purging is the civil branch's equivalent. A contemnor in civil contempt purges the contempt by compliance: by doing the act the order required, undoing the act the order forbade, or restoring the position the disobedience disturbed. Courts frame orders in civil contempt with purging in view, fixing dates for compliance after a finding, recording that the sentence will not be executed if the direction is carried out, and discharging the respondent when the affidavit of compliance is filed and verified. The doctrine has limits that the cases state plainly. Compliance extracted by the proceeding does not erase a contempt already complete where the delay itself has caused the mischief the order existed to prevent, and a contemnor who complies only in part, or complies in form while defeating the order's purpose, has not purged. Nor does purging answer the costs of the proceeding, which fall on the contemnor as a matter of course. These doctrines complete the portrait of Section 12 as an instrument with two registers: the sentence in the text, six months and two thousand rupees, which is almost never imposed at its ceiling, and the working sanctions of the jurisdiction, which are the finding, the order for compliance under threat, the costs and the recorded apology. Commentators who measure the Act by its maximum fine mismeasure it; the jurisdiction's real weight is procedural, and it is felt by respondents, including governments, long before any question of imprisonment arises.

## Before the statutes: contempt in the chartered High Courts

The contempt power exercised in India before 1926 was not a statutory creation but part of the inheritance of the chartered High Courts, established under the Indian High Courts Act 1861 and their Letters Patent as courts of record on the English model. The best known early episode is also the most instructive. In 1883 the Calcutta High Court committed Surendranath Banerjea, the editor of The Bengalee, for contempt on account of an article criticising a judge of the court in terms the court held to impute unfitness; Banerjea appealed to the Privy Council, which recognised the High Court's power as a court of record to punish contempt of itself and declined to interfere with the sentence, treating appellate interference with contempt orders as exceptional. The episode fixed two features of the Indian law that have never left it. The power was judicial in source and self-executing, needing no statute to exist, and its exercise against a prominent public critic of the court generated, from the beginning, the same argument that has attended it ever since: the editor's supporters treated the proceeding as the court punishing its critic, and the court's defenders treated the article as an imputation on the administration of justice that a court of record could not ignore. The statutory century that followed, from 1926 to 1971 and beyond, is best understood as a series of attempts to surround that original, inherited power with definitions, exclusions and limits, without ever displacing it.

The Privy Council's later interventions refined the temper of the jurisdiction rather than its existence. Ambard, quoted earlier in this article, supplied its most durable sentence about criticism. The general posture of the appellate cases across the period was to affirm the power, to insist that it be used with restraint, and to refuse appellate review of contempt sentences save in exceptional circumstances, a posture the Supreme Court inherited and which explains why so much of the operative doctrine in India is stated in terms of guidelines and restraint rather than in terms of appellate correction of individual orders. When the Sanyal Committee came to the subject in 1961 it therefore faced a power that was constitutionally entrenched, judicially administered and almost entirely uncodified, and its recommendations are notable for what they did not propose: no abolition, no transfer of the jurisdiction to ordinary criminal courts, no jury, no external tribunal. The committee proposed codification of definition, defences, punishment and procedure, and Parliament enacted the proposal. The Indian settlement, in other words, was chosen at the point of maximum information about the alternatives, by the generation that had written the Constitution's courts of record clauses a decade earlier, and arguments for unsettling it carry the burden of that history whether or not they acknowledge it.

## Sentencing and consequence in practice

Sentencing in contempt repays attention because the distance between the statutory maximum and the ordinary outcome is where the jurisdiction's real character shows. Imprisonment at or near six months is rare and is associated with contumacious disobedience maintained after findings, or with scandalising publications of exceptional gravity repeated after warning. The ordinary outcomes form a ladder. At its base is the closure of the proceeding on apology or compliance, without a finding pressed to conviction. Above it stands the finding of contempt with a reprimand or with sentence deferred. Above that, the nominal fine, of which the one rupee fine imposed in the proceedings of 2020 is the most publicly discussed instance, a sentence whose function is declaratory: the court records that the conduct was contempt and marks the record with a token monetary consequence. Substantial fines within the two thousand rupee ceiling appear in commercial civil contempt, where the sum is trivial against the value of the disobedience and the accompanying directions do the real work. Short terms of imprisonment, measured in days or weeks, appear where a respondent has defied repeated orders or where a publication campaign continues after notice. The ladder is administered with the Section 13 threshold in view, and the restraint guidelines explain why so few proceedings climb it: notices are issued in numbers far exceeding convictions, a pattern each side of the reform debate reads to its advantage, abolitionists treating the volume of notices as evidence of a chilling jurisdiction in action, retentionists treating the ratio of notices to convictions as evidence of a jurisdiction that warns more than it punishes.

The collateral consequences of a contempt finding are frequently more serious than the sentence. A finding against an advocate may be placed before the Bar Council and may inform disciplinary proceedings under the Advocates Act 1961, whose machinery, and not the contempt court's, governs the right to practise, as the Supreme Court Bar Association judgment establishes. A finding against a public servant enters the service record and may be considered in promotion and disciplinary contexts. A finding against a company or its officers is a matter of public record with reputational consequences in regulated industries. A finding against a publisher or editor attaches to the publication's standing in every subsequent proceeding. These consequences explain features of contempt practice that baffle observers of the sentences alone: why respondents litigate findings with a tenacity disproportionate to a two thousand rupee fine, why apologies are negotiated with the care of settlements, and why the threshold question, whether conduct is contempt at all, absorbs more forensic energy than the question of punishment. The jurisdiction's centre of gravity is the finding, and the Act's defences are valuable to speakers precisely because they prevent findings, not merely sentences.


## Contempt, defamation and the ordinary criminal law

A publication attacking a judge may engage three different bodies of law, and the choice between them is a recurring source of confusion. Defamation protects the reputation of the individual: a judge defamed in his personal capacity has the same remedies as any citizen, a civil suit for damages and, in the period the penal code governed, a prosecution for defamation, and the truth of the statement, published for the public good, is a defence in the criminal law of defamation in a form broader than the gated defence Section 13(b) allows in contempt. Contempt protects the administration of justice: its subject is not the judge's reputation but the public confidence and the unobstructed course of proceedings, its procedure is summary, its prosecutor is in substance the court, and its sanction is the Section 12 sentence. The ordinary criminal law protects the proceeding itself against specific interferences: intentional insult or interruption to a public servant sitting in a judicial proceeding, threats to witnesses, fabrication of evidence and kindred offences defined in the penal code, each triable in the regular courts with the regular safeguards. The proviso to Section 10, which bars the High Court from taking cognizance as contempt of a subordinate court of an act that is an offence punishable under the penal code, is the statutory marker of the boundary between the second and third bodies at the subordinate level. At the level of the superior courts the systems overlap rather than divide, and the courts' practice is to treat the contempt jurisdiction as the instrument for imputations on the administration of justice, leaving personal attacks to the personal remedies, a division the Bathina Ramakrishna Reddy line of cases states and the restraint doctrine reinforces.

The distinction carries a practical moral for critics of courts. A statement that a judge is dishonest in private dealings, or incompetent, or rude, may be defamatory and actionable, and may be rude, unfair or wounding, without being contempt at all, because it does not touch the administration of justice. A statement that a judge decided a case for a bribe, under political instruction or from communal bias is contempt in its paradigm form even if published in measured language, because it imputes corrupt motive in the judicial capacity. Tone is therefore a weaker predictor of contempt than content, and the advice given to editors and to litigants writing their own representations converges: argue the decision, document the grievance through the channels the Act protects, and understand that the allegation of motive is the act the jurisdiction exists to answer. That the same allegation, if true, concerns the gravest failure a legal system can contain is the tension at the centre of the truth defence debate, and it is why the 2006 settlement, truth by permission where the public interest and bona fides are shown, is attacked from one side as a gate too narrow for the allegation that matters most and defended from the other as the necessary protection against converting every contempt hearing into an unregulated inquiry into a judge's integrity.

## Contempt powers elsewhere in the statute book and at the legislature's door

The 1971 Act is the general law of contempt, but Parliament has distributed specific contempt powers across the statute book where a body's work requires them, and the pattern of grants and withholdings is a map of legislative judgment about the jurisdiction. The Administrative Tribunals Act 1985 confers on the Central Administrative Tribunal and the State tribunals the same jurisdiction and powers as a High Court in respect of contempt of itself, to be exercised in accordance with the 1971 Act's framework as that statute adopts it. The Companies Act 2013 gives the National Company Law Tribunal and its Appellate Tribunal the power to punish for contempt on the High Court model. Commissions of inquiry, consumer commissions and most regulatory authorities, by contrast, were given penal or coercive powers of narrower kinds, fines, prosecution through magistrates, or enforcement through the courts, and must send disobedience of their orders into the ordinary machinery. The withholding is as instructive as the grant. Where a body decides disputes between parties in a judicial manner and its orders are terminal within its sphere, Parliament has tended to arm it. Where a body inquires, recommends or regulates, Parliament has tended to leave its authority to be vindicated elsewhere, a division that has generated recurring litigation when parties disobey bodies in the second class and discover that the contempt route does not exist.

At the boundary of the courts stands the contempt jurisdiction's constitutional neighbour, the privilege jurisdiction of the legislatures. Articles 105 and 194 empower Parliament and the State Legislatures to punish for breach of privilege and contempt of the House, a jurisdiction the courts do not administer and the 1971 Act does not touch. The two jurisdictions collided in the authorities every student of the subject reads: the Searchlight case, M.S.M. Sharma v. Sri Krishna Sinha, decided in 1959, where the Supreme Court upheld the privileges of a legislature against a free speech challenge by an editor, and the Presidential Reference of 1965 arising from the Uttar Pradesh Assembly's confrontation with the Allahabad High Court in the Keshav Singh affair, where the Court mapped the limits of each power against the other. Those cases belong to the law of privilege, and they are noticed here for a single reason: they show that contempt, in the Indian constitutional design, is not one power but a family of powers, judicial and legislative, each sourced in the Constitution, each jealous of its sphere, and each therefore resistant to reform by ordinary statute in a way the abolition debate about Section 2(c) sometimes forgets. The statute this article has examined governs the judicial member of that family. It is the member with definitions, defences, a punishment ceiling and a limitation period, which is more codification than any of its constitutional relatives possess, and the fact is worth holding in view when the Act is criticised as an archaic survival: within the family of contempt powers in the Constitution, it is the most regulated of them all.


## A contempt petition's journey: the Act in operation

The doctrines of the preceding sections assemble, in practice, into a recognisable sequence, and stating the sequence shows where each safeguard in the Act does its work. A civil contempt matter begins with non-compliance: a direction with a date in it passes unperformed, or an injunction is breached, or an undertaking falls due and is not honoured. The aggrieved party, usually after a lawyer's notice that itself produces a substantial share of all compliance the jurisdiction ever achieves, files a contempt petition in the court that made the order, annexing the order, proof of its service or of the respondent's knowledge, and an affidavit of the facts constituting disobedience. The registry examines the petition against the court's contempt rules, and the matter is listed before the bench. At the first hearing the court asks the questions the doctrine prescribes: is the order clear, was it known, is the default wilful, and has time run under Section 20 from the act of contempt alleged. Notice issues, ordinarily with a direction for the respondent's personal appearance or appearance through counsel as the rules and the court's order provide, and Section 17's requirements govern what the respondent receives: the petition, the annexures and the material said to establish the charge, so that the defence is prepared against a known case.

The respondent's reply affidavit is the centre of the proceeding. In it the defences recognised by the Act and the cases are marshalled in their practical forms: that the order bore a meaning different from the petitioner's and was complied with on that meaning; that compliance was rendered impossible by events outside the respondent's control, documented rather than asserted; that compliance is complete, or is complete in substance with a residue explained; that the officer arrayed is not the officer responsible for the obligation; or, in the publication cases, that the matter is a fair and accurate report within Section 4, fair criticism within Section 5, a complaint within Section 6, an innocent publication within Section 3, or that the Section 13 threshold and, where invoked and permitted, the truth defence answer the charge. Affidavits are the evidence; cross-examination is exceptional; and the hearing that follows is an argument on documents, which is why the quality of the paper record, the certified copy of the order, the proof of service, the correspondence showing demand and default, decides most petitions before doctrine is reached. The court's order then selects a point on the ladder described in the sentencing section of this article: discharge on apology or compliance, a finding with a deferred or conditional sentence, a fine, or committal, and the order is appealable under Section 19 in the criminal and the High Court civil jurisdictions the section covers, with the appellate powers of suspension and bail available to hold the position while the appeal is heard.

A criminal contempt matter travels the same road by a different gate. Its journey begins either with the Advocate General's consent on a private application, with a reference from a subordinate court under Section 15(2), or with the court's own motion, taken when a publication or an act in a proceeding is brought to the court's attention by a party, by the registry or by the bench itself. The notice stage carries the heaviest doctrinal load in this branch: the charge must be specified, the material supplied, personal service effected, and, by Section 18, the hearing placed before a bench of two judges. The respondent's options at the hearing include the apology at the threshold, which in this branch functions as the accused's election to end the matter by repair rather than by contest, and the defences on the merits, including, since 2006, the application for permission to justify by truth, an application that is itself argued and decided as a preliminary question of public interest and bona fides before any evidence of truth is received. The judgment that ends the matter is expected to deal separately with the definition, the defences and the Section 13 threshold, and its findings, rather than its sentence, carry the consequences traced earlier in this article for advocates, public servants and publishers.

Two features of the journey explain the statistics the Law Commission recorded. The first is attrition by design: consent refusals, discharges on apology, closures on compliance and sub-threshold discharges end most matters before conviction, so the large pending figures the Commission cited are a picture of a jurisdiction used as a lever and a warning system far more than as a sentencing machine. The second is duration: contempt petitions, listed among the arrears of the busiest courts in the system, can take years to conclude, a delay that blunts the civil branch's coercive edge, since compliance extracted after years is worth less than the order promised, and that sharpens the criminal branch's fairness problem, since a respondent in a speech case lives under a pending charge while the public controversy that produced it recedes. Reform commentary on the Act attends to the definition of the offence and rarely to either feature, yet for the litigants and officers who pass through the jurisdiction, the journey and not the definition is the law they experience. The Act, read whole, is therefore two instruments in one binding: a shield drafted around speech about courts, in Sections 3 to 8 and Section 13, and a lever placed under court orders, in Sections 2(b), 10 and 12, and its two halves are held together by a single constitutional premise, that the authority both halves protect belongs to courts of record under the Constitution and not to the statute that regulates it.

## Open courts, recordings and circulation: the publication questions in a new form

The publication limbs of Section 2(c) were drafted for newspapers, pamphlets and speeches, and they are asked to govern a world in which a hearing is watched as a recording, a judgment circulates as a portable document within the hour, and comment appears beside the material itself in the same feed. The doctrinal questions have not changed, but their factual settings have, and three developments mark the adjustment. The first is the open court principle's technological extension. In Swapnil Tripathi v. Supreme Court of India, decided in 2018, the Supreme Court approved the live streaming of proceedings in constitutional and other matters of national importance, subject to safeguards and exclusions for sensitive categories, treating public access to the hearing as an incident of the open court rule rather than a concession. Streaming changes the contempt calculus in both directions: a fair and accurate report is easier to verify against the recording, and the raw material of prejudicial or scandalising comment, the clipped excerpt, the remark removed from its context, circulates with an authority the written report never carried. The Act's categories absorb the change without amendment, because Section 4 protects the fair and accurate report of the proceeding and a faithful recording is the report in its most literal form, while Section 2(c) fastens on the commentary and the tendentious excerpt, the additions that carry the imputation or the prejudice.

The second development is the judgment as a freely circulating document. Court websites and public repositories have made the full text of orders and judgments the common property of any reader with a connection, and the practical consequence for contempt law is a narrowing of the mischief the reporting offences addressed. A scandalising imputation that misdescribes a judgment can be answered by the judgment itself, linked and read in the same exchange, which strengthens the retentionist concession that criticism anchored to a decision is safe and the abolitionist argument that the institution's defence is the quality and accessibility of its reasons. The third development is velocity. A statement about a court can reach a national audience before the registry has listed the matter it discusses, and the one year limitation in Section 20, the consent filter in Section 15 and the two judge bench in Section 18 operate at institutional speed against material that moves at network speed. Courts have responded within the Act's existing categories, treating republication as a fresh act of publication for limitation purposes in accordance with the ordinary principles the case law states, and treating the tendency of material, the statutory test, as a question answered by its foreseeable circulation rather than by its original audience. None of this has altered the text. It has altered the evidence by which tendency, prejudice and substantial interference are proved, and it is the likeliest site of the doctrine's continued development, in the interstices of a statute whose drafters in 1971 were regulating a press of print runs and evening editions.

## Costs, consequences and the limits of settlement

A final practical subject completes the account: what a contempt proceeding costs, and why it so often cannot simply be settled away. Costs in contempt follow the court's discretion, exercised on principles the cases make predictable: a contemnor who has forced a party to petition for the enforcement of an order pays the costs of the petition as a matter of course, and a petitioner who launches a contempt proceeding on an ambiguous order, or as a tactical adjunct to ordinary execution, may be left to bear his own costs and, in aggravating cases, the respondent's. The discretion matters because the money at stake in the costs order will ordinarily exceed the Section 12 fine by a large multiple, so that the financial centre of a contempt matter, for the respondent, is the combination of his own legal expenses, the adverse costs risk and the recorded finding, with the fine as a token at the margin. Parties who assess the jurisdiction by the two thousand rupee figure in the statute misjudge their exposure in both directions: petitioners overestimate the terror of the sanction and are surprised by the court's restraint, and respondents underestimate the proceeding and are surprised by everything except the fine.

Settlement has a defined and limited role. In the civil branch, the parties may compose the underlying dispute, and compliance tendered under a composition commonly ends the contempt, because the jurisdiction's object is spent; but the contempt is of the court and not of the party, so the petitioner's forgiveness is a factor and not a conclusion, and a court may proceed notwithstanding a private settlement where the disobedience was public, contumacious or injurious to third parties or to the administration of justice itself. In the criminal branch settlement has no formal place at all: the charge is the court's or the law officer's, the apology is addressed to the court, and a private arrangement between the publisher and the person aggrieved by the publication does not bind the bench. This allocation, the party may forgive the debt but not the defiance, is the institutional logic of the whole Act in miniature. What is injured by contempt, in the theory the statute enacts, is a public good, the working authority of court orders and the confidence that sustains them, and private persons can neither create the offence by their anger, as the consent filter and the restraint doctrine provide, nor extinguish it by their pardon. The citizen's part in the scheme is correspondingly specific and correspondingly protected: to obey orders or appeal them, to report proceedings fairly, to criticise decisions freely, to complain through the channels the Act names, and to leave the imputation of corrupt motive, the one act the statute reserves, to the rare proceeding in which a court permits it to be proved.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What conduct amounts to contempt of court?

Contempt of court, under Section 2 of the Contempt of Courts Act 1971, is either civil contempt or criminal contempt. Civil contempt is wilful disobedience to a judgment, decree, direction, order, writ or other process of a court, or wilful breach of an undertaking given to a court. Criminal contempt is the publication of any matter, or the doing of any other act, which scandalises or tends to scandalise or lowers the authority of a court, which prejudices or interferes with the due course of a judicial proceeding, or which interferes with or obstructs the administration of justice in any other manner. Sections 3 to 8 then declare several kinds of conduct not to be contempt at all.

### Q: What is the difference between civil and criminal contempt?

The difference is one of subject and purpose. Civil contempt, defined in Section 2(b), is disobedience: a party wilfully defies an order or breaks an undertaking given to the court, and the proceeding exists to compel compliance and to vindicate the right of the party who obtained the order. Criminal contempt, defined in Section 2(c), is interference: publication or conduct that scandalises a court, prejudices a pending proceeding or obstructs the administration of justice, and the proceeding exists to protect the institution and the public interest in the administration of justice. Both are punished under Section 12, both must be proved beyond reasonable doubt, and civil contempt is by far the more common in practice.

### Q: Which conduct amounts to scandalising the court?

Scandalising is the first limb of criminal contempt in Section 2(c)(i): publishing matter, or doing any other act, which scandalises or tends to scandalise, or lowers or tends to lower, the authority of any court. In the case law the term covers attacks that impute dishonesty, corruption, bias or other improper motive to a judge in the judicial capacity, and publications calculated to erode public confidence in the administration of justice. It does not cover fair criticism of a judicial act, which Section 5 protects after the case is decided, nor reasoned disagreement with a judgment, however strongly expressed, that addresses the decision rather than the motives of the judge who delivered it.

### Q: Is truth a defence to contempt?

Truth is a defence only within the gate Parliament built in 2006. Before that year the Act contained no provision permitting justification by truth. The Contempt of Courts (Amendment) Act 2006, Act 6 of 2006, substituted Section 13 and added clause (b), under which the court may permit justification by truth as a valid defence if it is satisfied that the defence is in the public interest and that the request for invoking it is bona fide. The defence is therefore real but not available as a matter of right: the court must grant permission, the public interest condition must be met, and the request must be made in good faith before the truth of the imputation can be proved.

### Q: What is the punishment for contempt?

Section 12(1) of the Act provides that a contempt of court may be punished with simple imprisonment for a term which may extend to six months, or with fine which may extend to two thousand rupees, or with both, save as otherwise expressly provided. The fine figure stands in the statute in the amount fixed in 1971. The same subsection allows the court to discharge the accused, or remit the punishment, where an apology is made to the satisfaction of the court, and an apology is not to be rejected merely because it is qualified or conditional if it is made bona fide. In practice courts frequently impose nominal fines, short sentences or no sentence after a finding, particularly where an apology is accepted.

### Q: Who can initiate a contempt proceeding?

Civil contempt is set in motion by the party in whose favour the disobeyed order was made, by petition to the court whose order it is. Criminal contempt follows Section 15. The Supreme Court or a High Court may act on its own motion, on a motion made by the Advocate General, or on a motion made by any other person with the consent in writing of the Advocate General; for a High Court for a Union territory, a specified law officer takes the Advocate General's place in the scheme. For contempt of a subordinate court, Section 15(2) provides for a reference by that court or a motion by the Advocate General. The consent requirement makes the Advocate General a filter between private grievance and the court's jurisdiction.

### Q: Is there a time limit for contempt?

Yes. Section 20 of the Act provides that no court shall initiate any proceedings for contempt, either on its own motion or otherwise, after the expiry of a period of one year from the date on which the contempt is alleged to have been committed. The Supreme Court held in Pallav Sheth v. Custodian in 2001 that Section 20 applies also where the court proceeds under its constitutional power in Articles 129 and 215, so the one year bar governs contempt proceedings generally and not only proceedings framed under the Act. The question of when the period begins, including by reference to the date on which the applicant acquired knowledge of the contempt, is addressed in that judgment.

### Q: Should scandalising the court remain an offence?

The question is contested and this article records both cases without adopting either. Abolitionists argue that the offence belongs to an era of judicial remoteness, that its defining terms are undefined and subjective, that the court sits as complainant and judge, and that England and Wales abolished the offence in 2013 without loss of respect for courts. Retentionists argue that the judiciary cannot answer public attacks, that unfounded imputations of corruption erode the confidence on which compliance with judgments depends, and that the defences and the Section 13 threshold confine the offence. The Law Commission, in Report No. 274 of 2018, recommended retention, reasoning that deletion would not remove the constitutional power and would remove the statutory safeguards.

### Q: Does an apology end contempt proceedings?

An accepted apology ordinarily ends them. The proviso to Section 12(1) empowers the court to discharge the accused, or to remit the punishment awarded, on apology being made to the satisfaction of the court, and the Explanation to the subsection provides that an apology shall not be rejected merely on the ground that it is qualified or conditional, if the accused makes it bona fide. The court assesses genuineness and timing: an apology tendered at the first opportunity carries more weight than one offered after the court has indicated its conclusion, and an apology that maintains the conduct was justified is not an acknowledgment of wrongdoing. Where the court is not satisfied, the proceeding continues to a finding and sentence.

### Q: Can a contempt order be appealed?

Yes. Section 19 of the Act grants an appeal as of right from an order or decision of a High Court in the exercise of its jurisdiction to punish for contempt. Where the order was made by a single judge, the appeal lies to a bench of not less than two judges of that court; where the order was made by a bench, the appeal lies to the Supreme Court, and the section provides further appeals to the Supreme Court in the cases it specifies. The appellate court may suspend execution of the punishment or order appealed against and may order release on bail. Appeals must be filed within thirty days where the appeal is to a bench of the same court and within sixty days where it is to the Supreme Court.

### Q: Does the Contempt of Courts Act apply to judges themselves?

Yes, in the terms Section 16 lays down. It provides that a judge, magistrate or other person acting judicially shall be liable for contempt of his own court or of any other court in the same manner as any other person, subject to the protection the section gives for acts and statements within its specified terms, including statements made in good faith in the situations it covers. A judicial officer who wilfully disobeys the order of a superior court is therefore not placed outside the Act by office, and proceedings against judicial officers, though rare, rest on this provision read with Section 10. The provision's presence in the Act answers the objection that contempt is a jurisdiction for the protection of judges as a class.

### Q: What happens when contempt is committed in the face of the court?

Section 14 of the Act provides a summary procedure for contempt committed in the presence or hearing of the Supreme Court or a High Court. The court may cause the person to be detained in custody, shall inform him of the charge, afford him an opportunity to make his defence, take such evidence as may be necessary and hear argument, and may then determine the matter and pass sentence, with the section directing the sequence to be completed on the same day where that is practicable and permitting the person to request that the charge be tried by a judge other than the judge or judges in whose presence or hearing the offence was committed, a request the section requires to be considered in the terms it states.

### Q: Can a High Court punish contempt of a subordinate court?

Yes. Section 10 of the Act gives every High Court the same jurisdiction, powers and authority to punish contempt of courts subordinate to it as it has in relation to contempt of itself, subject to the proviso that no High Court shall take cognizance of a contempt alleged to have been committed in respect of a court subordinate to it where the contempt is an offence punishable under the Indian Penal Code. Section 11 adds that a High Court may inquire into or try a contempt of itself or of a subordinate court committed outside its local limits by a person within or outside those limits. Section 15(2) supplies the route: a reference by the subordinate court, or a motion by the Advocate General or the specified law officer.

### Q: Is a fair and accurate report of court proceedings contempt?

No. Section 4 of the Act provides that a person shall not be guilty of contempt for publishing a fair and accurate report of a judicial proceeding, or any stage of it, whether published contemporaneously with the proceeding or subsequently, subject to the provisions of any law for the time being in force and to the terms of the section itself. The protection covers reports of proceedings held in public; it does not protect a report that is inaccurate, that adds prejudicial comment presented as part of the proceeding, or that publishes matter a court has prohibited from publication. The defence is the statutory foundation of court reporting, and it is the fairness and accuracy of the report, not its sympathy with the outcome, that the section examines.

### Q: When is publishing about a pending case not contempt?

The Act gives two main protections. Under Section 3, a person who publishes matter in connection with a pending proceeding is not guilty of contempt if, at the time of publication, there were no reasonable grounds for believing that the proceeding was pending; a distributor of a publication is likewise protected where there were no reasonable grounds for believing the publication contained contemptuous matter. A proceeding is pending, under the Explanation to Section 2, broadly from the presentation of the plaint or the issue of process or taking of cognizance, as the case may be, until disposal. Beyond Section 3, a fair and accurate report of the proceeding itself is protected by Section 4. Publication that prejudices or tends to interfere with a pending proceeding, made with knowledge that it is pending, falls within Section 2(c)(ii).

### Q: What is the substantial interference threshold in Section 13?

Section 13(a) of the Act, in the form substituted by the amendment of 2006, provides that no court shall impose a sentence in respect of a contempt of court unless it is satisfied that the contempt is of such a nature that it substantially interferes, or tends substantially to interfere, with the due course of justice. The clause operates as a condition on sentence rather than on the definition: conduct may fall within Section 2 and still be unpunishable because its effect on the course of justice is trivial, technical or marginal. It is the statute's proportionality control, and a court sentencing for contempt in a case involving speech or publication is expected to address it expressly rather than treat a finding on the definition as sufficient for punishment.

### Q: How does civil contempt enforce a court order?

A party who has obtained an order files a contempt petition in the court that made it, identifying the order, proving that the other party knew of it, and showing wilful disobedience or wilful breach of an undertaking. The court hears the alleged contemnor, applies the standard of proof beyond reasonable doubt, and if contempt is established may proceed under Section 12, but the characteristic outcome is compliance rather than imprisonment: further directions for implementation, time fixed for performance, and sentences imposed or held in reserve against continued default. The mechanism converts a paper direction into a performed act, which is why civil contempt is described as the enforcement engine of the civil courts and attracts little of the controversy that surrounds the criminal limb.

### Q: What did the 2006 amendment change in contempt law?

The Contempt of Courts (Amendment) Act 2006, Act 6 of 2006, made one change of substance: it substituted Section 13 of the 1971 Act. The substituted section has two clauses. Clause (a) restated the requirement that no sentence be imposed unless the court is satisfied that the contempt substantially interferes, or tends substantially to interfere, with the due course of justice. Clause (b) was new: it empowered the court to permit justification by truth as a valid defence in a contempt proceeding where the court is satisfied that the defence is in the public interest and the request for invoking it is bona fide. No provision of the earlier Act had allowed truth as a defence, so a true statement imputing improper motive to a court was previously answerable as contempt without inquiry into its truth.

### Q: How does contempt law fit with freedom of speech under Article 19?

Article 19(1)(a) guarantees freedom of speech and expression, and Article 19(2) permits reasonable restrictions in the interests of contempt of court, a ground that has appeared in the clause since the First Amendment of 1951. Contempt is therefore not an exception carved out of the free speech guarantee by implication; it is one of the restrictions the constitutional text contemplates. The discipline runs both ways: a contempt law or action that travels beyond the ground, or that is unreasonable as applied to fair criticism, is open to challenge under Article 19. The statutory defences for fair and accurate reporting in Section 4 and fair criticism in Section 5, and the substantial interference threshold in Section 13, are the points at which the Act internalises that discipline.

### Q: What did the Law Commission recommend about scandalising the court?

The Law Commission of India, in Report No. 274 of 2018, titled Review of the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971 (Limited to Section 2 of the Act), examined whether the definition of criminal contempt should be amended to remove the scandalising limb and recommended that no amendment was necessary. Its central reasoning was constitutional: Articles 129 and 215 vest the Supreme Court and the High Courts with the power to punish for contempt independently of the Act, so deletion would not remove the power, while it would remove the statutory definitions, defences, punishment ceiling and limitation period that discipline its exercise. The Commission also noted the volume of pending contempt cases in India, in contrast to the disuse of the offence in England before abolition there, and the risk that courts would develop divergent definitions without a statutory text.
