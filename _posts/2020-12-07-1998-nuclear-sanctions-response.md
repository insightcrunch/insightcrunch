The 1998 nuclear sanctions response began within hours of the second round of detonations at Pokhran on 13 May 1998, when the government of Atal Bihari Vajpayee learned the precise price that American law attached to the tests and chose, in public and on the record, to pay a large part of it rather than bargain away the decision that had caused it. What followed over thirty months was not an improvisation. It was a constructed response with four load-bearing features: a finance ministry and a central bank positioned under two named officeholders, Yashwant Sinha and Bimal Jalan, and coordinated as a single command; a bond sold to the Indian diaspora, the Resurgent India Bonds of August 1998, which raised 4.23 billion US dollars in nineteen days; a monetary stabilisation programme that defended the rupee through the Asian crisis's worst quarter and then eased by design; and a diplomatic and financial trajectory, running through the Jaswant Singh and Strobe Talbott dialogue and the India Millennium Deposits of October 2000, that converted a punishment regime into a schedule of waivers. This article defends one claim about that record: the post-1998 sanctions response operates as political-economic architecture, and the four-feature framework captures what the rival readings, the sanctions-response reading and the technical economic policy reading, each leave out.

## The Price Tag Arrives: The Glenn Amendment and the Coalition of May 1998

The sanctions were not negotiated, threatened in advance for bargaining effect, or tailored to Indian circumstances. They were automatic. Section 102(b) of the Arms Export Control Act, inserted by the Nuclear Proliferation Prevention Act of 1994 and named for its sponsor, Senator John Glenn of Ohio, required the President of the United States to impose a defined schedule of penalties on any non-nuclear-weapon state, as the Non-Proliferation Treaty defines that term, that detonated a nuclear device. President Bill Clinton signed the determination on 13 May 1998, the day the last two Pokhran devices fired, and the machinery engaged the same day. The penalties terminated American economic assistance except humanitarian relief, ended military sales and financing, barred American government credits and credit guarantees to India through the Export-Import Bank and the Overseas Private Investment Corporation, prohibited American commercial banks from lending to the Indian government except for food and agricultural commodities, required Washington to oppose loans to India in the international financial institutions except for basic human needs, and banned the export of defined categories of dual-use technology. The tests themselves, their device physics, their yield quarrels and their domestic political theatre, are the subject of [the Vajpayee-era architectural-political-moment framework](/2020/10/13/1998-pokhran-ii-tests-reanalysed/) in the Pokhran II article; the subject here begins where that account ends, at the moment the bill arrived.

The multilateral layer assembled within weeks. The Group of Eight, meeting at Birmingham from 15 to 17 May 1998 under the British presidency, condemned the tests in its communiqué and called on India to join the test ban treaty and the non-proliferation treaty without conditions. The United Nations Security Council adopted Resolution 1172 on 6 June 1998, unanimously, demanding that India and Pakistan halt their weapons programmes, sign both treaties as non-nuclear-weapon states, stop fissile material production and refrain from weapon deployment. New Delhi rejected the resolution's premises in a formal statement, on the ground that the Council had assigned rights and obligations by a treaty India had never signed, and the rejection held: no Indian government statement of the period conceded any element of the resolution's demands. Japan, India's largest bilateral donor, froze new yen loan commitments and grant aid outside emergency and grassroots categories, a step Japanese officials quantified in Diet answers as affecting the pipeline of official development assistance loans on which Indian power and transport projects drew. Germany and other European donors reviewed their programmes. Australia suspended defence relations and non-humanitarian aid. In November 1998, the United States Department of Commerce published its Entity List additions, naming Indian atomic energy units, space centres, defence laboratories and public sector firms whose access to American technology required licences that policy presumed denied.

The financial markets priced the punishment before the politicians had finished denouncing or applauding it. Moody's Investors Service cut India's foreign currency sovereign rating from Baa3 to Ba2 on 24 June 1998, below investment grade, and Standard and Poor's placed its BBB minus rating under negative scrutiny. The Bombay Stock Exchange Sensex, already shaken by the Asian crisis and by coalition friction with the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam, slid through June. The rupee, which had crossed 40 to the dollar in mid-May as the Reserve Bank of India records the sequence, pressed toward 42 by the middle of the year. Yet the punishment had a structural weakness that Indian officials identified in their first internal assessments and that the later scholarship confirmed: India in 1998 was not an aid-dependent economy. American bilateral assistance was small, trade with the United States was a modest share of Indian commerce, and the government's borrowing was overwhelmingly domestic. The sanctions could raise the cost of capital, delay multilateral loans and complicate technology access. They could not, by themselves, stop the Indian state from functioning, and everyone in the finance ministry on Raisina Hill understood that the real contest would be fought on confidence: on whether depositors, portfolio investors and the diaspora believed the government could hold the external account together through the storm.

### What did the Glenn Amendment sanctions actually prohibit?

The Glenn Amendment package, imposed by President Clinton's determination of 13 May 1998, cut American aid except humanitarian relief, stopped military sales and financing, barred Export-Import Bank and Overseas Private Investment Corporation credits, restricted American bank lending to the Indian government, required opposition to non-human-needs lending at the World Bank, and banned defined dual-use technology exports.

The domestic political framing was set in the first fortnight. Vajpayee's statement to Parliament on 27 May 1998 defended the tests as a considered act of national security and refused the language of apology, and the parliamentary debate that followed showed the government's opponents attacking the theatre and the timing more than the external account. The Congress party, which had itself preserved the nuclear option through the Rao years, split its fire between the government's secrecy and its economic management. The Left parties opposed both the tests and the sanctions, a combination that let the government present endurance as the patriotic default. The coalition arithmetic that underlay every one of these exchanges, a government formed on 19 March 1998 from parties with incompatible programmes, had been mapped in [the post-1998 NDA institutional-political context](/2019/06/11/1998-nda-formation-vajpayee-era/); the sanctions crisis was the first external shock that arithmetic had to absorb, and it absorbed the shock by converting the punishment into proof of the resolve the coalition had been assembled to display.

## The Refusal in Parliament: Sinha's Pledge and the Budget Debate of June 1998

Before the architecture had instruments, it had a pledge, and the pledge was made where pledges bind in a parliamentary system, on the floor of the Lok Sabha. Through the budget debate of June 1998, opposition speakers pressed the finance minister on the obvious question the sanctions posed: whether the government would be driven, as the Chandra Shekhar and Rao governments had been driven in 1991, to the International Monetary Fund and to the multilateral lenders' conditions, cap in hand, within the year. Sinha's answer, repeated across the debate and recorded as the ministry's settled position in his memoir, was that India would not seek Fund assistance, would finance its deficit and its external gap from its own instruments, and would treat the sanctions as a cost to be carried rather than a verdict to be appealed. The pledge had an operational consequence that shaped every later feature: it foreclosed the cheapest financing first, in public, so that the expensive financing actually chosen, the diaspora premium, the rate defence, the oil pool's absorption, could be presented as the execution of a promise rather than the confession of a failure. Governments that drift into diaspora bonds look desperate; a government that has sworn off the Fund in Parliament and then sells the bonds looks deliberate, and the difference between desperation and deliberation was worth measurable basis points in the very market whose judgement the government affected to ignore.

The pledge also disciplined the coalition. The National Democratic Alliance's partners, several of whom had campaigned against the economic policies of the Congress decade and some of whom had opposed the tests' timing in private, were handed a formula they could all repeat: no Fund, no rollback, no apology, a trinity in which each partner could find its own emphasis. The formula survived the government's near-death experiences of the autumn of 1998, when the AIADMK's ultimatums over the Cauvery and the Tamil Nadu administration consumed the Prime Minister's weeks; it survived the winter session's economic debates; and it survived, most remarkably, the government's fall and resurrection across the Kargil summer, returning with the enlarged majority of October 1999 as something close to a ratified national position. When Sinha greeted the final waiver in September 2001 with his sentence about sanctions having no meaning, he was quoting, in compressed form, a pledge first issued in the budget debate of June 1998 and kept, at a price his own ledgers record, for three years. Feature One's institutional positioning is incomplete without this element: the two officeholders mattered because of what their institutions could do, but the pledge mattered because it determined, in advance and in public, what those institutions would be forbidden to do, and a forbidden option is as much a part of an architecture as an open door.

## The Sanctioners' Map: Who Closed What, Channel by Channel

A precise map of the punishment is the necessary baseline, because the response's designers worked channel by channel, and because later argument about whether sanctions hurt depends entirely on which channel one examines. The American bilateral channel closed almost completely and mattered least in money terms: development assistance, never large for India, terminated outside humanitarian categories; military sales and financing stopped, freezing a defence relationship that had only begun to thaw after the Cold War; the Export-Import Bank and the Overseas Private Investment Corporation withdrew credit and insurance cover from Indian projects, which raised the cost of American capital goods rather than preventing their purchase through other suppliers. The American commercial channel closed selectively: banks could not lend to the Indian government, with food and agricultural commodities excepted, but private trade finance for Indian companies continued through a market that priced risk rather than obeying flags. The multilateral channel was the serious one. Washington's instruction to its executive directors at the World Bank and the Asian Development Bank was to oppose loans other than those meeting basic human needs, and the effect was delay more than denial: board calendars slipped, appraisals slowed, and several Indian operations were postponed by quarters while the classification argument ran. Some lending, classed as basic needs or as state-level reform support, proceeded through the same months, which is why the Indian finance ministry's internal reckoning treated the multilateral channel as a tax on time rather than a wall.

The Japanese channel cut differently. Japan's freeze on new yen loan commitments struck the project pipeline of the Indian state itself, power stations, rail and road corridors, water systems, where Japanese concessional loans had become a standard financing layer, and it struck India's largest bilateral relationship, which gave the measure a diplomatic weight beyond its yen value. Japanese grant aid outside emergency and grassroots categories stopped with it. The technology channel, administered through the Entity List additions of November 1998 and the dual-use export bans, struck the strategic enclaves directly: atomic energy units, the Indira Gandhi Centre for Atomic Research, space centres, defence laboratories and their supplier firms faced licence presumptions of denial for American-origin equipment and components, and the enclaves responded with accelerated indigenisation programmes whose emblem that year was the Centre for Development of Advanced Computing's PARAM 10000 supercomputer, dedicated to the nation in 1998 after a decade in which denied American machines had made parallel computing a sovereignty project. The defence channel pushed procurement toward Russia, which sold without political conditions and accepted rupee-linked arrangements of older vintage, a re-sourcing whose consequences for India's inventory ran for two decades.

The multilateral channel's definitional battle produced the map's most instructive single entry. The American instruction opposed lending other than for basic human needs, a category the World Bank's board had to construe loan by loan, and the construal was tested almost immediately: on 30 June 1998, six weeks after the Glenn determination, the Bank approved an economic restructuring loan of more than half a billion dollars for Andhra Pradesh, a state-level adjustment operation whose poverty and social sector framing carried it through the basic needs gate over the sanctions' spirit if not their letter. Indian officials read the approval as the multilateral system's own verdict on the punishment's workability, and cited it accordingly; American officials read it as the exception the category existed to permit. Both readings could cite the same board minute, which is why the episode belongs in any honest map of the sanctions: the regime's tightest channel leaked at its first serious test, through a definition, and the leak was administered not by the sanctioned government but by the sanctioning system's own institution, weighing a poor state's school and health budgets against a non-proliferation instruction from its largest shareholder.

Against this map, the sanctions' aggregate macroeconomic weight was modest and their sectoral weight was sharp, and both halves of that sentence structured the politics. The finance ministry could truthfully tell Parliament that the punishment touched a small fraction of India's external finance, and the opposition could truthfully answer that the fraction it touched, official credit, technology licences, the ratings floor, was precisely the fraction that compounded. The International Monetary Fund's consultation record for 1998 supplies the neutral ledger: portfolio flows had turned negative before the tests on Asian contagion alone, exports were contracting on East Asian demand, spreads on Indian corporate borrowing abroad had widened beyond the point of issuance, and the current account deficit stayed contained near one and a half percent of gross domestic product. A country running a small external deficit, borrowing mostly at home, holding six months of import cover and receiving large remittances through channels no sanction reached was not a country that economic punishment could quickly bend. The designers of the response knew the map in this detail, and the four features that follow are best read as answers addressed to its specific closures: the multilateral delay answered by diaspora money, the ratings floor answered by a subscription that priced patriotism instead of credit, the technology denial answered by enclave indigenisation budgets, and the diplomatic isolation answered by a dialogue that made the sanctioner negotiate with the sanctioned.

## Feature One: Two Men, Two Institutions, One Command

The first feature of the response was positional, and it predated the tests. When Vajpayee formed his government on 19 March 1998, the finance portfolio went to Yashwant Sinha, a former finance minister of the Chandra Shekhar government of 1990 to 1991 who had presented the crisis budget of that earlier emergency and who carried, into the new office, the standing of a man who had already managed one external payments scare. At the Reserve Bank of India in Mumbai, Bimal Jalan had taken office as governor on 22 November 1997, in the middle of the Asian financial crisis, succeeding C. Rangarajan. The pairing mattered because the sanctions response would be executed through exactly two instruments, the Union budget and the central bank's balance sheet, and both instruments were in the hands of officeholders whose authority inside their institutions was settled before the crisis began. There was no apprenticeship during the emergency. Sinha's memoir, Confessions of a Swadeshi Reformer (2007), reconstructs the coordination from the finance minister's chair: regular consultation between North Block and the Reserve Bank, an agreed division in which the Bank handled the exchange rate and liquidity while the ministry handled the fiscal signal and the political narrative, and a shared refusal, recorded in the memoir as a settled position rather than a slogan, to treat the sanctions as a reason to reopen the nuclear decision.

Jalan's contribution began before Pokhran. In January 1998, with the Asian crisis driving speculators against the rupee, the Reserve Bank under his governorship had executed the period's most aggressive defence: the Bank Rate raised from 9 to 11 percent, the cash reserve ratio raised in two phases to 10.5 percent, repo rates driven from 4.5 to 9 percent, and the interest surcharge on import finance doubled from 15 to 30 percent. The measures, set out in the Reserve Bank's Annual Report for 1997-98, broke the speculative attack and taught the market that the Bank would accept domestic pain to defend the currency. Some of the tightening was rolled back in March 1998 as inflows returned. The significance for May 1998 was institutional memory: when the sanctions hit, the central bank did not have to design a defence from first principles, because it had rehearsed one four months earlier, and the finance ministry did not have to guess at the governor's reactions, because it had watched them. The sanctions-response reading, which starts the story on 13 May, misses this inheritance; the response's speed in August is unintelligible without January.

The fiscal leg of the positioning was Sinha's budget of 1 June 1998, presented nineteen days after the sanctions determination. The budget raised defence outlays in the shadow of the tests, set a disinvestment target of 5,000 crore rupees, held the fiscal deficit target at 5.6 percent of gross domestic product, and wrapped the programme in the vocabulary of swadeshi, which Sinha, in the budget speech and in his later memoir, defined as competitiveness and self-reliance rather than autarky: the doctrine that Indian industry should be made strong enough to need no protection from the world it traded with. The rhetorical move was the political core of Feature One. A finance minister who had merely announced austerity under foreign punishment would have owned the punishment; Sinha instead announced a national project to which the sanctions were, by construction, irrelevant, and he carried the argument into the teeth of the ratings downgrade that followed three weeks later. The government's critics replied, in Parliament and in the press, that vocabulary was not a balance of payments. The reply had force: the deficit target would be missed, and the external account still had to be financed in hard currency. Financing it was the work of the second feature.

## The Ministry in Session: North Block's Working Machinery

Architecture is operated by people on a calendar, and the calendar of North Block in the sanctions summer repays reconstruction. The finance ministry Sinha inherited was staffed at its senior levels by officials whose formation matched his own: Shankar Acharya, chief economic adviser from 1993 to 2001, the ministry's in-house economist through both the reform decade and the sanctions; the department of economic affairs, which ran the external account's daily arithmetic of reserves, debt falling due and multilateral board schedules; and the Reserve Bank liaison that Jalan's governorship converted from correspondence into consultation. The working method, on the testimony of the participants' later accounts, was a division of anxieties. The ministry watched the political variables, coalition arithmetic, parliamentary temperature, the price of petrol and diesel at the pump, which the government declined through the whole sanctions period to raise to full import parity, absorbing the difference in the oil pool account rather than in the voter's pocket. The Bank watched the market variables, the forward premia, the call money rate, the size and direction of portfolio flows. The two lists met in the persons of the minister and the governor, and the absence of any public disagreement between North Block and Mint Street across thirty months of pressure was itself an instrument: markets that hunt for daylight between a finance ministry and its central bank found none to hunt in.

The pump-price decision illustrates the machinery's political character better than any rate decision. Administered petroleum pricing meant that the oil shocks of 1999 and 2000, in which crude prices roughly tripled from their February 1999 trough, landed first on the oil pool account and the budget rather than on retail prices, and the government rationed the pass-through in negotiated instalments, each announced with its fiscal apology attached. A technical manager, optimising the external account alone, would have floated prices early and let the inflation print fall where it fell. The Sinha ministry instead treated price stability at the pump as part of the sanctions settlement with its own citizens: the state that asked the diaspora to subscribe at a premium and the voter to endure a downgraded flag could not also present the householder with a sanctions surcharge at the petrol station. The oil pool deficit that accumulated in consequence was a real liability, carried into the following decade's budgets, and the opposition's economists entered it in their ledger with justice. But its political logic was the response's logic in miniature, to move costs from the visible present, where they would compound politically, to the financed future, where they could be serviced by growth. The diaspora bonds did the same operation in dollars that the oil pool did in rupees, and recognising the rhyme between the two instruments is recognising the architecture.

## The Inheritance of 1991: Why the Fund Was the Line Not to Cross

Every senior participant in the 1998 response carried a formative memory from seven years earlier, and the response cannot be understood without it. In the balance of payments crisis of 1990 to 1991, India had run its reserves down to a few weeks of imports, air-freighted gold abroad as collateral in two operations that seared themselves into the official imagination, twenty tonnes sold through the State Bank in May 1991 and 46.91 tonnes pledged by the Reserve Bank to the Bank of England in July, and accepted an International Monetary Fund programme whose conditions reached into the budget, the trade regime and industrial licensing. The political trauma of that summer was not the adjustment, much of which the Rao government converted into the reform programme of 1991. It was the visibility of dependence: the gold aircraft, the Fund's missions, the sense, recorded across the memoirs of the period's officials, that the Indian state had been weighed and found light. Sinha had been finance minister of the Chandra Shekhar government during the crisis's first act and had presented the interim budget of a government negotiating with the Fund; Jalan had served the finance ministry at senior levels through the same years. The two men who would manage 1998 had therefore both stood inside a room where India's external weakness had dictated terms, and their shared determination, legible in every instrument they chose, was that no Indian government would be photographed pledging gold again.

The determination explains choices that pure technique cannot. A bridge loan from the Fund or the Bank in the summer of 1998 was arithmetically available, India had drawn nothing and its credit stood, but it would have arrived with a mission, a review calendar and a public association between the nuclear decision and external tutelage that the government refused at any coupon. The diaspora bond cost more than Fund money and was chosen anyway, because its conditionality was a family appeal rather than a staff report. The reserves doctrine of the following years, accumulation far beyond the textbook import-cover norms, was the same refusal generalised into policy: the stock itself was the guarantee that the Fund conversation of 1991 would never need to be reopened. Critics of the response, then and later, treated this refusal as expensive vanity, and their arithmetic on any single instrument was often right. The designers' answer, in Sinha's memoir and in Jalan's essays alike, was that the alternative's price was not denominated in basis points but in the negotiating freedom of the Indian state, and that 1991 had taught them the denomination by heart. The sanctions-response reading, which begins its story with the American determination of 13 May 1998, cannot see this motive, because the motive's date is 1991. The political-economic reading can, because it treats the response as a structure built by men whose blueprint was a memory.

## The Rupee Front: Reserves, Rates and the August Squeeze

The external account Sinha and Jalan had to defend was strained but not broken, and the figures, stated with their sources, define the room the response had to work in. The International Monetary Fund's Public Information Notice of 22 September 1998, summarising the Article IV consultation of that year, recorded gross official reserves of 29.4 billion US dollars at the end of March 1998 and about 26.5 billion by the middle of August, equal to roughly six months of imports and more than twice the debt falling due in 1998-99. The same notice recorded that the rupee had depreciated by 17 percent against the dollar between the onset of the Asian crisis in July 1997 and late August 1998. Portfolio investment had turned negative in the second half of 1997-98, exports were falling in dollar terms as East Asian demand contracted, and the spreads on India's corporate borrowing abroad had widened to levels that discouraged the commercial borrowing the sanctions had not even needed to ban. The sanctions, in other words, landed on an economy already managing a contagion; their specific contribution was to close the official channels, American bilateral credit, multilateral lending speed and technology licences, at the moment the private channels were narrowing on their own.

The Reserve Bank's answer in the sanctions' first quarter combined presence with patience. It sold dollars into the market to curb volatility, in the pattern its Annual Report describes for the period, while letting the exchange rate find a lower level rather than defending a number: the rupee crossed 42 in the middle of 1998 and settled around 42.5 by December, on the Bank's record of the movement. The decisive tightening came in August, timed with the bond issue described below. Effective from the fortnight beginning 29 August 1998, the cash reserve ratio was raised from 10 to 11 percent of net demand and time liabilities, absorbing liquidity from the banking system, and the fixed repo rate was raised by three percentage points, from 5 to 8 percent. The Reserve Bank's chronology of monetary measures for 1998-99 lists both steps in a single entry, and their joint purpose was the one Jalan's essays on the period describe as the governor's doctrine: to make speculation against the rupee expensive at exactly the moment a large foreign currency inflow was arriving, so that the inflow would register as strength rather than as an invitation to bet on reversal. The August squeeze was thus not a panic measure bolted onto the bond. It was the bond's other half, designed in the same weeks and announced into the same market. The autumn that followed tested the combined position against a world market in disorder: the Russian default of August 1998 was followed within weeks by the near-failure of Long-Term Capital Management in September and a global seizure in credit markets that punished every emerging economy's currency and paper indiscriminately. India's insulation in that quarter was the sanctions summer's unintended gift. Having been shut out of the commercial borrowing channels by politics, Indian borrowers carried little of the short-dated foreign debt whose rollover failure destroyed stronger-rated economies that season, and the Fund's September notice had already recorded the structural fact: reserves at mid-August equalled more than twice the debt maturing in 1998-99, and the short-term share of India's external debt was a small fraction of the totals that made Thailand and Korea hostages to their creditors' calendars. Jalan's essays return to this proportion as the true variable of external safety, the ratio of reserves to everything that can be demanded within a year, and the response's monetary leg is best understood as the deliberate widening of that ratio: reserves rebuilt from 26.5 billion dollars at the crisis's low point to about 32.5 billion by March 1999, against a debt service schedule that the diaspora inflows had lengthened by five years at a stroke. The August tightening, read in isolation, looks like the most expensive paragraph of the response, a deliberate domestic squeeze imposed by a central bank on a slowing economy. Read against the ratio it defended, it was the premium on the same insurance the diaspora bond bought in another currency.

The fiscal and monetary legs also had to survive the coalition. Through the second half of 1998, the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam's demands on the government, over the Cauvery dispute and the dismissal of the Tamil Nadu government, consumed the Prime Minister's political capital and periodically threatened the majority, and every such tremor moved the bond and currency markets that Sinha and Jalan were managing. The response's architecture had to be robust to the government's own fragility, which is one reason its designers preferred instruments that, once launched, did not require repeated parliamentary or coalition approval: a central bank's reserve ratio, a state bank's bond, a finance ministry's administrative pricing. The sanctions crisis therefore selected for exactly the institutional instruments that a weak coalition could operate, and the selection is itself part of the political-economic reading this article defends: the response's form was dictated by the domestic distribution of authority as much as by the external pressure it answered.

### Why did the government choose a bond sold only to non-resident Indians?

The diaspora bond solved three problems at once. It raised foreign currency outside the sanctioned official channels, since a State Bank of India issue to individual savers engaged no American government credit and no multilateral vote. It priced Indian risk through patriotic savings rather than the downgraded sovereign market. And it required no legislation and no coalition vote.

## Feature Two: The Resurgent India Bonds and the Price of Diaspora Money

The Resurgent India Bonds were announced as the centrepiece of the government's external financing for 1998-99 and floated by the State Bank of India on 5 August 1998, with the approval of the government and the Reserve Bank, as the Bank's press record of the scheme states. The design was precise. The bonds were five-year instruments denominated in three currencies, US dollars, pounds sterling and Deutsche marks, sold exclusively to non-resident Indians and overseas corporate bodies owned by them, transferable among that class of holders, and giftable to resident relatives, a provision that converted family obligation into a distribution channel reaching kitchens in Kerala and Punjab as directly as bank branches in Dubai and New Jersey. The prospectus coupons were 7.75 percent in dollars, 8 percent in sterling and 6.25 percent in Deutsche marks, in each case a substantial premium over the sovereign's sanctioned market access: the International Monetary Fund's September 1998 notice recorded the dollar issue at a spread of about 215 basis points over LIBOR, and the closing-day reporting in Euromoney put the dollar coupon at 225 basis points over United States Treasury yields. The exchange risk on redemption was carried inside the Indian official family, under a maintenance-of-value arrangement whose apportionment between the government and the State Bank the Reserve Bank's redemption press release of 1 October 2003 describes in detail; the diaspora subscriber, by design, bore none of it.

The subscription window ran from 5 August to 24 August 1998, nineteen days that coincided with the Russian default of 17 August and the near-collapse of emerging-market sentiment worldwide. The bonds closed with 4.23 billion US dollars raised, from more than 74,000 subscribers in more than thirty countries, on the closing figures reported by Euromoney and carried, rounded to 4.2 billion, in the Reserve Bank's own later presentations on external account management. Foreign and private banks did much of the collecting: a former State Bank official who had organised the issue recalled to Euromoney in 2000 that around 3 billion of the 4.23 billion had been gathered by foreign banks, several of which lent subscribers the purchase price against the bonds themselves, and Citibank alone accounted for 822 million dollars of sales. Some early summaries of the issue carried a lower figure of about 4.16 billion dollars, the estimate in circulation while subscriptions were still being tallied across time zones; the documented closing record, from the issue's own closing-day coverage and the Reserve Bank's retrospective figure, is 4.23 billion dollars raised over a subscription window of nineteen days, from 5 to 24 August 1998. The instrument's own precedent mattered to its credibility. In the balance of payments crisis of 1991, the State Bank had floated the India Development Bonds, which raised 1.6 billion dollars from the same diaspora on the same principle, that Indians abroad would lend to the Indian state when markets would not; the 1991 precedent, set inside the crisis management examined in [the reform-as-politics account of the Rao government](/2019/05/26/1991-rao-government-silent-reforms/), gave the 1998 issue a track record to invoke and a subscriber register to reopen.

The issuing institution carried its own memory into the design. The State Bank of India in 1998 was chaired by M. S. Verma, who held the bank's chairmanship from 1997 to 2000, and the bank had run the India Development Bonds operation of 1991 from the same overseas network, under the same statutory relationship with the Reserve Bank, that the Resurgent issue would use. Institutional continuity of this kind is invisible in aggregate accounts of the sanctions summer, which treat the bond as a finance ministry idea executed by an anonymous market. It was closer to a repertoire piece performed by an ensemble that had rehearsed it: the prospectus mechanics, the collecting bank agreements, the arrangements for the Reserve Bank to take the proceeds and carry the exchange risk, the missions' role in the Gulf and North America, all had a 1991 draft in the State Bank's files, and the speed of the 1998 execution, a prospectus in the market eleven weeks after the sanctions determination, in the middle of a monsoon parliament session and a currency defence, is unintelligible without the older draft. The point carries the article's argument into the banking system: India's sanctions response was architectural partly because its bricks were already fired. A state that has once financed itself through its diaspora owns an instrument that later crises can invoke by name, and the names accumulate their own credibility, Development Bonds in 1991, Resurgent Bonds in 1998, Millennium Deposits in 2000, a lineage that functioned for subscribers as a single sovereign promise renewed across decades and governments of different parties.

The bond must also be read as a political text, because it was designed to be one. Its name was a claim: resurgent, the adjective of a government that had announced the tests as national assertion, then attached to a savings product. Pravasi savers were not merely buying yield; they were invited, in the marketing the State Bank and the finance ministry conducted through Indian missions and diaspora organisations, to underwrite the state's refusal to be punished, and the subscription pattern, heavily weighted to the Gulf and North America, mapped the geography of the constituency that the diaspora article in this series treats as [the diaspora-as-foreign-policy-and-domestic-political-constituency framework](/2020/11/04/diaspora-foreign-policy-constituency/) describes. The government's domestic opponents saw the same text and read it in reverse: the Left parties and Congress speakers charged in Parliament that a premium coupon paid to foreign-resident savers, with the exchange risk socialised at home, was an expensive confession that ordinary market access had failed, and that the taxpayer would settle the bill in 2003. Both readings were available because the instrument was genuinely both things: a financing device that worked, and a plebiscite that the government won. The sanctions-response reading records only the financing. The political-economic reading records the plebiscite, and notes that the government designed for it deliberately, down to the gift provision that let a subscriber in Toronto enrol a parent in Ludhiana as a beneficiary of the state's defiance.

The issue's machinery deserves the same precision as its price, because the machinery was the message's delivery system. The State Bank sold through its own overseas branch network and through appointed collecting banks across the Gulf, North America, Britain, continental Europe and Southeast Asia, the regions where the post-1970s emigration had deposited its savings, and Indian diplomatic missions lent the campaign their premises and their convening power without formally marketing a security. Subscriptions were denominated to be reachable by salaried savers rather than only by the wealthy, the five-year tenor was set against the maturity profile of the external debt the state wished to avoid refinancing under pressure, and the transfer and gift provisions were drafted so that a bond bought in Dubai could circulate inside an extended family's Indian branch, binding the resident household into the subscriber's act. The documented window contains a further fact the designers cited with satisfaction: the subscription held through the Russian default of 17 August 1998 and the flight from emerging markets that followed, a week in which a purely commercial Indian borrower would have pulled its issue or paid crisis spreads, while the diaspora book continued to fill. Euromoney's closing report recorded the bankers' astonishment precisely on this point. The instrument had been priced for a community, and the community behaved as one, which is the sense in which the bond was a political instrument before it was a financial one: its order book was a roll of citizens of the exchequer, and the government read it aloud as such in the parliamentary defence of the issue that autumn.

The redemption's design completed the political circuit. By carrying the exchange risk inside the official family, through the maintenance of value account shared between the government and the Reserve Bank, the state told the subscriber that the promise was sovereign and indivisible: the rupee's movement over five years would be the state's problem, on the state's books, and the 2003 settlement described in the closing section of this article executed that promise to the crore. The arrangement had a fiscal cost that materialised exactly as the opposition predicted, and the designers' willingness to pre-commit it in 1998 was the clearest available signal that the bond's purpose included demonstration. A treasury minimising cost would have shifted the currency risk to the subscriber and accepted a smaller book. A government demonstrating creditworthiness to the market that had downgraded it chose the opposite allocation, and the size of the book it thereby obtained became the demonstration.

### What were the Resurgent India Bonds and how much did they raise?

The Resurgent India Bonds were five-year foreign currency bonds floated by the State Bank of India on 5 August 1998 and sold only to non-resident Indians and their overseas corporate bodies. They paid 7.75 percent in dollars, 8 percent in sterling and 6.25 percent in Deutsche marks, and they closed on 24 August 1998 with 4.23 billion US dollars raised.

## The Contest at Home: Defiance, Cost and the Opposition's Ledger

The response was contested from its first week, and the contest is evidence, not decoration, because it fixed the terms in which endurance could be claimed as victory. The government's case, stated by Vajpayee in Parliament on 27 May 1998 and by Sinha through the budget debates of June, held that India had acted on a security judgement no foreign legislature could price, that the economy's fundamentals, six months of import cover, a low share of short-term debt, a borrowing programme conducted in rupees, made the sanctions survivable by construction, and that a government which trimmed its defence decisions to Washington's appropriations calendar would have paid a sovereignty price exceeding any coupon. The opposition's case, stated most sharply by the Left parties in the parliamentary debates and by Congress speakers on the economic resolutions, held that the tests had been a theatrical purchase of prestige at a measurable price: the downgraded rating of 24 June 1998, the delayed multilateral pipeline, the premium on the diaspora bond, and the burden placed on an exchequer that was cutting nothing to pay for any of it. The exporters' associations added a third ledger, of cancelled lines of credit and technology licences refused under the Entity List regime, which the commerce ministry's statements of the period acknowledge in the language of facilitation and review.

Each side's strongest evidence deserves its weight. For the government: the sanctions schedule, read carefully, exempted food and agriculture, spared trade in goods not on control lists, and left the largest single channel of India's external finance, remittances and software earnings, untouched; American assistance to India was small enough that its termination was a diplomatic fact more than a fiscal one, which is what Sinha meant, three years later, in the remark examined below. For the opposition: the costs were real where they fell, on defence procurement lines that had to be re-sourced, on public sector projects whose Japanese and multilateral financing slipped by seasons, on a ratings floor that raised every Indian borrower's price abroad, and on the 2003 redemption bill embedded in the bond's own terms. The adjudication this article defends does not require the costs to be small. It requires only the observation that the response was built, consciously, as a political structure for carrying the costs in public: rates raised and announced, a bond sold as a national subscription, a budget presented as a national project. A purely technical adjustment would have been quieter, cheaper in presentation and impossible for a coalition government to survive politically. The noise was load-bearing.

The contest had an organisational calendar as well as a parliamentary one. The Congress party's Pachmarhi conclave of 4 to 6 September 1998, the first great internal gathering of the Sonia Gandhi presidency, adopted an economic resolution that indicted the government's handling of the post-tests economy, charged it with squandering the inheritance of 1991, and committed the party to resist the conversion of a security decision into an economic alibi. The Left's parliamentary campaign ran on the premium's arithmetic: every point of coupon above the sovereign's hypothetical market rate, multiplied across 4.23 billion dollars and five years, presented as a transfer from the Indian taxpayer to the foreign-resident saver, arranged by a government that had simultaneously pleaded poverty before the pay commissions. The government's replies, in the autumn debates, rested on the counterfactual its opponents declined to price: the cost of the Fund programme India had refused, the cost of the multilateral delay imposed anyway, and the cost of a reserves crisis in a country whose 1991 gold flights remained within living memory of every member of the House. The argument was never settled on a single ledger because the two sides were accounting in different currencies, one in basis points, the other in negotiating freedom, and the persistence of that difference is itself a datum for the adjudication below: an episode that can be costed in two incommensurable currencies is not exhausted by a technical reading of either.

## The Dollar's Price: What the Response Cost, Line by Line

The response's defenders could never honestly call it cheap, and a complete account prices it on the same pages as its achievements. The first line is the Resurgent India Bonds' own bill. Against 4.23 billion dollars raised in August 1998, the redemption outgo of 1 October 2003 was 5.185 billion dollars, a difference of about 955 million dollars that represents the interest and accumulated accruals across the three currency tranches over five years, after the State Bank's payment of 22,151.83 crore rupees to the Reserve Bank for the foreign exchange. The dollar coupon of 7.75 percent, set against five-year Treasury yields near 5.5 percent at the time, priced a spread the closing-day reporting put at 225 basis points over Treasuries, and the Fund's September 1998 notice put the same instrument at about 215 basis points over LIBOR; the spread's exact measurement depends on the benchmark chosen, but its order of magnitude, two hundred or more basis points of political premium, is common ground. The second line is the exchange guarantee's fiscal shadow: the maintenance of value account absorbed the rupee's depreciation between issue and redemption on the apportionment shared between government and bank, so that the treasury's true cost exceeded the coupon alone, by an amount the Reserve Bank's redemption release documents in flows but does not summarise as a single price. The third line is the India Millennium Deposits' higher coupon, 8.5 percent in dollars against reserves earning about 5 percent, the 350 basis point gap Rajwade converted into the deposits' headline indictment.

Against these lines must be set the counterfactual prices the response avoided, because the opposition's ledger is incomplete without them. A Fund programme in 1998, had one been sought, would have carried its own conditions on the fiscal deficit, the exchange regime and the oil pool, conditions whose political cost no finance minister prices in basis points. A forced multilateral financing, had the United States pressed its opposition beyond delay into denial, would have left project pipelines exposed to a vote in Washington. A market borrowing under the Ba2 rating of June 1998 would have carried spreads at least as wide as the diaspora premium without the diaspora's political yield. The response's true unit price, on this fuller accounting, is therefore not the coupon but the difference between the premium paid and the sovereignty discounted, and the only honest verdict is that the difference is a judgement, not a number. The designers judged the premium cheap. Their critics judged it dear. Both judgements are in the record, and the record's later chapter, the smooth redemption of 2003 and the unrepeated but unrepudiated Millennium Deposits, favours the designers on execution while leaving the price question, as all price questions involving sovereignty should be, open to the historians.

## The Four-Feature Map of the Sanctions Response

The framework can be stated as a findable map, and the map is the article's citable contribution: four features, each with named institutions, named officeholders and dated instruments, which together convert the sanctions response from a sequence of reactions into a single piece of political-economic architecture. The first feature is the positioning of May to August 1998, Sinha at the finance ministry from 19 March 1998 and Jalan at the Reserve Bank from 22 November 1997, with the January 1998 defence as rehearsal and the budget of 1 June 1998 as the fiscal signal. The second is the Resurgent India Bonds, floated 5 August 1998, closed 24 August with 4.23 billion dollars. The third is the stabilisation programme, the August 1998 tightening and the calibrated easing of March to May 1999, which held the external account until the inflows returned. The fourth is the trajectory, the Jaswant Singh and Strobe Talbott dialogue from June 1998, the waiver calendar of November 1998, October 1999 and September 2001, and the India Millennium Deposits of October 2000, which proved the diaspora instrument had become a repeatable part of the state's financing repertoire rather than an emergency expedient. The Four-Feature Map of the Post-1998 Sanctions Response is set out below.

## Feature Three: Stabilisation Through 1999, Easing by Design

The third feature is the least theatrical and the most consequential, because it determined whether the bond's nineteen days would be remembered as a bridge or as a postponement. Its first phase, through the autumn of 1998, was holding: the August tightening stood, the Reserve Bank continued to sell dollars against volatility while accepting the weaker rupee level, and the finance ministry absorbed the fiscal cost of dearer oil and the Kargil build-up that began the following spring without reopening the external programme. The International Monetary Fund's consultation record for the year carries the external verdict the government cited most: a current account deficit contained near one and a half percent of gross domestic product, reserves rebuilt rather than spent, and no recourse to Fund financing requested or required. Growth, on the Central Statistical Organisation figures published for the year, held above six percent for 1998-99, carried by a strong agricultural recovery, even as exports contracted in dollar terms under the Asian demand shock; the sanctions appear in that arithmetic as a cost on the capital account's official lines, not as a macroeconomic event.

The easing, when it came, was sequenced like the tightening, which is the signature of design rather than drift. Effective 1 March 1999, the Reserve Bank reduced the Bank Rate by one percentage point to 8 percent, cut the fixed repo rate by two points to 6 percent from 2 March, reduced the cash reserve ratio by half a point to 10.5 percent from the fortnight beginning 13 March, and by a further half point to 10 percent from 8 May 1999, the last step augmenting the lendable resources of the banking system by about 3,250 crore rupees on the Bank's own published estimate. Reserves, the number every participant watched, closed the financial year at about 32.5 billion dollars, higher than the level at which the sanctions had struck eleven months earlier. The fiscal leg was less tidy, and honesty requires the entry: the deficit overshot Sinha's 5.6 percent target, printing near 6.5 percent of gross domestic product on the accounts of the day, swollen by pay commission arrears, small savings interest and the security build-up. The opposition's ledger entry was therefore accurate on its own line. The government's answer was that the overshoot was a domestic fiscal fact, older than the sanctions and visible in every budget since 1996, and that the external crisis the sanctions were supposed to cause had failed to occur on the Reserve Bank's own numbers. Both statements were true, and the third feature consists precisely in the fact that both could be true at once: stabilisation held the external account while the fiscal account slipped, and the architecture's designers had chosen, in effect, which account they would defend to the last basis point. The private capital account ratified the defence in the following year: foreign institutional investors, net sellers to the extent of about 390 million dollars in 1998-99 on the Reserve Bank's balance of payments statistics, returned as net buyers of about 2.1 billion dollars in 1999-2000, a swing executed while the Glenn sanctions' economic core was still formally in place and before the October 1999 waiver, which is to say that the market's verdict on the stabilisation preceded the sanctioner's own pardon by two quarters. The sequence is the third feature's epitaph. Stabilisation is conventionally measured in reserves, and the reserves obliged; but its real product was this order of events, credibility first, capital second, absolution third, and the designers, who had insisted from Sinha's pledge onward that the sequence would run in exactly that order, were entitled to point at the calendar.

## Kargil and the Caretaker Summer: The Architecture Under Fire

The stabilisation was stress-tested by politics before it was tested by markets. On 17 April 1999, the Vajpayee government lost a confidence vote in the Lok Sabha by a single vote, 269 to 270, after the All India Anna Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam withdrew its support, and India entered the summer of 1999 with a caretaker government, a dissolved parliament's election calendar ahead, and, within weeks, a war. Pakistani regulars and infiltrators were discovered across the Line of Control in the Kargil sector in May 1999, and the Indian military's Operation Vijay to evict them ran to 26 July 1999, through the very months in which the easing sequence of March to May was still being executed. The conjunction is the strongest evidence available for the third feature's quality. A caretaker finance ministry, constitutionally barred from new policy adventures, nonetheless serviced the external programme without interruption; the Reserve Bank's announced reductions took effect on their printed dates in the middle of a shooting war; and the markets, which had punished coalition tremors the previous autumn, absorbed a Himalayan conflict, a supplementary defence effort and a suspended parliament without a run on the rupee. The architecture, built for a punishment regime, proved load-bearing under artillery.

The war also completed the fourth feature's diplomatic circuit in a way no negotiator could have scheduled. The Jaswant Singh and Strobe Talbott dialogue had given Washington a working channel into the Indian government's thinking, and when the Kargil crisis peaked, President Clinton's meeting with Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif in Washington on 4 July 1999 produced the Pakistani commitment to withdraw, an outcome New Delhi publicly welcomed and privately registered as the dialogue's dividend. Fresh elections in September and October 1999 returned the National Democratic Alliance with 303 seats, Vajpayee was sworn in again on 13 October 1999, and Sinha retained the finance portfolio, a continuity of personnel that amounted to a ratification of the sanctions response by the only electorate with jurisdiction over it. The budget Sinha presented in February 2000, for the millennium year, could therefore treat the external emergency as a closed chapter and argue about taxes and disinvestment instead. Episodes of statecraft are rarely granted so legible a sequence of examination and result: the punishment arrived in May 1998, the architecture carried the state through a lost confidence vote and a border war, and the architects were returned to office by the voters with an enlarged majority before the punishment had been fully lifted.

## Feature Four, First Movement: The Dialogue and the Waiver Calendar

The fourth feature converted endurance into schedule. In June 1998, Jaswant Singh, then deputy chairman of the Planning Commission and the Prime Minister's special envoy, began a structured dialogue with Strobe Talbott, the United States Deputy Secretary of State, which continued through fourteen meetings in seven countries on three continents to September 2000, as Talbott's own account, Engaging India: Diplomacy, Democracy, and the Bomb (2004), counts them. The dialogue's existence was itself the first concession the sanctions were supposed to prevent: Washington, having imposed automatic penalties to isolate the decision, appointed its second-ranking diplomat to negotiate with the decision's authors. Its institutionalisation deepened the concession: in December 1998 Jaswant Singh moved from the Planning Commission to the external affairs portfolio itself, becoming foreign minister while remaining Talbott's interlocutor, so that the channel the sanctions had forced open acquired cabinet rank on the Indian side and a permanent address in South Block. The pairing of the two men, a deputy secretary who wrote his account in the first person and a minister who treated the dialogue as a seminar in civilisational statecraft, gave the negotiation a durability that electoral and bureaucratic cycles in both capitals could not interrupt: it survived the fall of the Vajpayee government in April 1999, resumed with the same principals after October, and absorbed the Kargil war as an agenda item rather than a termination. Fourteen meetings across twenty-seven months is a count; the substance was that the country under punishment and the country imposing it built, under fire, the densest diplomatic channel in their history to that date, and the channel's traffic consisted substantially of the punishment's staged removal. Talbott presented five benchmarks at Delhi in July 1998, on his own record: signature of the test ban treaty, a halt to fissile material production pending a cutoff treaty, strategic restraint in missile and nuclear deployment, strengthened export controls, and a resumption of dialogue with Pakistan. Jaswant Singh's counter-position, held with variations for two years, offered India's unilateral test moratorium, declared on the day the series closed, movement on export controls and fissile material talks, and no signature on the test ban treaty on any calendar Washington set. The economic significance of the dialogue was that it gave the waiver process a political fuel supply: every round let the administration report progress to Congress, and every report loosened one bolt of the Glenn schedule. The dialogue's hardest item was always the test ban treaty, and its denouement belongs to the economic story because the treaty signature was the price Washington had most publicly attached to normalisation. Through 1999 Jaswant Singh held the position that India would move toward adherence only as the treaty's own entry-into-force politics and India's security environment permitted, and Vajpayee told the United Nations General Assembly in September 1999 that India would not stand in the way of the treaty's entry into force, a formulation Talbott's account treats as the outer limit of what the dialogue could extract. On 13 October 1999 the United States Senate rejected the treaty's ratification, extinguishing the demand's practical force from the demander's side, and the waiver legislation that completed its passage that same month, Title IX of Public Law 106-79, was framed in a Washington that had just declined to bind itself by the instrument it had spent seventeen months pressing India to sign. The sequence mattered to the response's domestic politics beyond its irony: the finance ministry's claim that endurance was cheaper than compliance had rested on the prediction that the punishment coalition would dissolve before the Indian position did, and the Senate vote of October 1999 was the prediction's documentary fulfilment.

The waiver calendar repays exact statement, because it is the fourth feature's documentary spine. In October 1998, Congress enacted the Brownback Amendment, named for Senator Sam Brownback of Kansas, inside the omnibus appropriations law, Public Law 105-277, granting the President one year of authority to waive the Glenn sanctions in defined areas. On 3 November 1998, President Clinton exercised it, restoring Export-Import Bank, Overseas Private Investment Corporation and Trade and Development Agency programmes, authorising International Military Education and Training, and lifting the restrictions on American banks, as his letter to the congressional leadership of that month records. In October 1999, Congress widened the authority in Title IX of the Department of Defense Appropriations Act, Public Law 106-79, and by presidential determination of 27 October 1999 Clinton waived the economic sanctions on India outright, leaving military and dual-use restrictions standing. Between those two acts of relief came the Kargil war of May to July 1999, in which, as Talbott's account documents for the first time in insider form, Washington's pressure on Pakistan to withdraw across the Line of Control made the dialogue's strategic value concrete in Indian eyes and dissolved much of the remaining congressional appetite for punishment. President Clinton's state visit of March 2000, the first by an American president since 1978, and the Vision Statement signed with Vajpayee during it, closed the diplomatic arc the dialogue had opened. Both principals later wrote the dialogue from their own papers, Talbott in Engaging India in 2004 and Jaswant Singh in his essays and memoirs, which is why the episode's documentary trail runs through personal archives of exactly the kind whose protection is treated in [a practitioner's guide to how journalists protect their sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/). The final Glenn restrictions on India were removed by President George W. Bush's determination of 22 September 2001, in the strategic reordering after the attacks of that month, and Sinha, still finance minister, greeted the waiver in an interview with the Press Trust of India on 23 September 2001 with the sentence that summarised the government's whole account of the episode: as far as the Indian economy itself was concerned, he said, except for certain defence supplies, sanctions had no meaning.

The sentence was a victor's compression, and its limits should be marked. Defence supplies were not a small exception to a government re-equipping after Kargil, and the Entity List's residue outlasted the waiver, with named Indian organisations still licence-restricted after 2001, as the Congressional Research Service recorded in 2002. But as a claim about the macroeconomic contest, the sentence was defensible on the numbers the previous sections set out, and its political function was exact: it retired the sanctions story on the government's terms, as an ordeal survived by architecture rather than by luck. The dialogue's longer consequence, the corridor from Talbott's benchmarks through the Clinton visit to the civil nuclear negotiations of 2005 to 2008, is traced in the accounts of [the economic-policy-as-politics framework](/2020/11/15/1991-reform-as-politics-reanalysed/) and of the 2008 settlement; here it is enough to register that the fourth feature's diplomacy did what its designers intended, which was to make the sanctions temporary by negotiation while the first three features made them bearable by construction.

### Why did Washington begin waiving its own sanctions within six months?

Three pressures converged by the autumn of 1998. The dialogue with Jaswant Singh gave the administration reportable movement on restraint and export controls. American business and farm lobbies pressed Congress over lost sales and blocked credits. And the sanctions were visibly failing to move India's nuclear decision, which made their cost to American exporters a price paid for nothing.

## The Second Track: Wheat, Business Councils and the Sanctioner's Own Politics

The waiver calendar had an American domestic engine that the dialogue alone does not explain, and the response's designers understood and exploited it. The Glenn schedule's food exception, which spared agricultural commodities from the lending ban from the first day, marked the seam where American farm politics entered the sanctions regime: the wheat and soybean states had no intention of surrendering markets to Australian and Canadian exporters as the price of a non-proliferation gesture, and Senator Brownback of Kansas, whose amendment began the dismantling, represented precisely the constituency that grew the wheat. The organised business lobby worked the same seam from the commercial side. The US-India Business Council and the American chambers testified through the sanctions period that the schedule punished exporters in the sanctioning country at least as reliably as borrowers in the sanctioned one, pointing to capital goods orders lost to European and Japanese competitors whose governments condemned the tests in communiqués while imposing nothing comparable in law. The European pattern is documented in the period's trade reporting: Britain and Germany aligned with the Birmingham condemnation and the Security Council's demands, and neither enacted a Glenn-style automatic schedule, so that European export credit and technology flowed under political weather that American firms, bound by statute, could not match. The asymmetry was the Indian negotiators' most repeated exhibit in Washington, and its logic ran downhill through the waiver sequence: each restoration of an American programme, export credit in November 1998, economic sanctions entire in October 1999, was defended by its congressional sponsors in the vocabulary of American competitiveness as fluently as in the vocabulary of Indian reform.

The sanctions literature rarely grants the sanctioned state an American strategy, but the record shows one. New Delhi's Washington effort in 1998 to 2000, conducted by the embassy under Ambassador Naresh Chandra and amplified by the Indian-American community whose organisations lobbied the Hill on the sanctions question, treated the punishment as an American domestic coalition problem to be dissolved rather than a verdict to be appealed. The diaspora appears in this track in its second capacity, political rather than financial: the same community that subscribed the Resurgent India Bonds also staffed the constituency argument that sanctions injured Americans, and the bond's order book, published and celebrated, functioned in congressional testimony as evidence that India could finance itself elsewhere if Washington insisted on absence. Jaswant Singh's dialogue supplied the diplomatic text, but the subtext that moved the waiver votes was written by farm-state arithmetic and exporter frustration, and the response's architecture deserves credit for having arranged, deliberately or by fortunate design, for both texts to be read in Washington at once. The point generalises the article's claim once more: the sanctions response was built simultaneously in Mumbai's money market, in the diaspora's savings accounts and in the American legislature's committee rooms, and a reading that confines it to any one of those rooms will misprice it.

## Feature Four, Second Movement: The India Millennium Deposits of October 2000

The proof that the diaspora instrument had entered the state's permanent repertoire came in October 2000, when the State Bank of India opened the India Millennium Deposits, a five-year foreign currency deposit scheme for non-resident Indians modelled directly on the Resurgent India Bonds and chaired, on the issuing side, by the State Bank's chairman G. G. Vaidya, who told the press at the launch that the bank had commitments approaching 5 billion dollars before the window opened. The occasion was different and the difference is the point. No sanctions were in force on the economic side by October 2000; the pressure was an oil import bill that Euromoney's contemporary analysis expected to double toward 18 billion dollars for the year, a trade deficit widening on the same account, portfolio outflows, and a balance of payments quarter that had printed a deficit of about 1 billion dollars after a surplus of 3.32 billion in the previous quarter. The deposits, denominated in dollars, sterling and euros, paid 8.5 percent in dollars, 7.85 percent in sterling and 6.85 percent in euros, opened on 21 October 2000 for thirty days, and closed with about 5.5 billion dollars mobilised, the figure the Reserve Bank's later presentations carry. Vaidya had prepared the market in person, travelling to Dubai, Bahrain and Abu Dhabi three weeks before the opening to take the diaspora's temperature, on the report of the launch carried by the financial press, and the bank paid collecting agents a commission structure, reported by Euromoney at 150 basis points with half the collections relent to the collecting banks in rupees at 10 percent, that reproduced the 1998 distribution machine almost exactly. The deposits' maturity in December 2005 completed the demonstration a second time: the State Bank met the redemption date of 29 December 2005 with the rupee at 45.34 to the dollar, stronger than the 46.65 at which the deposits had been issued in 2000, offered rollover products across its deposit and mutual fund arms, and expected on its own public statements to retain between fifteen and twenty percent of the proceeds, converting a maturity wall into a retention campaign. An instrument built for punishment had become, in twenty-six months, an instrument of ordinary external management; that conversion is the fourth feature's second movement and the strongest single evidence for the political-economic reading, because a technical device does not change its meaning in this way, while an architectural one does.

Two features of the 2000 book repay attention, because they show the instrument maturing rather than merely repeating. The first is its currency structure: where the 1998 bonds had been sold in dollars, sterling and Deutsche marks, the Millennium Deposits replaced the mark with the euro, the currency created in January 1999, a substitution that tracked the diaspora's own geography as the Gulf and North American tranches were joined by a continental European book that the new currency made natural to denominate. The second is the identity of the marginal subscriber. Vaidya's pre-launch tour had been routed through the Gulf, and the Gulf's Indian workforce, remitting wages rather than investing portfolios, supplied the deposits with a breadth the 1998 issue had pioneered and the 2000 issue normalised; the state's financing constituency had widened, in two years, from the professional diaspora of the bond roadshows to the wage-earning diaspora of the Gulf's labour camps, and the widening was a political fact about who counted as a creditor of the Indian state quite as much as a financial fact about the balance of payments. A sanctions expedient had become, by its third performance, something close to a representative institution, and the finance ministry's comfort in reaching for it, in a quarter when the emergency was an oil bill rather than a punishment regime, measures how completely the response of 1998 had been absorbed into the ordinary constitution of Indian economic management.

The deposits also drew the cost critique in its most documented form, and the critique belongs inside the feature, not outside it. A. V. Rajwade, the foreign exchange consultant whose columns in the Business Standard were the period's most persistent technical opposition to diaspora borrowing, pointed out that the 8.5 percent dollar coupon stood far above the return of about 5 percent that India earned on the reserves the deposits would swell, so that the state was paying a spread of roughly 350 basis points for insurance it partly did not need. T. B. Kapali's column in the Business Line of 21 October 2000 attacked the issue's presentation rather than its price, charging the State Bank with justifying a fixed-rate instrument by a floating-rate benchmark in what he called one of the least transparent exercises in Indian financial market history. The finance ministry's defence, conducted through the same newspapers, held that the relevant comparison was not the yield on reserves but the cost of the alternatives in a rising oil market: dearer domestic interest rates, a forced compression of imports, or a return to the multilateral lenders on political terms. The adjudication between these positions is genuinely open, and later practice suggests a split verdict: the deposits were never repeated on that scale, which concedes something to the cost critique, while the reserves doctrine they financed, accumulation as self-insurance, became the settled policy of the following decade, which concedes more to the designers.

## Adjudicating the Three Readings

Three readings of the 1998 to 2000 record compete, and the article's claim can be adjudicated against its two named rivals. The sanctions-response reading, carried in its most rigorous form by Sumit Ganguly and Devin T. Hagerty in Fearful Symmetry: India-Pakistan Crises in the Shadow of Nuclear Weapons (2005), treats the episode as a coercion case study: Washington imposed penalties to extract non-proliferation concessions, extracted few, and dismantled the penalties as their futility became plain, a reading in which the Indian economy figures as the terrain on which American leverage was tested and found slight. The technical economic policy reading, carried by the period's Fund and Bank documentation and by the Reserve Bank's own retrospective writing, treats the episode as crisis management: a sequence of rate decisions, reserve operations and financing instruments whose success is measured in the external account's survival, and whose politics are background. Each reading is true about its own object. The first correctly measures the sanctions' coercive yield, which was small. The second correctly describes the instruments, which worked. What neither captures is the construction: that the instruments were chosen, sequenced and narrated as a single political artefact by named officeholders who understood endurance as a public performance with a financing plan attached, and that this construction, not the sanctions and not the techniques, is what later governments inherited and reused, in the Millennium Deposits first of all.

The strongest counter to this reading must be given its full force: that the political-economic architecture is a flattering redescription of a sanctions-response reality, that the Glenn schedule and the Group of Eight's pressure set every deadline the designers met, and that to call the bond architecture is to rename obedience to circumstance. The counter fails on the chronology and on the instruments' surplus meaning. The chronology: the positioning predated the punishment, in Jalan's appointment and January defence, and the trajectory outlasted it, in a deposits scheme floated when no economic sanctions remained to answer. The surplus meaning: a response priced to circumstance alone would have minimised the premium, borrowed quietly, and avoided naming a savings product Resurgent in the middle of a ratings downgrade; the designers did the opposite at each point, paying for visibility, and the visibility was the politics. The comparison with the founding generation's treatment of institutional moments is not decorative here. The doctrine traced in [the most-consequential-constitutional-moment framework](/2020/07/28/basic-structure-most-consequential-doctrine/) showed how a single decision, defended as architecture, reorganised a field for decades; the sanctions response, a smaller artefact in a smaller field, reorganised India's external financing doctrine for the decade that followed, and the mechanism in both cases was the same, a decision taken in a crisis and then maintained, deliberately, as a structure. The long view across the whole period, in which foreign economic pressure and domestic positioning continually remake each other, is the argument of [the foreign-policy-as-domestic-politics framework](/2020/09/21/non-alignment-indian-domestic-politics/), and the sanctions response is among its cleanest instances.

The technical reading deserves its strongest documents before it is set aside. The Fund's Public Information Notice of 22 September 1998 is a genuinely impressive artefact of the rival case: it reads the Indian economy of that summer as a set of manageable ratios, commends the authorities' monetary tightening and exchange flexibility, records reserves at six months of imports, and treats the sanctions as one adverse factor among several in an economy whose difficulties, Asian contagion, export contraction, fiscal slippage, substantially predated the tests. Jalan's essays make the companion argument from inside, presenting the governorship's task as the continuous management of an exchange rate and a reserve stock against shocks of every origin, in which a sanctions regime differs from a monsoon failure or an oil spike in politics but not in the operations it demands. If the episode had consisted only of these documents, the technical reading would be sufficient, and the architecture claim would be the kind of over-interpretation that gives political readings of economic events their poor reputation. The claim survives because the episode does not consist only of these documents. It also contains a finance minister swearing off the Fund in Parliament before knowing whether the summer could be financed, a bond named as a national adjective and sold through family gift provisions, a dialogue whose rounds were scheduled against waiver votes in a foreign legislature, and a deposits scheme repeated in peacetime at a higher coupon than the crisis original's critics had called indefensible. None of those facts is a ratio. All of them are in the primary record. A reading that cannot accommodate them is not wrong about its own territory; it is incomplete about the episode, and incompleteness, in a contest of readings, is the defeat that matters.

### What evidence separates the architecture reading from a plain sanctions-response account?

Three exhibits discriminate between the readings. The positioning predates the punishment, since Jalan's appointment and the January 1998 defence precede the tests. The diaspora instrument was repeated in October 2000, when no economic sanctions remained to answer. And the designers paid measurable premiums for visibility, in coupons, naming and exchange guarantees, which a purely reactive account cannot explain.

A further discriminating test is the identity of the inheritors. The Millennium Deposits were floated by a returned government in a second term, after an election fought partly on the management of 1998, and were administered by the same State Bank machinery under a chairman, G. G. Vaidya, who presented the scheme as an ordinary instrument of external management rather than as an emergency device. The Reserve Bank's reserve doctrine passed intact to Jalan's successor in November 2003 and beyond, which is how administrative habits announce themselves as doctrine. Ganguly and Hagerty, measuring the sanctions, are right that the punishment failed; the Fund's consultation record, measuring the management, is right that the management succeeded; the architecture reading subsumes both verdicts by identifying the artefact that produced the second and neutralised the first, and by showing that the artefact was still in service, refinanced and enlarged, five years after the punishment that supposedly explains it had been waived away. The reading also survives the falsification test its rivals are invited to apply. Had the record shown the finance ministry privately seeking Fund soundings while publicly refusing them, had the diaspora book failed and been quietly replaced by commercial borrowing under the downgraded rating, had the easing of 1999 been forced by reserve loss rather than sequenced against reserve recovery, the architecture claim would collapse into the sanctions-response account with better adjectives. The record shows the opposite on each point where the two readings predict differently, and a claim that specifies in advance what evidence would destroy it, and then meets that evidence, has earned more than narrative plausibility. It has earned the adjudication this article entered for it.

## The Trajectory After 2001: Redemption Without Drama

The architecture's last test was repayment, and it passed in the manner its designers had promised. The Resurgent India Bonds matured on 1 October 2003, and the Reserve Bank's press release of that day records the settlement in the flat language of successful operations: a foreign exchange outgo equivalent to 5.185 billion dollars, met by selling that amount to the State Bank against 22,151.83 crore rupees paid by the Bank, the exchange loss absorbed by the maintenance of value account for apportionment between the government and the Bank in the agreed ratio, 2.794 billion of the total met from the Reserve Bank's forward receivables and the balance from reserves, and, the sentence the designers would have underlined, a redemption process the release calls very smooth, with no volatility in the money, foreign exchange or securities markets. The India Millennium Deposits matured in December 2005 on the same pattern, with the State Bank offering rollover products and expecting, on its own statements to the press that month, to retain a fifth or more of the proceeds. The instruments that Congress speakers had predicted would detonate under the exchequer in 2003 instead expired as administrative events, reported on inside pages. One personnel detail completes the circuit the response had drawn: the finance minister who presided over the 2003 redemption was Jaswant Singh, the dialogue's Indian principal, who had taken the finance portfolio in July 2002, so that the man who had negotiated the sanctions' diplomatic unwinding also signed off, five years on, on the punctual repayment of the bond that had financed the endurance he negotiated from. The reserves against which that repayment was a rounding error had, by March 2002, carried the foreign currency assets of the Reserve Bank past 51 billion dollars, a figure the designers of August 1998, defending a stock of 26.5 billion, would have recognised as their doctrine's vindication in a single number.

The trajectory's diplomatic terminus confirms the conversion. In July 2005, seven years after the Glenn determination, the joint statement issued in Washington by President Bush and Prime Minister Manmohan Singh announced American readiness to work toward full civil nuclear cooperation with India, the programme whose 2008 completion closed the file the sanctions had opened, and the negotiating path to that statement ran through every station the fourth feature had built: the dialogue's habit of strategic conversation, the waiver precedents that taught Congress to legislate exceptions for India, and the economic credibility, reserves approaching 140 billion dollars by then on the accumulation doctrine's trajectory, that made India a partner to be courted rather than a problem to be priced. The sanctions had been designed to raise the cost of India's nuclear course until the course was abandoned. The response raised India's capacity to pay costs in general, financial, diplomatic and electoral, until the sanctioners repriced the relationship itself. That inversion, from cost imposition to capacity demonstration, is the deepest reason the episode resists the sanctions-response reading: the punishment's history ends in 2001, while the response's history is still the operating system of Indian external economic policy.

What endured was a doctrine and a repertoire. The doctrine, visible in Jalan's essays and in the Reserve Bank's practice under his successors, held that a country financing itself in a hostile or indifferent market accumulates reserves in fat years as the price of freedom in lean ones, and that the diaspora is a financing constituency of the state, to be cultivated in calm weather rather than summoned in storms. The repertoire included the demonstrated ability to raise four to five billion dollars from that constituency in under a month, at a premium, without conditionality, a fact every subsequent finance ministry has held in reserve against its own contingencies. Its most consequential later performance came in September 2013, when the Reserve Bank under Governor Raghuram Rajan, facing the taper tantrum's run on the rupee, opened a concessional swap window for foreign currency non-resident deposits that drew in about 25 billion dollars in three months and broke the panic; the instrument's genealogy runs directly through 1998 and 2000, diaspora money summoned by a premium and a promise, at a moment when the market's own price for India had become prohibitive, and its success was the sanctions response's doctrine operating fifteen years on under a different government and a different governor, which is what it means for a crisis artefact to have become architecture. Read against the whole series' argument, the sanctions response also belongs to the longer renegotiation of India's place that [the integrated three-tensions framework](/2019/07/09/modi-era-three-tension-renegotiation/) tracks for a later period: who counts as fully Indian was answered, in August 1998, by a bond certificate that treated the non-resident as a citizen of the exchequer; who governs whom was answered by a finance minister and a governor who governed the crisis between them; and the question of which past the present continues was answered by an instrument copied from 1991 and bequeathed to 2000. The detailed mechanics of that earlier settlement, whose managers first proved that Indian economic decisions are political architecture, are set out in [the detailed-political-architectural-substantive-content framework](/2020/12/01/1991-reforms-detail-reanalysed/) of the companion account of the 1991 reforms. The sanctions of May 1998 were meant to teach India the cost of defiance. The response taught the teachers a different lesson, recorded in their own waiver calendar: that a state which can finance its defiance at home and abroad, and narrate the financing as national subscription, has converted punishment into a constituent ritual, and rituals, unlike sanctions, do not expire.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What were the Resurgent India Bonds August 1998?

The Resurgent India Bonds were five-year foreign currency bonds floated by the State Bank of India on 5 August 1998, with the approval of the Government of India and the Reserve Bank of India, and sold exclusively to non-resident Indians and overseas corporate bodies owned by them. They were denominated in US dollars, pounds sterling and Deutsche marks, paying 7.75 percent, 8 percent and 6.25 percent respectively, were transferable among eligible holders, and could be gifted to resident relatives. The issue closed on 24 August 1998 with 4.23 billion US dollars raised from more than 74,000 subscribers in more than thirty countries, and the bonds were redeemed on maturity on 1 October 2003.

### Q: How did India respond to post-Pokhran II sanctions economically?

India's economic response combined refusal with construction. The government declined to bargain over the nuclear decision itself, and instead financed endurance: the Reserve Bank defended the rupee with interest rate increases and dollar sales, the finance ministry presented the 1 June 1998 budget as a national self-reliance programme, and the State Bank of India raised 4.23 billion US dollars from the diaspora through the Resurgent India Bonds of August 1998. Multilateral and bilateral channels closed by sanctions were replaced by diaspora savings and domestic borrowing, reserves were rebuilt to about 32.5 billion dollars by March 1999, and a parallel diplomatic dialogue converted the sanctions into a sequence of American waivers between November 1998 and September 2001.

### Q: Who was Yashwant Sinha in 1998?

Yashwant Sinha was India's finance minister in 1998, appointed when Atal Bihari Vajpayee formed the National Democratic Alliance government on 19 March 1998. A former civil servant who had served as finance minister in the Chandra Shekhar government of 1990 to 1991 and presented its crisis budget, he carried into the sanctions summer the authority of an officeholder who had managed an external payments emergency before. He presented the Union budget of 1 June 1998, framed the sanctions response as a swadeshi programme of competitiveness and self-reliance, coordinated the external financing strategy with Reserve Bank Governor Bimal Jalan, and recorded the episode in his memoir Confessions of a Swadeshi Reformer (2007).

### Q: What was the post-1998 economic stabilisation architecture?

The post-1998 stabilisation architecture was the monetary and fiscal structure through which the Reserve Bank of India and the finance ministry held the external account between the sanctions of May 1998 and the easing of 1999. Its instruments were the cash reserve ratio, raised from 10 to 11 percent from 29 August 1998, the fixed repo rate, raised from 5 to 8 percent in the same package, managed dollar sales against currency volatility, and a fiscal stance that defended the external position while the deficit overshot its target. Once reserves recovered, the Bank reversed the sequence by design, cutting the Bank Rate to 8 percent on 1 March 1999 and reducing the reserve ratio in steps to 10 percent by May 1999.

### Q: What was the post-1998 NRI bond mobilisation?

The post-1998 NRI bond mobilisation was the Indian state's systematic turn to its diaspora as a financing constituency after the Pokhran II sanctions. Its first instrument was the Resurgent India Bonds of August 1998, which raised 4.23 billion US dollars from more than 74,000 non-resident subscribers in nineteen days through the State Bank of India's overseas network and foreign bank distributors. Its proof of permanence was the India Millennium Deposits of October 2000, which mobilised about 5.5 billion dollars on the same principle in the absence of sanctions, against an oil-driven balance of payments squeeze. Together the two issues established diaspora borrowing, at premium rates and without policy conditionality, as a repeatable instrument of Indian external management.

### Q: How did sanctions response shape post-1998 economic policy?

The sanctions response shaped post-1998 economic policy in three durable ways. It entrenched reserve accumulation as doctrine: the Reserve Bank, under Bimal Jalan and his successors, treated a large reserve stock as self-insurance purchased in calm years against hostile markets. It normalised diaspora financing, demonstrated by the India Millennium Deposits of October 2000, which reused the sanctions instrument for an ordinary balance of payments purpose. And it taught the finance ministry the political value of framing external endurance as national subscription, a lesson visible in the government's presentation of every later external financing operation. The response's costs, premium coupons and a 5.185 billion dollar redemption in 2003, were absorbed into that doctrine as the price of financial autonomy.

### Q: What was the post-1998 forex crisis response?

The post-1998 foreign exchange crisis response was the Reserve Bank of India's defence of the rupee between May 1998 and the spring of 1999. Governor Bimal Jalan's Bank sold dollars to curb volatility while allowing the rate to settle lower, from below 40 to about 42.5 to the dollar by December 1998, and tightened liquidity sharply in August 1998, raising the cash reserve ratio to 11 percent and the fixed repo rate to 8 percent, to make speculation expensive as the Resurgent India Bonds inflow arrived. Reserves, 29.4 billion dollars at the end of March 1998 and about 26.5 billion in mid-August on International Monetary Fund figures, were rebuilt to about 32.5 billion by March 1999.

### Q: What did Japan do in response to the May 1998 tests?

Japan, India's largest bilateral donor, imposed the most consequential bilateral economic measures outside the American schedule. Tokyo froze new yen loan commitments, the concessional official development assistance loans on which Indian power, transport and water projects drew, and suspended grant aid outside emergency, grassroots and humanitarian categories. Japanese officials defended the measures in the Diet as the minimum consistent with Japan's non-proliferation policy as the only atomic-bombed state, while Indian officials pressed the asymmetry that existing projects continued and new commitments were the true sanction. The freeze bit slowly rather than sharply, delaying project pipelines by seasons, and its staged relaxation tracked the same improvement in relations that produced the American waivers of 1999 and 2000.

### Q: What did Moody's do to India's sovereign rating in June 1998?

On 24 June 1998, six weeks after the Pokhran II tests, Moody's Investors Service downgraded India's foreign currency sovereign rating from Baa3 to Ba2, moving the sovereign below investment grade. The agency cited the sanctions' effect on India's external financing, the deterioration in relations with the United States and the multilateral lenders, and the fiscal position of the central government. Standard and Poor's placed its BBB minus rating under negative scrutiny in the same period. The downgrade raised the price of every Indian borrower's access to foreign capital and became the opposition's most quoted single figure in the parliamentary argument over the tests' cost, which the government answered by pointing to the reserves and growth figures of the following spring.

### Q: How many non-resident Indians subscribed to the Resurgent India Bonds?

More than 74,000 non-resident Indian subscribers in more than thirty countries bought the Resurgent India Bonds during the subscription window of 5 to 24 August 1998, on the closing figures reported at the time and carried in later accounts of the issue. Distribution ran through the State Bank of India's overseas offices and, heavily, through foreign and private banks acting as collecting agents; a former State Bank official involved in the issue recalled that foreign banks gathered around 3 billion of the 4.23 billion dollars raised, with Citibank alone accounting for 822 million dollars. The Gulf and North America supplied the largest shares of subscriptions, mapping the geography of the post-1970s Indian emigration.

### Q: Who was Bimal Jalan in 1998?

Bimal Jalan was the Governor of the Reserve Bank of India in 1998, an office he had assumed on 22 November 1997, succeeding C. Rangarajan, in the middle of the Asian financial crisis. A former finance secretary and chief economic adviser, he had already directed the January 1998 defence of the rupee, in which the Bank Rate reached 11 percent and reserve requirements were raised to break a speculative attack, before the Pokhran II sanctions made external defence the central bank's standing task. He designed the August 1998 tightening that accompanied the Resurgent India Bonds, directed the calibrated easing of 1999, and set out his reading of exchange rate management and reserve accumulation in the essays of his governorship and after.

### Q: What was the Brownback Amendment of October 1998?

The Brownback Amendment, carried in the omnibus appropriations law, Public Law 105-277, enacted in October 1998 and named for Senator Sam Brownback of Kansas, gave the President of the United States one year of authority to waive the Glenn Amendment sanctions imposed on India and Pakistan after the May 1998 tests. It was Congress's admission, five months into the punishment, that an automatic schedule had deprived American diplomacy of flexibility and American exporters of markets without altering either country's nuclear course. President Clinton exercised the authority on 3 November 1998, restoring export credit, investment insurance and military training programmes and lifting restrictions on American banks, and Congress replaced the one-year grant with broader waiver authority in October 1999.

### Q: What did President Clinton waive on 3 November 1998?

On 3 November 1998, acting under the Brownback Amendment's one-year waiver authority, President Clinton restored a defined set of programmes suspended by the Glenn sanctions against India and Pakistan. The Export-Import Bank, the Overseas Private Investment Corporation and the Trade and Development Agency resumed their Indian programmes, International Military Education and Training was authorised, and the restrictions on lending by American commercial banks to the Indian government were lifted. In his letter to the congressional leadership, Clinton tied the waivers to progress in the non-proliferation dialogue then running between Jaswant Singh and Strobe Talbott. Military sales, dual-use technology controls and opposition to non-human-needs multilateral lending remained in force beyond that date.

### Q: Which Indian organisations were placed on the United States Entity List in November 1998?

In November 1998 the United States Department of Commerce published additions to its Entity List naming Indian organisations whose access to American goods and technology would require export licences subject to a presumption of denial. The list concentrated on the nuclear, missile and space establishments: units of the Department of Atomic Energy including the Bhabha Atomic Research Centre and the Indira Gandhi Centre for Atomic Research, centres of the Indian Space Research Organisation, laboratories of the Defence Research and Development Organisation, and defence public sector undertakings and subordinate facilities. The Entity List operated separately from the Glenn Amendment's financial schedule, and its residue proved the most durable layer of the 1998 measures, with named organisations still licence-restricted after the financial sanctions were waived in 2001.

### Q: What were the India Millennium Deposits of October 2000?

The India Millennium Deposits were a five-year foreign currency deposit scheme for non-resident Indians, issued by the State Bank of India from 21 October 2000 for thirty days and modelled directly on the Resurgent India Bonds of 1998. Denominated in US dollars, pounds sterling and euros, they paid 8.5 percent, 7.85 percent and 6.85 percent respectively, and were marketed by the State Bank's chairman, G. G. Vaidya, against a doubling oil import bill and a deteriorating quarterly balance of payments. The scheme mobilised about 5.5 billion US dollars, exceeding its 1998 predecessor without any sanctions emergency to motivate subscribers, and matured in December 2005, when the Bank offered depositors rollover products to retain the funds.

### Q: Why were the India Millennium Deposits criticised as costly?

The cost critique of the India Millennium Deposits was stated most sharply by A. V. Rajwade, the foreign exchange consultant whose Business Standard columns tracked both diaspora issues. Rajwade observed that the deposits paid 8.5 percent in dollars while India's foreign exchange reserves earned about 5 percent, so the state was paying a spread of roughly 350 basis points to enlarge a reserve stock it partly did not need, a subsidy from the taxpayer to the appearance of strength. T. B. Kapali, writing in the Business Line on 21 October 2000, attacked the issue's presentation, arguing that the State Bank justified a fixed coupon by a floating LIBOR benchmark in a manner he called disingenuous. The finance ministry answered that the true comparator was the cost of dearer domestic rates or compressed imports in an oil shock.

### Q: How did the 1991 India Development Bonds precedent shape the 1998 response?

The India Development Bonds of 1991 supplied the template, the credibility and part of the subscriber register for the Resurgent India Bonds. Floated by the State Bank of India during the balance of payments crisis of 1991, when the Rao government was pledging gold and negotiating with the International Monetary Fund, the Development Bonds raised 1.6 billion US dollars from non-resident Indians on the same principle later reused in 1998: that the diaspora would lend to the Indian state, in foreign currency, at a premium, when market and official channels were closed or conditional. Yashwant Sinha, finance minister in the Chandra Shekhar government that preceded the 1991 issue and again in 1998, knew the instrument from both sides of its history, and the 1998 designers invoked its successful redemption as evidence the promise would be kept.

### Q: What did Yashwant Sinha say when the last sanctions were waived in September 2001?

When President George W. Bush issued the determination of 22 September 2001 removing the remaining Glenn Amendment sanctions on India, Yashwant Sinha, still finance minister, welcomed the waiver in an interview with the Press Trust of India on 23 September 2001 and delivered the government's considered epitaph on the whole episode. As far as the Indian economy itself was concerned, Sinha said, except for certain defence supplies, sanctions had no meaning. The sentence compressed the finance ministry's three-year argument, that the punishment had never engaged the economy's real circuits of trade, remittance and domestic finance, into a single claim, and it was contested on its own terms by those who noted that defence supplies were not a trivial exception for a state re-equipping after the Kargil war.

### Q: What did Sumit Ganguly and Devin Hagerty argue about sanctions leverage?

In Fearful Symmetry: India-Pakistan Crises in the Shadow of Nuclear Weapons (2005), Sumit Ganguly and Devin T. Hagerty assessed the post-1998 sanctions as an exercise in coercive diplomacy and found the leverage slight. On their reading, the Glenn schedule extracted no rollback of the Indian weapons programme, no signature on the test ban treaty on Washington's timetable, and no fissile material halt, while its costs fell disproportionately on American exporters and on the bilateral relationship itself; what movement occurred came through the Jaswant Singh and Strobe Talbott dialogue's exchange of restraint declarations and strategic assurances, not through economic pain. Their account anchors the sanctions-response reading of the episode, against which the political-economic reading defended in this article measures itself.

### Q: How much did the Resurgent India Bonds cost to redeem in October 2003?

The Resurgent India Bonds matured on 1 October 2003, and the redemption's foreign exchange outgo was equivalent to 5.185 billion US dollars, against the 4.23 billion raised in August 1998, the difference representing the accumulated interest on the three currency tranches. The Reserve Bank of India's press release of the day records that it sold the full amount to the State Bank of India, meeting 2.794 billion dollars from its forward foreign currency receivables and the balance from reserves, against payment by the Bank of 22,151.83 crore rupees, with the exchange loss carried by the maintenance of value account for apportionment between the government and the Bank in the agreed ratio. The release described the redemption as very smooth, without volatility in the money, foreign exchange or securities markets.
