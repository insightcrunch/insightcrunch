The Representation of the People Act 1951 is the statute that decides who may stand for Parliament and the state legislatures, what conduct forfeits that eligibility, what a candidate must reveal before a single vote is cast, and which acts during a campaign can cost a returned candidate the seat itself. Its companion statute of the previous year built the electorate by providing for electoral rolls, delimitation and the allocation of seats, a division of labour examined in [the pillar on the Representation of the People Act 1950](/2018/01/01/representation-of-people-act-1950/), while the 1951 Act governs the contest: qualifications and disqualifications in Part II, the notification and conduct of elections in the Parts that follow, the registration of political parties, the machinery of the poll, corrupt practices and electoral offences, and the trial of election petitions in Part VI. A reader who finishes this article should be able to answer, with the section in hand, the questions that recur in every election season: what disqualifies a person, what must be disclosed in the nomination affidavit, what counts as a corrupt practice, and what the expenditure ceiling actually covers, which is narrower than almost anyone assumes.

![The Representation of the People Act 1951, showing the disqualification rules, candidate disclosure and corrupt practices - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-50.webp)

The Act is Act 43 of 1951. The number requires care, because the companion statute carries the identical number in its own year: the Representation of the People Act 1950 is Act 43 of 1950, and the statute treated here is Act 43 of 1951, so the year is not decoration but part of the identification. The 1951 Act received the assent of the President on 17 July 1951 and was enacted to give effect to the power that Articles 327 and 328 of the Constitution confer on Parliament and, subject to Parliament, on state legislatures to legislate on elections, a power that Article 324 couples with the superintendence of the Election Commission of India. It has been amended many times, and several of its most consequential features are not in the original text at all: the registration of parties arrived in 1989, the candidate disclosure affidavit in 2002 under judicial compulsion, and the removal of the protection that once allowed a convicted sitting member to continue in office was achieved not by Parliament but by the Supreme Court in 2013. The statute is therefore best read as a layered document, in which the 1951 skeleton carries provisions written in very different political moments, and the analysis below separates the layers wherever the distinction matters to a reader applying the text.

## The problem the Representation of the People Act 1951 was built to solve

A constitution can proclaim universal adult franchise, as Article 326 does, and can reserve the making of election law to the legislature, but it cannot by itself run an election or police a campaign. Between the constitutional promise and a counted vote there must be answers to questions of detail that are also questions of power: who is eligible to sit, who decides a disputed return, what a wealthy candidate may do that a poor one cannot, what a government servant may be made to do for a minister facing the electorate, and what follows when a winner has bought or frightened the constituency into returning him. The First General Elections of 1951 and 1952 were approaching when the Act was passed, conducted on the rolls prepared under the 1950 statute, and the new Parliament and assemblies had to be filled through a process that losers would accept as lawful. The 1951 Act supplied the conduct code for that exercise and for every general election since.

The tension inside the statute is permanent and worth naming at the outset. On one side stands the purity of the election, a phrase the courts use to describe a process in which the result reflects votes freely cast on adequate information. On the other side stands the freedom of the contest itself: candidates must be able to campaign vigorously, parties must be able to spend money organising support, and an election petition must not become a routine second chance for every defeated candidate to unsettle a result on technicalities. Nearly every contested provision in the Act is a point on the line between those two commitments. The disqualification chapters lean toward purity, excluding persons whose convictions or interests Parliament judged incompatible with membership. The expenditure chapter promises purity and then, by a single explanation, withdraws much of the promise. The corrupt practices chapter attempts the hardest line drawing of all, distinguishing persuasion, which is the essence of an election, from bribery, coercion, communal appeal and deception, which destroy it.

The Act also sits inside a wider field of election law that includes the constitutional text, the Conduct of Elections Rules 1961, the Election Symbols Order, and the Model Code of Conduct, which is not statutory at all; [the complete guide to election law in India](/2017/12/01/election-law-india-complete-guide/) maps that field as a whole, and this pillar stays with the 1951 statute, taking up the surrounding instruments only where a provision cannot be understood without them.

## What stood before the Act and why a single election code was needed

Colonial election law, contained in the Government of India Act 1935 and the rules and orders under it, governed legislatures elected on a restricted franchise divided by communal and property qualifications that the Constitution abolished at a stroke. A republic electing on adult suffrage, with reserved seats for the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes under Articles 330 and 332 and with joint electorates replacing separate ones, needed a fresh code rather than an adaptation. The Constituent Assembly had debated the design of the electoral system, including proportional representation, and settled on direct election from territorial constituencies for the House of the People and the Legislative Assemblies; the statutes of 1950 and 1951 translated that settlement into machinery. The 1950 Act dealt with the preparation of rolls, the qualifications of electors, delimitation and seat allocation. The 1951 Act took the contest itself, and its long life since then, through the replacement of ballot papers by electronic voting machines authorised by Section 61A, inserted in 1989, and through the introduction of the none of the above option after judicial direction in 2013, shows a text capacious enough to absorb technological and doctrinal change without replacement.

That longevity carries a cost a pillar article should record honestly. Because the Act is amended provision by provision, its parts are of very different vintages and philosophies. The disqualification list in Section 8 has grown by accretion as new special statutes were created, so that the section reads in part as an index of the criminal codes Parliament most feared at different moments. The disclosure regime, by contrast, is a coherent modern design, but one written by a court and only afterwards by Parliament. The expenditure provisions preserve a settlement of the nineteen seventies that every expert committee since has criticised and no government has replaced. Reading the Act whole, rather than as a bare list of sections, means keeping those vintages in view.

## How the Act is structured and where the weight falls

The Act runs to eleven Parts. Part I contains the short title and definitions, including the definition of a corrupt practice by reference to Section 123 and of disqualification by reference to the constitutional articles and Chapter III of Part II. Part II, the subject of the first half of this article, states qualifications for membership of Parliament and of the state legislatures, then the disqualifications for membership, then disqualifications for voting. Part III provides for the notification of general elections. Part IV governs administrative machinery, from the Chief Electoral Officer of a state to the presiding officers of polling stations. Part IVA, inserted in 1989, contains the single Section 29A on registration of political parties. Part V conducts the election itself: nomination of candidates, agents, the poll, counting, and the declaration of results. Part VA, on free supply of certain electoral roll material to candidates of recognised parties, is a small and specialised Part. Part VI, Sections 79 to 122, contains the dispute machinery of election petitions, which the specialist article in this cluster owns and which is summarised here only to the extent a candidate needs. Part VII contains the corrupt practices in Section 123 and the electoral offences in Sections 125 to 136. Part VIII deals with disqualifications arising from findings in election proceedings. Parts IX, X and XA cover by-elections, disputes regarding identity and miscellaneous matters including the bar on civil court jurisdiction in Section 170, and Part XI is general, containing the power of the Election Commission in relation to inquiries and the rule making power.

Two structural features deserve emphasis. The first is that the Act repeatedly works through the Election Commission rather than through the executive government of the day: the Commission decides disqualification questions referred under Articles 103 and 192 of the Constitution by giving its opinion to the President or the Governor, it orders disqualification for failure to lodge expense accounts under Section 10A, it registers parties under Section 29A, and it may remove or reduce most membership disqualifications under Section 11. The second is that the Act distinguishes sharply between a disqualification, which prevents a person from being chosen as or remaining a member, and an electoral offence or corrupt practice, which is conduct in an election that may found a prosecution, an election petition, or both. A candidate can commit an offence without being disqualified, can be disqualified without committing any offence, as where a government contract subsists, and can lose an election petition for a corrupt practice and then face the further question of disqualification under Section 8A. Keeping those tracks separate is the single most useful discipline in reading the statute.

## Qualifications for membership: the short chapters that open Part II

The qualification chapters are brief because the Constitution carries much of the load. Article 84 fixes the qualifications for Parliament: citizenship, an oath, a minimum age of thirty years for the Council of States and twenty five years for the House of the People, and such other qualifications as Parliament prescribes by law. Article 173 performs the same office for the state legislatures, with thirty years for a Legislative Council and twenty five for a Legislative Assembly. The 1951 Act supplies the statutory qualification in each case, and it is in substance a single idea: the candidate must be an elector. Section 3 provides that a person is not qualified to be chosen to fill a seat in the Council of States unless the person is an elector for a parliamentary constituency in India, the original requirement of an electorate within the state concerned having been removed by amendment in 2003, a change the Supreme Court upheld in Kuldip Nayar v Union of India in 2006 against the argument that it destroyed the federal character of the upper House. Section 4 requires a candidate for the House of the People to be an elector for a parliamentary constituency, with special provision for seats reserved for the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes and for autonomous districts of Assam. Sections 5 and 6 apply the elector requirement to the Legislative Assembly and the Legislative Council respectively, the Council candidate being required to be an elector for an assembly constituency in the state.

Three practical points follow from this spare design. First, the elector qualification connects the 1951 Act back to the 1950 Act, under which registration as an elector itself requires citizenship, an age of eighteen years, ordinary residence, and the absence of the voting disqualifications in Section 16 of that Act, so a defect in roll eligibility can surface as a defect in candidature. Second, a candidate for a reserved seat must belong to the caste or tribe for which the seat is reserved, in addition to being an elector, and contests over caste certificates are a recurring feature of scrutiny and of election petitions. Third, the constitutional disqualifications in Articles 102 and 191 operate alongside the statutory ones: holding an office of profit under the government, being of unsound mind as declared by a competent court, being an undischarged insolvent, and loss of citizenship disqualify at the constitutional level, while Section 10 of the Act, examined below, adds a narrower statutory cousin confined to offices in government companies. A candidate search that reads only the Act and not the Constitution will miss half the field, and a search that reads only the Constitution will miss the conviction disqualifications that dominate practice.

## Disqualifications for membership: the architecture of Sections 8 to 11

Chapter III of Part II is the heart of the pillar. Its sections divide the grounds into families, and the families differ in trigger, in whether a sentence length matters, in the period of exclusion, and in who can lift the bar. Section 7 supplies definitions, including that a person is disqualified where the disqualification is under the constitutional articles or under the Chapter. Section 8 deals with conviction. Section 8A deals with a finding of corrupt practice in an election petition. Section 9 deals with dismissal from government service for corruption or disloyalty. Section 9A deals with subsisting government contracts. Section 10 deals with office in a government company. Section 10A deals with failure to lodge the account of election expenses. Section 11 gives the Election Commission the power to remove or reduce a disqualification under the Chapter, except one under Section 8A. The table that follows is the findable artifact for the whole subject, and the prose after it works through each row.

| Section 8(1) listed offences, imprisonment imposed | Conviction and a sentence of imprisonment | No minimum threshold | Date of conviction | Until release and six years after release | Yes, under Section 11 | Same rule: the conviction must be stayed |

The table exposes the design choice that litigants most often miss. For the conviction grounds, Parliament did not trust the sentencing court to calibrate political exclusion, so it fixed periods in the statute and handed the softening power to the Election Commission under Section 11 rather than to the appellate process. The stay row matters because of a distinction the Supreme Court drew in cases including Rama Narang v Ramesh Narang and Ravikant S. Patil v Sarvabhouma S. Bagali: an appellate court may suspend the sentence, which relieves the convicted person of serving it for the time being, or may stay the conviction itself, a rarer order granted where the consequences of conviction are exceptional and irreversible, and only the second order arrests the disqualification. A headline that reports a stay without saying which kind was granted has not reported the legally decisive fact.

### How long does a conviction bar a person from contesting?

A conviction under Section 8(1) disqualifies from the date of conviction: for six years where only a fine is imposed, and, where imprisonment is imposed, throughout imprisonment and for six years after release. Section 8(2) applies the same pattern at a six month sentence, and Section 8(3) at two years.

The conviction provisions reward slow reading, because the three subsections of Section 8 work differently. Section 8(1) lists offences for which conviction alone disqualifies, irrespective of the sentence imposed, so that even a fine carries exclusion. The list in clause (a) covers specified provisions of the Indian Penal Code, including Section 153A on promoting enmity between groups, Sections 171E and 171F on bribery and undue influence at an election, offences against women in Sections 376 and the clauses that follow it, and Section 498A on cruelty by a husband or his relatives; the comparison of the old Penal Code with its replacement is treated in [the comparison of the Indian Penal Code and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita](/2011/02/01/ipc-vs-bns-comparison/), and the cross references in Section 8 have had to be read with the new code's corresponding provisions. The remaining clauses of Section 8(1) reach across the special statute book: the Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955, the Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act 1967, the Prevention of Corruption Act 1988, the Prevention of Terrorism Act 2002, the Foreign Exchange Regulation and Management Acts, the Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act 1985, the Commission for Sati (Prevention) Act, the Dowry Prohibition Act 1961 in the clause it shares with Section 8(2), the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act 1971, and the Pre-conception and Pre-natal Diagnostic Techniques Act. Section 8(2) covers offences under the laws against hoarding and profiteering, the adulteration of food and drugs, and the Dowry Prohibition Act, where the sentence is imprisonment for six months or more. Section 8(3) is the residuary clause: conviction for any other offence with imprisonment of not less than two years disqualifies from the date of conviction and for six years after release. The architecture is deliberate. Parliament picked out offences it considered corrosive of public life and made conviction for them disqualifying at any sentence, created a middle tier at a six month sentence, and set the general criminal threshold at two years, a line that ties disqualification to the gravity the sentencing court itself recorded.

The practical edge of Section 8(3) is that the length of the sentence, not the maximum for the offence, governs, so the same offence can disqualify one person and leave another eligible where the sentences differ. It is also the clause that produces the hardest cases in public debate, because a two year sentence for an offence involving defamation or a protest related conviction has the same electoral consequence as a two year sentence for a violent offence, and the statute makes no distinction between them once the threshold is crossed. Reform proposals from the Election Commission and the Law Commission have repeatedly addressed this area, including proposals to disqualify at the stage of framing of charges for serious offences, and Parliament has enacted none of them, so the trigger remains conviction by a trial court, with all the consequences that follow from the length of the appellate queue.

## Lily Thomas and the end of the convicted member's protection

Until 2013 the working of Section 8 was transformed for sitting members by Section 8(4), which provided that a disqualification under the conviction subsections did not take effect for a member of Parliament or of a state legislature until three months after the conviction, and, if an appeal or revision was filed within that period, until the appeal or revision was disposed of. The result was that a convicted legislator who appealed promptly continued to sit, vote and draw a salary for the whole life of the appeal, which in the Indian appellate system could mean the remainder of the term and beyond. A candidate who was not a member had no such shelter. The provision also sat in evident tension with Articles 101(3)(a) and 190(3)(a) of the Constitution, which provide that a seat becomes vacant when its holder becomes subject to a disqualification, and with Articles 102(1)(e) and 191(1)(e), which empower Parliament to make law on disqualifications without an obvious power to postpone the ones it makes.

In Lily Thomas v Union of India, decided on 10 July 2013 by a bench of A. K. Patnaik and S. J. Mukhopadhaya JJ., the Supreme Court struck Section 8(4) down as beyond the competence of Parliament. The reasoning was structural rather than moral. Articles 102(1)(e) and 191(1)(e) empower Parliament to prescribe disqualifications by law, and the same law governs a person being chosen as a member and a person being a member, because the Constitution uses the disqualification idea for both situations and provides in Articles 101 and 190 that the seat of a member who becomes disqualified falls vacant. A law that says a disqualification exists but shall not take effect for one class of persons, sitting members who appeal, is not a law prescribing a disqualification but a law suspending the constitutional consequence of one, and that Parliament could not do. The Court made the declaration prospective in effect for members whose appeals were already pending and protected, so the ruling fell on later convictions. The government of the day introduced a bill and then an ordinance to restore the protection; the ordinance was never promulgated and the bill was withdrawn, leaving the judgment as the governing position.

The consequence is the rule every candidate and journalist needs in its exact form. Disqualification under Section 8 operates from the date of conviction. A sitting member convicted in circumstances falling within Section 8(1), (2) or (3) loses the seat at once, subject to one qualification: if an appellate court stays the conviction itself, as distinct from merely suspending the sentence or granting bail, the disqualification is held in abeyance while the stay operates, and a member whose conviction is stayed has been treated as restored because the foundation of the disqualification has been judicially suspended. If the conviction is later affirmed, the disqualification revives. If the appeal succeeds in acquittal, there was a disqualification during the interval but no continuing bar, although a seat already filled by a by-election is not unwound. Both sides of the policy argument deserve their strongest statement, because the brief for this pillar requires it and because the question is genuinely open. The case for instantaneous disqualification, pressed by the petitioners in Lily Thomas and by the Election Commission in its reform proposals, is that a person whom a court has convicted of a serious offence ought not to make laws while an appeal meanders for years, and that the pre 2013 position created a perverse privilege of incumbency in which the convicted candidate was excluded and the convicted member was not. The case against, pressed by the Union in defence of Section 8(4), is that appellate reversal is not rare, that trial court convictions in politically charged cases may be vulnerable, and that a member who loses a seat and a term on a conviction later set aside has suffered an irreversible harm for which the legal system offers no remedy, since the by-election cannot be undone and the lost years cannot be restored. The Court answered the constitutional question and left the policy tension standing, and a fair reading of the statute records both.

## The remaining disqualifications: dismissal, contracts, company office and expenses

Section 9 disqualifies a person who, having held office under the Government of India or a state government, has been dismissed for corruption or for disloyalty to the State, for five years from the date of dismissal, and it makes a certificate of the Election Commission on the fact conclusive, subject to the dismissed person being heard before an adverse certificate issues. Section 9A disqualifies a person while a contract entered into in the course of trade or business with the appropriate government for the supply of goods or the execution of works subsists, with an explanation that a contract fully performed by the contractor does not subsist merely because the government has not performed its part. The ground is self curing: it lasts exactly as long as the interest lasts, and its rationale is the conflict between a member's duty to scrutinise the executive and a contractor's interest in the executive's favour. Section 10 disqualifies a person who is a managing agent, manager or secretary of a company or corporation, other than a cooperative society, in whose capital the appropriate government holds not less than twenty five per cent. This is narrower than the constitutional office of profit disqualification and should not be confused with it: a director of a government company is not caught by Section 10 unless holding one of the three named offices, although the constitutional provision may bite independently, and the long line of office of profit controversies, from the parliamentary secretary cases to the disqualification references decided on the opinion of the Commission, proceeds under Articles 102 and 191 and the Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Act 1959 rather than under this section.

Section 10A is the enforcement tail of the expenditure code. Where the Election Commission is satisfied that a person has failed to lodge an account of election expenses within the time and in the manner required, and has no good reason or justification for the failure, the Commission declares the person disqualified for three years from the date of the order published in the Official Gazette. The Supreme Court's reading of the neighbouring expenditure provisions in L. R. Shivaramagowda v T. M. Chandrashekar illustrates the temper of this jurisdiction: the account provisions are mandatory in purpose but the consequences the statute attaches are the ones it specifies, and a court may not enlarge them. Section 10A is also the provision through which expense enforcement actually bites in practice, because disqualification orders for non filing and for filing in the wrong manner are made in numbers after every general election, while a finding that a returned candidate exceeded the ceiling is a matter for an election petition and is correspondingly rare.

Two further heads complete the map. Section 8A provides that where the High Court, in trying an election petition, finds a person guilty of a corrupt practice, the question whether the person shall be disqualified, and for what period up to six years, is decided by the President after obtaining the opinion of the Election Commission, to which the case is submitted after the period for appealing the High Court order has run or any appeal has been decided. And Chapter IV adds disqualifications for voting: Section 11A disqualifies a person found guilty of a corrupt practice from voting for the period of the Section 8A disqualification, and a person convicted of offences under Sections 171E, 171F, or the offences in Sections 125, 135 and 135A, from voting for six years, with the Commission empowered by Section 11B to remove voting disqualifications. A person can therefore be barred from the ballot while remaining eligible to stand, and barred from standing while remaining on the roll, and the two bars have different sources and different custodians.

### Can the Election Commission remove a disqualification?

Section 11 empowers the Election Commission, for reasons recorded in writing, to remove any disqualification under Chapter III of Part II except one arising under Section 8A, or to reduce the period of any such disqualification. The power extends to the conviction disqualifications in Section 8 and to the Section 10A expense disqualification.

The Section 11 power is little known relative to its significance. It has been used sparingly, and its most discussed modern exercise was the reduction in 2019 of the Section 10A disqualification period of a Sikkim legislator, Prem Singh Tamang, from three years to thirteen months, shortly before he contested and won a by-election; the order recorded reasons and was defended by the Commission as within the text, and criticised by commentators who saw a broad mercy jurisdiction sitting uneasily beside fixed statutory periods. The provision exists because fixed periods are blunt: a disqualification that is just for a grave offence can be disproportionate for a technical expense default or an old conviction, and Parliament chose to locate the discretion in the constitutional election authority rather than in the government of the day. The exclusion of Section 8A from the power is equally deliberate, because a corrupt practice disqualification follows a judicial finding and a presidential decision, and Parliament left that chain undisturbed by administrative mercy.

## Nomination, scrutiny and rejection

Part V converts eligibility into candidature. The returning officer notifies the election, nomination papers are presented within the window fixed under Section 30, and Section 33 prescribes the form: a nomination paper completed in the prescribed form and subscribed by the candidate and by a proposer who is an elector of the constituency, with the number of proposers rising to ten where the candidate is not set up by a recognised party in a parliamentary or assembly election, a device that prices frivolous candidature without barring independents. Section 34 requires a deposit, twenty five thousand rupees for a parliamentary election and ten thousand for an assembly election, halved for candidates belonging to the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, forfeited under Section 158 where the candidate is not elected and fails to poll more than one sixth of the valid votes in the circumstances the section specifies. Before or at nomination the candidate must make the oath or affirmation required by the Constitution, administered as Section 33 requires. The general reader who needs the mechanics of a deposit or a proposer count for a specific election should treat the figures above as the statutory baseline of the Act and confirm the operative notification for the election in question, since the working detail sits in the Conduct of Elections Rules 1961 and the returning officer's handbook; the analysis here is legal information about the statutory scheme and not advice on any individual candidature, and nomination defects are decided on unforgiving timelines that make early professional advice essential in a live case.

Section 36 governs scrutiny, held on the date appointed after the last date for nominations, at which the returning officer examines the papers and decides objections, with power to reject a nomination on the grounds the section lists: that on the date fixed for scrutiny the candidate is not qualified or is disqualified under the Constitution or the Act, that the nomination paper or the oath requirement has not been complied with, or that a signature of the candidate or a proposer is not genuine. The returning officer may not reject a nomination for a defect that is not of a substantial character, a proviso that has generated a steady jurisprudence on the line between a substantial defect, such as a wrong electoral roll number that misidentifies the proposer, and an insubstantial one. Scrutiny is a summary proceeding and not a trial: the returning officer does not finally determine disputed questions of disqualification that turn on evidence, and the Constitution reserves the trial of a returned candidate's qualification to the election petition, which is why a candidate whose nomination was accepted may still be unseated, and why the improper acceptance or rejection of a nomination paper is itself a ground of voiding an election under Section 100, with the important asymmetry that improper rejection of a nomination voids the election without proof that the result was materially affected, while improper acceptance requires that proof.

## The constitutional frame within which the Act operates

The Act is subordinate legislation in the constitutional sense, and its provisions take colour from the six articles that frame it. Article 324 vests the superintendence, direction and control of the preparation of electoral rolls and the conduct of elections in the Election Commission, a plenary grant that the Supreme Court in Mohinder Singh Gill v Chief Election Commissioner in 1977 described as a reservoir of power sufficient to act where the enacted law is silent, though never to act against the enacted law. Article 325 forbids exclusion from a roll on grounds only of religion, race, caste or sex. Article 326 establishes adult suffrage, with the voting age reduced from twenty one to eighteen by the Constitution (Sixty first Amendment) Act 1988. Article 327 empowers Parliament to legislate on all matters relating to elections to Parliament and the state legislatures, including rolls and delimitation, and Article 328 gives state legislatures a parallel power subject to Parliament's. Article 329 bars interference by courts in electoral matters except through an election petition under law made by the appropriate legislature, the provision that channels every challenge to a completed election into Part VI and that the Court applied from N. P. Ponnuswami v Returning Officer in 1952 onwards to hold that an election process, once set in motion, cannot be interrupted by litigation. The disqualification articles complete the frame: Articles 102 and 191 state the grounds and empower Parliament to add to them by law, which is the source of Sections 8 to 10A; Articles 103 and 192 route disputes about a member's disqualification to the President or the Governor acting on the opinion of the Commission, whose opinion the Court in Brundaban Nayak v Election Commission treated as effectively decisive because the constitutional authority is bound to act in accordance with it. A candidate reading the Act without this frame will misunderstand the status of almost every actor in it: the returning officer acts under the Commission's superintendence, the Commission acts under a constitutional grant that the Act organises but does not create, and the courts intervene at the two points the Constitution marks, the petition after the election and the constitutional remedies against the legal framework itself.

The frame also explains a feature of the Act that puzzles first readers, its silence about the Model Code of Conduct. The Code is a set of conventions evolved by the Commission with the parties, enforced through the Commission's Article 324 power and its control of the election calendar rather than through the Act, and a breach of it is not an offence or a corrupt practice unless the same conduct also falls within a provision of the Act, such as Section 123(2) on undue influence or Section 126 on the silence period. The distinction matters in practice because most campaign controversy is Code controversy, while most legal consequence is Act consequence, and the two vocabularies are routinely confused in reporting.

## The amendment arc in outline

The Act of 1951 has never been replaced, so its history is a history of surgical amendment, and the pillar records the operations that changed its working character. The original text provided for election petitions to be tried by election tribunals appointed by the Election Commission; the Nineteenth Amendment of the Constitution in 1966 abolished the tribunals and the Representation of the People (Amendment) Act 1966 transferred the trial of petitions to the High Courts, where it remains, on the reasoning that a judicial forum of that rank better matched the gravity of unseating a member. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act 1974 inserted the expenditure explanation that reversed Kanwar Lal Gupta. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act 1975, passed during the Emergency, amended provisions including those on corrupt practices in ways that were substantially revisited after 1977, an episode that belongs to the political history of the statute and that explains the care with which later courts have read executive sponsored changes to the definition of corrupt practice. The year 1989 brought a cluster: Section 29A on party registration, Section 61A authorising electronic voting machines, Section 58A on countermanding for booth capturing, and the booth capturing offence in Section 135A. The Representation of the People (Amendment) Act 1996 made a set of changes that included the paid holiday in Section 135B and increases in deposits and proposer requirements directed at non serious candidature. The disclosure settlement of 2002 and 2003 has been told in its own section. The Representation of the People (Amendment) Act 2010 dealt with the overseas elector's registration through the 1950 Act and adjusted the 1951 Act in consequence. The Election Laws (Amendment) Act 2021, Act 49 of 2021, principally amended the 1950 Act to permit the voluntary linking of electoral roll data with the Aadhaar ecosystem, and made gender neutral language changes across the election statutes, substituting spouse for husband and wife in the service voter provisions. Each layer answers a pressure of its decade: tribunals to judicialisation, machines to the scale of the poll, disclosure to the criminalisation debate, and data linkage to the purity of the rolls.

## The disclosure affidavit: a regime written by a court and then by Parliament

No part of the Act better illustrates its layered character than candidate disclosure. The original statute required nothing of a candidate beyond qualification and a valid nomination. The modern affidavit exists because voters organised to demand it. In Union of India v Association for Democratic Reforms, decided on 2 May 2002, the Supreme Court held that the voter's right to know the antecedents of a candidate flows from the freedom of speech and expression in Article 19(1)(a), since a vote cast without knowledge of the candidate's criminal record, assets and education is not the informed exercise of the franchise the Article protects, and it directed the Election Commission to require every candidate to furnish information on affidavit: whether the candidate had been convicted, acquitted or discharged of a criminal offence or punished with imprisonment or fine; whether, during the six months before nomination, the candidate was accused in a pending case of an offence punishable with imprisonment of two years or more in which a charge had been framed or cognizance taken; the assets of the candidate, spouse and dependants; liabilities, in particular dues to government and public financial institutions; and educational qualifications. The Commission issued its order in June 2002. Parliament responded with the Representation of the People (Third Amendment) Act 2002, Act 72 of 2002, which inserted Section 33A requiring a narrower disclosure, criminal matters in the two categories of conviction and of pending cases in which a charge had been framed or cognizance taken, and Section 33B, which provided that no candidate was bound to disclose anything beyond what that Act required, notwithstanding any judgment or order of any court. In People's Union for Civil Liberties v Union of India, decided on 13 March 2003, the Court struck Section 33B down as violating the voter's Article 19(1)(a) right, holding that a legislature may not curtail a fundamental right that the Court had recognised by the device of declaring the judgment overridden, and it directed that the fuller disclosure operate. The resulting settlement is the law: Section 33A states the statutory floor, and the Commission's directions and Form 26 of the Conduct of Elections Rules 1961 carry the complete affidavit, covering convictions, pending cases in which charges have been framed, assets and liabilities of the candidate, spouse and dependants, sources of income added by later rule amendment, government dues, contracts and educational qualifications.

The enforcement provision is Section 125A, inserted by the same 2002 amendment. A candidate who fails to furnish information required under Section 33A, or gives false information which the candidate knows or has reason to believe to be false, or conceals information in the nomination paper or the affidavit, is punishable with imprisonment for a term which may extend to six months, or with fine, or with both. The sanction looks slight against the value of a seat, and prosecutions are uncommon, but the affidavit has acquired a second life beyond Section 125A: a false or suppressed disclosure can be deployed in an election petition, the affidavit is a public document published by the returning officer and displayed for voters, and the Commission's directions require a candidate against whom a criminal case is disclosed to publicise that fact in newspapers and on television during the campaign, a requirement the Supreme Court endorsed and elaborated in the contempt proceedings arising from its orders on criminal antecedents, including the directions of February 2020 requiring political parties to publish the criminal antecedents of their selected candidates and their reasons for selecting them. The disclosure regime is therefore best understood as an information remedy rather than a disqualification remedy: Parliament considered proposals to bar candidates against whom charges had been framed, the Law Commission's Report No. 244 of 2014 recommended disqualification at the charge framing stage for offences punishable with five years or more, with safeguards, and none of this was enacted. What the voter receives is knowledge, and the political cost of that knowledge is left to the election.

### What happens when a candidate files a false affidavit?

Filing an affidavit that the candidate knows to be false, or concealing required information in the nomination paper or affidavit, is an offence under Section 125A punishable with imprisonment of up to six months, or a fine, or both, and the same falsehood can be relied on in an election petition against a returned candidate.

The limits of the mechanism deserve the same precision as its contents. The affidavit is self declared, returning officers do not verify the truth of the entries at scrutiny, and the entries on assets are not reconciled against tax records by any election authority as a matter of routine. Analysis of affidavits by the Association for Democratic Reforms, which publishes candidate wise compilations from the affidavits themselves, has repeatedly shown large numbers of candidates disclosing pending criminal cases, which is the disclosure system working as designed and simultaneously an index of how little disclosure alone deters. The information remedy assumes a voter who reads, a press that reports, and a party that pays a price for nominating a candidate with a grave record; where those assumptions hold the affidavit disciplines, and where they fail it merely documents.

## Political parties: registration under Section 29A and the money provisions

Part IVA, containing Section 29A alone, was inserted by the Representation of the People (Amendment) Act 1988, Act 1 of 1989, with effect from 15 June 1989. An association or body of citizens calling itself a political party must apply to the Election Commission for registration within thirty days of its formation, furnishing its memorandum and rules, which must contain a provision that the party bears true faith and allegiance to the Constitution and to the principles of socialism, secularism and democracy and upholds the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India. The Commission registers the party after considering the material and hearing representatives, and its decision is final for the purposes of the Act. Registration is the gateway to the practical privileges of party status: recognition as a national or state party and the reservation of an election symbol are governed by the Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order 1968 rather than by the Act, but an unregistered party can obtain neither, and registration also underpins the tax and reporting regime. The Commission's power to deregister is narrow on the statute as read by the Supreme Court in Indian National Congress (I) v Institute of Social Welfare in 2002: the Commission may deregister only in limited situations, including where the registration was obtained by fraud, where the party itself communicates that it has changed its constitution in the manner Section 29A forbids, or where it is declared unlawful, and not merely because a party has ceased to contest elections or has become dormant, a gap that explains the long roll of registered unrecognised parties that contest nothing.

The money provisions for parties sit in Sections 29B and 29C, inserted by the Election and Other Related Laws (Amendment) Act 2003. Section 29B permits a political party to accept contributions offered by any person or company, other than a government company and subject to the companies legislation, and Section 29C obliges the treasurer of a party to prepare a report of contributions received in a financial year in excess of twenty thousand rupees from a person or a company, and to submit it to the Election Commission before the due date for its income tax return, with the consequence of default being loss of the tax exemption the party would otherwise enjoy. The electoral bond scheme, created by the Finance Act 2017, amended Section 29C to exempt contributions received through electoral bonds from the reporting requirement, and thereby removed the largest channel of party funding from the one disclosure the Act imposed; the scheme itself, together with the related amendments, was struck down by a five judge bench of the Supreme Court in Association for Democratic Reforms v Union of India on 15 February 2024 as violating the voter's right to information, and [the article on the electoral bonds law](/2018/02/15/electoral-bonds-law-india-explained/) owns that scheme and its fall, so this pillar records only the junction: the expenditure and contribution provisions of the 1951 Act cannot be understood in isolation from the bond interlude, because for the years the exemption operated, a party could lawfully receive sums through bonds that never entered its Section 29C report at all.

## Election expenditure: the account, the ceiling and the explanation that empties it

Section 77 is the provision on which the statute's reputation for weakness rests, and it must be quoted closely before it is criticised. Subsection (1) requires every candidate, personally or through the election agent, to keep a separate and correct account of all expenditure in connection with the election incurred or authorised by the candidate or the election agent between the date of nomination and the date of declaration of the result, both dates inclusive. Subsection (2) provides that the total of that expenditure shall not exceed such amount as may be prescribed, and the prescription sits in Rule 90 of the Conduct of Elections Rules 1961, which fixes different ceilings for parliamentary and assembly constituencies varying with the state, revised from time to time, most substantially in 2014 and again in 2022; the operative figure for any election is the figure in Rule 90 for that constituency at that election, and this article states the architecture rather than a number that the next revision would falsify. Section 78 requires every contesting candidate, within thirty days of the declaration of the result, to lodge with the district election officer a copy of the Section 77 account. Section 10A, already examined, disqualifies for three years a person who fails to lodge the account in time and in the required manner without good reason.

The decisive text is Explanation 1 to Section 77(1), and it has two limbs. The limb inserted after the judgment in Kanwar Lal Gupta v Amar Nath Chawla, in which the Supreme Court had held in 1974 that expenditure incurred by a political party in connection with a candidate's election fell within the candidate's account, declares that expenditure incurred by a political party or by any other association or body of persons or by any individual, other than the candidate or the election agent, shall not be deemed to be expenditure incurred or authorised by the candidate. Parliament reversed the judgment by the Election Laws (Amendment) Act 1974, Act 58 of 1974, and the explanation has survived challenge since; the Supreme Court in Common Cause v Union of India in 1996 read the account obligation strictly against the candidate's own expenditure, directed that the accounts be scrutinised meaningfully, and left the explanation intact because it is the enacted text. A second limb of Explanation 1, concerning the travel of party leaders, provides that expenditure incurred by leaders of a political party on travel by air or other means for propagating the programme of the party is not candidate expenditure, with Explanation 2 capping the leaders who qualify at forty for a recognised party and twenty for another party named before the election.

This is the article's namable claim, and it should be stated without ornament: the ceiling is on the candidate, not the campaign. A limit reported in every election as a cap on campaign spending binds exactly one participant in the campaign, the candidate together with the election agent, and expressly excludes the spending of the party that selected the candidate, of associations campaigning for the candidate, and of every individual supporter, however large and however directed at that constituency. The consequence is not a loophole discovered by ingenious accountants; it is the text, defended in Parliament in 1974 on the ground that a party campaigns for many candidates at once and that apportioning its general expenditure among them is impracticable, a defence with genuine force, since a national television campaign or a leader's tour serves hundreds of constituencies at once and any rule of attribution would be arbitrary at the margin and litigated in every close seat. The counter argument, made by the Law Commission in its Report No. 170 of 1999 on reform of the election laws and repeated by the Election Commission and the committee chaired by Indrajit Gupta on state funding of elections in 1998, is that the exclusion is so wide that the ceiling performs no real discipline on the money that decides visibility, and that the reforms which would give it effect, a ceiling on party expenditure or a statutory rule of apportionment, have been recommended repeatedly and not enacted. The Indrajit Gupta Committee recommended partial state funding in kind rather than a party ceiling; the Law Commission recommended regulatory strengthening; the voter's accurate conclusion is that India has a rigorously audited limit on the smallest of the three purses in an election, the candidate's own, and no limit on the other two.

### Does the expenditure ceiling limit what a party can spend?

The ceiling in Section 77 binds the candidate and the election agent alone. Explanation 1 to Section 77(1) declares that expenditure by a political party, an association or any other individual is not deemed to be expenditure incurred or authorised by the candidate, so party spending stands outside the candidate ceiling altogether.

Three corollaries keep the analysis honest. First, exceeding the candidate ceiling is a corrupt practice under Section 123(6), so the ceiling is not toothless within its own field: a returned candidate proved in an election petition to have incurred or authorised expenditure beyond the prescribed maximum faces the voiding of the election, and the evidentiary difficulty of proving authorisation beyond the account is the practical reason such findings are rare. Second, the account obligation itself is real and enforced through Section 10A in substantial numbers, and the Commission publishes expense summaries and takes action against defaulters and against accounts it finds incorrect after scrutiny by its expenditure observers, so the regime is best described as strong on filing and weak on totals. Third, the gap the explanation creates is filled in part by disclosure rather than by limits: parties file contribution reports under Section 29C and annual audited accounts for tax purposes, and candidates disclose assets in the Form 26 affidavit, so the system has chosen transparency about money over control of money at every point except the candidate's own ledger.

## Corrupt practices: Section 123 read clause by clause

Section 123 defines the corrupt practices for the purposes of the Act, and a finding that a returned candidate, or an agent or a person acting with the consent of the candidate or the election agent, committed a corrupt practice is the gravest result an election petition can produce: under Section 100 it voids the election, and under Section 99 the High Court names the persons proved guilty, which starts the Section 8A process. The standard of proof is correspondingly strict; an allegation of corrupt practice is treated as quasi criminal in character and must be proved beyond reasonable doubt on clear and cogent evidence, a discipline the Supreme Court has repeated from Rahim Khan v Khurshid Ahmed onwards. The clauses, in the order of the section, are these. Section 123(1) is bribery: any gift, offer or promise by a candidate or agent or, with consent, by another person, of any gratification to any person, with the object of directly or indirectly inducing an elector to vote or refrain from voting, or a person to stand, withdraw or retire from candidature, and the receipt of or agreement to receive gratification as a motive or reward for such conduct; gratification is not confined to money or to what is estimable in money, while a promise of a public action, as distinct from a personal benefit, falls outside the clause on its terms. Section 123(2) is undue influence: any direct or indirect interference or attempt to interfere with the free exercise of an electoral right, expressly including threats of injury of any kind, including social ostracism and excommunication or expulsion from a caste, and inducing a belief of divine displeasure or spiritual censure, while a declaration of public policy or a promise of public action is declared not to be interference. Section 123(3) is the appeal on the ground of religion, race, caste, community or language, or the use of religious or national symbols, to vote or refrain from voting, examined separately below. Section 123(3A) is the promotion of, or attempt to promote, feelings of enmity or hatred between different classes of citizens on grounds of religion, race, caste, community or language, by a candidate or agent or with consent, for the furtherance of the candidate's prospects or to prejudice another's election. Section 123(4) is the publication of a false statement of fact in relation to the personal character or conduct of a candidate, or in relation to candidature or withdrawal, which the publisher believes to be false or does not believe to be true, and which is reasonably calculated to prejudice the candidate's prospects; criticism of policy and of public conduct, and true statements, are outside it. Section 123(5) is the hiring or procuring, whether on payment or otherwise, of a vehicle or vessel for the conveyance of electors to or from a polling station, subject to exceptions for the candidate's own use, for hired vehicles used by electors at their own cost, and for public transport. Section 123(6) is incurring or authorising expenditure in excess of the Section 77 ceiling. Section 123(7) is obtaining or procuring, or attempting to obtain, any assistance, other than the giving of a vote, from a person in the service of the government belonging to the classes the clause specifies, revenue officers, police, excise and others listed in the clause, for the furtherance of the candidate's prospects, with a proviso protecting acts done in the ordinary discharge of official duty. Section 123(8), on booth capturing, was omitted by amendment in 1989 when booth capturing was made the subject of the offence in Section 135A, and a reader of an older text should not be confused by the gap in numbering.

The drafting pattern is consistent and instructive. Each clause identifies a way in which money, power, identity or deceit can displace the voter's free and informed choice, and each is hedged by a saving for legitimate campaigning: promises of public action are not bribery or undue influence, true statements are not within Section 123(4), and the candidate's own and voters' own transport arrangements are carved out of Section 123(5). The election petition cases are overwhelmingly arguments about which side of those hedges the proved facts fall on, which is why the section repays citation in its full texture rather than in the slogan form, corrupt practice means buying votes, in which it is usually summarised.

## The listed offences in Section 8(1) examined one family at a time

The list in Section 8(1) is the part of the Act most often quoted and least often read, and it rewards a family by family examination. The first family is the Penal Code group. It includes the offences that attack the electoral and communal order directly: Section 153A, promoting enmity between different groups on grounds of religion, race, place of birth, residence, language and other grounds, and doing acts prejudicial to the maintenance of harmony; Section 171E, bribery at an election; and Section 171F, undue influence at an election and personation, the Penal Code's own electoral offences, so that a conviction under the general criminal code for corrupting an election disqualifies under the election code without more. It includes the sexual offences in Sections 376 to 376D, rape and its aggravated and connected forms, and Section 376E, so that conviction for those offences disqualifies at any sentence, a placement that expresses a legislative judgment about fitness which the sentencing range does not qualify. It includes Section 498A, cruelty by a husband or his relatives, the provision whose electoral consequence is the least known in the list and which connects the disqualification code to the dowry and cruelty provisions elsewhere in Section 8.

The second family is the special statute group, and its logic is thematic rather than chronological. The Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955 punishes the enforcement of religious and social disabilities arising from untouchability, and a conviction under it disqualifies because the offence denies the equal citizenship the franchise presupposes. The prevention of insults legislation, the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act 1971, covers insult to the flag and the Constitution and prevention of the singing of the national anthem. The economic and regulatory statutes in the list, the foreign exchange legislation in its successive forms and the narcotics statute, treat trafficking and large scale exchange offences as fitness questions. The Unlawful Activities (Prevention) Act 1967 and the former Prevention of Terrorism Act 2002 place terrorism offences in the list, with the terrorism statute's repeal leaving its historical convictions in place. The Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act 1987 and the statute on pre natal diagnostic techniques, the Pre-conception and Pre-natal Diagnostic Techniques (Prohibition of Sex Selection) Act 1994, place offences against women at the two ends of life in the same list as the sexual offences of the Code. The Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 places the bribery of public servants in the list, so that the two bribery offences, the giver and taker offences of the corruption code and Sections 171E of the Penal Code, meet in a single disqualification. The analytical point across the families is that Parliament has used Section 8(1) as a register of offences it regards as disqualifying in kind, and the disputes in practice are rarely about whether an offence is listed; they are about the stage the criminal case has reached, since nothing in the section operates before conviction.

## Consent, agency and the proof discipline of Section 123

An election petition alleging a corrupt practice is fought over three questions that the statute's structure imposes: what was done, by whom, and with whose consent. The corrupt practice must be committed by the returned candidate, or by the election agent, or by another person with the consent of the candidate or the election agent, if the election is to be voided on that ground under Section 100(1)(b); a corrupt practice by a supporter acting independently, however helpful to the winner, falls under the different and harder ground in Section 100(1)(d)(ii), which requires proof that the result was materially affected. Consent may be express or inferred from circumstances, but it may not be presumed from benefit: the Supreme Court's cases, including Razik Ram v Jaswant Singh Chouhan and the line of decisions on the speeches of religious and political leaders made in a candidate's support, insist on evidence connecting the candidate to the act, such as presence at the meeting, prior arrangement, subsequent adoption of the appeal, or a relationship of agency in fact. The standard of proof is the criminal standard. The Court in Rahim Khan v Khurshid Ahmed in 1974 laid down that a charge of corrupt practice is akin to a criminal charge, to be proved beyond reasonable doubt, and that the evidence of witnesses in election cases must be assessed with the caution appropriate to interested testimony in a partisan contest; later cases, including Jeet Mohinder Singh v Harminder Singh Jassi, restate that the petitioner who alleges the charge bears the whole burden and that suspicion, however strong, is not proof.

This discipline explains the shape of election litigation. Petitioners plead particulars with the precision Section 83 demands because a vague charge will be struck out or will fail at proof; respondents defend on consent and on the savings inside each clause, the promise of public action against bribery, the statement of policy against undue influence, the truth of the statement against Section 123(4); and courts, conscious that they are annulling a democratic result, repeatedly say that the evidence must be cogent and the inference necessary rather than available. The institutional consequence is a body of law that is strict in statement and sparing in application, and a candidate who understands the consent doctrine understands the Act's central practical lesson: the campaign acts the candidate authorises, adopts or knowingly benefits from after the fact are the campaign's legal acts, and distance from a toxic supporter is a legal position that must be real to be worth anything.

## The religion appeal after Abhiram Singh: a widened clause and a strong dissent

Section 123(3) as enacted made it a corrupt practice for a candidate or agent, or a person with consent, to appeal to vote or refrain from voting on the ground of his religion, race, caste, community or language. The pronoun carried the case. On a narrow reading, his referred to the candidate, so that an appeal trading on the candidate's religion was barred while an appeal addressed to the voter's religion, asking electors of a community to vote as a community without tying the appeal to the candidate's own identity, escaped. On a wide reading, his covered the religion of the candidate, the agent, the voter and any person whose identity the appeal invoked. In Abhiram Singh v C. D. Commachen, decided on 2 January 2017 by a seven judge bench, the Supreme Court adopted the wide reading by a majority of four to three. The majority, in opinions led by Madan B. Lokur J. with the concurrence of T. S. Thakur C.J. among the four, reasoned purposively: an election is a secular exercise, the State has no religion, the constitutional commitment to secularism informs the reading of the provision, and the mischief of the clause is an appeal that converts religious identity into electoral currency, whichever side of the transaction supplies the identity. The appeal barred by the clause is therefore an appeal on the ground of the religion of the candidate, the agent, the voter or any other person referenced by the appeal.

The dissent, delivered by D. Y. Chandrachud J. for himself and two colleagues, is among the most consequential dissents in election law and the stance charter of this series requires it at full strength. It argued that the text's pronoun, in its grammatical setting, referred to the candidate; that reading it to cover every identity invoked in political speech would forbid a candidate from addressing the legitimate grievances of religious, caste and linguistic groups, since a demand for the uplift of a disadvantaged community, or a protest against discrimination practised on religious grounds, is itself an appeal framed in the vocabulary of identity; and that identity based mobilisation has been a vehicle through which historically subordinated groups have entered democratic politics, so that a prohibition drawn too wide chills the speech of the marginalised more than the majoritarian abuse at which the clause aims. The majority answered that the clause bars an appeal to vote on the ground of identity and not discussion of a community's concerns, but it did not elaborate a test separating the two, and election courts have worked case by case since. The fair verdict on the authorities is this: the wide reading is the law, its rationale is the secular character of the electoral process, and its boundary with legitimate representation of group interests remains the least settled working line in Section 123, to be drawn by election courts on facts, speech by speech.

### Is an appeal to religion a corrupt practice after 2017?

An appeal to vote or to refrain from voting on the ground of religion, race, caste, community or language is a corrupt practice under Section 123(3), and after the seven judge decision in Abhiram Singh in 2017 the ground includes the religion of the candidate, the agent, the voter or any other person the appeal invokes.

Two neighbouring provisions keep the clause in proportion. Section 123(3A) separately catches the promotion of enmity or hatred between classes of citizens on the same grounds, so a speech that vilifies another community is reachable even if it never asks for a vote in terms, and Section 125, among the offences, makes promoting enmity between classes in connection with an election a criminal offence punishable with imprisonment of up to three years, or fine, or both. The same speech can therefore be a corrupt practice that voids a seat, an offence that founds a prosecution, and a ground of action under the Penal Code's enmity provisions, and the three proceedings run on different standards of proof and different timelines.

## Electoral offences: Sections 125 to 136 and the paid holiday

Part VII's second half creates the electoral offences, which are criminal prohibitions enforceable by prosecution independently of any election petition. Section 125 punishes promoting enmity between classes in connection with an election. Section 126 prohibits public meetings during the forty eight hours ending with the hour fixed for the conclusion of the poll, the silence period, punishable with imprisonment of up to two years, or fine, or both. Section 127 punishes disorderly conduct at or near election meetings, and Section 127A regulates the printing of election pamphlets and posters, requiring the name and address of the printer and publisher on the face of the document and the deposit of copies with the election authorities. Section 128 imposes the duty of maintaining secrecy of voting on officers, candidates and agents present at polling and counting. Section 129 forbids officers acting for a candidate or influencing voting. Section 130 prohibits canvassing in or within one hundred metres of a polling station on polling day, and displaying election matter or acting in a manner calculated to influence, within that perimeter. Section 131 punishes disorderly conduct in or near a polling station, Section 132 provides for the removal of a person who misconducts himself there, and Section 132A prescribes penalties for breaches of the official duty provisions. Section 133 addresses the breach of official duty in connection with an election by a returning officer or other official, Section 134 breaches of duty by officials in specified circumstances, Section 134A the penalty for a government servant acting as an election agent, polling agent or counting agent of a candidate, and Section 134B the prohibition on going armed to or near a polling station. Section 135 punishes the removal of ballot papers from a polling station, and Section 135A, inserted by amendment in 1989, creates the offence of booth capturing, capturing a polling station or a place of counting, compelling the surrender of ballot papers or voting machines, intimidating electors or obstructing the poll, punishable with imprisonment of not less than one year and extending to three years and fine, and, for a person in government service, not less than three years and extending to five years and fine; the same amendment expanded the Election Commission's power in Section 58A to adjourn a poll or countermand an election on the ground of booth capturing. Section 135B, inserted in 1996, requires every person employed in a business, trade or industrial undertaking or other establishment to be granted a paid holiday on the day of the poll, so that employment is not the price of the franchise, with a penalty for an employer in breach and an exception for an employee whose absence would cause danger or substantial loss in relation to the employment; establishments close or release staff under corresponding directions for state elections as well. Section 135C prohibits the sale and distribution of liquor within a polling area during the forty eight hours ending with the close of poll, and Section 136 collects other offences, including fraudulent defacement or destruction of nomination papers, unauthorised supply of ballot papers, and fraudulent application for a postal ballot, with their penalties.

The offences repay attention for what they reveal about the statute's picture of the voter. The prohibitions cluster around the polling station's perimeter and the last forty eight hours, the space and time in which pressure is hardest to resist and least visible; the paid holiday and the liquor ban protect the conditions of choice rather than the choice itself; and the booth capturing offence, born of the era in which strongmen seized polling stations in parts of the country and compelled presiding officers to surrender ballots, addresses the collapse of the poll as a physical event. A modern reader who knows elections as a contest of advertising should register that the statute's security provisions were written against the capture of paper, premises and persons, and that the offences against the information environment, impersonation, false statements and paid news among them, are handled by the older and narrower tools of Sections 123(4), 125, 127A and the impersonation offence in Section 136 together with the Penal Code.

## The dispute machinery in outline: Part VI and what Article 198 owns

Part VI, Sections 79 to 122, provides that no election shall be called in question except by an election petition presented to the High Court, Articles 329(b) of the Constitution having barred every other route, and it governs the parties to a petition, its contents, the relief that may be claimed, trial, the grounds in Section 100 on which an election may be declared void, the naming of persons guilty of corrupt practices in Section 99, appeals to the Supreme Court under Section 116A, costs and security, and the communication of the High Court's orders to the Election Commission and the presiding officers. The grounds in Section 100 repay a summary here because they define what the rest of this article is ultimately for: an election is void where the returned candidate was not qualified or was disqualified on the date of election, where a corrupt practice was committed by the returned candidate or the election agent or with consent, where a nomination was improperly rejected, or where the result was materially affected by improper acceptance of a nomination, by a corrupt practice committed in the interests of the returned candidate by another person, by improper reception, refusal or rejection of votes, or by non compliance with the Constitution, the Act or the rules, in the cases the section distinguishes. An election petition must be filed within forty five days of the date of election of the returned candidate, tried by a single judge of the High Court, and concluded, as the statute exhorts, within six months, an exhortation practice routinely exceeds. The procedure in depth, the disqualification process that follows a finding, and the case law on material effect belong to [the article on election petitions and disqualification](/2018/03/15/election-petitions-disqualification-india/), which owns those questions for the cluster; this pillar supplies the substantive law of eligibility, disclosure and conduct that a petition tests.

Two features of the machinery bear on the substance and so are recorded here. The first is the severity of the corrupt practice jurisdiction: the charge must be pleaded with full particulars under Section 83, proved beyond reasonable doubt, and a finding unseats a member on the conduct of agents and consenting supporters as well as the candidate's own hand, which is why consent is the most litigated word in Section 123 litigation. The second is the division of labour between the petition and the disqualification reference: a question whether a sitting member has become disqualified under the statutory or constitutional grounds is decided by the President or the Governor under Articles 103 or 192 on the opinion of the Election Commission, and not by an election petition, which lies only against an election and only at the instance of a candidate or an elector of the constituency and within its short limitation. Confusing the two routes is the commonest procedural error in public discussion of a disqualification, and the distinction explains why a conviction operates automatically while a corrupt practice finding travels through a petition, a presidential decision and the Commission's opinion.

## Three worked passages: the code applied to the cases that recur

A statute pillar earns its length where it applies the text to the situations readers actually face, and three recur constantly. The first is the convicted sitting member. A member of a Legislative Assembly is convicted of an offence under the general criminal law and sentenced to imprisonment for two years. On the day of conviction the disqualification in Section 8(3) attaches, the seat falls vacant under Article 190(3)(a), and the Assembly secretariat notifies the vacancy; the member's appeal and application for bail proceed, and if the appellate court suspends the sentence the member leaves custody but the disqualification and the vacancy stand, because the conviction is untouched. If the appellate court, persuaded that the case falls in the exceptional class, stays the conviction itself, the disqualification is suspended and the member's position revives for the life of the stay. If no stay of conviction is obtained, the Election Commission proceeds to a by-election within the statutory period, the constituency elects a new member, and the appellate outcome, even an eventual acquittal, does not displace the successor. Every step in that sequence is in the text or in Lily Thomas, and every common error about it, that bail restores the seat, that filing an appeal suspends the disqualification, that acquittal restores the member, is a survival of the Section 8(4) world that ended in 2013.

The second passage is the candidate with pending cases. A candidate against whom charges have been framed for a serious offence, but who has not been convicted, is not disqualified by anything in Sections 8 to 10A, and a returning officer who rejected the nomination on that ground would act unlawfully. The statute's answer runs through disclosure instead: the Form 26 affidavit must reveal the case, the candidate must publicise the criminal antecedents in the manner the Commission directs, the party must publish its reasons for the selection, and the voter's remedy is at the ballot. The reform debate about moving the trigger to the framing of charges, recommended with safeguards by the Law Commission in its Report No. 244 of 2014 and resisted on the ground that charges are an accusation and not a finding, with the attendant risk of motivated prosecution timed to an election, is a debate about replacing this design and not a description of it.

The third passage is the independent candidate and the machinery of nomination. A candidate not set up by a recognised party needs ten proposers who are electors of the constituency, the statutory deposit, a completed nomination paper in the prescribed form, the oath taken within the window the Act prescribes, and a Form 26 affidavit that is complete, because an affidavit left blank in a material particular has been treated in the case law, including the Supreme Court's decisions on wholly blank affidavits, as a defect going to the validity of the nomination itself, while a false entry travels the different route of Section 125A and the election petition. At scrutiny the returning officer rejects for substantial defects only; at the count the deposit turns on the one sixth line in Section 158; and after the result the expense account is due in thirty days whatever the margin of defeat. The independent's passage through the Act is the same road as the party candidate's, with the tolls set a little higher at the entrance, by the design of Sections 33 and 34 against non serious candidature.

## The poll and the count: Part V machinery that the substantive law presupposes

The conduct provisions of Part V are usually left to the machinery articles, but three of them carry substantive consequences a candidate must know. The first is the countermanding rule in Section 52. In its original form the death of any candidate before the poll required the election to be countermanded and begun afresh, a rule open to abuse and indifferent to the difference between a party candidate and a fringe independent; the 1996 amendment confined countermanding to the death of a candidate set up by a recognised political party, with the party permitted to nominate a replacement within the period the section prescribes, while the death of another candidate leaves the poll to proceed among the remaining contestants. The second is the adjournment and repoll machinery of Sections 57 and 58 and the destruction or tampering provisions of Section 58, together with Section 58A on booth capturing, which together mark the boundary between an irregularity that an election petition will later weigh for material effect and a breakdown so complete that the Commission stops the process and begins the affected part again. The third is the technological text: Section 61A, inserted in 1989, authorises the use of voting machines at elections in the constituencies the Commission specifies, and the machine became universal at parliamentary and assembly elections in the years after its insertion; the paper trail unit followed administrative and judicial pressure, and the none of the above option on the machine followed the Supreme Court's judgment in People's Union for Civil Liberties v Union of India in 2013, which held that a voter must be able to record a rejection of all candidates in secrecy as an incident of the freedom of expression in voting. Counting is governed by Part V's later chapters and the Conduct of Elections Rules, with the returning officer declaring the result in the statutory form, a declaration whose finality is the premise of the forty five day petition clock recorded in this article.

The service voter and postal ballot provisions deserve a sentence in a pillar because they decide who can participate from a distance: members of the armed forces and of forces to which the Army Act applies, persons employed under the Government of India in posts outside India, and certain state police personnel serving away from home, together with their spouses residing with them, register as service voters under the companion Act and vote by postal ballot or by proxy in the classified service voter scheme, while election duty staff, electors under preventive detention and specified categories vote by postal ballot under the rules. The 2021 amendment's substitution of spouse for the older gendered words in these provisions is the most recent textual change in this corner of the Act.

## How a disqualification actually proceeds: orders, references and the Gazette

A reader who knows the grounds but not the procedure will still misread the news, because each ground travels by its own road. A conviction disqualification under Section 8 needs no order at all: it operates by force of the statute on the conviction, the House secretariat notifies the vacancy that Article 101 or 190 declares, and any dispute about whether the conviction falls within the section is fought in the courts or, where a question of a sitting member's disqualification is raised in the constitutional form, through the Article 103 or 192 reference. A Section 10A disqualification exists only when the Election Commission makes and publishes its order in the Official Gazette after finding the failure to lodge the account and the absence of good reason, and the three years run from that order, not from the election. A Section 8A disqualification exists only at the end of the petition, the naming under Section 99, the submission of the case, the Commission's opinion and the President's decision. A corrupt practice finding against a person who is not the returned candidate travels the same Section 8A road and cannot be short circuited by the Commission acting alone. An office of profit question against a sitting member travels by reference to the President or the Governor, who obtains the opinion of the Commission and acts in accordance with it. A Section 11 removal or reduction is an order of the Commission for recorded reasons, published and open to judicial review on the ordinary administrative law grounds. The discipline of asking by which road a reported disqualification has arrived, and at which milestone the reported case stands, resolves most of the confusion in public discussion of the subject, where a charge sheet, a conviction, a Commission order and a presidential decision are routinely reported as though they were the same event.

The same procedural map explains the tempo of the code. The grounds that operate automatically, conviction and the constitutional grounds, are the grounds whose facts are established by another forum's record, a court's conviction or the fact of an office held. The grounds that require an order are the grounds whose facts the election authority must itself find, the expense default, or whose gravity must be assessed after a judicial finding, the corrupt practice. The Act trusts records it does not create and inquires into facts it cannot borrow, and that division, more than any single section, is its administrative philosophy.

## Inside the expense account: what the candidate's ledger must capture in practice

The Section 77 account is not an abstraction, and a section on its working contents shows why the ceiling debate is really two debates. Within the candidate's own field, the account must capture the expenditure the candidate and the election agent incur or authorise on the constituency campaign: public meetings and processions in the constituency and the share of joint meetings the instructions attribute, posters, banners, handbills and hoardings, advertisements in print and electronic media placed for the candidate, vehicles used in the constituency, the campaign office, agents and workers' payments and refreshments, and travel of the candidate within the constituency. The Election Commission's expenditure monitoring apparatus, built under its Article 324 superintendence around the statutory account, deploys expenditure observers, maintains shadow observation registers in which its teams record events and their estimated cost from independent sources, issues notices where the observed figure and the candidate's day to day register diverge, and conducts a final scrutiny before the account is taken as lodged; candidates must also file the affidavit of expenditure particulars the Commission's instructions require with the account. The result is that within its field the Indian regime is administratively serious: a candidate who omits a rally that the observers recorded will be asked about it in writing, and the Section 10A jurisdiction for accounts lodged in the wrong manner has real content.

Outside the candidate's field, the same administrative seriousness has nothing to attach to, because the explanation removes the subject matter. The party's advertising that names the party and its leader, the association's campaign that supports a slate, the supporter's independent effort, none of it enters any account that the Act requires to be reconciled against a ceiling. Reform drafting has therefore always had to choose its instrument: bring defined categories of party expenditure in the election period inside a ceiling of their own, attribute a share of party expenditure to candidates by a statutory formula, or leave the structure and strengthen disclosure. The first two are legislative choices Parliament has not made; the third is the direction practice has taken by default, through contribution reports, audited party accounts for tax exemption, and the affidavit disclosures of candidates. A reader evaluating any future amendment should ask first which of the three instruments it is, because amendments described as expenditure reform have repeatedly turned out to be disclosure reform in the third sense, leaving the explanation, and with it the article's namable claim, exactly where it stood.

## Section 123(4) and the line between the personal and the political

Of the corrupt practices, Section 123(4) is the one whose boundary does the most work in a modern campaign, because it governs attack. The clause requires a statement of fact, not opinion; in relation to the personal character or conduct of a candidate, or to candidature or withdrawal; which the maker believes to be false or does not believe to be true; and which is reasonably calculated to prejudice the candidate's prospects. Each element excludes a territory. Statements about a candidate's public record, policy, competence in office and public conduct are the currency of elections and outside the clause even when harsh, exaggerated in emphasis or unfair in implication, provided they are statements the maker believes true; statements of opinion and comment are outside it because they are not statements of fact; and a false statement of fact about a matter unconnected with personal character or conduct, a misstated date or a wrong figure about a public project, strains the clause because the personal element is missing. The litigated core is the allegation about private life, financial probity in personal dealings, and fabricated incidents presented as fact to the electorate, and the cases turn on proof of the statement, of its factual character, of the maker's belief, and of consent where the maker was not the candidate. In an information environment in which a fabricated statement can be distributed by accounts unconnected with any candidate, the consent requirement and the belief requirement together mean the clause reaches the campaign that adopts or authors the falsehood and leaves the unattached falsehood to the criminal law of defamation and the platform's own processes, a gap that is structural in the section and not a failure of its drafting for the era of the printed pamphlet, which Section 127A's printer and publisher identification was built to police.

## The two Acts together: reading the 1951 statute against its 1950 companion

The shared Act number of the two Representation of the People Acts is a coincidence of the legislative lists of consecutive years, but their division of labour is a deliberate settlement that a pillar should state in one place. The 1950 Act answers the question who the people are for electoral purposes: it allocates seats in the House of the People and the assemblies, provides for delimitation of constituencies, defines the elector through citizenship, age and ordinary residence, disqualifies persons from registration in the cases Section 16 states, including non citizenship, unsoundness of mind declared by a competent court, and disqualification for corrupt practice or other election offences for the period the law fixes, and builds the registration machinery of electoral registration officers and rolls. The 1951 Act presupposes that electorate and answers who may be chosen from it and by what rules the choosing proceeds. The seams between them are where practical questions live. A person struck off the roll under the 1950 Act cannot satisfy the elector qualification that Sections 3 to 6 of the 1951 Act demand for candidature, so a roll defect becomes a candidature defect. A person disqualified from voting under Section 11A of the 1951 Act remains on the roll in the sense of registration law unless the 1950 Act's provisions also apply, which is why the statutes speak of disqualification from voting rather than removal from the roll. Delimitation under the 1950 Act and the Delimitation Acts fixes the constituencies within which the 1951 Act's returning officers, deposits calculated by election type and expenditure ceilings prescribed by rule operate. Reserved seats illustrate the join exactly: the Constitution reserves them, the 1950 Act's framework and the delimitation process identify the constituencies, and the 1951 Act's Sections 4 and 5 confine candidature for them to members of the castes and tribes concerned. The practitioner reads the two Acts as one code in two volumes, the journalist who confuses them misattributes every provision at the seam, and the candidate verifies eligibility in both before filing anything, because the nomination the returning officer scrutinises under Section 36 of the 1951 Act fails if either volume defeats it.

## The institutions the Act works through

The Act creates no commission of its own; it presupposes the Election Commission of India established by Article 324 and builds the field machinery beneath it. Sections 13A to 13CC provide for a Chief Electoral Officer for each state, designated in consultation with the state government and subject to the Commission's control, district election officers, electoral registration officers shared with the 1950 Act's roll machinery, returning officers for each constituency, and assistant returning officers; Part IV continues with observers whom the Commission may nominate, presiding and polling officers drawn in practice from government service, and police arrangements at the poll. The significance of this administrative chapter for the substantive provisions is easy to miss. The returning officer is the officer who receives the nomination and the affidavit, conducts scrutiny, and accepts or rejects; the district election officer receives the expense account under Section 78; the Commission's expenditure observers, deployed under its plenary superintendence, shadow the account process; and the Commission's jurisdiction under Sections 10A and 11 and its advisory jurisdiction under Articles 103 and 192 make it the continuing custodian of the disqualification code between elections. The 1951 Act's scheme is therefore unintelligible as a pure court enforced code: its day to day life is administrative, in the hands of the returning officer and the Commission, and its courtroom life, in the election petition, is the exceptional and retrospective part.

The Act also governs the special elections that interrupt the cycle: Part IX provides for by-elections to fill casual vacancies within the periods the Constitution and the section prescribe, subject to the Commission's certification of difficulty, and the same qualification, disclosure and expenditure rules apply to the by-election candidate, a point that matters because the disqualification consequences of Lily Thomas are usually worked out precisely at a by-election, when the seat vacated by a convicted member is filled while the appeal is pending and the constituency acquires a new member whose election the appellate result does not disturb.

## The honest assessment: what works, what does not, and why

The disqualification code works, in the limited sense that it operates. Its triggers are objective, conviction, dismissal, subsistence of a contract, non filing of an account, and they do not depend on the discretion of the government of the day; after 2013 its most criticised shelter is gone; and the Section 11 mercy power, whatever controversy attends individual exercises, is textual, reasoned and located in the Commission. Its weaknesses are of scope rather than of operation: the trigger is conviction at the end of a trial process that is itself slow, so that the candidate against whom grave charges have been framed and who may be convicted years after the term ends is untouched; the two year threshold in Section 8(3) treats the sentence as the measure of gravity, which imports the accidents of sentencing into the law of eligibility; and the list in Section 8(1) grows by reference to special statutes, so that its coherence depends on the drafting of statutes enacted for other purposes. The disclosure regime works as an information system and fails as a deterrent system: the affidavit is public, standardised and machine readable in practice, parties and candidates know their records will be compiled and compared, and the proportion of legislators facing criminal cases has not fallen, which suggests that voters and parties price the information differently from the reformers who demanded it, a finding about democratic preferences that the statute cannot by itself reverse. The expenditure regime works as an accounting system and fails as a limiting system, for the textual reason this article has made its centre: the ceiling governs the candidate's purse only, and the explanation holds the party's purse outside it. The corrupt practices code works as a standard and struggles as a remedy: its definitions are elaborate and its case law deep, but the petition route is slow, the proof standard is properly demanding, and a finding arrives, when it arrives, years into the term it annuls. The offences code works best where its prohibitions are physical and immediate, the silence period, the polling station perimeter, the paid holiday, booth capturing, and least where the harm is informational and scaled, in the flood of unattributed digital campaigning that Sections 127A and 123(4) were not built to reach.

A verdict follows from that pattern, and the stance charter requires one. The 1951 Act is a strong statute of eligibility and a weak statute of money. Where it speaks in prohibitions enforced by objective events, conviction, dismissal, non filing, it bites; where it must govern the flow of resources through organisations, it was disarmed by its own explanation in 1974 and has not been rearmed. The reform agenda that would change the balance is known and repeatedly written down: a ceiling or apportionment rule for party expenditure in the election period, disqualification consequences attached to a false affidavit that go beyond a six month offence, and a decision, one way or the other, on the Law Commission's proposal to move the criminal trigger from conviction to the framing of charges for the gravest offences. Each has been recommended by a body Parliament respects and enacted by no Parliament, which is itself the finding: the distribution of advantage under the present text suits those who would have to amend it, and the statute's most important provision remains an explanation that most voters have never read.

Readers building a working command of this cluster should take the dispute and process questions to the election petitions article linked above, the voter side of the settlement to the 1950 Act pillar, the bonds episode to its own article, and the field as a whole to the election law guide. For a reader keeping research notes on a statute of this density, with its disqualification table, its amendment layers and its case law, the discipline that pays is a private file organised by section rather than by headline; a reader may [keep a protected research file on this statute and its cases](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) in exactly that form. What the pillar leaves with the reader is the One Test answer in its final shape: what disqualifies a person is Sections 8 to 10A, read with the constitutional grounds; what must be disclosed is the Form 26 affidavit built on Section 33A; what counts as a corrupt practice is Section 123, read after Abhiram Singh; and what the expenditure limit covers is the candidate and the agent under Section 77, and no one else, which is the sentence about Indian election law most worth remembering exactly.

## After the finding: Section 99 naming and the consequences beyond the seat

The consequence structure of a corrupt practice finding is wider than the loss of one election, and it completes the pillar's map of what a campaign act can cost. At the conclusion of the trial of an election petition, Section 99 requires the High Court, where a charge of corrupt practice is made in the petition and a person other than the returned candidate, or the returned candidate in the circumstances the section covers, is proved guilty, to record the names of all persons proved guilty of a corrupt practice and the nature of the practice, after hearing persons who are not parties in the manner the section and the proviso prescribe. The naming is not a formality: it is the trigger of the Section 8A submission, on which the President, on the Commission's opinion, may disqualify the named person for up to six years, and it carries a voting disqualification under Section 11A for the same period. A person named may therefore lose no seat, never having held one, and yet be excluded from candidature and from the ballot for years, which is why agents, star campaigners and party officials are within the real reach of the corrupt practices code and why the consent findings against them are fought as hard as the finding against the candidate. Section 99 also requires the High Court to report cases in which a corrupt practice is proved in connection with an election so that the machinery of submission is set in motion, and the orders are communicated to the Commission and the presiding officers as Part VI directs. The design, accusation by petition, proof to the criminal standard, naming by the court, exclusion by the President on the Commission's opinion, distributes the gravest non criminal sanction in election law across three institutions, none of which can impose it alone, and that distribution is the deliberate constitutional temper of the whole Part.

One further seam between eligibility and the roll deserves record, because it decides real candidatures. Registration as an elector is constituency specific, and the elector qualification in Sections 4 and 5 is satisfied by registration in any parliamentary or assembly constituency as the section in question requires, not necessarily the constituency contested, which is why a candidate may lawfully contest a seat in a state or constituency where the candidate does not reside. The system thereby separates the voter's local stake, expressed through ordinary residence in the roll, from the candidate's national eligibility, and the recurring political complaint against outside candidates is a complaint the statute has considered and answered in favour of openness, subject only to the reserved seat conditions and the Council of States framework that Kuldip Nayar tested.

The same openness explains the treatment of the candidate who is an elector in one state and a contestant in another for the House of the People: the nomination is examined against the roll entry the candidate claims, certified extracts of the roll accompany the paper where the entry lies outside the constituency, and a mistake in transcribing the roll particulars is judged at scrutiny by the substantial defect standard of Section 36, which protects the candidature where identity is clear and defeats it where the entry cannot be found or belongs to another person.

## The reform record: what has been recommended and what has been enacted

The gap between the statute and its critics is documented with unusual completeness, because the critics are official. The Committee on Electoral Reforms of 1990, chaired by Dinesh Goswami, recommended measures on state funding, the strengthening of the Commission's hand and the discouragement of non serious candidates, parts of which entered the 1996 amendments. The Indrajit Gupta Committee of 1998 recommended partial state funding of elections in kind, free airtime and supplies among them, on the express ground that the money race distorted competition, and its scheme was not enacted as a system. The Law Commission's Report No. 170 of 1999, on reform of the election laws, addressed the expenditure explanation among a wide field of proposals and recommended a regulatory architecture for parties, including accounts and audit, that has been enacted only in fragments, notably the contribution reporting of Sections 29B and 29C in 2003. The Election Commission's own compendia of proposed electoral reforms, issued and reissued over the decades, have consistently sought the disqualification of persons against whom charges have been framed for serious offences, a bar on a person contesting from more than one constituency, and stronger expense enforcement, and the Commission repeats the proposals because they remain unenacted. The Law Commission's Report No. 244 of 2014, on electoral disqualifications, recommended disqualification at the stage of framing of charges for offences punishable with imprisonment of five years or more, confined by safeguards including that the charge be framed at least a year before the scrutiny of nominations and that the trial be expedited, and a conviction based regime for the rest; it also recommended a punishment enhancement for false affidavits. The Second Administrative Reforms Commission and the National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution addressed the same territory in their reports on ethics in governance and on the working of the Constitution respectively.

Set against that record, the changes that have actually been made to the working of the Act since 1989 divide instructively. The courts supplied the two largest: the disclosure regime, against Parliament's initial resistance, and the removal of the convicted member's shelter in Lily Thomas. Parliament supplied the administrative and participatory improvements: machines, party registration, the paid holiday, deposit and proposer discipline, and the roll reforms of the companion Act. The reforms that would redistribute advantage among candidates and parties, party expenditure control, charge stage disqualification, state funding, remain proposals. A pillar article should end that account without euphemism: on the evidence of three decades, the Act changes where the change disciplines individuals and stalls where it would discipline organisations, and the explanation to Section 77 is the standing monument of that asymmetry.

## A comparative glance: how another Westminster statute draws the same lines

Comparison disciplines description, and one comparison is safe because the kinship is textual. The United Kingdom's Representation of the People Act 1983, which shares the Indian statute's name by descent from the same parliamentary tradition, disqualifies a person from membership of the House of Commons, under the Representation of the People Act 1981, where the person is detained in a penal institution serving a sentence of more than one year, a conviction based trigger with a lower sentence threshold than Section 8(3) and without the Indian list of offences disqualifying at any sentence. The British statute's corrupt and illegal practices descend from the same nineteenth century legislation as Section 123's ancestors, and its election court retains the power to void an election for corrupt practice with consequences for the candidate's future eligibility that resemble the Section 8A process. The instructive divergence is on money: the British scheme caps the candidate's expenses and, since the Political Parties, Elections and Referendums Act 2000, separately caps and registers party campaign expenditure at the national level, which is precisely the second ceiling that the Indian explanation withholds. The comparison proves nothing by itself about what India should enact, since constituency scale, party structure and enforcement capacity differ, but it disposes of the argument that a party ceiling is a conceptual impossibility in a Westminster system: a closely related system operates one, and the Indian choice to the contrary is a choice, located in a single explanation, reversible by a short amendment, and not a necessity of electoral administration.

## The affidavit anatomised: what Form 26 actually asks and how it is policed

Form 26 repays a walk through its parts, because each part answers a different anxiety in the disclosure design. The criminal part comes first and is the most litigated: the candidate declares cases in which convicted, with the section of the Act and a description of the offence, the court, the case number, the dates, the punishment imposed, and whether an appeal is pending; then cases pending in which a charge has been framed by a court for an offence punishable with imprisonment of two years or more; then cases in which cognizance has been taken; then cases in which the candidate was acquitted or discharged, the design being that the voter's picture is of record and not of allegation alone in the first category and of accusation judicially entertained in the second. The asset part requires movable assets in detail, cash, bank deposits, investments in bonds, shares and units, personal loans and advances given, motor vehicles, jewellery and other assets, for the candidate, the spouse and the dependants, and immovable property with its location, survey numbers, area, inherited or self acquired character, date and cost of purchase and approximate market value. The liability part requires loans from banks, financial institutions and others, and dues to government departments itemised by the service concerned, housing, water, electricity, telephone, transport and tax dues among them, with a declaration whether any other liability is in dispute. The candidate declares sources of income of self, spouse and dependants, contracts with the appropriate government and public companies, the profession or occupation of self and spouse, and educational qualifications with the institutions and years of the highest examination passed. The affidavit is sworn before an oath commissioner, notary or magistrate, and the returning officer publishes it; the candidate's own obligation to publicise criminal antecedents in print and electronic media during the campaign period, on the Commission's directions as approved by the Supreme Court, converts the filing from a record into a broadcast.

The policing of the form is deliberately light at the door and heavier afterwards. The returning officer at scrutiny does not try the truth of the entries, but the Supreme Court in Resurgence India v Election Commission of India, decided in 2013, held that the affidavit is an integral part of the nomination, that a voter can make an informed choice only on a complete disclosure, and that a returning officer may reject a nomination where the affidavit has been left blank in material respects or the information required has not been furnished at all, after giving the candidate an opportunity to file a complete affidavit within the time. The distinction that results is principled: completeness is policed at scrutiny, truth is policed by Section 125A prosecution and by the election petition, in which suppression of a criminal case or a material asset has been argued, with varying success on varying benches, as a corrupt practice of undue influence or as a ground of material non compliance. Lok Prahari's litigation seeking consequences for unexplained growth in the declared assets of legislators across successive affidavits produced directions on the examination of affidavit discrepancies by the tax authorities rather than a new disqualification, which is consistent with the design: the affidavit creates the record, other institutions act on it, and the voter's judgment remains the sanction of first resort.

## The office of profit: the constitutional ground that overshadows Section 10

No disqualification generates more references to the Election Commission than the office of profit, and its placement must be exact. Articles 102(1)(a) and 191(1)(a) disqualify a person holding any office of profit under the Government of India or a state government, other than an office declared by the legislature by law not to disqualify its holder. The Act's Section 10 is not this provision; it is a separate and narrower bar on three named offices in government companies. The office of profit field is governed by the constitutional text, by the Parliament (Prevention of Disqualification) Act 1959 and its state counterparts, which list offices that do not disqualify, and by the case law on what an office of profit is. The tests the courts apply ask whether the government appoints the holder, whether the government can remove the holder, whether the government pays remuneration, whether the office carries functions the government controls, and whether the office yields profit in the sense of any pecuniary gain, a test the Supreme Court applied in Jaya Bachchan v Union of India in 2006 to hold that the chairpersonship of the Uttar Pradesh Film Development Council was an office of profit notwithstanding that the holder said she drew no benefit, because the office was capable of yielding profit and was held under the state government. The recurring modern controversy, the appointment of legislators as parliamentary secretaries and to boards and corporations, turns on the state exemption laws and on whether the appointment evades the constitutional cap on the size of ministries, and it is decided through the Article 103 and 192 reference to the President or Governor on the Commission's opinion. The candidate's practical rule is therefore two staged: check Section 10 for the statutory company offices, and then check the constitutional ground and the exemption statute of the relevant legislature for everything else, because the second check is where disqualifications in this family are actually found.

## The vote itself: Section 62 and the disqualifications for voting

The Act also decides, at the level of the individual election, who may cast a vote in it. Section 62 provides that a person whose name is entered in the electoral roll for the constituency shall be entitled to vote, and then withdraws the entitlement in the cases that matter. Section 62(5) bars a person confined in a prison, whether under a sentence of imprisonment or in the lawful custody of the police, from voting, excepting a person subjected to preventive detention under a law in force; the Supreme Court upheld the bar in Anukul Chandra Pradhan v Union of India in 1997 against an equality and franchise challenge, reasoning that the franchise is statutory in its exercise and that the custodial restriction is a reasonable incident of lawful confinement administered through the roll machinery. The consequence, much debated in the criminalisation literature, is an asymmetry the reader should register precisely: a person in custody may contest an election, the custody being no disqualification under Sections 8 to 10A and the nomination being capable of completion through the processes the rules allow, and may not vote in it, the vote being barred by Section 62(5) until release. Parliament considered and declined proposals to bar candidature by persons in custody or against whom charges are framed, and the asymmetry is therefore the enacted position and not an oversight. Section 11A adds the electoral disqualifications for voting recorded earlier in this article, for corrupt practice findings and for conviction of the specified election offences, and the two sets together, custody and Section 11A, complete the Act's law of the excluded voter.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Representation of the People Act 1951 govern?

The Act governs the conduct of elections to Parliament and to the legislatures of the states. It states the qualifications for membership, the disqualifications that bar a person from being chosen as or remaining a member, the manner in which elections are notified and conducted, the registration of political parties, the corrupt practices and electoral offences that pollute a contest, and the trial of election petitions by the High Court. It is Act 43 of 1951. Its companion statute, the Representation of the People Act 1950, governs the preparation of electoral rolls, the allocation of seats and delimitation, so the two Acts divide election law between the electorate and the contest.

### Q: What disqualifies a person from contesting an election?

The statutory grounds sit in Chapter III of Part II of the Act. Section 8 disqualifies on conviction for listed offences at any sentence, for a middle group of offences at six months of imprisonment, and for any other offence at two years or more. Section 8A covers a finding of corrupt practice, Section 9 dismissal from government service for corruption or disloyalty for five years, Section 9A a subsisting government contract, Section 10 a managerial office in a government company, and Section 10A failure to lodge the election expense account for three years. The constitutional grounds in Articles 102 and 191, including an office of profit, operate alongside these.

### Q: When does a conviction disqualify a sitting legislator from the House?

From the date of conviction itself. Section 8(4) once postponed the disqualification for a sitting member who appealed within three months, until the appeal was decided, but the Supreme Court struck that provision down in Lily Thomas v Union of India in 2013 as beyond the competence of Parliament, because Articles 101 and 190 make the seat of a disqualified member vacant. The member therefore loses the seat on conviction within Sections 8(1), 8(2) or 8(3), unless an appellate court stays the conviction itself. A stay or suspension of the sentence alone, or the grant of bail, leaves the disqualification operating.

### Q: Which acts count as a corrupt practice in an election?

Section 123 lists them. Bribery, in the giving or receiving of gratification to induce a vote or a candidature; undue influence, meaning interference with the free exercise of an electoral right, including threats and appeals to divine displeasure; an appeal to vote or refrain from voting on the ground of religion, race, caste, community or language; promoting enmity or hatred between classes of citizens on those grounds; publishing a false statement of fact about a candidate's personal character or conduct; hiring vehicles to convey electors to the poll; spending beyond the prescribed expenditure maximum; and obtaining the assistance of specified government servants. A proved corrupt practice voids the election under Section 100.

### Q: What must a candidate disclose when filing nomination?

The candidate files an affidavit, in Form 26 under the Conduct of Elections Rules 1961, built on the duty in Section 33A of the Act. It discloses convictions and any acquittal or discharge, pending criminal cases in which a charge has been framed or cognizance has been taken, the assets of the candidate, the spouse and dependants, liabilities including dues to government and public financial institutions, sources of income, government contracts, and educational qualifications. The regime descends from the Supreme Court's direction in Association for Democratic Reforms in 2002, which grounded the voter's right to know in Article 19(1)(a), and from the judgment in 2003 striking down the legislative attempt to confine it.

### Q: What are the limits on election expenditure?

Section 77 requires every candidate to keep a separate and correct account of all expenditure incurred or authorised by the candidate or the election agent between the date of nomination and the date of declaration of the result, and Section 77(2) caps the total at the amount prescribed in Rule 90 of the Conduct of Elections Rules 1961, which varies by election and by state. The account must be lodged within thirty days of the declaration under Section 78. The limit binds the candidate alone: Explanation 1 to Section 77(1) excludes expenditure by the political party, by any association and by any other individual from the candidate's account.

### Q: How are political parties registered?

Under Section 29A, inserted in 1989, an association or body of citizens of India calling itself a political party applies to the Election Commission for registration within thirty days of its formation. The application carries the party's name, its memorandum or rules and regulations, and the names of its office bearers, and the memorandum must affirm true faith and allegiance to the Constitution of India and to the principles of socialism, secularism and democracy, and the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India. The Commission considers the material, hears the party's representatives where it thinks fit, and registers the party, a step distinct from recognition as a national or state party under the Symbols Order.

### Q: What electoral offences does the 1951 Act create?

Part VII creates offences alongside the corrupt practices. They include promoting enmity between classes in connection with an election under Section 125, holding a public meeting during the forty eight hours before the close of poll under Section 126, disorderly conduct at meetings and polling stations, canvassing within one hundred metres of a polling station under Section 130, removing ballot papers, booth capturing under Section 135A, acting as an agent while a government servant, and breaches of official duty by election officials. Section 135B requires employers to grant a paid holiday on polling day, and Section 135C bars the sale of liquor in a polling area during the closing period.

### Q: How does a disqualification under Section 8A arise?

Section 8A is the only disqualification in the Act that begins with a judicial finding and ends with a political authority. Where the High Court trying an election petition finds a person guilty of a corrupt practice and names the person under Section 99, the case is submitted, after the appeal period or any appeal, for the decision of the President on the question whether the person should be disqualified and, if so, for what period up to six years. The President decides after obtaining the opinion of the Election Commission. The Election Commission's mercy power in Section 11 does not extend to a Section 8A disqualification.

### Q: What deposit must a candidate make to contest an election?

Section 34 requires a deposit with the nomination: twenty five thousand rupees for an election to the House of the People and ten thousand rupees for an election to a Legislative Assembly, and half those amounts where the candidate belongs to a Scheduled Caste or a Scheduled Tribe, the reduced rate applying also in specified cases for reserved constituencies. The deposit may be made in cash with the returning officer or in the Reserve Bank or a government treasury, and a nomination paper not accompanied by the required deposit is liable to rejection at scrutiny. The figure is a statutory baseline that the candidate confirms against the election notification.

### Q: When is a candidate's security deposit forfeited?

Section 158 governs the return and forfeiture of deposits. The deposit is returned where the candidate's nomination was not accepted, where the candidate withdraws in time, where the candidate dies before the poll, and where the candidate is elected. Where the candidate is not elected, the deposit is forfeited if the total number of valid votes polled by the candidate does not exceed one sixth of the total number of valid votes polled in the constituency, with special computation for multi member and proportional contests. The one sixth threshold is why the deposit operates as a filter against token candidature while leaving a serious losing candidate whole.

### Q: What does the returning officer examine at the scrutiny of nomination papers?

Under Section 36 the returning officer examines the nomination papers of all candidates on the date fixed for scrutiny, hears objections, and decides them summarily. A nomination may be rejected where the candidate is not qualified or is disqualified on the date fixed for scrutiny under the Constitution or the Act, where the nomination paper or the oath requirement has not been complied with, or where a signature of the candidate or a proposer is not genuine. The officer may not reject a paper for a defect that is not of a substantial character. Scrutiny is not a trial, so disputed questions of fact fit for evidence are left to the election petition.

### Q: What is the difference between a stay of conviction and a stay of sentence?

The distinction decides whether a convicted legislator keeps a seat. A stay or suspension of the sentence suspends its execution, so the person does not serve the imprisonment while the appeal is heard, but the conviction stands and the Section 8 disqualification continues to operate. A stay of the conviction itself, which an appellate court grants sparingly where the consequences of the conviction are exceptional and cannot be undone, suspends the foundation of the disqualification, and the bar is held in abeyance while the stay lasts. The Supreme Court drew the line in decisions including Rama Narang and Ravikant S. Patil, and a report of a stay is incomplete without saying which kind was granted.

### Q: Why does the two year sentence threshold in Section 8(3) matter?

Section 8(3) is the residuary conviction disqualification: it catches a conviction for any offence not listed in Sections 8(1) or 8(2) only where the sentence imposed is imprisonment for not less than two years. The governing figure is the sentence the court actually awards, not the maximum the offence carries, so two persons convicted of the same offence can stand differently, one disqualified from conviction and for six years after release, the other eligible throughout. The threshold ties political exclusion to the gravity the sentencing court recorded, and it is also why the precise sentence, and any appellate change to it, is the first thing to verify in any reported disqualification.

### Q: What is booth capturing and how does the Act punish it?

Section 135A, inserted in 1989, makes booth capturing an offence. It covers capturing a polling station or a place fixed for the poll or counting, compelling a presiding or polling officer to surrender ballot papers or voting machines, taking possession of a polling station and allowing only supporters to vote, and intimidating or obstructing electors and officials in connected ways. The punishment is imprisonment of not less than one year and up to three years with fine, and, where the offender is a person in government service, imprisonment of not less than three years and up to five years with fine. Section 58A empowers the Commission to adjourn or countermand the poll for booth capturing.

### Q: Must an employer grant a paid holiday on the day of the poll?

Yes. Section 135B, inserted in 1996, provides that every person employed in a business, trade, industrial undertaking or other establishment, who is entitled to vote at an election to the House of the People or a Legislative Assembly, shall be granted a holiday on the day of the poll, and that no deduction shall be made from wages on account of the holiday. An employer in contravention is punishable with fine. The obligation has an exception for an employee whose absence would cause danger or substantial loss in relation to the employment. The provision treats the wage for the polling day as part of the machinery of a free election.

### Q: What is the period for filing an election petition?

An election can be called in question only by an election petition under Part VI of the Act, presented to the High Court within forty five days from the date of election of the returned candidate, the date specified in Section 81. The petition may be presented by a candidate at the election or by an elector of the constituency, must state the material facts and, where a corrupt practice is alleged, full particulars under Section 83, and is tried by a single judge of the High Court, with an appeal to the Supreme Court under Section 116A. Missing the forty five days is fatal, since the petition jurisdiction is entirely statutory.

### Q: What contributions must a political party report to the Election Commission?

Section 29C requires the treasurer of a political party to prepare, for each financial year, a report of contributions in excess of twenty thousand rupees received from any person, and of contributions received from companies, and to submit the report to the Election Commission before the due date for furnishing its income tax return for that year. The sanction for default is indirect but real: a party that does not submit the report loses the income tax exemption on its income for that year. The electoral bond exemption inserted in 2017 removed bond contributions from this report until the scheme was struck down in 2024.

### Q: What is the silence period before the close of poll?

Section 126 prohibits the convening, holding or attending of any public meeting or procession in connection with an election in a polling area during the period of forty eight hours ending with the hour fixed for the conclusion of the poll in that area, and bars the display of election matter by cinematograph, television or similar apparatus, and the propagation of election matter at musical concerts and entertainments, in the same period. Contravention is punishable with imprisonment of up to two years, or fine, or both. The Election Commission's campaign end directions and the Model Code operate alongside the section, but Section 126 is the statutory prohibition.

### Q: What follows if a returned candidate is proved to have exceeded the expenditure maximum?

Exceeding the ceiling is not merely an accounting default. Section 123(6) makes the incurring or authorising of expenditure in contravention of Section 77 a corrupt practice, so a petitioner who proves the excess against a returned candidate in an election petition obtains a declaration under Section 100 that the election is void, and the candidate may then face the Section 8A process on the strength of the finding. Separately, failure to lodge the account at all, or to lodge it in the required manner without good reason, attracts disqualification for three years under Section 10A on the order of the Election Commission. Proof of authorisation is the practical battleground in the ceiling cases.
