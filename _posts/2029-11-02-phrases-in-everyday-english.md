The phrases of Romeo and Juliet in everyday English are the play's most widely distributed property, and also its most carelessly catalogued. Speakers who have never opened the text call a doomed couple star-crossed, curse two quarrelling parties with a plague on both their houses, dismiss a hopeless search as a wild-goose chase, and take leave with parting is such sweet sorrow, and each of these utterances is a small act of quotation performed without a quoter. The standard account of this traffic says that the play coined its phrases, that a single inventive mind stocked the language the way a merchant stocks a shelf. The record, examined phrase by phrase against the dictionaries, supports a different and more interesting verdict. The play invented less than the legend claims and achieved more than invention would explain. Its distinctive gift was naturalization: it took compounds, curses, proverbs, and racing slang, set them at the emotional pressure points of a story everyone would retell, and sent them out with enough force to survive the death of the world that produced them. A census that grades each claimed export by the documentary record, rather than by the warmth of the claim, finds a small core of full idioms, a penumbra of quotations that behave like idioms on ceremonial occasions, a set of famous lines that never left the quotation marks, and a residue of phrases credited to the play that belong to Chaucer, to another Shakespeare play, or to the following century. That graded shape, and not a raw count, is the real measure of the play's gift to English.

![The phrases of Romeo and Juliet passing into everyday English speech - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-83.webp)

The verdict can be stated plainly at the outset, because everything that follows is its evidence. Of the expressions habitually listed as the play's donations, only a handful pass every test a lexicographer can apply: attestation in the text, first surviving record in the text or decisive popularization by it, and a living career outside quotation marks in the mouths of speakers who owe the play nothing. Star-crossed and wild-goose chase pass all three tests, though the second passes them in a sense its first speaker would not recognize. The plague curse passes as a grammatical template rather than as a fixed string. The balcony scene's three great exports, wherefore art thou, the rose sentence, and the sweet sorrow couplet, pass the first two tests and live a double life, quoted by people who know the source and used by people who do not, which is why companion studies in this series treat each of them at full length. Fortune's fool and the Friar's violent delights remain quotations, one at steady low heat, the other after a dormancy of four centuries. And the tail of the popular lists, the fool's errand, love is blind, break the ice, fails the first test outright: those words stand in other texts, or in no text of the period at all. A census that prints the failures beside the passes is not pedantry. The failures are data, because they show the gravitational pull the play exerts on the language's memory, attracting neighbouring phrases into its orbit until the attribution itself becomes a fact about the play's fame rather than about its text.

## What Should Count as a Phrase the Play Gave English?

Every popular list of the play's phrases smuggles in its own definition of giving, and the lists disagree because the definitions do. One compiler counts any string of words that appears in the play and also appears in speech, which credits the play with good night, a phrase English speakers had exchanged for centuries before the Capulet orchard was imagined. Another counts only strings the dictionaries record first in the play, which is a defensible rule that still confuses the survival of paper with the birth of speech. A third counts by fame, and fame is the worst witness of all, because fame is precisely what bends attribution toward the most famous available source. The census applied here uses three tests in sequence, and an expression earns a place among the play's exports only by its performance against all of them, graded rather than gated, so that partial passes are reported as partial.

The first test is presence: the words, in recognizably the exported form, must stand in the play's text in a reputable edition. This test sounds too easy to fail, and it eliminates a surprising share of the standard lists, including two of the most repeated items in the cluster this article was commissioned to survey. The second test is priority or propulsion: either the surviving written record begins in this play, or the play can be shown to have carried an older expression into general currency. Priority is a claim about documents, and it must be stated in the documents' own grammar, as a first known use with a date, not as a birth certificate. Propulsion is harder to prove and is claimed here only where the pre-play record is thin and the post-play record is thick and continuous. The third test is independence: the expression must do work for speakers who are not quoting, in headlines, conversation, and prose that makes no gesture toward Verona. A line that appears only inside quotation marks, however often, is a quotation, and the census has a grade for quotations; it simply refuses to call them idioms.

### What three tests does a phrase have to pass to enter this census?

A phrase enters the census as a full export only if the words stand in the play, if the documentary record either begins there or thickens decisively there, and if speakers use the expression without quoting it. Weaker performances are not discarded but graded downward, as popularizations, templates, quotations, or attributions the record does not support.

The grading language matters because the alternative, the flat verb coined, has done measurable damage to the public understanding of how phrases travel. David Crystal, the language scholar whose Shakespeare's Words, written with Ben Crystal, remains the standard glossary of the playwright's vocabulary, has spent a career insisting on the distinction between a word's first surviving record and its invention. The Oxford English Dictionary's earliest citations for the playwright's works number in the thousands; a University of Oxford count of the dictionary's quotation base puts his total citations at roughly 33,300, with just over 2,000 of them standing as first quotations, the earliest instance the dictionary's readers had found. Popular retelling converts that archival fact into the claim that one man invented 1,700 or more words, and the conversion is an error about what a citation is. A first citation marks where the paper trail starts. It cannot mark where speech started, because the spoken language of the 1590s survives only where someone wrote it down, and the plays were written down, printed, reprinted, and preserved with a care granted to almost no other vernacular writing of the decade. The playwright is therefore the earliest surviving witness for hundreds of expressions he may merely have been the first to write where posterity could read it.

Crystal's discipline is the correct starting point, and this census adopts it, with one adjudication that cuts the other way and matters for everything that follows. For single words, the first-record evidence is at its weakest, because a lone word can circulate in speech for generations beneath the notice of print. For set phrases, the evidence firms up considerably. A phrase is long enough to be a fingerprint. When a distinctive string like star-cross'd lovers or a plague o' both your houses has no earlier attestation and then appears in the play and then everywhere, the inference that the play launched it is far stronger than the parallel inference for a compound adjective standing alone. The popularizers are therefore wrong in their method and partly right in their conclusion, at least for this play's phrases: the gift is real, but it is a gift of launching and fixing rather than of minting from nothing, and the census grades say so entry by entry. The disagreement between the coinage legend and Crystal's correction is adjudicated here in those terms: Crystal wins on what the evidence is, and the legend keeps only the residue that survives his correction, which for set phrases is substantial.

The tests earn their keep on the hard cases, and two worked examples show the grading in motion before the glossary prints its results. Take good night. Presence passes trivially, since the words stand in the play a dozen times over; priority fails outright, the exchange being centuries old when the play was written; independence passes in a sense that proves nothing, since the phrase was independent before the playwright was born. The composite grade, popularization at most, records a real phenomenon without theft: the play took a universal formula and built its most famous scene of parting out of its repetition, and the scene's fame re-taught the formula to every later century as a lovers' instrument. The play is the phrase's greatest exhibitor and not its donor, and a census that cannot say so will end up crediting it with the alphabet. Take death-marked from the other end. Presence passes; priority passes, the compound's first surviving record standing in the prologue; independence fails completely, since outside editions and scholarship the word has no life at all. The composite grade, stayed home, is the mirror image of good night's: there the play exhibited what it did not make, here it made what it could not exhibit into currency. Between those two failures, in the narrow band where presence, priority, and independence coincide, the genuine exports live, and the narrowness of the band is the census's first quantitative finding: of everything the play is praised for giving the language, almost nothing passes all three gates, and the little that does is extraordinary precisely for passing.

Two full idioms, one template, three ceremonial quotations, two quieter quotations, one compound that stayed home, one popularization, two boundary cases, and three exclusions: that is the census of the most phrase-famous play in English. The legend promises a shelf of coinages; the record delivers a distribution, and the distribution has a shape that the rest of this survey exists to explain. The passes cluster where the play's rhetoric runs hottest, in curses, fate-labels, contest metaphors, and the great scene of parting, and the failures and silences cluster around the play's machinery, the letters, potions, and errands that move the plot and donate nothing. Before the shape can be argued, the principal entries deserve their individual hearings, at the depth a census allows: full for the entries no companion study owns, and deliberately summary for the three balcony exports and the star-crossed compound, whose deep records stand in their own articles in this series.

A word on reading the grades, since the glossary will be misused by anyone who treats it as a league table. The grades are not ranks of merit, and the census's stayed-home entries are not its failures of art. Death-marked is a finer piece of word-making than several expressions that out-travelled it, and fortune's fool has consoled more essayists than the wild-goose chase has headline writers. The grades measure one thing only, the documentary career of a string of words in the wild, and they measure it because that career, and not the critic's admiration, is what the phrase in everyday English means. A reader who finishes the glossary persuaded that the play gave the language exactly two idioms has read it correctly and understood it least; the glossary's subject is the whole system, passes, custodies, near misses, boundary cases, and thefts together, and the system is the gift.

## Star-Crossed: The Prologue's Compound and What It Shed

The census's strongest entry is also its most compressed. Fourteen lines into the play, before any character has spoken, the Chorus delivers the compound on which the whole export trade is founded: a pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life. The early quartos spell the word starre-crost, a spelling worth pausing over because it shows the compound being assembled in the printing house from living parts, star and crossed in the astrological sense of thwarted by an adverse aspect, rather than borrowed as a finished unit. Merriam-Webster's dictionary entry for star-crossed, defined as not favored by the stars, ill-fated, gives the first known use as 1597, the year of the play's first quarto, and prints the prologue line as its illustrative quotation. No earlier attestation of the compound has been produced. By the census's three tests this is as clean a pass as the evidence of the period permits: presence in the text, priority in the record, and an independence so complete that the word is applied to business ventures, political campaigns, and sporting fixtures by writers who could not say which play it came from. The deep study of this phrase, its astrology and its afterlife, stands in this series under the title [Star-Crossed Lovers: What the Phrase Really Means](/2022/03/25/star-crossed-lovers-meaning-explained/); the census needs only its grade and one observation about its drift that the census alone is positioned to make.

### What did the phrase star-crossed lose on the way into everyday English?

The phrase lost its death warrant. In the prologue the compound arrives yoked to take their life, and it names a doom total, swift, and known in advance. Everyday use kept the thwarting and dropped the dying, until star-crossed could describe couples who merely face disapproval or bad luck, and who are expected to survive both.

That softening is the single most consistent pattern in the whole census, and star-crossed is its clearest instance because the textual starting point survives for comparison. The prologue's sentence is a spoiler delivered as a legal finding: the lovers will die, the feud will end over their bodies, and the stars are entered as the cause. Nothing in that sentence is romantic in the greeting-card sense. The everyday phrase, by contrast, functions as an intensifier of sympathy; to call lovers star-crossed in a newspaper is to predict obstacles, not corpses, and very often to predict that love will find a way through them. The astrological machinery that made the compound a causal claim has evaporated with the belief system that powered it, leaving the word as a fossil of a cosmology, beautiful, faintly fatalistic, and available for use by speakers who hold no astrology at all. A phrase that can survive the death of its own worldview has passed the independence test in its hardest form, and it is worth noticing that the play's other great fate-label, death-marked, spoken in the same breath of the same prologue, failed the same journey completely. Death-marked named the doom too exactly to be borrowed for lesser disappointments. Star-crossed could be diluted; death-marked could only be quoted. The census draws the moral early, because it recurs: the expressions that travel are the ones that can afford to mean less than they meant.

The dictionary's treatment of the compound shows custody and forgetting proceeding side by side, in the same institution. Merriam-Webster prints the prologue sentence itself as the entry's illustrative quotation, so that the word's dictionary home carries its birthplace on its face; yet the definition the entry gives, not favored by the stars, ill-fated, is already the diluted sense, thwarting without corpses, and the citations of living use that accumulate under such entries come from journalism about ventures and romances in which nobody dies. The word has also changed its grammar on the journey. In the prologue it is a predicate locked inside a prophecy, part of a sentence that announces an ending. In everyday English it floats free as an attributive label, attachable to lovers, certainly, but equally to a merger, a candidacy, a football season, any enterprise the writer wishes to mark as thwarted by forces beyond its managers. That grammatical emancipation is the deepest form of the independence test: the compound no longer needs the noun lovers, the play, or the stars themselves, only the posture of cosmic unfairness, which it supplies in two syllables of prefix and one of stem. A word that can survive the death of its cosmology, the loss of its noun, and the dilution of its doom has earned the census's first grade by the hardest route available, and its career is the standard against which the weaker entries are measured.

## A Plague on Both Your Houses: The Curse That Became a Frame

Mercutio's dying curse is the census's most instructive pass, because what everyday English took from it was not exactly a phrase. The text, at 3.1, gives a plague o' both your houses, and gives it three times, as the wound turns and the joke curdles: the repetition is the sound of a man discovering that the wit who stood between two households belongs to neither and will be buried by both. Everyday use respells the line, smoothing the elided o' into on and regularizing the whole into a plague on both your houses, and then does something more interesting than quotation. It generalizes the curse into a frame. A plague on the traffic and the weather, a plague on both parties, a plague on the committee and its critics: the frame accepts any two opposed parties whose quarrel has injured a bystander, and it carries Mercutio's exact structural judgment, that the speaker's grievance is with the feud itself and with both houses equally. The phrase survives, in other words, as a piece of portable syntax with a built-in verdict, which is a rarer and more useful thing than a string of words.

The census grades this entry as an idiom in template form, and the grading solves a puzzle in the play's reception. Mercutio is the character the play can least spare and the one its plot discards earliest, yet his linguistic estate is the richest of any single speaker's in the census: the curse, the wild-goose chase in the scene of wit that precedes the catastrophe, and the Queen Mab set piece that donated nothing at all. The distribution is not accidental, and it supports the shape argument developed later in this survey. Mercutio speaks in verdicts. His lines are engineered as detachable judgments, curses, scores, and summings-up, delivered by a character who stands at an angle to the action and comments on it like a chorus with a sword. Detachable judgment is exactly the property a phrase needs for export, because a speaker in another century and another quarrel needs a sentence that arrives with its stance included. The curse needs no context beyond two parties and a grievance, and so it has never lacked for occasions. It is also, for the census, the clearest case of the play improving on the proverb tradition it inherited: curses of the form a plague on were in the air of a plague-haunted city, but the balanced double target, both houses weighed and damned in one breath, is this scene's construction, and the record shows no earlier attestation of the balanced form. The play did not invent cursing. It built the curse that English kept.

Two features of the scene fitted the curse for its journey, and the census notes both because they recur across the export list. The first is repetition. Mercutio speaks the sentence three times as the wound declares itself, turning a cry into a refrain, and refrains are memory's favourite cargo: a line that arrives pre-repeated carries its own rehearsal inside it, and hearers leave the theatre already able to say it. The exports of this play are disproportionately its repeated or refrain-like units, the good nights traded at the window, the prologue's formulae, the curse said thrice over a body, as if the text had done the anthology's work in advance. The second feature is the speaker's structural position. Mercutio is kin to the Prince and friend to Romeo, a man of neither house, and his curse is therefore the judgment of the feud's collateral victim, the one stance in the play that later speakers in later quarrels can occupy without translation. Every user of the frame since has stood exactly there, in the doorway between two camps, injured by a fight that was never theirs. The word plague itself did quiet work as well: in a city where plague closed the theatres and marked the doors of the infected, the curse named a real and dreaded visitation, and some residue of that dread clung to the formula long after the visitations ended, lending a committee-room curse a gravity out of proportion to its occasion. Gravity, refrain, and the bystander's stance: the curse travelled with its engine built in.

## Wild-Goose Chase: The Phrase Whose Meaning Was Replaced

The wild-goose chase is the census's prize exhibit, the entry where every easy claim about the play's coinages goes to be corrected. The popular version is tidy: Mercutio, conceding a duel of wits to Romeo in 2.4, says nay, if thy wits run the wild-goose chase, I have done, and thereby coins the expression for a fruitless pursuit. The documentary record untidies every clause of that sentence. Begin with the thing itself. A wild-goose chase was a horse race, a follow-the-leader contest in which the trailing riders were bound to follow the leader's course at a set distance, as geese follow in formation. Gervase Markham described the race in print in his Discourse of Horsmanshippe in 1593, before the play reached print and in the same years it reached the stage, with the hindmost horse bound to follow the foremost. The term was therefore stable sporting vocabulary before it was dialogue. What Mercutio does with it is a transfer, not a coinage: Romeo has just cried switch and spurs, the language of the saddle, and Mercutio answers inside the same metaphor, scoring their repartee as a race in which Romeo's wits keep the lead and his own five wits cannot hold the distance. The line means that the contest is being run at a pace and on a course the loser cannot follow. Nothing in it yet means a search for something that does not exist.

### Was Mercutio chasing a goose in Act 2, Scene 4?

No goose was being chased. The wild-goose chase of the 1590s was a horse race run in imitation of geese in flight, one rider leading and the rest bound to follow his course. Mercutio borrows the race to score a contest of wits he is losing, and the bird in his sentence is a formation, not a quarry.

Then the race died, and the phrase was orphaned, and the orphaning produced the modern meaning by a process the lexicographical record lets us watch in dated steps. With the game forgotten, hearers reanalysed the compound in the only way its surface allowed: a chase after a wild goose, an enterprise proverbially hopeless. Samuel Johnson's Dictionary of 1755 already certifies the replacement, defining the term as a pursuit of something as unlikely to be caught as the wildgoose, which is the reanalysis wearing a lexicographer's authority. Francis Grose, collecting the vulgar tongue in 1811, completed the certificate: a tedious uncertain pursuit, like the following a flock of wild geese, who are remarkably shy. The sporting writers had kept the truth on file in the meantime; Nicholas Cox, in The Hunter in 1685, explained the race and its goose-formation name correctly, a full seventy years before Johnson printed the error. Here the census must adjudicate a genuine disagreement among its authorities, and the adjudication is the entry's real finding. On the phrase's origin, Markham and Cox defeat Johnson outright: the dictionary of record misdescribes what the words first named. On the phrase's meaning in Johnson's own century, Johnson defeats the sporting writers, because by 1755 the reanalysed sense was the living one, and a dictionary's first duty is to the sense its readers will meet. Both verdicts belong in the glossary, because together they state the law of this entry: a phrase can keep its words, change its referent entire, and owe both its survival and its new meaning to the forgetting of its first world.

### Why do the edition and the dictionary give the wild-goose chase two different referents?

They are describing different centuries. The edition, responsible for the text of the 1590s, glosses what the words denoted when Mercutio spoke them, the horse race; the dictionary, responsible for the living language, glosses what the words denote for the readers who consult it, the fruitless pursuit Johnson certified. Each is accurate about its own period.

The divergence is not a scandal but a division of labour, and the census exploits it deliberately, because the two institutions keep the two halves of every export's biography. An edition is an act of historical custody: its glosses answer the question of what a line meant to the audience that first heard it, and modern editions of this play, the Folger text used throughout this survey among them, gloss Mercutio's metaphor by the race, sending the reader to Markham's stable yard. A general dictionary is an act of contemporary registration: its definitions answer the question of what a string of words will do in the next sentence a reader writes, and for that purpose the goose of Johnson's reanalysis, not the horse of Markham's record, is the operative animal. Trouble begins only when one institution's answer is mistaken for the other's, when a dictionary's first-known-use date is read as a certificate of original meaning, or an edition's gloss is read as a ruling on what the phrase may mean in a headline. The popular coinage claim is exactly that confusion wearing a laurel: it takes the dictionary's archival date for the play and spends it as an edition's semantic warrant. The census keeps the two ledgers apart, which is why its wild-goose entry can hold, without strain, that the play supplied the transfer, the seventeenth century lost the race, and the eighteenth supplied the meaning.

Merriam-Webster's modern entry handles the double record with a precision the census adopts as its own wording: first known use of wild-goose chase is given as about 1595, in the defined sense of a complicated, lengthy, and usually fruitless pursuit. The date deserves a slow reading. The dictionary is not claiming that Mercutio in 1595 meant what a frustrated traveller means by the phrase in a headline. It is recording that the string enters the surviving written record, in transferred use, at the play, while the sense under which it is filed was assembled later, by Johnson's century, out of the string's misunderstood remains. The etymological dictionary tradition states the same structure from the other side, dating the figurative use to the play and noting the earlier literal sense as real but unrecorded in the sources it surveys. The coinage claim therefore fails twice over, once on the thing (the race term is in Markham, in print, in 1593) and once on the meaning (the fruitless sense is an eighteenth-century folk construction). What remains to the play is the transfer itself, and the transfer is not a small thing. Shakespeare's scene is the bridge over which a piece of stable-yard terminology walked into figurative English, and a bridge is a structure other traffic uses for centuries without knowing its builder. The census knows of no better emblem for its whole argument: the play as the crossing point where expressions changed their state, from jargon to metaphor, from quotation to idiom, from one world to the next.

## Fortune's Fool and the Lines That Stayed in Quotation Marks

Against the two full idioms the census sets a class of expressions that everyone overestimates: lines of great fame that live entirely inside quotation, applauded, anthologized, and never once used by a speaker who was not performing the act of quoting. The type specimen is Romeo's cry over Tybalt's body, O, I am fortune's fool, at 3.1. The sentence is among the most assigned in the play; study guides print it with glosses, examination rubrics reward its citation, and the cry has the epigrammatic balance that is supposed to guarantee export, fortune personified as a keeper of fools, the speaker filing himself in her household. Yet the dictionaries do not enter fortune's fool as a headword, speakers do not reach for it in unstudied speech, and its natural habitat is the quotation bank and the essay. Even its address in the text will not sit still: editions number the cry differently, one widely used text placing it at 3.1.127 and another at 3.1.142, a small drift that is itself diagnostic. Idioms acquire fixed addresses in dictionaries; quotations keep the shifting addresses of editions, because their home is the book and not the mouth.

The census insists on the distinction because conflating the two classes is the mechanism by which the play's phrase-count gets inflated in popular accounting. A list that counts fortune's fool beside wild-goose chase is adding unlike quantities: a line the culture preserves by deliberate custody, in classrooms and quotation dictionaries, and a phrase the culture preserves by use, in sentences whose speakers have forgotten there was ever anything to preserve. Custody is not failure, and the quotation class contains some of the play's most potent sentences. But custody requires custodians, and the census's independence test exists precisely to register the difference between an expression that needs the institution of Shakespeare-reading to survive and one that would survive the institution's disappearance. Star-crossed would survive it. Wild-goose chase would survive it twice over, having already survived the death of the sport that named it. Fortune's fool would fade to a footnote within a generation, and death-marked, the prologue compound that never left the scholarship at all, shows the far end of the same spectrum: a genuine first record in the play, a word the dictionaries credit and the language declined, preserved only where the play is studied line by line. The export ledger, honestly kept, has a stayed-home column, and the stayed-home column is what makes the travelled column mean something.

John Bartlett understood the custody system from inside, having built its central depot: his Familiar Quotations, first published in 1855, turned the memorable lines of English literature into a reference commodity, retrievable by author and keyword, and gave Romeo and Juliet a permanent garrison in the quotation trade. The quotation dictionary is the great ambiguous machine of this census. It preserves lines exactly, with attributions, and at the same time it saws them off at the joints, detaching the couplet from the scene, the curse from the corpse, the rose sentence from the girl reasoning in the dark, and it is that detachment which prepares a line for idiom-hood by making it portable. Some lines complete the journey and are naturalized. Some, like fortune's fool, settle permanently at the border, naturalized nowhere, deportable nowhere, quoted forever. The census records the machine's existence here because its selections silently govern what the public believes the play's phrases are: the canon of exports is largely the canon of what the quotation dictionaries chose to carry, a point the detachment argument later in this survey takes up in full.

Custody in the modern period has its own institutions, and the census names them because they explain why the quotation class is so stable in membership. The study guide and the examination syllabus are the quotation dictionary's heirs: they maintain fixed lists of the play's important sentences, fortune's fool prominent among them, and they reproduce those lists in every cohort of students with a fidelity Bartlett could only envy. A line on the syllabus is a line with an annuity; it will be glossed, memorized, and cited by millions who never choose it, and its fame is therefore real but mortgaged, dependent on the institution's continued operation. The distinction shows in the texture of the fame. Ask a room of graduates for the play's phrases and the syllabus answers first, fortune's fool and the dagger and the defiance of the stars, delivered with their act and scene numbers attached like luggage tags; listen to the same graduates talk for an hour about their own feuds, searches, and partings, and the idioms answer instead, star-crossed and the wild-goose chase and the plague on both houses, with no numbers attached because no numbers are needed. The census surveyed the second conversation, not the first, and its grades will look perverse to any reader who mistakes the syllabus for the language. They are different countries, and this play, alone among the canon, holds dual citizenship in both, which is the final form of its density: not the most lines preserved, but the most lines preserved twice, once by love of the text and once by use of the tongue.

## The Quotation Trade: How the Canon of Exports Was Manufactured

Between the play and the phrase stands an industry, and no census is honest that does not audit it. The industry's founding document for English is John Bartlett's Familiar Quotations, published in 1855 by a Cambridge, Massachusetts bookseller who had grown tired of being asked, across his own counter, who said what. Bartlett's innovation was retrieval: memorable lines arranged by author, then indexed, so that a speaker preparing an address could find the mot for an occasion the way a merchant finds stock. The book's success built the template every later collection followed, through edition after edition and, in the following century, through the Oxford Dictionary of Quotations, first published in 1941, which gave the trade its academic registry. What these volumes collectively did to Romeo and Juliet was decisive and double. They preserved its lines with an accuracy no oral tradition could have matched, attribution attached, wording fixed against the drift that corrupted so much else. And they converted the play from a sequence of scenes into a warehouse of extracts, each extract priced by portability, so that the culture's memory of the text reorganized itself around the extractable.

The reorganization had a curriculum. School anthologies and elocution manuals drew their Shakespeare from the quotation trade's shelves, printing the balcony scene as a set piece and the prologue as a recitation, and generations of readers met the play first as a garland of its famous sentences. The garland then governed the fame it merely reported: lines already selected were taught because they were famous, and grew famous because they were taught, in a loop that no individual line could break into from outside and none needed to defend from inside. The census's membership is unintelligible without this loop. Death-marked had the misfortune to stand three lines from star-crossed in the same speech; one compound was garlanded and the other was not, and four centuries of differential repetition did the rest. Nothing in the intrinsic quality of the two words predicts the divergence with anything like the confidence that the selection history predicts it. The quotation trade is thus the census's hidden variable, the mechanism that converted small initial advantages, a Chorus's position at the front of the play, a couplet's epigrammatic snap, into unbridgeable leads.

A researcher maintaining a living census of the kind printed in this survey, its grades revised as dictionaries antedate and redate their first records, keeps the working file where it can be amended as the evidence moves; a personal research vault such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/) serves exactly that office, holding the glossary, the dictionary findings, and the disputed attributions in one amendable place. The need for such a file is the trade's own lesson read backward. Bartlett's selections hardened into fact because print hardened; a census kept in an amendable form can stay honest where the printed canon could not, demoting an entry the season an earlier Markham surfaces for it. The point generalizes beyond method. Every age gets the phrase canon its retrieval technology prescribes: the nineteenth century's was a bookseller's index, the schoolroom's was the anthology, and the search engine's is the ranked list, which reproduces the garland at higher speed and with less attribution than Bartlett ever permitted himself. The census's exclusions are aimed at that newest layer as much as at the oldest, because the ranked list is where love is blind and the fool's errand pick up their false addresses in our time, copied from list to list by compilers who trust the ranking as Bartlett's customers trusted the index.

The trade must nonetheless be adjudicated fairly, since this survey's own artifact is a product of it. The quotation dictionary is an amputator, and amputation is also a form of rescue: the couplet sawn from its scene can be carried into a farewell address in Ohio, which the unamputated scene cannot. Bartlett did to the play what the play's own Chorus does to the story, compressing it into portable utterance and trusting the compression to travel where the whole cannot go. The census's complaint is not against the trade but against the amnesia about the trade, the pretence that the canon of famous lines is a natural formation rather than a manufactured one. Once the manufacturing is admitted, the census's questions become the right ones: not which lines are greatest, but which lines the machines of four centuries selected, what properties the selection rewarded, and what the unselected majority, the machinery, the vows, the situated cries, can tell us by their staying home.

## The Balcony Scene's Three Exports: One Scene, Three Kinds of Afterlife

Act 2, Scene 2 is the most productive single scene in the census, and its productivity is a system fact before it is a tribute. Within a few hundred lines, one speaker at a window and one in the orchard below produce the misread question, the naming argument, and the farewell couplet that together account for three of the glossary's entries and for a large share of the play's total presence in everyday speech. The scene's structure explains the yield. It is built almost entirely from detachable units: a sequence of speeches in which Juliet reasons aloud in propositions, each proposition shaped as a self-standing sentence with its own logic complete inside it. What's in a name is a thesis. Parting is such sweet sorrow is a paradox stated as a proverb. Wherefore art thou Romeo is a question that sounds like a complete thought even to hearers who misconstrue it. The scene is a string of aphorisms wearing the costume of a conversation, and aphorisms are the natural emigrants of any text, because they arrive pre-packaged for travel. The census treats the three at summary length here, since this series gives each its own full study, and confines itself to the comparative point that only a census can make.

The wherefore sentence is the export that travelled with a defect, and the defect became part of the cargo. Juliet's question asks why the man in the orchard must be Romeo and a Montague; wherefore is the interrogative of cause, a cousin of therefore, and the speech that follows, deny thy father and refuse thy name, is unintelligible on any other reading. Popular reception rebuilt the word as a question about place and rebuilt the scene to match, producing the serenade tableau of a girl calling down to a lover she cannot find. The full adjudication of that error, its mechanisms and its cost to the play's argument about names, stands in ['Wherefore Art Thou' and the Great Misreading](/2029/08/10/wherefore-art-thou-misreading/). For the census the entry matters because it proves that detachment does not merely preserve phrases imperfectly; it can preserve a misconstrual at the same strength as the phrase, so that the language holds both the sentence and its error in a single entry, and correcting the error becomes a second, smaller industry riding on the first.

The rose sentence is the export that became a proverb, which is a different social rank from an idiom and worth distinguishing in a census. That which we call a rose, Juliet argues, by any other word would smell as sweet; the Folger text prints word, following the Second Quarto and the Folio, while the proverb that conquered the world runs on name, the reading of the First Quarto as later centuries of editing transmitted it, so the everyday form of the play's most everyday sentence is not the form the standard edition prints. The sentence escaped the scene and entered the world's naming disputes, cited wherever a label is said not to touch the thing, in arguments its speaker would have lost, since the play itself is the refutation of her thesis: the name Montague does touch the thing, lethally. The full record, from Mill on denotation to the failed corporate renamings that quote Juliet against herself, stands in ['A Rose by Any Other Name' as Everyday Phrase](/2029/08/24/rose-by-any-other-name-idiom/). The census registers the pattern: of the scene's three exports, the proverb is the one whose everyday career most directly contradicts its scene, because a proverb answers to the needs of its users and not to the argument of its author.

The farewell couplet is the export that became a ritual. Good night, good night, parting is such sweet sorrow, that I shall say good night till it be morrow: spoken in darkness as a device for not ending the conversation, the couplet was detached by the quotation trade, truncated to its first half in most ceremonial use, and installed as the standard furniture of leavings, graduations, retirements, and farewell addresses, occasions its speaker never faced. The documented career, from Bartlett's pages to a president's farewell gloss that divided the sweet from the sorrow, stands in ['Parting Is Such Sweet Sorrow' Enters English](/2029/08/17/parting-is-such-sweet-sorrow-idiom/). The census draws the comparative moral across the three entries. One scene produced a misconstrual that travels as a picture, a thesis that travels as a proverb, and a paradox that travels as a ceremony. No other scene in the play, and few in the language, exports in three different modes at once, and the modes share one condition: each unit was complete before it was detached, a finished thought that the scene happened to contain rather than a fragment the scene was needed to complete. The machinery of the same scene, the ladder, the orchard wall, the nurse's interruptions, exported nothing. Even here, at the very centre of the play's phrase-fame, the census's law holds: what travels is the rhetoric of extremity and judgment; what stays is the equipment.

The scene contains its own control case, and the census would be negligent to pass it by. Romeo speaks in the orchard too, at greater length than Juliet and with the scene's most famous imagery, the sun and the moon, the eyes in his head exchanged for stars, the hand's glove envied by a cheek; and Romeo's share of the scene exported nothing. Not one of his sentences from that night became an instrument of the language. The asymmetry is total and it is grammatical. Romeo's speeches are apostrophes and wishes, addressed to a particular girl in a particular garden, predicating her radiance and his longing; they cannot be detached because their subjects stay behind when the sentences leave, and a speaker in another garden, or in a boardroom, finds no slot in them for his own case. Juliet's speeches in the same scene are arguments, with premises any stranger can re-run: a name is not a hand or foot; a rose renamed keeps its scent; a parting prolonged is sweet because it is sorrowful. The window scene thus performs the census's whole doctrine inside twenty minutes of stage time, the imagery applauded for four centuries and the propositions employed for four centuries, applause and employment being the two different eternities the play's language was always going to have. Editors have long divided the scene's honours between its poetry and its rhetoric; the census adds the colder finding that the language itself made the same division, keeping the rhetoric and framing the poetry, and that the division was predictable from the sentence shapes alone.

## The Near Misses: Famous Lines That Never Became Phrases

A census that lists only successes cannot explain success, so the glossary's most valuable evidence may be its near misses, the lines of indisputable fame that never crossed into source-free use. The class is large and distinguished. My only love sprung from my only hate, Juliet's cry at the feast when the Nurse names the stranger she has kissed, is among the most quoted sentences in the play, printed on anthologies and examination papers for a century, and it has no everyday career whatsoever: no headline writer reaches for it, no speaker applies it to a situation of her own, because its paradox is welded to a unique predicament, a love discovered to be a feud enemy in the same instant as the loving. O happy dagger, at the tomb, is shorter, stranger, and more famous still, and equally immobile, because its apostrophe requires the dagger. Romeo's defiance in Mantua, then I defy you, stars, has the brevity and the stance that export is supposed to reward, yet it too stayed a quotation, and its failure is diagnostic in a second way: the cry addresses the stars as a personal adversary, and as the astrology that made the addressee real drained out of the culture, the sentence lost its target and survived as a gesture, quoted by people performing defiance of a fate they no longer localize in the sky. Star-crossed, drawn from the same cosmology, escaped that fate by becoming an adjective, a description rather than an address; descriptions can be re-aimed at anything, while addresses die with their addressees.

The parting formulae supply the class's subtlest member. Good night was old when the play was new, an exchange worn smooth by centuries of use, and the census grades it a popularization at most; yet the scene built on it, the lovers' iterated farewell in which good night is traded back and forth as a device for refusing to end the night, became one of the culture's permanent choreographies, re-staged at every reluctant parting since. The distinction the near miss enforces is between a phrase and a routine. English took the routine, the shape of the leave-taking, the repeated word as a stay against separation, and left the words themselves in the common stock where it found them. Something similar holds for the aubade debate of the wedding morning, lark against nightingale, a situation so famous it has a scholarly name and not one exportable syllable. Situations, however celebrated, do not enter dictionaries; the census's subject is the narrower class of verbal instruments, and the near misses patrol its border, proving that fame of every other kind, fame of scene, of situation, of speaker, guarantees a phrase nothing.

Set against the exports, the near misses sharpen the law of the census to its final edge. My only love sprung from my only hate is a verdict, by the earlier argument, and verdicts were said to travel; why did this one stay? The refinement is that a travelling verdict must be re-issuable by a stranger without borrowing the original case. The curse re-issues because feuds recur in identical structure; the rose thesis re-issues because naming disputes recur; Juliet's cry cannot re-issue because its structure, love and hate in a single object discovered at a single moment, is the plot of this play and of almost nothing else in a speaker's week. Portability is therefore not a property of sentences alone but of the situations sentences judge: the export list of Romeo and Juliet is, read coldly, a list of the situations in later life that most resemble the play's, feuds, hopeless quests, thwarted attachments, leavings, and namings, and its silences are the situations that never recurred. The play did not donate its language to the language at large. It donated it to the recurring emergencies that rhyme with its own.

## These Violent Delights: The Export That Slept

The Friar's warning at the wedding's threshold, these violent delights have violent ends, and in their triumph die, like fire and powder, which, as they kiss, consume, at 2.6, presents the census with its strangest temporal pattern: four centuries as a quotation of the second rank, admired in studies of the play's imagery and unknown outside them, followed by a sudden return to wide currency when a television series built a season's machinery around the sentence in 2016. The series' use of the line, its transmission from host to host as a waking formula, is documented in this series' study of the Westworld afterlife and is passage material here. The census's interest is narrower and colder: what does the episode prove about the quotation class? It proves that dormancy is not death. A line held in custody by editions and quotation dictionaries remains available for reactivation at full strength whenever a new medium needs a sentence with exactly its properties, and the Friar's sentence has properties no copywriter could commission: brevity, symmetry, a built-in plot, and a warning that doubles as a promise. The reactivation also clarifies its grade. Even in its television currency the sentence travels as a quotation, attributed by its aura, spoken by characters who know it is old and borrowed. It has not become an idiom; nobody applies it to a rich dessert or a fast car without the inverted commas audible. It is a quotation that a mass audience learned to recognize, which is a different and rarer event than naturalization.

### Why is the Friar's warning the odd one out among the exports?

Every other export promises something its users want: a label for doomed love, a curse for a feud, a proverb for a farewell. The Friar's sentence warns against appetite itself, and its career shows the strain: centuries of custody, no naturalization, and a revival that needed a story about programmed pleasures turning violent.

The oddity is instructive for the census's shape argument, because the warning is the one export whose meaning survived detachment almost undamaged. Star-crossed softened; the curse generalized; the rose sentence inverted; the wild-goose chase changed referents entire. The Friar's sentence means in a television studio almost exactly what it meant at the cell door, because its content is a mechanism rather than a label: delights of a certain intensity consume themselves as fire consumes powder. What it could not do was find everyday occasions, since everyday speech has limited use for a doctrine of self-consuming pleasure outside of sermons and title sequences. The census therefore files it as the control case of the whole survey: proof that fidelity of meaning and breadth of travel are not merely uncorrelated but can trade against each other, the phrases that travel farthest being, with eerie consistency, the ones that consent to mean less, or other, than they meant.

The sentence's construction explains both the dormancy and the return, and the census closes the entry with the mechanism. The Friar does not label a feeling; he states a physical law with a demonstration attached, delights of a certain intensity consuming themselves as fire consumes powder at the kiss, and a law, unlike a label, can lie dormant for centuries without ageing, because it waits for a world that runs on its principle. The worlds that quoted it politely for four hundred years ran on other principles, of providence, of decorum, of romance, and the sentence stayed an ornament of the study. The world that reactivated it runs on engineered appetite, on pleasures designed to intensify until they turn, and for that world the sentence is not an ornament but a description awaiting only its occasion. The census draws the general rule for its quotation class: dormant lines are not failed idioms but stored laws and labels whose worlds have not yet recurred, and the glossary's grades are therefore dated documents, true of the language as surveyed, revisable by any decade that builds a machine, a market, or a pleasure garden in the shape of a stored sentence.

## The Boundary Cases: A Name That Became a Noun, a Title That Became a Nickname

Two entries in the glossary face outward, testing the census's definition from the far side, and a survey of the play's phrases is incomplete without their hearings. The first is the hero's name. The dictionaries enter Romeo as a common noun, a male lover, an ardent or practised pursuer of women, and the demotion of a proper name into a dictionary lemma is a linguistic event of the first order, the same event that befell the Casanovas and Don Juans of other literatures. This series gives the word's career its own study, and the census confines itself to the classification problem the noun poses: a name is not a phrase, it carries no syntax and states no judgment, yet it plainly belongs to the play's total export, and its drift rehearses the whole census in miniature. In the play the name is a death sentence and a love token in one; in the dictionary it is a character type; in speech it arrives with an irony the play never licenses, since to call a man a Romeo is rarely to prophesy his tomb and often to doubt his sincerity. The name travelled, like star-crossed, by consenting to mean less, and its success stands as the limiting case of the census's law: maximum portability at maximum dilution, a whole character compressed into a raised eyebrow.

The second boundary case is stranger and, for the detachment argument, decisive. American law knows a body of provisions, close-in-age exemptions and registration reliefs in the sexual offence codes of states including Texas, Florida, and Indiana, that commentary calls Romeo and Juliet laws. This series examines the provisions and their texts in their own article; the census registers the phrase's classification, which is unique in the glossary. The lovers' names appear in no statute: the nickname lives entirely in commentary, advocacy, and journalism, a phrase about the play that the play does not contain, coined by lawyers and campaigners who needed the culture's most famous young lovers as an argument. Here detachment reaches its theoretical limit, a phrase whose connection to the text is purely reputational, doing policy work in legislative debate on the strength of a story its users may know only as a title. If the census's gravity metaphor needed a proof, this entry is it: the play's pull bends phrases that were never in its text at all, and holds them in orbit by fame alone.

The two cases also fix the census's outer boundary by contrast, and the contrast protects the core grades. A name and a nickname are not phrases, and this survey declines to swell its count with them, however dazzling their careers; they are entered in the glossary as boundary rows precisely so that the rows above them, the idioms and quotations built from the text's own sentences, keep their meaning. The discipline cuts against the survey's own interest, since a census of the play's total linguistic estate would look grander with the noun and the nickname counted in full. That is the point of a census as opposed to a celebration. The glossary's authority to say that star-crossed is a full idiom derives from its willingness to say that a Romeo is a neighbouring phenomenon, magnificent and inadmissible, and to print the exclusion where a list-maker would print a bonus.

## The Phrases That Are Not the Play's: Three Exclusions That Discipline the Census

A census earns its authority by what it refuses, and the glossary's exclusion rows are not a grudging appendix but the proof that the tests bind. Three expressions in wide circulation as the play's gifts fail the first test, presence in the text, and each fails it in a different way, which is why they are heard separately. Together they reveal the force that bends attribution across the whole field: the play is the most famous love story in the language, and famous sources attract phrases the way a great house attracts furniture from demolished neighbours, until the inventory and the legend no longer match.

Love is blind fails as an immigrant from two directions at once. The sentence is a proverb with a long prior record: Chaucer's Merchant's Tale, written in the last decade of the fourteenth century, states it outright, for love is blind alday and may nat see, behind which stands the older iconography of the blindfolded Cupid. When the playwright himself wanted the aphorism as an aphorism, he put it in another play: Jessica, escaping in boy's clothes in The Merchant of Venice at 2.6, comforts herself that love is blind, and lovers cannot see the pretty follies that themselves commit. Romeo and Juliet contains the idea twice and the sentence never. Romeo, in the first scene, complains that love, whose view is muffled still, should without eyes see pathways to his will, which is the proverb dissolved into a conceit; and in the orchard hunt Benvolio says of the hidden Romeo, blind is his love, and best befits the dark, to which Mercutio answers in the same key, if love be blind, love cannot hit the mark. The play is saturated with the proverb and innocent of the sentence, and the popular attribution reverses the credit, giving the play the proverb's fame because the play is where modern readers meet the idea in its most famous dramatic weather.

### Does anyone in this play say the words love is blind?

No character in Romeo and Juliet speaks the sentence love is blind. Benvolio calls Romeo's love blind in the orchard hunt and Mercutio builds a bawdy answer on the adjective, but the aphorism itself belongs to Chaucer's Merchant's Tale two centuries earlier, and the playwright's own use of the full sentence stands in The Merchant of Venice, in Jessica's mouth.

Break the ice fails as a case of mistaken identity within the canon. The metaphor is the playwright's, and its first appearance as a social figure is genuinely his, but the play is The Taming of the Shrew, where Tranio, plotting the campaign for Bianca, tells Petruchio that if you break the ice and do this feat, achieve the elder, set the younger free, at 1.2: the ice is Katherine's resistance and the elder sister's unmarried state, and the metaphor is born social, already meaning the first breach in a frozen situation. Lists of the love play's phrases absorb it because list-making about Shakespeare habitually defaults to the most famous title in the pile, and because a compiler working from a general Shakespeare phrase-list who needs a Romeo and Juliet page will misfile from the canon into the play without noticing the join. The exclusion matters beyond tidiness. Break the ice is by any measure one of the most successful Shakespearean phrases in everyday use, a full idiom of the first rank, and its misassignment flatters this play's census with stolen density: remove it, and the play's genuine count stands revealed as smaller and stranger than the legend, two full idioms in four hundred lines of fame rather than a shower of coinages.

On a fool's errand fails as an anachronism, and it is the failure this census was specifically charged to examine, because the commissioning brief for this survey listed the expression inside the play's cluster. The words stand nowhere in the play. They stand nowhere in the period's drama at all, so far as the phrase dictionaries can find: the expression's currency is dated to the early eighteenth century, with the dictionaries' quotations beginning in its first years, more than a century after the first quarto. The custom behind the words is older and is documented on the continent: a Flemish poem by Eduard de Dene, in 1561, already describes a nobleman sending his servant on absurd errands as an April the First sport, and John Aubrey records the Fooles holy day in England in 1686, but the errand custom and its later name are not the play's property by any test the census applies. What the play does contain is the concept's great rival: the wild-goose chase covers the same semantic ground, the hopeless quest, and covers it a century earlier, so the popular cluster that lists both expressions as this play's twin gifts is crediting the play with its own phrase and with that phrase's eighteenth-century synonym in a single breath. The exclusion of the fool's errand is therefore the census in miniature: a plausible-sounding attribution, destroyed by two dates, and instructive precisely because it was plausible. Attribution by vibe is the enemy; the record, with its Markhams and its 1705s, is the only census-taker whose returns can be trusted.

The three exclusions share a mechanism that deserves its own name in the census, because it operates on every page of the popular literature. Attribution in quotation culture flows toward the most famous plausible owner, as sayings in the wider culture migrate toward the most famous plausible speaker, and a love phrase in search of an owner will arrive at the most famous love play in the language with the reliability of water finding a drain. Chaucer loses love is blind because Chaucer is, for most speakers, a name from a syllabus and the play is a living presence; The Taming of the Shrew loses break the ice because the canon itself is searched by title fame; the eighteenth century loses the fool's errand because a century is nobody's brand. The pull is strong enough to have operated inside this survey's own commission, whose cluster of the play's phrases, drawn up carefully and in good faith, contained the fool's errand among the genuine articles. A census exists to resist the pull with the only instruments that have ever resisted it: dates, texts, and the willingness to print a refusal. The refusals are not losses to the play. A fame that must be fed with stolen phrases would be a frailer thing than the fame the record supports, and the genuine census, smaller and stranger, is the more remarkable object: two idioms and a curse-frame, from one play, in a language that has forgotten almost everything else about the decade that produced them.

## Why the Extremity Travelled and the Machinery Stayed

With the census graded and the exclusions entered, its shape can be argued, and the shape is the survey's main claim. Sort the entries by kind and a law emerges that no counting of the legend's kind can see. Everything that achieved full independence is a speech act of extremity or judgment: a fate-label pronounced before the action starts, a curse pronounced over a body, a contest verdict pronounced by a loser, a thesis about names, a paradox about parting, a warning about excess. Everything that stayed home belongs to the play's machinery: the letter that miscarries, the potion and its timings, the apothecary and his poverty, the feud's origins, the ladder, the tomb's logistics, the whole causal apparatus by which the plot actually runs. The play's plot is famous; the plot's vocabulary is extinct outside the scholarship. Its judgments are immortal; its equipment is museum stock. A pair of star-crossed lovers, the continuance of their parents' rage, misadventured piteous overthrows: the prologue alone exports its astrology and buries its plot summary, since no one in everyday speech has ever described an event as a misadventured piteous overthrow, while the fearful passage of their death-marked love survives only where footnotes keep it.

The law has a mechanism, and the mechanism is portability of stance. A phrase travels when a stranger can pick it up and find a complete position inside it, a way of standing toward a situation that recurs: two parties are feuding and I am collateral; this search is hopeless and I concede it; these lovers are thwarted by forces larger than their wishes; names are not things. The feud's causes cannot be picked up in this way, because they are not positions but facts, specific to Verona and unusable in Ohio. The miscarried letter is the purest test case. It is the hinge of the whole tragedy, the single device on which the ending turns, and it has donated no phrase at all, not a syllable, because a miscarried letter is an event, not a judgment, and events cannot be re-performed by a sentence in the way judgments can. Friar John's quarantine, the plague that seals the house and stops the message, closes the loop with an irony the census notes without pleasure: the only plague in the play's machinery is literal, and it stayed home, while the figurative plague of Mercutio's curse, which is machinery of nothing and judgment of everything, went everywhere.

The lovers' own unargued extremity confirms the law from the negative side. Juliet's refusal of the moon oath, Romeo's bounty speech with its sea and its depth, the dawn debate of lark and nightingale: these are among the most anthologized passages in the play, and none of them exported a phrase, because hyperbole of feeling predicates only the speaker's interior state, and an interior state cannot be handed to a stranger as an instrument. A headline cannot borrow my bounty is as boundless as the sea to describe a budget, and no farewell was ever improved by the inconstant moon. What the lovers contribute to the census they contribute as reasoners, in the one scene where Juliet argues in propositions at a window; what they contribute as feelers stays in the anthology, admired and unusable. The play's phrase-fame is thus almost an accident of its argumentative density: it is a love story that keeps breaking into disputation, and the disputations are what the language kept.

A second pattern reinforces the first, visible in the grades column. The exports are disproportionately the work of the play's commentators rather than its lovers: the Chorus, who speaks no line inside the action, contributes the strongest idiom; Mercutio, the licensed outsider, contributes the template and the transferred race; the Friar, the play's resident moralist, contributes the reactivated warning. The lovers themselves contribute the balcony aphorisms, which are propositions, argued sentences with subjects and predicates, rather than cries. What the lovers say in extremity without argument, the swoons, the vows by the moon, the dagger speeches, stays quoted or stays home. The census's law can thus be stated in its strongest form: the play exports its verdicts and retains its events, and its hold on everyday English is the hold of a judiciary, not of a diary. English speakers do not re-live the play in their phrases; they re-issue its rulings, on their own feuds, searches, partings, and dooms, most of them unaware that a ruling was ever handed down. That is the density the brief for this survey asked to have explained, and it is not a density of coinage. It is a density of portable judgment per page, higher in this play than in any comparable text because the play is built, scene by scene, from characters delivering verdicts under pressure.

## The Density Question: One Play Against the Rest of the Canon

The commissioning brief asked what the density of the play's coinage reveals, and the census, having replaced coinage with naturalization, can answer the question in its corrected form: what does the density of naturalization reveal? The comparison that matters is not with other authors but with the playwright's own shelf, because the same pen, the same theatre, and the same quotation trade were available to every play, and the outcomes differ by kind. Hamlet, a longer and in bulk a more quoted play, exports its phrases as attributed treasure: to be or not to be, the rest is silence, something rotten, are worn by their users as quotations, with the prince's name attached or at least his melancholy. The love play's leading exports are worn as tools, with no name attached at all. Macbeth contributes superstition and a handful of labels that still smell of the heath; the comedies contribute sparkling sentences that remain recognizably performances. Only Romeo and Juliet achieved a conversion of its stock into anonymous instruments at this rate, two full idioms and a curse-frame from some three thousand lines, and the census's explanation returns to construction. This is an early play, written in the most sententious manner its author ever sustained, built from sonnet, proverb, oxymoron, and set debate, its young lovers educated by the audience in a rhetoric of finished sentences. The mature plays think in soliloquy and image, forms that resist extraction; the early play thinks in aphorism, the one form that extraction cannot injure. Density of naturalization is therefore a property of the play's youthful architecture, and the revelation the brief asked for runs against the usual story of growing genius: the play stocked the language not because its author was at his greatest, but because he was at his most quotable, writing verdicts before he learned to write thoughts. The finding also disciplines the census's pride in its subject. A phrase census of the mature tragedies would be a study of custody, of lines preserved by reverence; a phrase census of this play is a study of employment, of lines preserved by work. Employment is the rarer immortality, and the more democratic: it asks nothing of its beneficiaries, not literacy in the source, not reverence, not even awareness, and it is renewed every time a stranger curses a feud, concedes a search, or labels a love in words an orchard audience first heard four centuries ago.

## Detachment: How a Phrase Forgets Its Play, and What the Forgetting Costs

The commissioning brief named the complication honestly: the phrases circulate among speakers with no memory of the source, and the survey was asked to engage the detachment rather than deplore it. The census's graded evidence permits a cooler account than either celebration or lament. Detachment is not an accident that befalls the play's phrases; it is the third test of the census, the very property that makes an export an export. A phrase has fully arrived in everyday English when the source has become optional information about it, and the machinery of arrival is identifiable: the quotation dictionary detaches the line and files it under a keyword; the school anthology prints the extract without the act; the dictionary of the language eventually enters the string as a headword with a definition that mentions no play; journalism reaches for the string as ready-made stance. At each stage the phrase trades context for currency, and the exchange rate is visible in the glossary: star-crossed bought full independence at the price of its death warrant; wild-goose chase bought it at the price of its referent; the rose sentence bought proverb status at the price of being quoted against the play that refutes it; the wherefore sentence, refusing all trades, keeps its meaning and pays with a permanent error carried as hand luggage.

Journalism is where detachment does its daily work, and the headline is its purest instrument, because a headline must compress a stance into a handful of words for readers who will grant it one second. The census's exports are headline-shaped by birth: star-crossed fits over any thwarted enterprise, the wild-goose chase over any failed search, the plague frame over any two-sided quarrel, and each arrives with its editorial verdict pre-loaded, saving the writer the cost of an argument. Sports pages, business pages, and political columns draw on the stock precisely because the phrases ask nothing of the reader, no recognition, no attribution, no act of literary memory; a source that demanded acknowledgment would be unusable at that speed. The play's phrases conquered journalism by becoming invisible as phrases, and their invisibility in the medium that most English speakers read most is the working definition of the everyday in this survey's title.

Whether the forgetting is loss depends on what a phrase is for, and here the survey adjudicates its final disagreement, between the custodians and the naturalizers. The custodial position, implicit in much teaching, holds that a detached phrase is a diminished phrase, star-crossed without the prologue a label without its law. The naturalizing position, held in practice by every dictionary that prints a headword without a sermon, holds that a phrase's life in the wild is its success, and that the source is an etymology, interesting and optional. The census's verdict splits the difference along the line its own grades draw. For the full idioms, the naturalizers win: wild-goose chase does its work perfectly in the mouths of speakers who picture an actual goose, and no misunderstanding of theirs obstructs a single journey the phrase makes; the goose error is not a cost but the very mechanism of the modern sense, as Johnson's dictionary entry proves. For the quotations, the custodians win: the rose sentence detached is not merely diminished but reversed, deployed to bless the doctrine that the play's corpses refute, and the wherefore error shows a misconstrual compounding for centuries inside a phrase that kept its wording immaculate. Detachment, in short, is benign for tools and dangerous for arguments, and the play exported both kinds. The complete account of its gift to English must hold the two findings together: the language took the play's instruments and lost the play's instructions, and the same process accounts for the triumph and the damage.

The detachment has one more property the census should record, because it predicts the future of the census itself. The export is closed. The text is fixed, its canon of famous lines was settled by the quotation trade generations ago, and the process that naturalized phrases across three centuries of print has no new material to work on and a slowing engine: the shared print culture that carried Bartlett's selections into every schoolroom has fragmented, and the phrases already naturalized reproduce from speaker to speaker without reference to any book. New entries can still be reactivated, as the Friar's warning was reactivated by a television series in 2016, but reactivation draws on the standing stock of the quoted; it does not mint. The census the glossary prints is therefore very likely final in its membership, however often its grades may be revised by antedating, the scholarly process by which an earlier example surfaces and a first record quietly moves house. The play's account with the language is settled. What remains is the auditing, and the auditing is not a melancholy task, because the settled account is extraordinary: one play of some three thousand lines, holding two full idioms, a curse-frame in daily service, three ceremonial quotations from a single scene, and a gravity strong enough to pull three foreign phrases into its orbit and hold them there against the documentary record.

## The Verdict on the Play's Gift to English

The play's gift to English, restated at the census's standard of proof, is this. It did not stock the language with coinages in the manner of the legend; the dictionaries, properly read, credit it with first records that shrink under audit and with popularizations that swell under the same audit, and the net of the two movements is not a number but a function. The play is the language's great naturalizing house for the rhetoric of extremity. It took the fate-label, the curse, the race, the thesis, the paradox, and the warning, set each at the point of maximum pressure in the most retold story in English, and launched them with a force that carried two of them into complete independence, three into ceremonial permanence, and all of them into a fame so gravitational that the language misremembers other men's phrases as its own. The cliché against which this series has argued from its first article treats the famous lines as literary ornaments, exhibits in a museum of quotation. The census shows the opposite condition. The lines are tools in daily service, worn smooth by hands that never held the book, and restoring their grades, their dates, and their losses is not antiquarianism. It is the recovery of the play's second text, the one written across four centuries of other people's sentences, in which Romeo and Juliet is still being performed every day, by speakers who would deny being in the cast.

One test separates this census from the legend it replaces, and the survey ends by applying it to itself: the graded account predicts, and the legend only applauds. Given a phrase, the legend can say that the play is great and the phrase famous, which explains everything and nothing. The census can say, from its grades and its law, what will happen next: that the full idioms will continue to soften and spread without custodians; that the ceremonial quotations will hold their occasions as long as farewells and weddings need furniture; that the dormant lines will surface again only when a new medium arrives with a plot shaped like their warning; and that the false attributions will keep regenerating in the lists, because the gravity that produced them is undiminished. A theory of the play's language that can be wrong is worth more than a tribute that cannot, and the documentary record assembled here, Markham's horses and Johnson's goose, Bartlett's index and the dictionaries' dates, is offered in that spirit: as the audited account of how one play's sentences became the mother tongue's small change, spent daily by millions who have never seen the mint. The glossary printed in this survey is the mint's ledger, kept at the standard the mint itself never had to meet, and its balances are the verdict: the play gave English fewer phrases than the legend advertises and more language than the legend understands.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How many phrases did Romeo and Juliet actually give to everyday English?

By the census's graded standard, the honest answer is a distribution rather than a number. Two expressions, star-crossed and wild-goose chase, stand as full idioms with dictionary headwords and source-free everyday use. One more, the plague curse, operates as a living template. Three balcony exports live at quotation strength with ceremonial idiom force, and two further lines remain quotations under institutional custody. Beyond those, the count dissolves into boundary cases, popularizations, and misattributions. Popular lists reach far higher totals by counting every famous line as a donation and by absorbing phrases from other plays and other centuries, which is why published counts disagree so widely: each counter is quietly using a different definition of giving.

### Q: Why do so many of the play's exported phrases come from the Chorus, Mercutio, and one scene of Juliet's?

Because export rewards verdicts, and those speakers deliver them. The Chorus exists to pronounce judgment before the action, and its fate-label became the strongest idiom in the census. Mercutio stands at an angle to the plot, scoring and cursing like a commentator, and his curse and his racing metaphor both travelled. Juliet's great scene is built from argued propositions, a thesis about names, a paradox about parting, a question about identity, each complete inside itself and therefore detachable. The lovers' cries of feeling, by contrast, need their moments to live. Phrases emigrate when they arrive as finished judgments a stranger can re-issue, and the play concentrates its finishable judgments in exactly those mouths.

### Q: Which of the play's phrases became dictionary headwords, and which remained quotations?

Star-crossed and wild-goose chase became headwords, entered in general dictionaries with definitions that define the expression itself, Merriam-Webster dating the first known use of the one to 1597 and of the other, in its fruitless sense, to about 1595. The plague curse survives inside entries as a formula rather than as a lemma in its own right. Everything else in the census remained a quotation: fortune's fool, death-marked, and the Friar's warning have no headwords, and the balcony sentences live in quotation dictionaries and editions rather than in the dictionary of the language. The headword line is the census's independence test made visible, because a headword certifies that speakers need the meaning explained without any reference to a play.

### Q: Is "a fool's errand" one of the phrases this play gave English?

No, and the case is a useful test of attribution by plausibility. The words stand nowhere in the play, and the phrase dictionaries date the expression's currency to the early eighteenth century, more than a hundred years after the first quarto, with the errand custom behind it documented earlier still, in a Flemish poem of 1561 and in John Aubrey's record of the Fooles holy day in 1686. The play covers the same semantic ground a century earlier with the wild-goose chase, which is presumably how the later synonym drifted into the play's cluster in popular lists. The census excludes the fool's errand and treats its presence in the cluster as evidence of the play's gravitational pull on neighbouring phrases.

### Q: Is "love is blind" a quotation from Romeo and Juliet?

No. The sentence is Chaucer's before it is anyone else's: the Merchant's Tale, written in the last decade of the fourteenth century, states that love is blind alday and may nat see, and behind Chaucer stands the blindfolded Cupid of classical iconography. When the playwright wanted the aphorism whole, he gave it to Jessica in The Merchant of Venice. Romeo and Juliet plays variations on the proverb without stating it: Romeo complains that love, whose view is muffled still, should without eyes see pathways to his will, and Benvolio calls the hidden Romeo's love blind while Mercutio answers that if love be blind, love cannot hit the mark. The play is the idea's most famous weather, not its source.

### Q: Did "break the ice" come from Romeo and Juliet?

No. The metaphor is the playwright's and its birth as a social figure is genuinely early, but the play is The Taming of the Shrew. Tranio, briefing Petruchio on the campaign for Bianca, urges him to break the ice and do this feat, achieve the elder, set the younger free: the ice is the elder sister's frozen resistance and unmarried state, and the phrase already means a first breach in a stiff situation. The expression then became one of the most successful Shakespearean idioms in the language, which is exactly why list-makers misfile it under the most famous title available. Its exclusion from this census removes the single largest item of stolen density in the popular accounting of the love play's gifts.

### Q: Why did the play's plot machinery, the letter, the potion, and the apothecary, contribute no everyday phrases?

Because events cannot be re-performed by a sentence, and judgments can. The miscarried letter is the hinge of the tragedy, the apothecary's poverty its most quietly argued scene, the potion its most famous device, yet none of them supplies a stance a stranger can adopt toward a new situation. A speaker facing two feuding committees can re-issue Mercutio's curse; a speaker facing a hopeless search can concede the wild-goose chase; no speaker has ever needed to say that a message failed in Friar John's exact manner. The census's stayed-home column is filled with the causal apparatus for this reason: the machinery is specific to Verona, and specificity is the opposite of portability. The play exports its rulings and retains its equipment.

### Q: Why do the play's phrases tend to soften as they spread?

Because a phrase that travels must be usable on ordinary occasions, and the play's occasions are lethal. Star-crossed arrives in the prologue yoked to take their life and to death-marked love, a doom total and announced; everyday use keeps the thwarting and drops the dying, until the word can bless couples who face nothing worse than disapproval. The farewell couplet is cut in half so that its sweetness can be used without its death-haunted scene. Even the curse is regularized in spelling and aimed at nuisances. Dilution is the purchase price of currency: expressions that can afford to mean less get spent more widely, while a compound like death-marked, which named the doom too exactly to be weakened, was never spent at all.

### Q: What decides whether a line from the play earns a place in a dictionary of quotations?

Detachability decides it, as the history of John Bartlett's Familiar Quotations shows from 1855 onward. A quotation dictionary needs lines that survive removal: complete sense inside the extract, a stance a reader can borrow, and wording compact enough to file under a keyword. The play's aphoristic construction makes it a rich mine for such extraction, since its speakers argue in propositions and curse in balanced sentences. Lines that fail the test are not weaker lines; the potion soliloquy and the tomb speeches are greater poetry than several entries in this census, but they are woven into their moments and cannot be lifted whole. The quotation trade's selections then harden into the public's belief about which phrases the play contains, a feedback loop the census exists to audit.

### Q: How did Mercutio's curse become a template for cursing any two sides at once?

By generalizing its grammar. The text at 3.1 gives a plague o' both your houses three times, always aimed at the pair of households whose feud has mortally wounded a bystander, and everyday use regularized the wording to a plague on both your houses and then abstracted the frame: a plague on X and Y, where any two opposed parties fit the slots. The frame carries its judgment built in, since to use it is to declare both sides equally at fault and the speaker their collateral damage, which is Mercutio's exact position in the scene. Templates travel farther than strings because they manufacture new sentences, and the curse manufactures them for traffic disputes, committee rows, and political feuds its speaker never imagined.

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch phrase glossary, and how are its entries graded?

The InsightCrunch phrase glossary is this survey's census table: every expression the play is credibly said to have given everyday English, with its place in the Folger text, the documentary finding stated at dictionary precision, and a grade. The grades run from full idiom, for star-crossed and wild-goose chase, through the curse's template status and the ceremonial quotations of the balcony scene, down to stayed-home compounds like death-marked, and they end in exclusions for phrases the record assigns elsewhere, love is blind to Chaucer, break the ice to The Taming of the Shrew, the fool's errand to the eighteenth century. The glossary's purpose is comparative: set any popular list beside it and the inflation in the list becomes measurable entry by entry.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch phrase glossary to argue that the play stocked English by naturalizing lines rather than inventing them?

Start from the grades rather than from a count. Show that the strongest entries are transfers and fixings, not mintings: the wild-goose chase was sporting vocabulary in print by 1593 before the play carried it into metaphor, and star-crossed is a compound assembled from living astrological parts whose first surviving record the dictionaries date to the play. Then use the exclusions as the control group, since a method that can refuse love is blind and the fool's errand is a method whose acceptances can be trusted. The essay's conclusion follows the evidence: the play's demonstrable power was to launch, fix, and make unforgettable, which is a rarer gift than invention and the one the documentary record actually supports.

### Q: Why did "these violent delights" wait four centuries for wide currency?

Because the sentence is a doctrine, and doctrines need occasions. For four centuries the Friar's warning at 2.6 lived the quotation's life, admired in studies of the play's imagery of fire and powder, without any everyday situation calling for it: ordinary speech seldom needs a compressed theory of pleasures that consume themselves. When a television series in 2016 built a story about engineered delights turning violent, the occasion arrived fully formed, and the sentence was reactivated at full strength from the custody of editions and quotation dictionaries. The episode proves that dormancy in the quotation class is not death. It also proves the grade's limit, since the revived sentence still travels as a quotation, recognized and attributed, rather than as an idiom speakers use unawares.

### Q: Which phrases in the popular lists of the play's gifts fail the census's tests?

Three fail the first test, presence in the text. Love is blind is Chaucer's sentence, stated in the Merchant's Tale two centuries earlier and used by the playwright himself in The Merchant of Venice, while this play only varies the idea. Break the ice is Tranio's metaphor in The Taming of the Shrew, misfiled under the more famous title. On a fool's errand is an eighteenth-century expression that stands in no text of the play's period. Two more fail the independence test while passing presence: fortune's fool and death-marked are genuine lines and genuine first records that never became idioms, living under institutional custody. The failures share one cause worth naming: fame attracts attribution, and the most famous love play in English collects phrases the way a great house collects furniture from demolished neighbours.

### Q: What did "wild-goose chase" mean before it meant a fruitless search?

It named a horse race. In the follow-the-leader contest described by Gervase Markham in his Discourse of Horsmanshippe in 1593, the leading rider set the course and the rest were bound to follow at a fixed distance, as geese follow in formation, which is where the name comes from. Mercutio's use in 2.4 transfers the race to a duel of wits he is losing, and Merriam-Webster dates the first known use in the fruitless-pursuit sense to about 1595 on the strength of that transfer. The modern meaning was assembled later, after the race was forgotten and hearers reanalysed the compound as a chase after a bird; Samuel Johnson's dictionary of 1755 already defines it as a pursuit of something as unlikely to be caught as the wildgoose.

### Q: Why does the census treat "good night" as a near miss rather than an export?

Because the play fixed a scene, not a phrase. English speakers had exchanged good night for centuries before Juliet leaned from the window, so presence and priority both fail as claims of donation, whatever the scene's fame. What the play did contribute is a choreography: the iterated farewell, good night repeated until the parting itself becomes the lovers' device for prolonging the meeting, crowned by the sweet sorrow couplet that turned the exchange into ceremony. Later culture quotes the choreography whenever it stages a reluctant parting, which is a real influence and a different category from phrase export. The census keeps a popularization grade for such cases, since influence without donation is still influence, and honesty about the boundary is what makes the full-idiom grades worth anything.

### Q: How does this one play's phrase export compare with the rest of the Shakespeare canon?

By density rather than by bulk. Across the whole canon the playwright's presence in the quotation record is unmatched: Bill Bryson's count found a tenth of the entries in the Oxford Dictionary of Quotations drawn from Shakespeare, and Hamlet alone supplies more famous lines than this play. What distinguishes Romeo and Juliet is the conversion rate of its stock into source-free tools. Hamlet's exports remain heavily attributed, worn as quotations by speakers who know they are quoting; this play's two full idioms and its curse-frame circulate anonymously, inside sentences whose speakers owe the text no acknowledgment. Measured by lines that became instruments rather than ornaments, the love play's three thousand lines outperform every comparable text in the canon, which is the defensible form of the density claim made for it.

### Q: What is the strongest evidence that a phrase has fully detached from the play?

A dictionary headword with a source-free life in unstudied prose. When Merriam-Webster enters star-crossed with a definition and a first known use of 1597, and newspapers apply the word to football fixtures and merger talks without gloss or attribution, the detachment is complete: the expression is explained to readers as vocabulary, not as literature. Misreading is corroborating evidence of a darker kind. Wherefore survives with its error attached, used in the place-sense by speakers who have built the wrong scene around it, which could only happen once the sentence had escaped every custodian who might correct it. Detachment is proved, in short, when the phrase can be misunderstood in its own right, no longer protected by the context that once made it unmistakable.

### Q: Can new phrases still join the play's export, or is the census closed?

The membership is effectively closed, though the grades remain open to revision. The text is fixed, and the canon of its famous lines was settled by the quotation trade generations ago; the shared print culture that carried Bartlett's selections into every schoolroom has fragmented, so no new line receives the centuries of uniform repetition that naturalization required. Reactivation remains possible, as the Friar's warning showed in 2016, but reactivation promotes from the standing stock of the quoted rather than minting new stock. What scholarship can still change is the paperwork: antedating, the discovery of an earlier example, quietly moves first records, and a future Markham for some other entry could demote a grade. The play's account with the language is settled; only the auditing continues.

### Q: What does the shape of the export reveal about how the play itself is built?

It reveals a play built from verdicts. The exports are judgments delivered under pressure: a Chorus sentencing the lovers before they appear, a dying wit cursing both houses, a friar warning against excess, a girl arguing that names are nothing while the plot prepares to prove her wrong with corpses. The stays-at-home are the play's events, its letters, potions, ladders, and timings, famous as story and sterile as language. A text whose scenes are engineered as confrontations of finished sentences will naturally donate sentences; a text whose power ran through situation and spectacle would donate images instead. The census of phrases is therefore also a description of the play's construction, read in its effects: Romeo and Juliet holds everyday English because it was written as a machine for producing portable judgment.
