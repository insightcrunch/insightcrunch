## The Distance Between the Statute and the Decision

VA claims enter the law as a short and generous promise. Under section 1110 of title 38, United States Code, Congress authorized compensation for disability resulting from personal injury suffered or disease contracted in the line of duty in active military, naval, or air service. Nothing in the operative text announces the intake centers, the records requests sent to federal agencies, the medical examinations, the rating schedules, or the years that can separate an application from a final answer. The distance between the statute and the decision is the subject of the administration of veterans benefits, and that distance is measured in procedure. A benefit authorized in a single sentence reaches a claimant through a claims file assembled piece by piece, an examination conducted by a clinician who makes no decision, a rating assigned by an adjudicator who conducted no examination, and review options whose deadlines carry real financial consequences.

![VA claims process from intent to file through examination decision and review lanes - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-63.webp)

The formal identity of the system explains part of that structure. Claims administration rests in chapter 51 of title 38, which governs claims, notice, the duty to assist, decisions, and effective dates in sections 5101 to 5110. Representation rests in chapter 59, covering agents and attorneys in sections 5901 to 5904. Review inside the Department of Veterans Affairs rests in chapter 71, which establishes the Board of Veterans' Appeals, and judicial review rests in chapter 72, which establishes the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims. Two statutes rebuilt the machinery inside that frame. The Veterans Claims Assistance Act of 2000, Public Law 106-475, signed on November 9, 2000, rewrote the development duties in chapter 51 and eliminated the requirement that a claim be well grounded before the duty to assist arose. The Veterans Appeals Improvement and Modernization Act of 2017, Public Law 115-55, signed on August 23, 2017, and implemented on February 19, 2019, replaced the sequential appeal structure for decisions issued on or after that date with a choice among three review lanes.

Distance accumulates for structural reasons. The evidence of a service era is scattered across service treatment records held by the Department of Defense, treatment records held by the Veterans Health Administration, private medical records held by civilian providers, and personnel records spanning paper archives and electronic systems. The legal questions are also medical questions, because compensation turns on diagnosis, on severity measured against the schedule for rating disabilities in section 1155, and on a causal judgment connecting a condition in the record to an event, injury, or disease in service. Congress designed the system to be nonadversarial and to place development burdens on the government rather than on the claimant, yet that design still requires an institution capable of finding records, scheduling examinations, and applying a rating schedule consistently across a national caseload. The statute supplies the standard; capacity supplies the decision.

### Who decides a claim?

Regional offices of the Veterans Benefits Administration decide claims in the first instance, gathering records, ordering examinations, and issuing rating decisions. The Board of Veterans' Appeals reviews disagreements inside the department. The Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims reviews Board decisions from outside the department, on the administrative record alone.

The machine has three levels, and each level performs a different function. At the first level, the Veterans Benefits Administration, an administration inside the Department of Veterans Affairs, acts through regional offices located in every state. In the vocabulary of title 38, the regional office is the agency of original jurisdiction: the office that receives the claim, develops the record, and issues the first decision. At the second level, the Board of Veterans' Appeals, established under chapter 71 and seated in Washington, conducts appellate review inside the department, through Veterans Law Judges who decide appeals on the record, on evidence within defined windows, or after a hearing, depending on the docket. At the third level, the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims, an independent court established under chapter 72, reviews Board decisions under section 7252, which grants exclusive jurisdiction over Board decisions and confines review to the record before the Secretary and the Board. Decisions of the Court are subject to review in the Federal Circuit under section 7292, on legal questions.

Inside that machine, one administrative act carries a weight out of proportion to the effort the act requires, and the point deserves a name: the filing date is the money. Section 5110 fixes the effective date of an award, the date from which benefits are calculated and from which any past due amount is paid, and the general rule forbids an effective date earlier than the date of receipt of the application. An exception reaches back to the day following discharge for an application filed within one year of separation, and regulation supplies a second protective device. Under title 38, Code of Federal Regulations, section 3.155, a claimant may record an intent to file, and a complete claim submitted within one year of that recording is treated as though the claim had arrived on the earlier date. The same logic runs through review: under section 5110, a claim continuously pursued through a timely request for higher-level review, a timely supplemental claim, or a timely appeal to the Board retains the filing date of the initial application. A system designed to be navigable without counsel therefore turns, at the point of greatest financial consequence, on a step that takes minutes.

Development is where the nonadversarial design becomes concrete, and the Veterans Claims Assistance Act of 2000 is the development statute. Section 5103 requires notice, after receipt of a complete or substantially complete application, describing the information and evidence necessary to substantiate the claim. Section 5103A imposes the duty to assist: reasonable efforts to obtain evidence necessary to substantiate the claim, including records held by federal agencies, service records, department treatment records, and private records that the claimant identifies and authorizes the department to obtain. When records cannot be obtained, the statute requires notice identifying the records and explaining the efforts made. The 2000 act mattered because the duty had previously been gated: before November 2000, adjudication doctrine required a well grounded claim before assistance attached, so claimants least able to assemble evidence alone faced a threshold built from evidence the department had not yet helped to gather.

The examination sits at the center of development for disability compensation, and section 5103A, subsection (d), defines when the obligation attaches. An examination or medical opinion is required when the record contains competent evidence of a current disability or of persistent or recurrent symptoms, indicates that the disability or symptoms may be associated with service, and lacks sufficient medical evidence for a decision. The examination, commonly called a compensation and pension examination, is an agency obligation rather than a claimant expense, performed by department clinicians or by contractors under department arrangements. The examiner diagnoses, measures, and offers an opinion on association with service; the examiner decides nothing. A rating specialist then weighs the report against service records, treatment records, and lay testimony and assigns the outcome.

Evidence law inside the system is more permissive than evidence law in ordinary civil litigation, by deliberate statutory choice. Section 5107 places responsibility for presenting and supporting a claim on the claimant, and then directs that when positive and negative evidence on a material issue stands in approximate balance, the Secretary shall give the benefit of the doubt to the claimant. Lay testimony counts within that balance: statements from a claimant, fellow service members, spouses, and others are competent on matters within ordinary observation, such as symptoms experienced and onset noticed, even though diagnosis and causation generally require medical evidence. Section 1111 adds the presumption of sound condition, under which a veteran is taken to have been in sound condition at entry into service, except for defects noted at entry, unless clear and unmistakable evidence demonstrates that a condition existed before service and was not aggravated by service.

A rating decision converts the assembled record into three determinations, each independently reviewable. The decision grants or denies service connection, the legal finding that a disability is connected to service. The decision assigns a percentage evaluation under the rating schedule when service connection is granted, translating medical findings into the compensation rate, and assigns the effective date, which fixes the past due amount. Section 5104 requires notice of a decision, and the modernization act strengthened that notice so that findings, evidence considered, and review options appear in the decision letter itself. A disagreement aimed at only one element, the percentage, leaves the other two untouched unless each is identified for review.

For decisions issued before February 19, 2019, disagreement set a single sequential path in motion, and the path explains why reform legislation arrived in 2017. A notice of disagreement filed within one year opened review at the regional office. The agency then issued a statement of the case, and a substantive appeal on VA Form 9, filed within sixty days of that statement or within the remainder of the one year period from the original decision, completed the appeal to the Board. Additional evidence generated a supplemental statement of the case, and remands from the Board for further development were a standard feature, each remand restarting development, examination, and readjudication. The legacy structure treated review as a ladder with one route upward: every disagreement entered the same channel, and every evidentiary gap discovered late sent the file back to the bottom.

The modernization act replaced the ladder with a fork. For a decision issued on or after February 19, 2019, a claimant who disagrees selects among three lanes within one year of the decision, and the lanes differ in record, in decision maker, and in consequence. Higher-level review, under section 5104B, assigns the file to a more experienced adjudicator for review on the record as it stood at the initial decision, with no additional evidence admitted and an optional informal conference; identification of a duty to assist error returns the claim for correction. A supplemental claim, under section 5108, reopens decision making at the agency of original jurisdiction on the basis of evidence that is new and relevant, and the duty to assist attaches again. An appeal to the Board proceeds by notice of disagreement under chapter 71 and a selection among three dockets: direct review on the existing record, submission of additional evidence within a ninety day window without a hearing, or a hearing before a Veterans Law Judge. The lanes are alternatives rather than stages, an issue may occupy only one lane at a time, and movement between lanes inside the one year window preserves the original filing date under the continuous pursuit rule in section 5110. Legacy appeals pending at the February 2019 launch continued under the prior structure, with defined opportunities to opt in after a statement of the case.

Chapter 59 determines who may guide a claimant through that fork, and draws a bright line around recognized help. Section 5901 prohibits acting as a claims agent or attorney before the department without recognition, and sections 5901 to 5904 build an accreditation system administered by the department. Representatives of recognized veterans service organizations act under section 5902 and serve without charge. Individual agents and attorneys act under section 5904 after accreditation, which carries standards of conduct and exposure to suspension or exclusion from practice before the department. Accreditation is therefore the dividing line between representation inside the system and advice sold outside the system.

Fee law carries the longest memory in the system. A statutory cap of ten dollars on agent and attorney fees dated to the Civil War era and survived, in substance, into the late twentieth century. The Veterans Judicial Review Act of 1988, Public Law 100-687, which also created judicial review of Board decisions, permitted fees for services after a final Board decision in the case. The 2006 amendments in Public Law 109-461 moved the permissible point earlier, to services in cases in which a notice of disagreement had been filed, for notices filed on or after the date one hundred eighty days after enactment on December 22, 2006. Chapter 59 bars a fee for preparing the initial claim, and a fee agreement providing for direct payment by the Secretary out of past due benefits, at a percentage not exceeding twenty percent of the past due amount, is presumed reasonable under section 5904.

The backlog is the point at which procedure becomes arithmetic, and the figures require a definition before the figures mean anything. Department reporting has long defined the rating backlog as claims pending more than one hundred twenty five days. On that definition, the backlog peaked at more than 611,000 claims in March 2013, according to figures the Department of Veterans Affairs reported in April 2014, and department reporting placed the figure near 70,000 by September 2015 after mandatory overtime, additional hiring, and the transition from paper claims folders to the electronic record in the Veterans Benefits Management System. Measurement itself remained contested: a September 2018 report of the VA Office of Inspector General estimated that tens of thousands of overdue rating cases had been omitted or misclassified in the official count. Appeals formed a second, slower queue, counted separately.

Causation runs in more than one direction, and the expansion mechanism deserves the same precision as the failure mechanism. The rating backlog climbed after 2010 in substantial part because eligibility expanded: the Secretary added ischemic heart disease, certain leukemias, and Parkinson disease to the conditions presumed connected to Agent Orange exposure, and a court settlement required readjudication of 150,000 previously decided claims, while the department also received more than 100,000 claims for the added conditions, according to the department account published in April 2014. Every expansion of eligibility converts legal generosity into processing volume on a timetable set by enactment, while hiring and examination capacity expand on a timetable set by appropriations. The gap between the two timetables is the backlog.

The benefits system operates inside a department whose credibility on waiting and measurement was damaged in the health care system in 2014. The 2014 wait-time scandal, in which falsified scheduling records at the Phoenix health care system and elsewhere concealed the length of waits for medical appointments, produced resignations, inspector general investigation, and [the accountability and community care legislation enacted in response](/2019/04/01/veterans-choice-act-2014-guide/), the Veterans Access, Choice, and Accountability Act of 2014, Public Law 113-146, signed on August 7, 2014. Health care scheduling and benefits adjudication are separate operations, and conflating the two is an error. The connection is institutional: a department that reports a wait or a backlog is grading a queue that the department itself manages, and after 2014 every department figure on timeliness was read against the possibility that a reported number and an experienced wait could diverge.

Beneath the statutes sits a second body of law that claimants and adjudicators handle day to day, and the distance between statute and decision lives largely in that layer. The Code of Federal Regulations translates title 38 into adjudication rules in part 3, accreditation and fee rules in part 14, and Board procedure in part 20. The Adjudication Procedures Manual, designated M21-1, instructs rating and development personnel in operational detail that no statute contains: which records requests to generate and which development steps must be documented before a file is ready for decision. Rulemaking under the modernization act, published in final form in January 2019, rewrote the regulations governing review options, Board dockets, and representation, so that the system in force in June 2019 was a product of statute, regulation, and manual in combination.

The material that follows is general information about a public benefits system rather than guidance on any individual claim. Congress wrote a claimant protective standard, complete with a duty to assist, a benefit of the doubt rule, and presumptions that shift the cost of missing evidence to the government. Delivery depends on intake discipline measured in days, development capacity measured in records requests and examination appointments, and review design measured in lanes and dockets. The filing date is the money, the examination is the fulcrum, and the lane selection is the strategy, each a consequence of administrative design rather than statutory generosity alone.

## The Filing Clock That Fixes the Value of VA Claims

A disability claim has a value before any evidence is weighed, any examination is scheduled, or any rating is assigned, and that value is set by a date. The governing claim of the filing stage is that the filing date is the money. Title 38 separates two questions that ordinary language merges into one. The merits question asks whether a disability is connected to service and how severe the disability is under the rating schedule. The clock question asks from which date payment for a favorable answer runs. A claimant can prevail completely on the merits and still lose months or years of compensation because the clock question was answered late. Conversely, a modest administrative act performed at the start, the recording of an intent to file, can add a year of retroactive payment to an award that takes far longer than a year to decide. Every later stage of administration operates inside the boundary that the first recorded date establishes, and later advocacy cannot move a boundary the filing rules have fixed.

The general effective date rule appears in 38 U.S.C. section 5110(a) and in the implementing regulation at 38 C.F.R. section 3.400. Unless a specific statutory exception provides otherwise, the effective date of an award based on an original claim, a claim reopened after final adjudication, or a claim for increase is fixed in accordance with the facts found, and the effective date cannot be earlier than the date of receipt of the application. The regulation states the same rule in adjudicator form: the effective date is the date of receipt of the claim or the date entitlement arose, whichever is later. The two dates measure different things. The date of claim is an administrative event, the arrival of a filing in a form the regulations recognize. The date entitlement arose is a factual and legal event, the point at which the evidence shows that the statutory conditions for the benefit were met. Payment follows the later of the two because Congress tied compensation to a claim rather than to disability alone. A disability that existed for a decade before any filing generates no payment for that decade under the general rule, however well documented the disability is.

The arithmetic explains why adjudication delay and filing delay have opposite consequences. Delay after filing is compensated if the claim succeeds, because the award reaches back to the effective date and the interval between filing and decision becomes retroactive payment. Delay before filing is compensated for no one. Each month between the onset or recognition of a disability and the recording of a claim is a month subtracted from the eventual award at whatever rate the claim ultimately receives, and the subtraction is permanent. A claim decided after three years of development on a filing recorded promptly can produce three years of accumulated payment. A claim decided in three months on a filing recorded a year after the disability was first documented produces payment only from the later filing, and the speed of the decision cannot restore the missing year.

Two special rules qualify the general rule, and both reward prompt filing measured against a dated event. The first concerns separation from service. Under 38 U.S.C. section 5110(b)(1) and 38 C.F.R. section 3.400(b)(2), the effective date of an award of disability compensation is the day following the date of discharge or release from active service if the application is received within one year from that date of discharge or release. A filing that arrives eleven months after separation therefore holds an effective date at the start of civilian life. A filing received one day beyond the year receives no benefit from the rule at all, and the general rule supplies the date of claim instead. The rule operates as a window with a hard edge rather than as a sliding scale, and the value of the window depends on a filing, complete or preserved through an intent to file, landing inside the year.

The second special rule concerns claims for an increased rating of a service connected disability, and that rule looks backward from the filing rather than forward from a separation date. For increased ratings, 38 U.S.C. section 5110(b)(2) provides that the effective date is the earliest date as of which it is ascertainable that an increase in disability had occurred, if the application is received within one year from that date, and 38 C.F.R. section 3.400(o)(2) implements the provision in the same terms. Section 3.400(o)(1) states the baseline, the later of the date of claim or the date entitlement arose, and paragraph (o)(2) then permits the effective date to move back to the date an increase was factually ascertainable, provided the increase occurred within the one year period preceding receipt of the claim. Factually ascertainable means that the evidence of record, including treatment records and competent lay evidence describing observable worsening, shows the higher level of disability existed on that earlier date. An increase ascertainable eight months before filing can support an effective date eight months before filing. An increase ascertainable fourteen months before filing cannot, because the look back is capped at one year.

### What does an intent to file do?

An intent to file records the date a claim enters the system without requiring a complete application on that date. A complete claim filed within one year takes the intent date as the filing date, and the effective date for payment follows that earlier date rather than the later submission date.

The intent to file is a creature of regulation, codified at 38 C.F.R. section 3.155(b), and the design is deliberately minimal. A claimant, an authorized representative, a Member of Congress, or a person acting as next friend for a claimant not of full age or capacity may submit an intent to file a claim for benefits administered by the department. The submission must provide sufficient identifiable or biographical information to identify the claimant, and the intent must identify the general benefit sought, such as compensation, pension, or survivors benefits, because a separate intent is associated with a separate benefit type. No evidence accompanies the act, and no showing of entitlement is made or examined. The department, upon receipt, furnishes the appropriate application form prescribed by the Secretary. The legal effect attaches to receipt itself. If the department receives a complete claim appropriate to the benefit sought within one year of receipt of the intent to file, the complete claim is considered filed on the date the intent to file was received.

The regulation recognizes three channels, and each channel produces the same legal effect when the recording requirements are met. The first channel is a saved electronic application. When an application is initiated and saved in a claims submission tool within a department web based electronic claims application system before a complete claim is filed, the saved application is treated as an intent to file. The second channel is a written intent on the prescribed form, submitted signed and dated on VA Form 21-0966, Intent to File a Claim for Compensation and/or Pension, or Survivors Pension and/or DIC. The third channel is oral. An oral statement of intent communicated to department personnel designated to receive such a communication is accepted when the employee receiving the statement records the intent in writing and documents the date of receipt in the claimant records, following the procedure at 38 C.F.R. section 3.217(b). A telephone call to the department national call center operates through the oral channel.

The one year period that follows an intent is both generous and unforgiving. The complete claim must be received within one year of the date the intent was received, and receipt by the department, not dispatch, is the event the regulation measures. Only one complete claim for a benefit may be associated with each intent to file for that benefit, although multiple issues may appear within that claim. When multiple complete claims for the same benefit arrive within the year, only the first is associated with the intent, so a condition omitted from that filing and raised in a second filing takes the second filing date rather than the intent date. When an intent and an incomplete application form both precede the complete claim, the complete claim is considered filed on the earlier receipt date, provided perfection occurs within the necessary period. An intent that passes the one year mark without a complete claim expires without effect, and the period is not extended by a second intent recorded during the first period. The period also interacts with the separation window in section 5110(b)(1), because a perfected claim is deemed filed on the intent date, so an intent recorded inside the year after discharge places the deemed filing date inside that window.

The definition of a complete claim at 38 C.F.R. section 3.160(a) controls the transition from preserved date to pending claim. A complete claim is a submission of an application form prescribed by the Secretary that provides the name of the claimant, the relationship to the veteran where applicable, and sufficient information for the department to verify the claimed service. The submission must be signed by the claimant or by a person legally authorized to sign for the claimant. The submission must identify the benefit sought, and a description of symptoms or medical conditions on which the benefit is based must be provided to the extent the prescribed form requires that description. Pension claims carry an additional element concerning a statement of income to the extent the form requires one. The statutory foundation is 38 U.S.C. section 5101(a), which provides that a specific claim in the form prescribed by the Secretary must be filed in order for benefits to be paid under the laws administered by the Secretary. For disability compensation, the prescribed form is VA Form 21-526EZ, Application for Disability Compensation and Related Compensation Benefits. Receipt of a complete claim triggers the next stages, including the duty to assist in obtaining records and the scheduling of examinations where the record requires one, while an intent standing alone triggers no development at all.

The requirement that claims arrive on prescribed forms, with an intent to file as the sole general mechanism for preserving an earlier date, dates from a rulemaking that closed a much older and looser practice. The department published the standard forms final rule on September 25, 2014, and the rule took effect on March 24, 2015. From that effective date, new claims had to be filed on the standard application forms prescribed by the Secretary, and communications outside the prescribed forms and outside the intent to file procedure no longer functioned as claims for new filings. Claims arriving in unstructured formats had required department personnel to interpret correspondence before development could begin, and disagreements about whether a communication had started a claim generated substantial adjudication on the threshold question of what counted as a claim and when that claim began. Standardization traded interpretive flexibility for a filing event that could be identified, dated, and processed without a preliminary legal judgment, and the intent to file procedure was the compensating mechanism built into the same rule.

The practice displaced in March 2015 was the informal claim. Under the former version of 38 C.F.R. section 3.155(a), any communication or action indicating an intent to apply for one or more benefits, from a claimant, an authorized representative, a Member of Congress, or a person acting as next friend, could be considered an informal claim, provided the communication identified the benefit sought. A letter, a note, or another unstructured writing could start the clock. Upon receipt, the department forwarded an application form, and a formal claim received within one year from the date the form was sent was considered filed on the date of receipt of the informal claim. Related provisions treated certain records as informal claims as well, including reports of examination or hospitalization under former section 3.157 in defined circumstances. The breadth protected claimants who wrote without knowledge of forms or procedure, and the same breadth produced persistent uncertainty, because a claim could turn on the reading given to a sentence in a letter written for another purpose. The standard forms rule ended that regime for new filings while leaving pending claims under the rules that had governed the filings when made.

The transition was contested on exactly the ground the clock makes visible. The American Legion, the Military Order of the Purple Heart, the National Veterans Legal Services Program, AMVETS, and Vietnam Veterans of America filed a challenge to the rule in the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit in March 2015, arguing in substance that elimination of informal claims disadvantaged claimants unfamiliar with prescribed forms. Disabled American Veterans and Veterans of Foreign Wars brought a separate challenge on related grounds. The department position, stated during the rulemaking, was that standardized forms identified the nature of a claim more quickly, reduced delays from missing information, and that the intent to file procedure preserved the essential protection of the informal claim in a form the processing system could recognize without interpretation.

The reason the filing stage is the step most often missed follows from the structure of attention inside a benefits system. A claimant confronting a disability naturally concentrates on the disability, on treatment, on records, and on proof of connection to service, because the eventual rating depends on them. The intent to file, by contrast, presents as paperwork preliminary to the real claim, requires no evidence, decides nothing, and produces no visible response beyond an acknowledgment and a form. A step that takes minutes and settles no substantive question is easy to postpone in favor of gathering material, and postponement is precisely the error the clock punishes. Evidence gathering that occupies eight months before any filing consumes eight months of payment, while the same eight months spent after an intent is recorded consume nothing, because the claim filed at the end of the period inherits the date at the start. Claimants with representation or prior experience of the system are the most likely to record an intent promptly, while a first time claimant without either is the most likely to assemble a complete filing slowly and lose the interval.

Two further features of the clock complete the filing picture. The intent to file procedure at section 3.155(b) applies to claims governed by part 3 of the regulations, and the modernized review system that took effect on February 19, 2019 under the Veterans Appeals Improvement and Modernization Act of 2017, Public Law 115-55, treats supplemental claims differently, because a supplemental claim carries a separate form and a separate effective date mechanism based on continuous pursuit. An intent to file therefore belongs to the original filing stage and to claims for increase framed as new filings, rather than to the review lanes that follow an initial decision. The second feature concerns receipt. The date of receipt, defined at 38 C.F.R. section 3.1(r) as the date on which a claim, information, or evidence was received by the department, is the operative date throughout the filing rules, so the documented arrival of a filing matters more than the date written on the filing or the date a filing was sent. Electronic submission generates a system record, paper submission generates a date stamp and intake record, and oral intent generates the written documentation the receiving employee creates.

The filing stage therefore performs a function out of proportion to the effort the stage requires. The statute withholds payment for any period before a claim in recognized form reaches the department, subject to the separation window and the increased rating look back, and the regulations supply a low cost instrument, the intent to file, that moves the recognized date to the first signal of an intention to claim. A disability claim is ultimately paid as a monthly rate multiplied across time, and the filing rules decide where the time begins. Development can strengthen a rating, examination can document severity, and review can correct an error, but each of those later functions operates on a period that the first recorded date has delimited. The clock is set before the case is built, and the case is paid according to the clock.

## Building the Record After a Claim Is Filed

Development is the stage between filing and decision in which the Department of Veterans Affairs assembles the evidence on which a rating decision will rest. A claim form identifies the benefit sought and the conditions claimed, but the form alone rarely establishes a diagnosis, an event in service, or a connection between the two. Development fills those gaps in an ordered way, through records requests to the custodians who hold the evidence and through a medical examination when the assembled record raises a question that only medical judgment can answer. The stage operates inside a system Congress designed to be nonadversarial, in which the agency must help build the record before deciding whether the record supports an award.

Congress set that allocation of labor in statute in the Veterans Claims Assistance Act of 2000, Public Law 106-475, enacted on November 9, 2000. Before that enactment, a claimant first had to present a well grounded claim before the duty to assist attached, a threshold that could end a case before the agency had gathered any records at all. The 2000 act removed the well grounded claim requirement and codified the duties to notify and to assist as freestanding obligations, principally at 38 U.S.C. section 5103, which governs notice, and 38 U.S.C. section 5103A, which governs assistance. The department implemented those provisions in regulation at 38 C.F.R. section 3.159, which translates the statutory language into working rules about requests for records, followup efforts, and examinations.

The duty to notify under section 5103 begins when the department receives a complete or substantially complete application. At that point the Secretary must notify the claimant, and a representative if one has been appointed, of any information and any medical or lay evidence not already provided that is necessary to substantiate the claim. The notice must also divide responsibility, indicating which portion of the information and evidence the claimant is to provide and which portion the Secretary will attempt to obtain under section 5103A and any other applicable provision of law. The division matters because the categories of evidence sit in different hands, with service records in government custody, private treatment records with civilian providers who release them only on a signed authorization, and personal knowledge of symptoms, dates, and events with the claimant. Section 5103 also gives the claimant one year from the date of the notice to furnish the information and evidence described.

The duty to assist under section 5103A is stated in general terms before the statute turns to particulars. The Secretary must make reasonable efforts to assist a claimant in obtaining evidence necessary to substantiate a claim for a benefit under a law administered by the Secretary. The duty has a boundary stated in the same provision, since assistance is not required when no reasonable possibility exists that the assistance would aid in substantiating the claim. Within that boundary, three forms of assistance dominate disability claims. The agency must obtain service records and relevant records held by federal departments and agencies, must make reasonable efforts to obtain relevant records outside federal custody that the claimant identifies and authorizes the agency to pursue, and must provide a medical examination or obtain a medical opinion when an examination or opinion is necessary to make a decision on the claim.

Service records form the foundation of the file, because service connection turns on events that occurred during military service, often decades before the claim. Section 5103A directs the agency, in a disability compensation claim, to obtain the claimant's service medical records, called service treatment records in department practice, and, when the claimant has furnished information sufficient to locate them, other relevant records pertaining to active military, naval, or air service held or maintained by a governmental entity. The principal repository for separated service members is the National Personnel Records Center in St. Louis, Missouri, an office of the National Archives and Records Administration that holds military personnel, health, and medical records for discharged veterans of all services. A request to that center is a routine early step in development, and the response becomes part of the claims file.

Personnel records matter alongside treatment records, and development regularly reaches for both. Treatment records document complaints, diagnoses, and clinical findings during service, while personnel records document assignments, units, duties, and the places and periods of service that can corroborate an account of exposure or injury when clinical entries are thin. Clinical records created during inpatient hospital treatment were in many eras filed separately from outpatient treatment records, so a request that retrieves one set does not always retrieve the other, and a careful request identifies a hospitalization by facility and approximate date so that a custodian can search the correct series. Records can also be incomplete for reasons no request can cure, including loss in storage or in transfer between the service departments and the archives, and in such a case the file records the efforts made and the responses received.

A second channel of federal records runs through the department itself and through other federal agencies. Treatment records from department health care facilities are federal records the agency obtains from within its own system, and relevant department treatment evidence belongs in the claims file whether or not a claimant asked for inclusion. Records held by the Social Security Administration can be relevant when a disability determination by that agency rested on medical evidence overlapping the conditions claimed, and section 5103A also reaches any other relevant records held by any federal department or agency that the claimant adequately identifies and authorizes the Secretary to obtain. The standard governing federal records is the most demanding standard in the development scheme. When the Secretary attempts to obtain records from a federal department or agency, the efforts must continue until the records are obtained, unless the Secretary concludes that the records do not exist or that further efforts to obtain them would be futile.

The futility standard governs when a search may stop, and the standard is not satisfied by a single unanswered request or by a single negative response from one custodian when another custodian or another series might hold the records. The conclusion must rest on responses and information that make further effort pointless, such as a custodian's statement that the records sought were never created or were destroyed, or a search that closes the plausible locations where such records would be kept. When the agency concludes that records cannot be obtained, the Secretary must notify the claimant that the records could not be obtained, identify the records involved, explain briefly the efforts made to obtain them, and describe any further action the Secretary will take with respect to the claim. That notification makes the gap a documented fact of the record rather than an unexplained oversight.

Private treatment records follow a different rule, calibrated to the fact that the custodians stand outside federal control. Many claimants receive treatment after service from private physicians, hospitals, and clinics, and those records often carry the postservice diagnoses and symptom histories that a decision requires. Section 5103A obliges the Secretary to make reasonable efforts to obtain relevant private records, together with records from state and local governments and from current or former employers, when the claimant adequately identifies the records and authorizes the Secretary to obtain them. Authorization matters because privacy law can bar release without a signed release in a form the custodian accepts. Reasonable efforts for records outside federal custody are defined in regulation as an initial request and, if the records are not received, at least one followup request, with a followup unnecessary when a response to the initial request shows that the records do not exist or that a followup would be futile.

The claimant's corresponding duties give the agency duties something to work with. A claimant must cooperate fully with the agency's reasonable efforts, must provide enough information to identify and locate existing records, including the custodian holding the records, the approximate time frame the records cover, and, for medical treatment records, the condition for which treatment was provided, and must authorize the release of records in a form acceptable to the custodian when authorization is necessary. A reference to treatment somewhere in a given state during a given decade gives a custodian nothing searchable, and development can stall for reasons attributable to the request rather than to the agency. Claimants may also submit private records directly, and such evidence enters the same file as evidence the agency obtained.

### What is a compensation and pension exam?

A compensation and pension examination is a medical examination the Department of Veterans Affairs arranges and pays for during development of a disability claim. An agency clinician or contract examiner records findings on standardized questionnaires and, when asked, gives an opinion about diagnosis, severity, and possible connection to service.

The examination component of the duty to assist appears in section 5103A in direct terms. In a claim for disability compensation, the assistance provided by the Secretary must include providing a medical examination or obtaining a medical opinion when such an examination or opinion is necessary to make a decision on the claim. The statute then defines necessity through the evidence already of record, treating an examination or opinion as necessary when the record contains competent evidence that the claimant has a current disability, or persistent or recurrent symptoms of a disability, and indicates that the disability or symptoms may be associated with active military, naval, or air service, but does not contain sufficient medical evidence for the Secretary to make a decision on the claim. The examination exists to answer a medical question that the rest of the record has raised but cannot answer, most often whether a diagnosed condition is related to service, or what level of impairment a service connected condition produces under the rating schedule in 38 C.F.R. Part 4.

The Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims gave that statutory test its working shape in McLendon v. Nicholson, 20 Vet. App. 79 (2006). The court identified four elements that together trigger the duty, beginning with competent evidence of a current disability or persistent or recurrent symptoms of a disability, and evidence establishing that an event, injury, or disease occurred in service, or that a covered disease manifested during an applicable presumptive period. An indication must also exist that the disability or symptoms may be associated with service or with another service connected disability, while competent medical evidence on file remains insufficient for the Secretary to make a decision on the claim. McLendon fixed the meaning of the indication element, holding that the threshold for determining whether evidence indicates that a disability may be associated with service is a low one. The regulation the court construed, 38 C.F.R. section 3.159(c)(4), uses the language of indication and possibility rather than probability, and the court read that language as written. Lay evidence, service records showing a relevant event, and postservice records showing continuity of symptoms can supply the indication.

Examinations are performed by clinicians of the department and by examiners working under contract, and the contract channel has a statutory history of its own. Section 504 of the Veterans' Benefits Improvements Act of 1996, Public Law 104-275, authorized a pilot program under which no more than ten regional offices could conduct disability examinations through persons other than department employees. Authority for contract examinations was extended repeatedly after the pilot, the program expanded from ten regional offices to fifteen between 2014 and 2016, and management of the national contract examination program passed from the Veterans Health Administration to the Veterans Benefits Administration in 2016, with all regional offices gaining access to the mandatory contract program beginning in fiscal year 2017. In either channel the examination is arranged by the agency, scheduled by the agency or by a contractor acting for the agency, and paid for by the agency, and the resulting report enters the claims file as agency obtained evidence.

A compensation and pension examination is an adjudicative examination rather than a treatment encounter, and the examiner does not assume ongoing care of the claimant. The product of the appointment is a report for the claims file, built from review of the record, a history taken from the claimant, and the physical examination or testing the claimed condition requires. Standardized forms discipline that report and make findings comparable across examiners and facilities, since disability benefits questionnaires are condition specific forms that prompt an examiner through the diagnosis, symptoms, clinical findings, and functional effects that the rating criteria for a given condition require. A Veterans Health Administration directive issued on January 14, 2013, described the questionnaires as a documentation tool designed to provide the precise medical evidence needed for adjudication. A private physician may also complete a questionnaire as evidence, but the department does not reimburse a claimant for costs incurred when a private physician completes one.

The reason the examination is an agency obligation rather than a claimant expense runs deeper than convenience. The medical question in a service connection claim is a question the statutory scheme itself creates, since Congress conditioned compensation on a connection between a present disability and events in service that the government itself documented and archived. Requiring each claimant to purchase a competent medical opinion on that question would price the benefit according to ability to pay and would reproduce, inside a nonadversarial system, the expert market of ordinary litigation. The agency that holds the service records, employs or contracts with the examiners, and will decide the claim therefore bears the cost of generating the medical evidence a decision requires.

An examination that fails to answer the question presented does not discharge the duty, and the law of adequacy governs what happens next. In Barr v. Nicholson, 21 Vet. App. 303 (2007), the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims held that once the department undertakes to provide an examination in developing a service connection claim, the department must provide an adequate examination or, at minimum, notify the claimant why an adequate examination will not or cannot be provided. An adequate examination rests on consideration of prior medical history and examinations, describes the disability in sufficient detail for a fully informed evaluation, and addresses the medical question the development request posed. Reports fail that standard in recognizable ways, as when an examiner states that the claims file was never reviewed, or when an opinion rests on an inaccurate factual premise about the service record. An opinion may also offer a conclusion with no rationale connecting the conclusion to the evidence and the medical principles involved, or may ignore a claimant's reports of symptoms and continuity, even though lay reports form part of the record the opinion was meant to evaluate.

The remedy for inadequacy during development is further development rather than decision on a defective record. A report that is incomplete or unclear can be returned for clarification or an addendum opinion from the same examiner, and a report that cannot be cured in that way supports a new examination with a different examiner or a fresh medical opinion based on the assembled file. The Board of Veterans' Appeals applies the same logic at the review stage, remanding claims for new examinations or opinions when the examination of record proves inadequate for adjudication, because a decision resting on an inadequate examination inherits the defect.

Claimant duties complete the development picture, and the duty to report for a scheduled examination is the most consequential of them. Regulations at 38 C.F.R. section 3.326 provide that individuals for whom an examination has been authorized and scheduled are required to report for the examination. Section 3.655 attaches defined consequences when entitlement cannot be established or confirmed without an examination and a claimant, without good cause, fails to report, with good cause illustrated by examples such as the illness or hospitalization of the claimant or the death of an immediate family member. When a claimant fails without good cause to report for an examination scheduled in connection with an original compensation claim, the claim is rated on the evidence of record, a rule that can leave a claim to fail for want of the medical evidence the examination would have supplied. When the examination was scheduled in connection with another original claim, a supplemental claim for a benefit previously disallowed, or a claim for increase, the claim is denied.

Development ends when the assembled record permits a decision, and delay in claims processing is frequently delay in development, in a records request awaiting a custodian, in an examination awaiting an appointment, or in a report returned for clarification because the first opinion did not answer the question asked. The agency tells the claimant what a claim requires, gathers what government custody holds, makes bounded and documented efforts toward what private custody holds, and generates through examination the medical evidence that no custodian holds at all. The decision that follows therefore rests on a record the system, and not merely the claimant, had a fair opportunity to complete.

## Evidence and the Rating Decision

Development ends with a file. Service treatment records, post-service treatment records from Department of Veterans Affairs facilities and private providers, examination reports, and statements from the claimant and other witnesses sit in a single electronic claims folder, and the adjudicative task is to convert that accumulation into findings. The conversion is governed by evidentiary rules that differ from the rules of ordinary civil litigation in three respects that shape every outcome. The standard of proof favors the claimant at equipoise. Lay testimony carries a formal place in the record that statute and appellate case law protect against summary dismissal. A presumption about health at entry into service places the heaviest burden in the system on the government rather than on the claimant. A rating specialist who misapplies any one of the three rules produces a decision vulnerable on review, and correct application of the three rules can support a grant on a record that would fail under the preponderance standard used in most other benefit and civil contexts.

The starting point is completeness of consideration. Title 38 requires the Secretary to consider all information and lay and medical evidence of record, a command stated in section 5107(a) and reinforced by the regulation at 38 C.F.R. section 3.303(a), which directs that each disabling condition be considered on the basis of the places, types, and circumstances of service as shown by the service record, the official history of the organization in which the claimant served, the medical records, and all pertinent medical and lay evidence. Section 1154(a) imposes the same obligation through the service connection regulations. Consideration means more than presence in the folder. An examination report that the decision never mentions, a buddy statement that the decision never weighs, and a treatment record that contradicts the stated rationale without discussion each signal a failure of consideration, and appellate review has treated unexplained silence about favorable evidence as a defect independent of the ultimate outcome.

The standard of proof sits in section 5107(b) of title 38. The text provides that when there is an approximate balance of positive and negative evidence regarding any issue material to the determination of a matter, the Secretary shall give the benefit of the doubt to the claimant. Regulation states the same rule at 38 C.F.R. section 3.102 in terms of reasonable doubt. The formulation matters because of what the standard is not. A claimant does not have to establish an element by a preponderance of the evidence, and the government does not prevail merely because the favorable evidence falls short of a preponderance. Denial requires affirmative evidence against the claim of sufficient weight to tip the balance past equipoise.

Gilbert v. Derwinski, 1 Vet. App. 49 (1990), supplied the operative reading of the statute in the first years of judicial review of benefits decisions. The Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims in Gilbert held that a claimant need show only an approximate balance of the evidence, described in the opinion as relative equipoise, to prevail on a material issue, and that a denial rests on a permissible basis only where a fair preponderance of the evidence is against the claim. The distance between equipoise and a fair preponderance is the practical space in which the doctrine operates. Evidence in approximate balance resolves for the claimant as a matter of law, not as an act of discretion or generosity by the adjudicator. Evidence that preponderates against the claim defeats the claim even though some favorable evidence remains in the record, because the doctrine applies only at or near balance. The adjudicative error that Gilbert corrected most often was the silent application of a preponderance-in-favor threshold, under which a decision recited the favorable evidence, found the evidence insufficient to prove the element, and denied without asking whether the negative evidence affirmatively preponderated.

Application of the benefit of the doubt proceeds element by element rather than across the claim as a whole. A record can stand in equipoise on whether symptoms began in service while preponderating against a connection between a later diagnosed condition and those symptoms, and the doctrine resolves only the first question for the claimant. The doctrine also interacts with the quality of medical opinion evidence. An examination opinion that rests on an inaccurate factual premise, that offers a conclusion without a supporting rationale, or that fails to account for lay testimony about symptoms carries reduced probative weight, and reduced weight on the negative side of the ledger is often what leaves the balance in approximate equipoise. Conversely, a negative opinion with a full rationale, grounded in an accurate review of the service records and the post-service treatment history, can preponderate over a favorable opinion that asserts a conclusion in a sentence. The benefit of the doubt is not a counting exercise. Adjudicators weigh credibility and probative value first, and apply section 5107(b) only after that weighing leaves the scales level.

### Can lay evidence support a VA claim?

Yes. Lay testimony about symptoms, onset, and observable events counts as evidence in a VA claim when the subject lies within ordinary observation. An adjudicator may discount such testimony for credibility reasons, including inconsistency or bias, but may not reject the testimony solely because medical records do not corroborate the account.

The statutory foundation is section 1154(a) of title 38, which requires due consideration of all pertinent medical and lay evidence in every service connection case, with attention to the places, types, and circumstances of service. Lay evidence in this setting means evidence from a person without specialized medical education, training, or experience, offered on matters that a person can perceive and describe without such training. A claimant can describe pain, limitation of motion observed in daily activity, ringing in the ears, the onset of symptoms after a documented in-service event, and continuity of symptoms across the years after separation. A spouse, a fellow service member, or a family member can describe what was observed about the claimant during and after service. Such testimony speaks directly to elements that medical records often leave blank, because many conditions produce symptoms long before a diagnosis appears in any chart.

Competence is the threshold question, and competence is narrower than adjudicators once treated the concept. In Buchanan v. Nicholson, 451 F.3d 1331 (Fed. Cir. 2006), the Federal Circuit rejected an interpretation under which lay evidence could not establish a factual basis for service connection without support from contemporaneous medical records. The court held that competent lay evidence can be sufficient in itself, drawing on the regulation at 38 C.F.R. section 3.307(b), which provides that the factual basis for establishing a chronic disease may be established by medical evidence, competent lay evidence, or both. Buchanan also drew the line that governs later adjudication with precision. The absence of contemporaneous medical records is a fact that an adjudicator may consider and weigh against lay evidence, but the absence of such records does not, without more, render lay evidence not credible. A decision that moves directly from silence in the service treatment records to rejection of lay testimony repeats the legal error identified in Buchanan, because the decision substitutes a corroboration requirement that the statute and regulations do not impose for the credibility assessment that the law requires.

Davidson v. Shinseki, 581 F.3d 1313 (Fed. Cir. 2009), extended the same reasoning to the connection, or nexus, element and to claims for service connection for the cause of a veteran death. The Board of Veterans Appeals in Davidson had treated lay testimony as incompetent on a medical question and had held, in effect, that only medical evidence could establish the required connection. The Federal Circuit held that the categorical exclusion was error. Lay evidence can be competent and sufficient to establish a diagnosis or a connection in appropriate circumstances, and whether lay testimony can carry such an issue depends on the nature of the question presented. A question about observable symptoms and the sequence of events surrounding a death or an injury sits closer to ordinary observation than a question about the internal pathology of a complex disease process, and the competence inquiry has to engage the specific proposition the testimony is offered to prove. Davidson did not hold that lay testimony always establishes a medical connection, and adjudicators retain authority to find particular lay opinion beyond the competence of a witness without medical training where the question demands specialized knowledge.

Credibility is the second question, and credibility is where lay testimony is properly tested. Once testimony clears the competence threshold for the proposition offered, the adjudicator assesses whether the account is believable in light of the whole record. Inconsistency within the testimony, inconsistency between the testimony and contemporaneous documents, and conflict with more persuasive evidence all bear on credibility. Buchanan expressly preserved that function, holding the Board of Veterans Appeals fully justified in determining whether lay evidence is credible in itself, because of possible bias, conflicting statements, or similar considerations, and the same logic governs a rating specialist at the initial decision stage. The distinction between competence and credibility does real work in a written decision. A finding that testimony is not competent states that the law does not permit the testimony to prove the proposition at all. A finding that testimony is competent but not credible states that the testimony entered the balance and was disbelieved for stated reasons, a conclusion that must identify the inconsistencies or conflicts on which the disbelief rests. Decisions that collapse the second finding into the language of the first misstate the governing framework and obscure the reasoning that review exists to test.

Weight follows competence and credibility. Credible lay testimony about symptoms and continuity can supply the factual predicate on which a medical examiner renders a connection opinion, can corroborate an examiner account of onset, and in the circumstances recognized by Davidson can itself support a finding of connection. Discounting lay evidence without a lawful basis therefore carries a double cost. The decision loses the probative value the testimony held on the merits and exposes a possible development failure.

The presumption of soundness operates at the other end of the evidentiary structure, as a rule about the condition of the claimant at entry into service. Section 1111 of title 38 provides that every veteran shall be taken to have been in sound condition when examined, accepted, and enrolled for service, except as to defects, infirmities, or disorders noted at the time of the examination, acceptance, and enrollment. Regulation states the presumption at 38 C.F.R. section 3.304(b). The presumption answers a question that records from decades earlier often cannot answer directly. Entrance examinations vary in thoroughness, many conditions produce no findings on a screening examination, and the absence of a noted defect could reflect either sound health or an unexamined condition. Congress resolved the ambiguity by rule. Silence at entry counts as soundness, unless the government carries the rebuttal burden the statute prescribes.

Rebuttal demands clear and unmistakable evidence, the most demanding standard in the claims system. Clear and unmistakable evidence leaves no reasonable debate on the question, a standard well above a preponderance and well above the approximate balance that governs ordinary issues under section 5107(b). Wagner v. Principi, 370 F.3d 1089 (Fed. Cir. 2004), settled the structure of rebuttal. To rebut the presumption for a condition not noted at entry, the government must show by clear and unmistakable evidence both that the disease or injury existed before service and that the disease or injury was not aggravated by service. Failure on either prong leaves the presumption intact. Evidence that a condition plainly predated service does not complete the rebuttal if the record leaves room for debate about aggravation, and an opinion asserting preexistence without addressing aggravation cannot carry the second prong. The burden rests on the government throughout, so the examination and opinion in such a case have to address both prongs with a rationale strong enough to support an undebatable conclusion, or the presumption decides the entry condition question for the claimant.

The noted at entry exception requires separate handling. Where the entrance examination records a defect, infirmity, or disorder, the presumption of soundness does not attach to that condition, and the claim proceeds instead as a claim about aggravation under section 1153 of title 38, which presumes aggravation where a preexisting condition increased in disability during service, subject to rebuttal by clear and unmistakable evidence that the increase resulted from the natural progress of the disease. Adjudicators therefore read the entrance examination with care, because the difference between a condition noted at entry and a history recorded without a corresponding finding can determine which presumption governs the case. Misclassification at this step propagates through the entire decision, since the wrong presumption assigns the wrong burden and applies the wrong standard to the same body of evidence.

With the evidentiary rules in place, the decision itself is an act of structured judgment performed at a regional office. Rating specialists, the adjudicators within the Veterans Benefits Administration charged with deciding compensation claims, weigh the assembled record against the substantive standards for service connection and evaluation. The substantive standards that the rating specialist applies at this stage, including the elements of service connection and the criteria for assigning a percentage evaluation, receive full treatment in [the companion account of VA disability compensation law](/2019/05/15/va-disability-compensation-law/). The administrative contribution lies in the application. The rating specialist determines which issues the claim presents, matches the evidence to each element of each issue, resolves credibility and competence questions, applies any governing presumption, invokes the benefit of the doubt where the balance on a material issue stands in approximate equipoise, and, where service connection is established, assigns an evaluation under the schedule for rating disabilities authorized by section 1155 of title 38 and set out in Part 4 of title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations. The schedule assigns percentage evaluations to diagnostic codes according to the severity of symptoms and functional impairment described in examination reports and treatment records.

The product of that work is the rating decision, and after the Veterans Appeals Improvement and Modernization Act of 2017, Public Law 115-55, took effect in February 2019, the notice accompanying the decision carries a statutory content list that reflects the function of the document in the review system. Section 5104 of title 38, as amended by the 2017 act, requires notice of a decision to include an explanation of the procedure for obtaining review, identification of the issues adjudicated, a summary of the evidence considered, a summary of the applicable laws and regulations, identification of findings favorable to the claimant, identification of the elements not satisfied leading to a denial, an explanation of how to obtain or access the evidence used in making the decision, and, where applicable, identification of the criteria that must be satisfied to grant service connection or the next higher level of compensation. A favorable findings component carries added force because section 5104A of title 38, added by the same act, makes a finding favorable to the claimant binding on subsequent adjudicators within the Department of Veterans Affairs unless clear and convincing evidence is shown to the contrary.

Reasons are the connective tissue between the decision and everything downstream. The review structure created by the 2017 act offers a claimant three routes after a rating decision, higher level review on the closed record, a supplemental claim supported by new and relevant evidence, or an appeal to the Board of Veterans Appeals, and a rational choice among the routes depends on knowing why the decision came out as the decision did. A decision that identifies the missing element shows whether the defect is evidentiary, which a supplemental claim can cure with new and relevant evidence addressed to the gap, or legal and factual on a complete record, which higher level review or a Board appeal is designed to test. Conclusory decisions defeat each of those functions. A denial that recites a standard and announces a result without tying the evidence to the elements leaves the claimant unable to target additional evidence, leaves a higher level reviewer unable to locate the alleged error on a closed record, and leaves the Board and the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims without the reasons or bases against which the lawfulness of the outcome is measured.

The evidentiary rules and the reason giving obligation therefore form a single system rather than separate topics. The benefit of the doubt allocates the risk of an evenly balanced record. Lay evidence doctrine determines whose observations enter that record with full standing. The presumption of soundness removes the entry condition question from ordinary weighing unless the government satisfies a demanding rebuttal standard on both prongs. The rating decision records how each rule was applied to each element, identifies what was found in the claimant's favor and what element failed, and supplies the map on which the choice among the three review lanes is made. A claim decided without visible engagement with those rules cannot demonstrate correctness at all, and demonstrating correctness in a written, reviewable form is the central administrative obligation that the evidence stage exists to fulfill.

## Review After the Rating Decision

A rating decision from an agency of original jurisdiction settled very little under the appeals structure that governed veterans benefits claims for decades. Denial of service connection, a lower evaluation than the evidence was said to support, or a later effective date each opened a second stage of adjudication. That second stage divides into two eras. The first routed every disagreement through a single sequential queue toward the Board of Veterans' Appeals and allowed the record to grow at almost every step. The second, created by the Veterans Appeals Improvement and Modernization Act of 2017, replaced the single queue with three review lanes, each defined by whether the record stays closed or opens to additional evidence and whether the Department of Veterans Affairs owes a duty to assist in building that record.

The legacy sequence began with the notice of disagreement. A claimant had one year from the mailing of the notice of the rating decision to file a written notice of disagreement with the agency that had issued the decision, under the pre-reform version of 38 U.S.C. section 7105. From 2013 VA prescribed a standard form, VA Form 21-0958. The filing identified the issues in dispute and preserved the appeal. Without a timely filing, the decision became final, and the claim could be reopened afterward only under the separate reopened claim standard or attacked for clear and unmistakable error. With a timely filing, the agency reconsidered the matter, often through a Decision Review Officer, a more experienced adjudicator who could hold a conference or a hearing and could grant the benefit on the existing record.

If the agency did not grant the full benefit sought, the next document was the statement of the case. Required by section 7105, the statement of the case summarized the evidence, summarized the applicable statutes and regulations, explained how the law applied to the facts, and stated the decision on each issue with reasons. The substantive appeal, filed on VA Form 9, Appeal to Board of Veterans' Appeals, perfected the appeal. The Form 9 was due within sixty days from the mailing of the statement of the case, or within the remainder of the one year period from the mailing of the rating decision, whichever ended later. On the Form 9 the claimant identified the issues pressed, stated arguments, and elected or declined a hearing before a member of the Board. Only then did the Board docket the case for decision by a Veterans Law Judge. A missed deadline at either stage ended the appeal regardless of the merits.

The feature of the legacy system that drew the sharpest objection was the treatment of evidence and error. The record in a legacy appeal stayed open throughout the proceeding. Additional pertinent evidence submitted after the statement of the case obliged the agency to issue a supplemental statement of the case addressing the added material. The Board possessed limited fact development authority of its own. When the Board found the duty to assist unsatisfied, a medical examination inadequate, or a theory of entitlement unaddressed, the characteristic disposition was a remand ordering the agency to cure the defect and readjudicate. The appeal then returned automatically to the Board, where a second examination often disclosed a further defect and generated a second remand on the same claim. The same issue could circulate among the regional office, the Board, and the United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims, which itself remanded many Board decisions for inadequate reasons and bases. The structure earned a durable nickname: the hamster wheel. VA itself measured legacy appeals, in statements supporting implementation of the 2017 act, at an average of three to seven years.

Congress answered that record in the Veterans Appeals Improvement and Modernization Act of 2017, Public Law 115-55, signed on August 23, 2017. The act amended provisions across title 38, including sections 5104, 5104B, 5108, 5110, 7104, and 7105, and directed VA to issue implementing regulations and new forms. The modernized system applied to decisions issued on or after February 19, 2019, the date the Secretary certified implementation readiness. A decision issued before that date belonged to the legacy world unless the claimant elected into the modernized system through an authorized bridge. A decision issued on or after that date carried notice of the three review options, and disagreement had to be expressed through one of those options. Amended section 5104 required decision notices to identify the issues decided, summarize the evidence considered, state findings favorable to the claimant, and describe the available review options.

### What are the three review lanes for a VA claim?

The three lanes are Higher-Level Review on the closed record by a senior reviewer, a Supplemental Claim supported by new and relevant evidence with assistance in gathering proof, and an appeal to the Board of Veterans' Appeals under a direct review, evidence submission, or hearing docket.

The three lanes share a common architecture. Each lane begins from a decision and produces a new decision that opens the next set of choices. No lane reproduces the legacy pattern of automatic ascent to the Board, and no lane permits simultaneous pursuit of two forms of review on the same issue. The choice among lanes turns principally on the state of the evidence. A disagreement resting on material already in the file presents a record error suited to closed record review. A disagreement resting on proof absent from the file presents an evidentiary gap suited to a lane admitting new material. A disagreement calling for judgment by a Veterans Law Judge or testimony at a hearing presents a case for the Board track, at the price of a longer queue. VA stated processing goals for the February 2019 launch of an average of 125 days for Higher-Level Review and Supplemental Claims and 365 days for Board direct review, as administrative targets rather than statutory deadlines.

Higher-Level Review, codified at 38 U.S.C. section 5104B and requested on VA Form 20-0996, Decision Review Request: Higher-Level Review, assigns the contested decision to a more experienced adjudicator. The reviewer conducts a de novo review of the same record, giving no deference to the prior findings apart from findings favorable to the claimant, which bind later adjudicators unless clear and convincing evidence is shown to the contrary. The evidentiary record closes on the date of the notice of the decision under review. The reviewer may not consider evidence submitted after that decision, and the claimant may not supply new evidence with the request or afterward. Within the closed record, the reviewer may return a different outcome on a difference of opinion about the weight of the evidence or the application of the rating schedule, and may revise the decision for clear and unmistakable error.

One feature of section 5104B has no real legacy analogue. When the reviewer identifies a duty to assist error that occurred before the contested decision issued, such as records never obtained, notice never provided, or an examination inadequate on its face, the reviewer does not decide the claim on the defective record. Unless the benefit can be granted in full on the existing record, the claim returns to the agency of original jurisdiction for correction of the error and issuance of a new decision. The return is not a Board remand and places the claim on no Board docket. Correction happens inside the agency, and the decision emerging from correction carries fresh review rights. The mechanism answered the hamster wheel directly, since a pre-decision development failure discovered at the Board under the legacy structure consumed a place in two queues and often years of elapsed time.

Higher-Level Review also offers the optional informal conference. A claimant or representative may request, on the review request form, a one time telephone conference with the reviewer to identify specific errors in the decision. The conference is not a hearing: no testimony is taken under oath, no new evidence may be introduced, and the exchange is summarized for the record rather than transcribed. The conference lets a representative direct the reviewer to service treatment records the rating narrative never mentioned or to a regulation applied in an earlier version. VA cautioned in launch materials that electing a conference could lengthen processing, since review could not finish before the conference occurred. A Higher-Level Review request must be filed within one year of the notice of the decision under review, under section 5104B, and expiration of that year closes the lane for that decision. A Higher-Level Review decision cannot itself be taken to Higher-Level Review; the next steps are a Supplemental Claim or a Board appeal.

The Supplemental Claim, codified at 38 U.S.C. section 5108 and filed on VA Form 20-0995, Decision Review Request: Supplemental Claim, occupies the opposite evidentiary pole. Section 5108, as rewritten by Public Law 115-55, directs the Secretary to readjudicate a claim when new and relevant evidence is presented or secured in connection with a Supplemental Claim. Before the 2017 act, a finally decided claim could be reopened under the former section 5108 only on new and material evidence, construed to require evidence raising a reasonable possibility of substantiating the claim. That materiality threshold generated extensive gateway litigation before the merits could be reached. The 2017 act lowered the gateway by substituting new and relevant. New means evidence not previously part of the record before agency adjudicators, and relevant means evidence that tends to prove or disprove a matter in issue. A duplicate record fails the first element, and a treatment note for an unrelated condition fails the second. A private medical opinion supplying the missing link between an in service event and a present diagnosis satisfies both.

Once a Supplemental Claim presents or identifies new and relevant evidence, the duty to assist attaches again in full. That attachment is the sharpest contrast with Higher-Level Review, where no new development occurs. Under the duty to assist, codified principally at 38 U.S.C. section 5103A, VA must make reasonable efforts to obtain records the claimant identifies, including service records, VA treatment records, and authorized private records. VA must also provide a medical examination or obtain a medical opinion when the statutory criteria are met. A Supplemental Claim may therefore identify evidence rather than attach evidence, naming the provider and the treatment period, and the identification engages the agency obligation to pursue the records before deciding. The decision addresses the whole claim on the enlarged record, with earlier favorable findings binding unless clear and convincing evidence is shown to the contrary. A Supplemental Claim may also follow a Supplemental Claim decision, provided each filing presents or identifies evidence new and relevant relative to the record at the prior decision.

The third lane places the contested issues before the Board of Veterans' Appeals. Board authority rests on 38 U.S.C. section 7104, which commits final decisions on appeals to the Board, and on section 7105, as rewritten by Public Law 115-55, which governs initiation of appellate review. The filing that opens Board review is again called a notice of disagreement, but the resemblance to the legacy filing ends at the name. The modernized notice of disagreement is filed directly with the Board on VA Form 10182, Decision Review Request: Board Appeal (Notice of Disagreement), within one year from the mailing of notice of the agency decision. Section 7105 assigns questions of timeliness and adequacy of the filing to the Board. No statement of the case precedes the filing and no Form 9 follows the filing. The Form 10182 identifies each issue presented, states specific allegations of error of fact or law, and records the selection among the three Board dockets.

The first docket, direct review, is the Board analogue of Higher-Level Review. A Veterans Law Judge decides the appeal on the record that stood before the agency at the contested decision, supplemented only by argument in or with the Form 10182. No hearing is held and no additional evidence may be submitted. The docket held the shortest expected queue in agency projections for the February 2019 launch, reflected in the stated goal of an average of 365 days. The second docket, evidence submission, opens a window for additional proof without a hearing: evidence may be submitted with the Form 10182 or within ninety days after VA receipt of the form. Evidence arriving after the window closes may not be considered on that appeal. The third docket, hearing, reserves a proceeding before a Veterans Law Judge in Washington, by videoconference, or at a regional office during a Board travel visit. Evidence may be submitted at the hearing or within ninety days after the hearing, and the transcript becomes part of the record. The hearing docket carries the longest queue, and in exchange offers the only setting in the modernized system for addressing the deciding official directly and under oath.

Board disposition follows the statute rather than the legacy habit. The Board reviews each issue de novo and grants or denies the issue. Remand is available only on the grounds the modernized framework allows, principally correction of a duty to assist error that occurred before the agency decision or another error the statute and regulations make a proper basis for remand. The duty to assist does not operate at the Board as a development engine. A Board decision is the final decision of the Secretary, subject to appeal to the United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims under 38 U.S.C. section 7266, ordinarily within 120 days after notice of the Board decision is mailed. A Board decision also opens the option of a Supplemental Claim with new and relevant evidence. Favorable findings made anywhere in the chain bind the Board unless clear and convincing evidence is shown to the contrary. Higher-Level Review cannot answer a Board decision, because section 5104B reaches agency decisions rather than Board decisions.

Continuous pursuit holds the three lanes together as one system, and the doctrine operates entirely through dates. Under 38 U.S.C. section 5110, as amended by Public Law 115-55, an award keeps the effective date of the original claim when the claim is continuously pursued through timely filings after each decision. A Higher-Level Review request filed within one year after the agency issues a decision qualifies. A Supplemental Claim filed within one year after the agency issues a decision qualifies, as does a notice of disagreement filed within one year after the agency issues a decision. After a Board decision, a Supplemental Claim filed within one year after the Board decision qualifies. After a decision of the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims, a Supplemental Claim filed within one year after the court decision qualifies. Each timely filing restarts the one year interval from the next decision, so a chain moving from rating decision to Higher-Level Review to Supplemental Claim to Board appeal, with every link filed inside its year, carries the original effective date through every stage.

The clocks are related but not identical in consequence. The one year period for a notice of disagreement under section 7105 is a filing deadline for Board review of that decision, subject to extension for good cause under the standards the Board applies. The one year period for Higher-Level Review under section 5104B likewise operates as a condition of the lane itself. The Supplemental Claim behaves differently, because section 5108 imposes no absolute filing deadline. A Supplemental Claim with new and relevant evidence filed years after a decision must be readjudicated on the merits. For the Supplemental Claim, the one year period governs only the effective date. Filing inside the year preserves continuous pursuit and the original effective date, while filing outside the year breaks the chain and ties any award to the Supplemental Claim filing, subject to the general rules of section 5110. Missing the Higher-Level Review or Board window therefore forfeits that form of review for that decision while leaving the Supplemental Claim route open, and missing every window forfeits the original effective date while leaving the entitlement available for proof on a later filing.

The bridge between the two eras was the Rapid Appeals Modernization Program, known by the acronym RAMP. VA launched RAMP in November 2017, under pilot authority in Public Law 115-55, more than a year before the statutory system took effect. RAMP invited claimants with pending legacy appeals to withdraw from the legacy sequence and elect Higher-Level Review or a Supplemental Claim, with the Board lane withheld from the pilot. Elections began with invitations mailed in November 2017 to claimants holding some of the oldest pending appeals, and from April 2018 participation opened to claimants with a pending disability compensation appeal without an invitation. A RAMP election was voluntary and irrevocable as to the legacy appeal withdrawn, and effective date protections carried over. VA closed RAMP to new elections after February 15, 2019, completing elections already made before the February 19, 2019 launch.

Legacy appeals that never elected RAMP or another bridge did not vanish on February 19, 2019. The act preserved the legacy system for appeals already in that system, and after launch the two systems operated in parallel: the legacy inventory proceeded through statements of the case, supplemental statements of the case, Form 9 appeals, and Board decisions under the pre-reform rules, while every decision issued on or after February 19, 2019 entered the modernized lanes. A continuing bridge operated after launch as well. A legacy claimant who received a statement of the case or a supplemental statement of the case dated on or after February 19, 2019 could move the contested issues into the modernized system by filing the appropriate decision review form within sixty days of that statement. Claimants who declined every bridge remained in the legacy queue until the appeal concluded. The legacy sequence asked one question after a rating decision, whether the claimant wished to keep ascending a single staircase toward the Board. The modernized system asks three: whether the error lies in the record as it stands, whether the record needs evidence the file lacks, and whether judgment by the Board is the forum the dispute requires.

The claim pathway table gathers the sequence that the preceding sections describe in prose into a single reference, so that each stage can be located beside the deadline that governs that stage, the actor responsible for moving the claim at that point, the evidence permitted at that point, and the effect of that stage on the effective date from which payment is calculated. The table states the stages in the order in which a claim ordinarily encounters them, from the first recorded signal of an intention to claim through development, decision, and the review lanes, to review by the court outside the department.

## Judicial Review Outside the Agency

Agency adjudication ends with the Board of Veterans' Appeals, but the statute does not make the Board the final word on questions of law. Congress created a court for the single purpose of reviewing Board decisions. The move into court changed the proceeding in three respects. The record closed. The parties became adversaries in form, with the Secretary represented by counsel and the decision defended on the grounds the Board had given. The standard narrowed to error correction on a fixed record rather than a fresh determination of entitlement, and disappointment at the court stage often came from expecting the court to weigh new evidence, a function the statute withheld from the court.

The court is the United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims, established by the Veterans' Judicial Review Act of 1988 and codified in chapter 72 of title 38, at sections 7251 to 7298. Section 7252 gives the court exclusive jurisdiction to review decisions of the Board of Veterans' Appeals and provides that the Secretary may not seek review of a Board decision. Only a person adversely affected by a final Board decision could invoke the court's jurisdiction, and a Board order that merely returned a claim for further development was not a final decision the court reviewed, because the claim remained open inside the agency.

The deadline for reaching the court was short and strict in structure. Under section 7266, a person adversely affected by a final Board decision was required to file a notice of appeal with the court within 120 days after the date on which notice of the decision was issued under section 7104(e). The notice went to the court rather than to the Department of Veterans Affairs, and filing with a regional office or with the Board did not satisfy the requirement. Section 7266 also addressed filing by mail, treating the postmark as significant to timeliness. The period ran from issuance of notice of the Board decision, which made the Board stage the necessary gateway to court, and a claimant who allowed the period to lapse lost judicial review of that decision, although the claim could in many circumstances be pursued again inside the agency, with consequences for the effective date.

Review itself was confined by section 7252(b), which provides that review in the court shall be on the record of proceedings before the Secretary and the Board. That record consisted of the evidence and argument assembled during agency adjudication, together with the Board decision under review. No new evidence could be submitted to cure a gap, no witnesses testified, and the court conducted no trial. Section 7261(c) reinforced the limit by providing that findings of fact made by the Secretary or the Board were in no event subject to trial de novo by the court. The consequence was often decisive. An inadequate examination, a record that had never been obtained, or a symptom history that had never been recorded could not be repaired at the court stage by submission of a better document. The court could identify the failure, but the repair had to occur back inside the agency, on remand.

Within that closed record, section 7261 defined the scope of review. The court was directed, to the extent necessary to decision and when presented, to decide relevant questions of law, to interpret constitutional, statutory, and regulatory provisions, and to determine the meaning of an action of the Secretary. The court could compel action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed. The court could hold unlawful and set aside decisions, findings, conclusions, rules, and regulations found to be arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law, contrary to constitutional right, in excess of statutory authority, or without observance of procedure required by law. A finding of material fact adverse to the claimant could be held unlawful and set aside or reversed only if the finding was clearly erroneous, a deferential standard that asked whether the court was left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake had been committed rather than whether the court would have weighed the evidence differently in the first instance. Section 7261 also directed the court to take due account of the rule of prejudicial error and of the benefit of the doubt provision at section 5107(b).

Under section 7252(a), the court had power to affirm, modify, or reverse a decision of the Board or to remand the matter, as appropriate. Affirmance left the Board decision in place. Reversal was comparatively uncommon, because reversal required the record to permit a single lawful conclusion. Vacatur and remand, in which the court set the Board decision aside and returned the claim for further proceedings consistent with the court's opinion, accounted for a substantial share of dispositions and shaped the rhythm of the system.

Remand returned so many cases to the cycle for structural reasons. The Board decides claims through written reasons and bases, and a recurring ground of vacatur was a Board statement that did not explain why evidence had been credited or rejected, why an examination had been treated as adequate, or why a legal provision had been read in the manner the decision adopted. A court confined to the record could not supply the missing explanation and could not substitute findings of its own, so the lawful remedy was to send the claim back for a decision that could be reviewed. Other remands followed from the duty to assist. Where the agency had failed to obtain records identified in the file, had provided an examination that did not address the question presented, or had decided without an examination that the record required, the error lay in development, and development was agency work. Remand therefore reopened the evidentiary process, often with a new examination and a new Board decision, after which a dissatisfied claimant could again seek court review. The sequence could repeat, and proposals to reduce remand rates generally required fuller Board explanations and more complete development before the first Board decision, rather than enlarging the court's fact finding power, which Congress left unchanged.

Section 7252(b) also barred review of the schedule of ratings for disabilities adopted under section 1155 and of any action of the Secretary in adopting or revising that schedule. Application of the schedule to an individual claim, including the choice of diagnostic code and the evaluation assigned, remained reviewable under section 7261, but the content of the schedule itself stood outside the court's jurisdiction.

Further appeal lay to a single court and on narrow terms. Under section 7292, the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit held exclusive jurisdiction to review a decision of the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims. The Federal Circuit could review the validity of a statute or regulation, and the interpretation of a constitutional or statutory provision or a regulation, that the veterans court had relied upon. Except where an appeal presented a constitutional issue, the Federal Circuit could not review a challenge to a factual determination, and could not review a challenge to a law or regulation as applied to the facts of a particular case. The allocation was deliberate. The veterans court served as the sole fact reviewing court, the Federal Circuit served as a court of legal uniformity concerned with the governing rule rather than the individual outcome, and the Supreme Court retained certiorari jurisdiction over Federal Circuit decisions. A party seeking a second judicial forum therefore had to frame the objection as an error of law, a question of validity, or a constitutional claim capable of surviving the limits of section 7292.

Fees at the court stage operated under a statute from outside title 38. The Equal Access to Justice Act, codified for court awards at 28 U.S.C. section 2412(d), authorized fees and other expenses to a prevailing party where the position of the United States was not substantially justified and where eligibility conditions, including net worth limits, were met. The Federal Courts Administration Act of 1992 amended section 2412(d)(2)(F) to include the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims within the courts covered by the fee provision. Entitlement turned on whether the claimant had prevailed, most often through a vacatur and remand, and on whether the government position had been substantially justified, a standard that examined reasonableness in law and fact rather than whether the government had lost. Awards were paid by the government rather than taken from benefits, which distinguished the court stage mechanism from contingent fee arrangements inside the agency, and counsel could undertake an appeal for a claimant of limited means with a prospect of payment from the government if the appeal succeeded.

## Representation and the Accreditation Gate

Representation inside the agency was governed by a gate that had no close parallel in ordinary civil litigation. The claims system had been designed for informal, nonadversarial adjudication, with the agency under a duty to assist, and Congress paired that design with strict control over who could act for a claimant and on what financial terms. Accreditation was the condition of lawful practice, and the distinction between accredited and unaccredited assistance organized everything that followed, from free service organization help to attorney fee agreements to the enforcement problem created by advisers outside the gate altogether. The governing contrast runs between the codified claims text in title 38, which states who may be recognized and on what conditions, and the implementing regulations in part 14 of title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations, which supply the application, character, training, and discipline machinery, a contrast examined in the guide to [reading a codified statute against its implementing regulations](/2022/05/01/how-to-read-a-federal-statute/).

### Who can represent a claimant before the VA?

Only a person accredited by the Department of Veterans Affairs may prepare, present, and prosecute a claim for compensation in the ordinary course. Accreditation covers representatives of recognized veterans service organizations, claims agents who have passed an examination, and attorneys in good standing with a state bar, each subject to oversight by the Office of General Counsel.

Section 5901 stated the underlying prohibition, providing that no individual could act as an agent or attorney in the preparation, presentation, or prosecution of a claim before the Secretary unless recognition had first been obtained. The regulation at section 14.629 of title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations restated the rule, providing that no individual could assist claimants as an agent or attorney unless first accredited. Accreditation was personal rather than organizational in effect, because the Department accredited the individual named on the appointment of record and could withdraw that accreditation for cause. The appointment determined who could receive notices, submit evidence and argument, and appear at a hearing on the claimant's behalf.

The first and largest channel ran through recognized veterans service organizations under section 5902. That section authorized the Secretary to recognize representatives of organizations that met the statutory conditions and to recognize an organization itself for preparing, presenting, and prosecuting claims through its accredited representatives. National organizations, together with state, tribal, regional, and local organizations recognized through the same process, maintained service offices at regional offices and at other Department facilities, and accreditation of an individual representative under section 5902 depended on recommendation and certification by the recognized organization that the individual had demonstrated the ability to represent claimants. Representatives of recognized organizations were prohibited from soliciting, contracting for, charging, or receiving any fee or compensation in connection with a claim, which made the channel a source of representation at no charge at every stage. The section 5902 channel functioned as the default representation system for claimants who obtained help at all.

The second channel, claims agents, consisted of accredited practitioners who were neither attorneys nor organization representatives. Agents and attorneys were recognized under section 5904, subject to the qualifications the Secretary prescribed by regulation. The regulation at section 14.629(b) required an applicant to establish good character and reputation, qualification to render valuable assistance, and competence to advise and assist claimants. An agent applicant, lacking a bar license as a credential, was required to pass a written examination administered by the Department and to complete qualifying continuing education after accreditation. Section 5903 supplied a narrower and older route, recognition with respect to a particular claim, under which the Secretary could recognize an individual for a single claim where the individual certified that no fee or compensation of any kind would be charged or received, a provision suited to a family member or other individual assisting in one case without charge rather than to a practice conducted for compensation.

The third channel, accredited attorneys, rested on section 5904 as well, with the bar license performing part of the screening function that the examination performed for agents. An attorney applicant was required to be a member in good standing of the bar of a state, to satisfy the same character and reputation standard, and to complete qualifying continuing legal education after accreditation, but no Department examination was imposed, because admission to practice and state bar discipline supplied an external qualification regime that agents lacked. Accreditation did not follow automatically from bar membership, and an unaccredited attorney could not prepare, present, or prosecute a claim before the agency, a point the Office of General Counsel stated in published guidance. Once accredited, attorneys and agents, unlike organization representatives, could charge fees only within section 5904, which after the amendments of 2006 permitted a fee only after the agency of original jurisdiction had issued an initial decision, required a written fee agreement filed with the Department, and subjected reasonableness to review, with a fee of 20 percent of past due benefits treated as presumptively reasonable and a fee exceeding 33 and one third percent treated as presumptively unreasonable under the implementing regulation. No fee could be charged for preparation or filing of the initial claim itself.

Administration and discipline sat with the Office of General Counsel. The Secretary's authority over recognition had been delegated to the General Counsel in the regulations published in 1954 that established part 14 in its modern form, and the Office of General Counsel reviewed applications, made the character and fitness determination that section 14.629 required, administered the examination for agents, maintained the register of accredited individuals, and handled complaints and fee disputes. Section 5904(b) authorized suspension or exclusion from further practice of an accredited attorney or agent who violated the governing standards, on grounds that included failure to meet continuing education requirements, conduct involving dishonesty, fraud, deceit, or misrepresentation, and suspension or disbarment by a court or bar. The Office of General Counsel could also cancel the accreditation of an organization representative who presented a fraudulent claim or accepted compensation, and a recognized organization could itself request rescission.

State and county veterans service officers formed the public layer of the same system. States maintained departments of veterans affairs, and counties in many states maintained county veterans service offices assisting veterans and dependents with federal, state, and local benefits. Those officers acted before the Department through federal accreditation, most often as representatives of a recognized organization under section 5902, because recognized organizations included state and local government entities alongside private organizations. Authority to prepare, present, and prosecute claims therefore derived from federal accreditation rather than from state appointment alone, and free assistance at a county office rested on the same federal gate as assistance at a national organization office.

The documented weakness of the gate concerned actors who never entered it. In its report of August 2013, GAO-13-643, titled VA Benefits: Improvements Needed to Ensure Claimants Receive Appropriate Representation, the Government Accountability Office reported that the Department had received complaints regarding unaccredited individuals inappropriately charging claimants to apply for benefits, that by law only accredited individuals could assist claimants, that the Department was not aware of the extent to which unaccredited individuals operated, and that the Department was limited in the actions available to prevent unaccredited individuals from assisting claimants. The same report, based on accreditation decisions and complaints from 2012, found that the Office of General Counsel had four staff dedicated to overseeing thousands of applications each year in addition to monitoring approximately 20,000 accredited representatives, and that character and knowledge procedures relied on limited self reported information and on training requirements described as minimal and inconsistently monitored. An earlier Government Accountability Office report, GAO-12-540 of May 2012, titled Veterans' Pension Benefits: Improvements Needed to Ensure Only Qualified Veterans and Survivors Receive Benefits, documented an adjacent market in the pension program, reporting that over 200 organizations marketed financial and estate planning services to help pension claimants meet financial eligibility requirements, that investigative staff contacted 19 of those organizations, and that the majority of the 19 charged fees ranging from a few hundred dollars for benefits counseling to 10,000 dollars for establishment of a trust. Read together, the two reports described a structural gap: the accreditation system controlled practitioners inside the gate through character review, training, fee review, and withdrawal of accreditation, while the tools available against a person who charged fees without ever seeking accreditation consisted principally of investigation, correspondence directing the activity to stop, and referral to other authorities, rather than the direct sanctions that section 5904 supplied for accredited practitioners.

## Fees, Queues, and the Price of Help With a Claim

The fee rules and the backlog belong in the same section because both describe the distance between a legal entitlement and a delivered benefit. A claimant who cannot pay for help at the stage where help is first needed depends on free representation and on the agency duty to assist. A claimant whose file waits in a queue depends on staffing, examinations, and records systems that no statute can conjure into existence by declaration alone. The backlog is an arithmetic relationship between claims entering a system and trained people and completed examinations available to decide those claims.

### The Bar on Paid Help at the Start of a Claim

No fee may be charged for preparing or presenting an initial claim, and that prohibition is the oldest structural feature of the representation system. The design premise, carried from the pension system of the nineteenth century into title 38, held that a claim for benefits earned by service should be simple enough to present without paid assistance, and that free assistance would be supplied by the government and by veterans organizations. Section 5904 of title 38 therefore separates representation from payment. Accreditation controls who may represent a claimant before the Department of Veterans Affairs, and the fee provisions control when an accredited agent or attorney may charge for that representation. The answer for the initial claim stage has always been negative. Work performed before the statutory trigger cannot lawfully be charged to the claimant under a fee agreement governed by section 5904, and an agreement that attempts to charge for that stage fails at the threshold rather than at the reasonableness stage.

For most of the history of the system, the barrier went far beyond the initial claim. Section 12 of the Act of July 4, 1864, chapter 247, 13 Statutes at Large 387, fixed the fees of agents and attorneys for making out the papers necessary to establish a claim for a pension, bounty, or other allowance at ten dollars, payable only after the pension had been obtained, and the same enactment barred any contract for a percentage of the amount claimed. The ten dollar figure survived, in substance, through successive codifications and into the veterans benefits code. A ceiling set for a Civil War pension practice bore no relationship to the cost of legal work in the twentieth century, and competent paid representation was effectively unavailable, because no practitioner could sustain a practice at ten dollars a claim. Claimants relied on service organization representatives and on county and state veterans officers. The Supreme Court sustained the ceiling in Walters v. National Association of Radiation Survivors, 473 U.S. 305, decided in 1985. The first breach came with the Veterans Judicial Review Act of 1988, Public Law 100-687, which created judicial review and permitted a fee in a defined late stage, after the Board of Veterans Appeals had issued a final decision and subject to retention within one year of that decision. Paid help was admitted where a case had become appellate in character, while the initial claim remained a stage in which representation charged nothing.

### The Point at Which a Fee Became Chargeable

The modern fee system dates from the Veterans Benefits, Health Care, and Information Technology Act of 2006, Public Law 109-461, enacted on December 22, 2006. Section 101 of that act rewrote the fee provisions of 38 U.S.C. section 5904 and moved the trigger far earlier in the life of a claim. Under the 2006 amendments, a fee became permissible for services provided after a notice of disagreement had been filed with respect to the case, replacing the prior requirement of a final Board decision. The change took effect 180 days after enactment, on June 20, 2007, and applied to services in cases in which a notice of disagreement was filed on or after that date, a transition rule that the implementing regulation at 38 C.F.R. section 14.636 preserves for legacy cases. Paid representation was thereby admitted at the first expression of disagreement, the point at which evidence, rating criteria, and effective dates become contested.

The Veterans Appeals Improvement and Modernization Act of 2017, Public Law 115-55, then moved the trigger one step earlier and tied the fee to the initial decision itself. The 2017 amendment to section 5904(c) substituted the provision of notice of the agency of original jurisdiction initial decision under section 5104 for the filing of a notice of disagreement, so that in the modernized review system a fee may be charged for representation provided after the agency has issued notice of an initial decision. The sequence matters, and [the longer statutory sequence for veterans legislation](/2019/07/15/us-veterans-legislation-history/) places the two enactments in order: the 2006 act admitted fees at disagreement, and the 2017 act admitted fees at decision. Section 14.636(c)(1)(i) states the resulting rule, under which agents and attorneys may charge for representation provided after notice of an initial decision issued on or after the effective date of that system in February 2019, provided the power of attorney and fee agreement requirements have been met.

### Direct Payment Out of Past-Due Benefits

A fee that has become chargeable still requires a written agreement, and the agreement rather than an invoice is the operative document in the system. Under 38 C.F.R. section 14.636(g), every fee agreement must be in writing and signed by the claimant or appellant and by the agent or attorney, must name the veteran and any separate claimant, must state the applicable Department file number, and must state the specific terms under which the amount to be paid will be determined. A copy of a direct payment agreement must be filed with the agency of original jurisdiction within 30 days of execution, and a copy of an agreement that does not provide for direct payment must be filed with the Office of the General Counsel within the same period. An agreement that fails to state clearly that the Department is to pay the practitioner directly, or that specifies a fee greater than 20 percent of past due benefits, is treated as an agreement under which the practitioner collects from the claimant without agency assistance.

Direct payment is the mechanism that gives the fee system its practical force. Under 38 U.S.C. section 5904(d) and 38 C.F.R. section 14.636(h), a claimant and an accredited agent or attorney may agree that the fee will be paid by the Department directly to the practitioner out of past due benefits awarded. Past due benefits, as defined in section 14.636(h)(3), means the lump sum that represents the recurring cash payments that accrued between the effective date of an award and the date of the grant of the benefit, together with a nonrecurring payment resulting from a benefit granted on appeal or on readjudication after a denial. The Department honors a direct payment agreement only when the total fee payable, excluding expenses, does not exceed 20 percent of past due benefits awarded, when the fee is contingent on whether the claim is resolved in a manner favorable to the claimant, when the practitioner is accredited on the date of the fee allocation notice, and when the award results in a cash payment from which the fee may be deducted. Where those conditions hold, the agency withholds the fee and pays the practitioner, and the claimant receives the balance.

The 20 percent figure does double work in the scheme, because the same percentage that limits direct payment also supplies the presumption of reasonableness. Section 14.636(f)(1) provides that fees which do not exceed 20 percent of any past due benefits awarded shall be presumed to be reasonable if the agent or attorney provided representation that continued through the date of the decision awarding benefits, and that fees which exceed 33 and one third percent of past due benefits shall be presumed to be unreasonable. Both presumptions can be rebutted through the factors in section 14.636(e), and rebuttal requires clear and convincing evidence that a fee at or under 20 percent is not reasonable in the particular case, or that a fee above the higher threshold is reasonable in a specific circumstance. An agreement for a fee above 20 percent is not unlawful for that reason alone. Such a fee simply cannot be collected through direct payment, and the amount above the ceiling becomes a matter between practitioner and claimant, tested against the reasonableness factors if review is sought.

Review of fee agreements is built into the same regulation, and the review standard is substantive rather than deferential. The factors in section 14.636(e) direct attention to the extent and type of services performed, the complexity of the case, the level of skill and competence required, the amount of time spent, the results achieved including the amount of benefits recovered, the level of review reached, the rates charged by others for similar services, and the extent to which payment was contingent on the result. Under section 14.636(i), the agency that awards past due benefits issues a fee allocation notice deciding direct payment eligibility and stating a default allocation, which favors the practitioner whose representation continued through the awarding decision and otherwise divides the fee in an equal split among the eligible practitioners and the claimant. A party that disagrees with the default allocation may seek fee review by the Office of the General Counsel, whose decision rests on the reasonableness factors, the presumptions, and the direct payment limitation, and a party that disagrees with an eligibility determination may appeal to the Board.

### The Measures Behind the Reported Numbers

The backlog resists that kind of precision, because the word describes at least three different inventories that are often reported as one. The claims backlog, in the definition used in the Veterans Benefits Administration Monday Morning Workload Report, counts rating claims pending more than 125 days, and rating claims in that usage include disability compensation, dependency and indemnity compensation, and pension claims that require a rating decision. The claims inventory is the larger figure from which the backlog is drawn, since every pending rating claim sits in the inventory from filing until decision and only the portion aged beyond 125 days counts as backlogged. The appeals inventory is a separate stock again, counting cases in which a decision has already been made and disagreement has been expressed, and after February 2019 that stock divided into legacy appeals and appeals under the modernized review lanes.

The legacy appeals figures reported by the Department give the clearest period picture for the years surrounding the article date. The Department Periodic Progress Report on Appeals, issued under section 3 of Public Law 115-55, recorded 425,445 legacy appeals pending at the start of fiscal year 2019. The same reporting series recorded a total legacy inventory of 415,165 in the first quarter of fiscal year 2019, composed of 268,881 appeals at the Veterans Benefits Administration, 18,269 at the Veterans Health Administration, 121 at the National Cemetery Administration, and 127,894 at the Board of Veterans Appeals. The total therefore fell from 425,445 at the start of fiscal year 2019 to 415,165 in the first quarter of that year in that reporting series, a decline of roughly ten thousand appeals in a single quarter against a stock that remained above four hundred thousand.

Output figures from the surrounding years show a system completing work at high volume while the stock declined slowly, the signature of a queue rather than a standstill. The aged rating backlog, defined by the department as claims pending more than 125 days, had fallen from its March 2013 peak to near 70,000 by September 2015 on department figures, while the appeals stock recorded in the periodic progress reporting remained several times that size, because appeals move through more stages than initial claims and each stage holds its own inventory.

### The Sources of Processing Capacity

Capacity in the claims system has three components, and each component has a lead time that legislation cannot shorten. The first component is people. A rating veterans service representative must master the rating schedule, the evidentiary rules, and the procedural requirements of title 38 before independent production is possible, and the department prepared new rating specialists through its Challenge training program, so a hiring decision made in one budget year produced independent adjudicators well after it. The second component is examinations. A large share of rating claims cannot be decided until a medical examination has been performed and an opinion rendered, and examination capacity depends in part on contracted examiners outside the agency. A Government Accountability Office report of October 2018, GAO-19-13, recorded that the administration had not fully implemented recommendations to verify that contracted examiners completed required training and to collect information for assessing the effectiveness of that training.

The third component is the record itself, and the transformation of the record from paper to an electronic file changed what redistribution could accomplish. The Department created the Veterans Benefits Management System as an electronic claims file system for paperless processing, against the background of a claims backlog that peaked at more than 600,000 claims in March 2013. In a paper system, a claim could be worked only where the folder physically sat, so an overloaded regional office accumulated delay while another office with spare capacity could not reach the file. The paperless system placed the claim file in an electronic folder available regardless of location, and the National Work Queue, implemented in 2016, supplied the distribution logic, distributing claims electronically to regional offices across the country according to programmed rules and office capacity rather than funneling claims through the office with geographic jurisdiction. The combination removed a structural reason for claims to wait, by separating the location of the claimant from the location of the available labor.

### How Eligibility Expansions Outran Capacity

The recurring cause of queue growth in the record is not a fall in output but a rise in receipts generated by law. The Agent Orange Act of 1991, Public Law 102-4, established a presumption mechanism under which diseases shown by scientific review to be associated with herbicide exposure could be presumed service connected for veterans with qualifying Vietnam service, removing the need for each claimant to prove individual causation. The final regulation published on August 31, 2010 added ischemic heart disease and Parkinson disease as presumptive conditions and expanded the leukemia category to cover all chronic B cell leukemias, including hairy cell leukemia. A Department release of March 2014 stated that the backlog had been driven in part by the need to readjudicate 150,000 previously decided Agent Orange cases under the Nehmer consent framework and that the Department had also received and processed more than 100,000 new claims for the three conditions from veterans and survivors made eligible by the decision. A single regulatory expansion therefore generated a quarter of a million items of work, without any corresponding instant expansion in the number of trained raters available to decide that work.

The same pattern repeated at larger scale after the period covered by the figures above. The Sergeant First Class Heath Robinson Honoring Our Promise to Address Comprehensive Toxics Act, Public Law 117-168, enacted on August 10, 2022, expanded presumptions and eligibility for veterans exposed to toxic substances across several eras of service, and [the expansion and the claims surge that followed it](/2019/04/15/pact-act-2022-guide/) reproduced the 2010 sequence in a larger population. The Department PACT Act Year in Review release of August 10, 2023 reported 843,448 PACT Act specific claims submitted in the first year after enactment, alongside 1.95 million total claims submitted in fiscal year 2023 to that date, and reported 458,659 PACT Act claims among 1.65 million total claims processed in that fiscal year to that date. Congress enlarges the class of compensable claims by law, the enlargement takes legal effect on a date certain, the claims arrive promptly because effective date rules reward prompt filing, and the workforce that must decide the claims can only be enlarged by recruitment, training, and examination contracting conducted over succeeding fiscal years.

## The Gap Between the Standard and the Delivered Benefit

The administration of VA claims law can be summarized in the claim that has organized the preceding account: the filing date is the money. A statute may authorize compensation in generous terms, a regulation may supply an intent to file that protects a date at almost no cost, and a review system may preserve that date through continuous pursuit, yet none of those protections operates unless a filing is recorded, a record is assembled, and a decision is issued. The distance between entitlement in law and payment in fact is administrative distance, measured in recorded dates, records requests, examination appointments, and the capacity of trained adjudicators to convert a file into a reasoned decision.

The legal standard itself is unusually protective. Congress placed a duty to assist on the Secretary, required notice that divides responsibility for evidence between the department and the claimant, directed that evidence in approximate balance be resolved for the claimant under section 5107, protected lay testimony about matters within ordinary observation, and placed the heaviest burden in the system on the government through the presumption of sound condition and its clear and unmistakable evidence standard for rebuttal. A claimant in that design is not a plaintiff carrying a case against an adversary. The department must help build the record on which the department then decides, and the examination that often determines the outcome is an agency obligation rather than a purchase the claimant must finance.

Delivery, however, depends on capacity that the standard cannot create by declaration. Records sit with custodians inside and outside federal control, and federal records must be pursued until obtained or until further effort is shown to be futile. Examinations require clinicians, whether department staff or contractors, and an inadequate examination requires clarification, an addendum, or a new examination rather than a decision on a defective report. Rating specialists require training before independent production is possible, and the department prepared them through its Challenge training program, so staffing expanded on the timetable of a training pipeline rather than the timetable of an appropriation. Each element has a lead time, and eligibility expansions arrive on a date set by enactment while hiring and training expand on a slower timetable set by appropriations.

The figures from the opening of fiscal year 2019 show that arithmetic plainly. The legacy appeals inventory in the department periodic progress reporting under the 2017 act stood at 425,445 at the start of fiscal year 2019 and at 415,165 in the first quarter, after decades in which the sequential system had added appeals faster than the department disposed of them. High output and a slowly falling stock coexisted, because receipts, development, and review continued to add work as completed decisions removed work.

For a reader working through material of this kind, the practical discipline is documentary: dates, sections, findings, and review choices have to be kept in order if the system is to be understood. A reader can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) while tracing how an effective date, an examination, and a lane selection interact, and can also [practice and revise US government and civics material on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/us-government-civics-study.html) alongside the statutory account given here.

The material presented here is general information about how the system is administered rather than advice about any individual claim. A claim in a particular case turns on its own filing dates, its own service and treatment records, its own examination findings, and the findings in its own decision notice, and no general account can supply those facts or choose a review lane for a specific claimant. What a general account can do is name the points at which administration decides outcomes: the recorded filing date that fixes payment, the development that determines what the decision maker sees, the evidentiary rules that govern how the record is weighed, and the one year and 120 day clocks that govern review after a decision.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does a VA claims decision actually get made?

A rating decision is made at a regional office of the Veterans Benefits Administration after development has assembled service treatment records, department and private treatment records, examination reports, and lay statements into a single claims file. A rating specialist weighs that record issue by issue, applies the benefit of the doubt under section 5107 where positive and negative evidence stands in approximate balance, and decides service connection, the percentage evaluation under the rating schedule in section 1155, and the effective date under section 5110. Section 5104 then requires notice identifying the issues, the evidence, favorable findings, and the review options.

### Q: What is an intent to file in VA claims?

An intent to file is a minimal recorded signal, governed by section 3.155 of title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations, that a claimant intends to submit a claim for a stated type of benefit. The signal may arrive as a saved electronic application, as a signed VA Form 21-0966, or as an oral statement recorded in writing by department personnel. No evidence accompanies the act and no entitlement is decided at that stage. If a complete claim for that benefit reaches the department within one year of the intent, the complete claim is treated as filed on the earlier intent date, and payment is calculated from that preserved date.

### Q: What examination does the VA claims process require?

Section 5103A requires a medical examination or opinion when the record contains competent evidence of a current disability or persistent or recurrent symptoms, indicates that the disability or symptoms may be associated with service, and lacks sufficient medical evidence for a decision. The Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims in McLendon v. Nicholson, 20 Vet. App. 79 (2006), described that indication threshold as a low one. The examination, performed by a department clinician or a contract examiner at agency expense, records diagnosis, severity, and any requested opinion on association with service. The examiner decides nothing; a rating specialist weighs the report against the whole record.

### Q: How long does the VA claims backlog run?

Department reporting has defined the rating backlog as claims pending more than 125 days, a definition that separates the aged portion of the inventory from every pending claim. On that definition the backlog peaked at more than 611,000 claims in March 2013, according to figures the Department of Veterans Affairs reported in April 2014, and department reporting placed the figure near 70,000 by September 2015. Appeals formed a separate and slower queue. The department periodic progress reporting under the 2017 act recorded 425,445 legacy appeals pending at the start of fiscal year 2019 and 415,165 in the first quarter of that year, showing a large remaining stock in the queue the modernization act was designed to replace.

### Q: Can lay evidence support VA claims?

Lay evidence can support a claim when the matter lies within ordinary observation, such as pain, limitation noticed in daily activity, ringing in the ears, the onset of symptoms after an event in service, and continuity of symptoms after separation. Section 1154 requires due consideration of all pertinent medical and lay evidence. In Buchanan v. Nicholson, 451 F.3d 1331 (Fed. Cir. 2006), the Federal Circuit held that competent lay evidence can be sufficient in itself and that the absence of contemporaneous medical records does not, without more, render lay evidence not credible. An adjudicator may still weigh credibility, including inconsistency, bias, and conflict with more persuasive evidence in the record.

### Q: Who can represent you in VA claims?

Representation before the department is limited by accreditation under chapter 59 of title 38. Section 5901 bars an individual from acting as a claims agent or attorney without recognition, and section 5902 covers representatives of recognized veterans service organizations, who serve without charge. Accredited claims agents and accredited attorneys act under section 5904, subject to character, competence, training, and discipline requirements administered by the Office of General Counsel. Section 5903 supplies a narrower route for recognition in a single claim where no fee or compensation is charged. State and county veterans service officers act through federal accreditation, most often as representatives of a recognized organization under section 5902.

### Q: When can someone charge a fee for VA claims help?

No fee may be charged for preparing or presenting an initial claim. The permissible point moved across enactments, from after a final Board decision under the Veterans Judicial Review Act of 1988, Public Law 100-687, to services in cases in which a notice of disagreement had been filed under the amendments in Public Law 109-461, effective for notices filed on or after June 20, 2007. The 2017 modernization act then tied the trigger to notice of the initial decision of the agency of original jurisdiction under section 5104, for decisions issued in the system that took effect in February 2019. A written fee agreement and accreditation under section 5904 are also required.

### Q: What are the three review lanes for VA claims?

For a decision issued on or after February 19, 2019, the three lanes are Higher-Level Review, a Supplemental Claim, and an appeal to the Board of Veterans Appeals. Higher-Level Review under section 5104B assigns the closed record to a more experienced adjudicator, with an optional informal conference and a return for correction where a duty to assist error is identified. A Supplemental Claim under section 5108 readjudicates the claim on new and relevant evidence, with the duty to assist attaching again. A Board appeal proceeds under chapter 71 on a direct review, evidence submission, or hearing docket, each selected on VA Form 10182 within one year of the agency decision.

### Q: What is the duty to assist in developing a VA claim?

The duty to assist, codified at section 5103A and implemented at section 3.159 of title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations, requires the Secretary to make reasonable efforts to obtain evidence necessary to substantiate a claim. Assistance includes obtaining service records and relevant records held by federal departments and agencies, making reasonable efforts toward relevant private, state, local, and employer records that a claimant identifies and authorizes the department to pursue, and providing an examination or opinion where the statutory criteria are met. The Veterans Claims Assistance Act of 2000, Public Law 106-475, enacted on November 9, 2000, removed the former well grounded claim threshold, so assistance no longer waited on a preliminary showing built from evidence the department had not helped to gather.

### Q: What happens at a Board of Veterans' Appeals hearing?

A Board hearing takes place before a Veterans Law Judge on the hearing docket selected on VA Form 10182, in Washington, by videoconference, or at a regional office during a Board travel visit. The proceeding is the only setting in the modernized system for addressing the deciding official directly and under oath. Evidence may be submitted at the hearing or within ninety days after the hearing, and the transcript becomes part of the record. The Board then reviews each issue de novo and grants or denies the issue, with remand available principally to correct a duty to assist error that occurred before the agency decision. A Board decision is the final decision of the Secretary, subject to court review under section 7266.

### Q: How does continuous pursuit protect the effective date of a claim?

Continuous pursuit protects an effective date through timely filings after each decision, under section 5110 as amended by Public Law 115-55. A request for Higher-Level Review filed within one year of an agency decision qualifies, as do a Supplemental Claim and a notice of disagreement filed within one year of that decision. After a Board decision, a Supplemental Claim filed within one year qualifies, and after a decision of the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims, a Supplemental Claim filed within one year after the court decision also qualifies. Each timely filing restarts the one year interval from the next decision, so a chain with every link filed inside its year carries the original filing date through every stage.

### Q: What is the difference between Higher-Level Review and a Supplemental Claim?

Higher-Level Review and a Supplemental Claim differ in record, decision maker setting, and assistance. Higher-Level Review under section 5104B, requested on VA Form 20-0996 within one year of the decision, closes the record at that decision, admits no new evidence, and assigns review to a more experienced adjudicator who may identify a duty to assist error and return the claim for correction. A Supplemental Claim under section 5108, filed on VA Form 20-0995, requires evidence that is new and relevant, meaning evidence not previously in the record that tends to prove or disprove a matter in issue. Once that gateway is met, readjudication follows on the enlarged record and the duty to assist under section 5103A attaches again in full.

### Q: How long does a claimant have to take a Board decision to the veterans court?

Section 7266 requires a person adversely affected by a final Board decision to file a notice of appeal with the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims within 120 days after the date on which notice of the Board decision was issued under section 7104. The notice goes to the court rather than to the department, and filing with a regional office or with the Board does not satisfy the requirement. The period runs from issuance of notice of the Board decision, which makes completion of the Board stage the gateway to court. A claimant who allows the 120 day period to lapse loses judicial review of that Board decision, although pursuit inside the agency may remain available with consequences for the effective date.

### Q: What does accreditation require of a VA claims representative?

Accreditation requires recognition by the department before an individual prepares, presents, or prosecutes a claim, under section 5901 and the regulation at section 14.629 of title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations. An applicant must establish good character and reputation, qualification to render valuable assistance, and competence to advise and assist claimants. A claims agent applicant must also pass a written examination administered by the department and complete qualifying continuing education, while an attorney applicant must be a member in good standing of a state bar and complete qualifying continuing legal education. The Office of General Counsel reviews applications, maintains the register, and may suspend or exclude an accredited practitioner for dishonesty, fraud, deceit, misrepresentation, or failure to meet continuing education requirements under section 5904.

### Q: Why does the veterans court send so many cases back to the Board?

The court sends many cases back because review under section 7252 is confined to the record of proceedings before the Secretary and the Board, and section 7261 bars trial de novo on findings of fact. A recurring ground for vacatur and remand is a Board statement that does not explain why evidence was credited or rejected, why an examination was treated as adequate, or why a legal provision was read as the decision adopted. Other remands follow duty to assist failures, such as records never obtained or an examination that did not address the question presented. A court confined to a closed record cannot supply the missing explanation or repair the development, so the lawful remedy returns the claim to the agency for a reviewable decision.

### Q: What did the legacy appeals system require before the modernization act?

The legacy system required a notice of disagreement within one year from mailing of notice of the rating decision, under the pre-reform version of section 7105, with VA Form 21-0958 prescribed from 2013. If the agency did not grant the full benefit sought, the agency issued a statement of the case summarizing the evidence, the applicable statutes and regulations, and the reasons for the decision on each issue. A substantive appeal on VA Form 9 then perfected the appeal, due within sixty days from mailing of the statement of the case or within the remainder of the one year period from the rating decision, whichever ended later. Additional evidence generated a supplemental statement of the case, and Board remands for further development were a standard feature of the sequence.

### Q: Which records must the VA obtain before deciding a claim?

Under section 5103A, the department must obtain service treatment records and, where a claimant furnishes information sufficient to locate them, other relevant records pertaining to service held by a governmental entity, a category that reaches the National Personnel Records Center in St. Louis. Treatment records from department health care facilities belong in the file, and relevant records held by the Social Security Administration or another federal department or agency must also be pursued where a claimant adequately identifies and authorizes the request. Efforts toward federal records must continue until the records are obtained, unless the department concludes that the records do not exist or that further efforts would be futile. Where records cannot be obtained, notice must identify the records, explain the efforts made, and describe further action on the claim.

### Q: How are fees for claims help paid out of past-due benefits?

Direct payment operates under section 5904 and section 14.636 of title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations, where a claimant and an accredited agent or attorney agree in writing that the department will pay the fee directly out of past-due benefits awarded. Past-due benefits mean the lump sum representing recurring payments that accrued between the effective date of an award and the date of the grant, together with a qualifying nonrecurring payment. The department honors direct payment only where the total fee, excluding expenses, does not exceed 20 percent of past-due benefits, the fee is contingent on a favorable resolution, the practitioner is accredited on the date of the fee allocation notice, and the award produces a cash payment from which the fee may be deducted.

### Q: What was the Rapid Appeals Modernization Program?

The Rapid Appeals Modernization Program, known as RAMP, was a bridge launched by the department in November 2017 under pilot authority in Public Law 115-55, more than a year before the modernized system took effect on February 19, 2019. RAMP invited claimants with pending legacy appeals to withdraw from the legacy sequence and elect Higher-Level Review or a Supplemental Claim, with the Board lane withheld from the pilot. Elections began with invitations mailed in November 2017 to claimants holding some of the oldest pending appeals, and from April 2018 participation opened to claimants with a pending disability compensation appeal without an invitation. An election was voluntary and irrevocable as to the legacy appeal withdrawn, and effective date protections carried over. The department closed RAMP to new elections after February 15, 2019.

### Q: How does the National Work Queue route claims between regional offices?

The National Work Queue, implemented in 2016, distributes claims electronically to regional offices across the country according to programmed rules and office capacity, rather than funneling claims through the office with geographic jurisdiction over a claimant. The mechanism depends on the Veterans Benefits Management System, the electronic claims file system for paperless processing created against the background of a claims backlog that peaked at more than 600,000 claims in March 2013. In a paper system, a claim could be worked only where the folder physically sat, so an overloaded office accumulated delay while spare capacity elsewhere could not reach the file. An electronic folder available regardless of location removes that structural reason for a claim to wait in a single office queue.
