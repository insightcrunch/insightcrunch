Dependency theory is usually introduced as a set of propositions about trade, capital, and underdevelopment, and the propositions are argued over as though they had been composed in a vacuum by solitary authors. This article takes a different object. It asks where dependency theory was physically made: in which buildings, under which employers, in argument with which colleagues sitting in the same corridor. The answer turns out to be unusually concrete. A United Nations regional commission in Santiago produced the structuralist economics from which the theory grew, and a university research centre in the same city housed its principal theorists together during the years when the radical version was formed. In September 1973 a military coup destroyed that centre and scattered its researchers across two continents. Everything about how the theory was later read, especially in English, follows from that sequence of construction and destruction.

![A seminar table with Spanish-language journals, a wall map of Santiago and seminar notes from a university research centre, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-85.webp)

The formation story matters because the reception story cannot be understood without it. A body of work produced collaboratively, in Spanish and Portuguese, by scholars who corrected one another in seminars and journals, reached the English-speaking world piecemeal: one author translated early and read as the school's founder, another translated a decade later, the most technically original of them barely translated at all. English-language readers therefore met dependency theory as a shelf of competing individual positions and organised their textbooks accordingly. The claim this article names and defends is that the reception is an artefact of the dispersal. What looks like the theory's internal incoherence is, in considerable part, the shape imposed on it by the destruction of the institution that had held its arguments together and by the staggered, partial translation that followed the scattering.

## Dependency theory was made somewhere: the address as evidence

Economic theories are often treated as placeless, as though a proposition about the world economy were equally available to anyone who cared to think it. The history recorded here suggests the opposite. The dependency analysis required a particular vantage point: a city that was simultaneously a national capital, the seat of a United Nations commission charged with studying the region as a whole, and a refuge for exiled scholars arriving from harsher regimes elsewhere in South America. Santiago in the nineteen sixties was all three. The commission supplied the statistics, the regional frame, and a salaried community of economists. The universities supplied seminars, journals, and students. The exiles supplied experience of defeat and a determination to explain it. Remove any one of the three and the theory that emerged would have been different in identifiable ways.

This is why the article proceeds institutionally. It begins with the commission founded in 1948 and the Argentine economist who directed its thinking through the nineteen fifties, because the school began as an official economics before it became a Marxist one. It follows the policy programme that the commission sponsored, import substitution industrialisation, into its exhaustion, because the radical theory was born from that programme's failure rather than from its success. It then enters the research centre where the radicalisation happened, names the Brazilians, the Chilean hosts, and the German-American visitor who worked there, and reconstructs how their positions were formed in mutual argument. Only after that does it reach the coup, the destruction, and the diaspora, and the long afterlife in which successors and revivals kept returning to questions the Santiago group had posed.

Readers who want the theory's content, its propositions about unequal exchange and the structure of dependence, will find it treated in the companion study of [dependency theory explained](/2016/07/01/dependency-theory-explained/), and the wider tradition within which the school sits is surveyed in the [Marxism in Latin America guide](/2018/10/01/marxism-latin-america-guide/). The present article links to that material rather than restating it. Its own territory is the formation, the institutions, and the regional afterlife, and it stays on that territory deliberately, because the formation is the part of the story that standard accounts omit and the part that explains the rest.

### Why does the birthplace of a theory change how it should be read?

Because a theory carries the marks of its workplace: the questions its makers could ask, the data within reach, the colleagues in the corridor, and the audience addressed. Read in place, disagreements become intelligible as arguments among collaborators; read without place, the same disagreements harden into rival doctrines invented by isolated authors.

Consider what changes when the Santiago setting is restored. The famous disagreements inside the school, between those who held that dependence blocked development altogether and those who held that a dependent form of development was possible, were not disputes between strangers publishing in distant journals. They were disputes between men who had shared institutions, in some cases the same institution at the same time, and who were answering a common question with a common statistical inheritance. The debate had the character of a research programme arguing with itself about its own findings. Stripped of the setting, the same texts read as manifestos by rival prophets, and the student is invited to choose a prophet rather than to follow an argument. Restoring the setting does not settle the argument, but it restores the argument's actual shape, which is the precondition for judging it.

The setting also explains the school's peculiar combination of empirical ambition and theoretical sweep. The commission's economists were civil servants of an international organisation, obliged to produce usable statistics and defensible reports; the research centre's scholars were academics in a politicised university, obliged to publish and to teach. Both settings rewarded work that could be stated, checked, and argued about in a room. When the rooms were destroyed, the checks went with them, and the theory's later career in exile and translation was carried on under conditions that rewarded the portable formulation over the verifiable one. That shift, too, belongs to the formation story rather than to any defect of character in the theorists.

## A school that began in an office, not in a party

The first misreading to correct is the assumption that dependency theory began as a Marxist doctrine which later acquired academic respectability. The sequence ran the other way. The school began inside the United Nations system, as the working economics of a regional commission staffed by professional economists and statisticians, most of whom were not Marxists and several of whom were explicitly concerned to find a path for capitalist development in the region. Its founding documents were official reports. Its founding claim, that the terms of trade moved persistently against exporters of primary products, was presented as a statistical finding about prices, not as a theorem about exploitation. The Marxist radicalisation came later, in the nineteen sixties, and it came as an internal criticism: younger scholars, several of them formed inside the commission's own orbit, argued that the structuralist diagnosis had not gone far enough and that the obstacle was not a set of bottlenecks but the structure of dependence itself.

That origin explains features of the mature theory that otherwise look odd. The dependency writers, even at their most radical, retained a statistician's respect for series, balances, and national accounts, because their school had been raised on the commission's data. They also retained a policymaker's instinct that analysis should issue in a programme, because the commission existed to advise governments. And they inherited the commission's regional frame: the unit of analysis was Latin America as a whole, inserted into a world economy, rather than the single nation state developing in isolation. A Marxism formed in a party school would have begun from class and the state. This one began from prices, trade balances, and the structure of production, and arrived at class and the state by a road that passed through the statistics office. The order of the journey left permanent marks on the destination.

It also explains the school's vulnerability. An economics housed in international and university institutions is exposed to politics in a way that an economics housed in a party or a private institute is not. The commission could survive the coup, because it belonged to the United Nations and enjoyed diplomatic protection, though its Chilean environment was transformed around it. The research centre could not survive, because it belonged to a university that the military regime intervened and purged. The school's two halves therefore had different fates in 1973, and the difference between those fates is one of the keys to what survived and what was lost.

## The commission in Santiago: a United Nations experiment

The Economic Commission for Latin America, known by its Spanish initials as CEPAL and in English as ECLA, was established in 1948 by the United Nations Economic and Social Council and seated in Santiago de Chile. Its creation was itself an argument. The postwar settlement had produced regional commissions for Europe and for Asia, and Latin American governments, determined that their region would be studied as a region rather than administered as a collection of bilateral relationships with Washington, pressed for a body of their own. The United States, which preferred that hemispheric economics remain within inter-American institutions it influenced more directly, was unenthusiastic. The commission was founded anyway, with a mandate to study the Latin American economies, gather comparable statistics, and advise governments on development. In Spanish the body is la Comisión Económica para América Latina, and the adjective cepalino attached itself to a whole style of economic thinking.

What made the commission intellectually consequential was not its mandate but its method. From its earliest surveys it treated the region's economies as structures with histories: patterns of landholding, export specialisation, and industrial weakness that could not be read off from prices alone. Its annual economic surveys assembled, for the first time, comparable regional statistics, and comparability was a political act, because it allowed a Chilean or a Brazilian economist to argue from the region's aggregate experience rather than from national anecdote. The commission also trained people. Its courses and its Santiago offices formed a generation of Latin American economists who carried the structuralist vocabulary back into ministries, central banks, and universities across the continent. Long before there was a dependency theory, there was a cepalino network, and the later school grew inside that network's institutions and habits.

### Why did the United Nations place its Latin American commission in Santiago?

Santiago offered a capital at a distance from Washington's direct oversight, a Chilean state willing to host and house the new body, and a university culture able to staff it. The choice mattered: the commission grew up inside Chilean academic and political life, close to the region's debates and far from the hemisphere's dominant treasury.

Chile's own condition sharpened the commission's questions. The country was an exporter of copper and nitrates whose earnings swung with prices set elsewhere, a small industrialiser with an organised labour movement and a literate, politicised electorate. An economist sitting in Santiago did not need theory to tell him that the world economy had a geography of advantage; the national budget announced it every year. The commission's founding generation generalised that experience into a framework: some economies occupied the centre of the world system, generating and appropriating technical progress, while others occupied the periphery, supplying primary products and importing manufactures, with the gains from trade and from technical progress distributed unevenly between them. The vocabulary of centre and periphery, which the whole later school inherited, was the commission's first gift to it.

The commission's survival through the decades that followed, including through the dictatorship that destroyed so much around it, is part of the institutional map this article draws. It remained in Santiago, publishing, training, and revising its own doctrines, long after the research centre that radicalised its ideas had ceased to exist. The contrast between the durable commission and the destroyed centre runs through everything that follows: what belonged to an international organisation endured, and what belonged to a national university in a polarised republic did not.

## The Argentine who directed its thinking

The commission's intellectual direction was set by Raúl Prebisch, an Argentine economist who became its executive secretary in 1950 and held the post into the early nineteen sixties. Prebisch arrived with a formation unlike that of the academic theorists who later criticised him. He had run Argentina's central bank, had negotiated his country's trade under depression conditions, and had watched a primary-exporting economy discover, in the nineteen thirties, that the world market it depended on could simply close. His economics was built from that experience. Where the received theory of his youth treated international trade as a harmonising mechanism that rewarded each country for specialising in what it produced most cheaply, Prebisch treated the trading system as a structure with a history, in which the industrial centres retained the fruits of their technical progress while the periphery's gains leaked away.

His programmatic statement, prepared as the commission's early work and published at the turn of the nineteen fifties, argued that Latin America's underdevelopment was not an original condition, a mere lateness in following a path the centres had pioneered, but a product of the way the region had been inserted into the world economy as a supplier of food and raw materials. Development, on this account, would not arrive by deepening the specialisation that trade theory recommended. It required deliberate industrialisation, planned and protected, to change the structure of production itself. The document was an official text of a United Nations body, and its argument was correspondingly framed in the language of policy rather than of class struggle. Yet its central move, treating underdevelopment as produced by a relationship rather than as a natural starting point, is the move from which the whole dependency tradition descends.

### What did Prebisch bring to the commission from his Argentine years?

A central banker's knowledge of how export earnings govern a peripheral economy, a negotiator's scepticism about the benevolence of trade, and a depression decade's lesson that world markets can close without warning. His structuralism was built from administered experience of dependence before the word dependence had been given its later theoretical sense.

Prebisch's tenure also fixed the commission's institutional style: the long survey, the statistical appendix, the recommendation addressed to governments. He went on to lead the UN's new trade and development conference in the nineteen sixties, carrying the centre and periphery argument into global diplomacy and the campaign for a new international economic order. By then, younger scholars in Santiago were already treating his framework as a starting point to be surpassed. That the surpassing was done largely by men who had been trained, employed, or published within the commission's orbit is the central irony of the school's formation, and it is unintelligible unless the commission is understood as a school in its own right, capable of educating its own critics.

It is equally important to register what Prebisch was not. He was not a Marxist, and his programme aimed at a developed capitalism in Latin America, not at its supersession. The dependency theorists who radicalised his framework said so plainly, and the distance between the commission's reformism and the centre's Marxism is real. But the radicalisation was a development of the structuralist questions, not a repudiation of them: why the periphery's industrialisation stalled, why its trade position deteriorated, why its structures reproduced themselves. The Marxists changed the answers. They inherited the questions, the data, and in several cases the desks.

## Centre and periphery: the map before the theory

The distinction between centre and periphery did analytical work that the older vocabulary of rich and poor countries could not. Rich and poor describe outcomes; centre and periphery describe a relationship, in which the two poles are produced together by the same process. The centres, in the commission's account, were the industrial economies where technical progress originated and where its gains were retained, because their organised labour forces and their oligopolistic firms could hold the prices of their products while productivity rose. The periphery supplied primary commodities under competitive conditions, so that productivity gains there tended to be transferred outward through falling prices, while the periphery's own workers, abundant and weakly organised, could not retain them. The world economy, on this reading, was not a ladder on which all countries climbed at different speeds. It was a structure that systematically allocated the benefits of progress to one pole.

### What work did the centre and periphery distinction do that class analysis did not?

It located the mechanism of disadvantage in the trading relationship itself, in how prices formed differently at the two poles, before any question of domestic class structure arose. A country could therefore be exploited as a country, through its insertion, whatever the character of its own ruling groups.

This was a genuinely heterodox move within the economics of the time, and its heterodoxy should not be understated because the vocabulary later became commonplace. The dominant postwar development economics, centred in the United States and Britain, treated underdevelopment as a stage, characterised by shortages of capital and skills that time, aid, and trade would remedy. The Santiago framework treated it as a position, reproduced by the ordinary functioning of the world market. The two frameworks implied opposite policies: deepen integration and wait, or restructure production under protection and plan. Latin American governments of the nineteen fifties, facing the lived experience of wartime shortages and postwar price swings, mostly chose the second, and the commission's economics became the nearest thing the region had to an official doctrine of development.

The framework also had limits that its own authors could see. It analysed the relationship between nations more confidently than the relationships inside them. It had little to say, in its early form, about landholding, about the internal alliances that sustained export specialisation, or about the classes that gained and lost from the structure within each peripheral country. Those silences were the space into which the Marxist generation moved. The radicalisation of the nineteen sixties can be described, in one sentence, as the filling of the centre and periphery map with the class structures the commission had left blank, and the redrawing of its policy conclusions accordingly.

## The terms of trade: a claim built from price series

The commission's central empirical claim concerned the terms of trade, the ratio between the prices a country receives for its exports and the prices it pays for its imports. Prebisch argued, from long statistical series reaching back to the nineteenth century, that this ratio had moved persistently against exporters of primary products: over the long run, a given quantity of the periphery's commodities bought a shrinking quantity of the centre's manufactures. The British economist Hans Singer arrived at a closely related conclusion independently at the same moment, and the proposition entered the literature under both names. Its significance for the school's formation lies less in its later fortunes as an empirical proposition than in the kind of claim it was: a structural tendency, assembled from price data, that no individual transaction revealed and no market participant intended.

The mechanism proposed to explain the tendency joined the two poles of the map. In the centres, technical progress raised productivity in manufacturing, but strong unions and concentrated firms converted the gains into higher incomes rather than lower prices, so the centres kept the fruit of their progress. In the periphery, technical progress in export production, mining, or plantation agriculture met a labour market crowded with underemployed workers, so productivity gains were competed away into lower export prices, transferring the fruit of the periphery's progress to its customers abroad. Progress at both poles, impoverishment of the terms on which one pole traded: that asymmetry was the empirical heart of structuralism, and it taught a generation of Latin American economists to distrust the surface of trade statistics.

### How was the decline claim assembled from price series?

From long runs of British trade statistics, in which the prices of primary imports into the industrial centre could be tracked against the prices of its manufactured exports across many decades. The method was crude by later standards, open to objections about quality change and composition, but it made a structural argument from official numbers.

The claim was contested from the beginning, and the contest belongs to the formation story because it shaped the school's habits. Critics objected that the series conflated price movements with changes in the composition and quality of goods, that freight costs distorted the comparison, and that the chosen endpoints flattered the conclusion. Defenders replied, refined, and recomputed. What matters here is the institutional consequence: the argument was conducted over statistics, in journals and commission documents, by economists who expected claims to be checked. The radical generation inherited that expectation even when its own claims outran the available data, and the tension between the inherited standard and the new claims became one of the school's internal fault lines, examined later in this article when the strongest criticisms are stated.

No figures from those series are reproduced here, and the omission is deliberate and principled. The argument of this article does not depend on the size of any movement in any index, and the school's history is full of numbers quoted from contested series as though they were measurements of a physical quantity. The formation story needs only the claim's existence, its method, and its effects: it gave the commission a doctrine, gave governments a reason to industrialise, and gave the next generation a standard of evidence against which its own, larger claims would be judged.

## Structuralism as a working method

Beneath the famous claim sat a method, less often described because it lived in the commission's working papers rather than in manifestos. Structuralist analysis proceeded by identifying bottlenecks: points where the economy's structure, rather than its prices, blocked expansion. The supply of food from a stagnant agricultural sector could not stretch to feed growing cities, so urbanisation generated inflation. The export sector could not earn foreign exchange fast enough to pay for the imported machinery that industrialisation required, so growth strangled on the balance of payments. The industrial sector, built behind protection in fragments, lacked the interconnections that made manufacturing self-sustaining elsewhere. Inflation, in the structuralist account pressed by economists such as the Chilean Aníbal Pinto and the Brazilian Celso Furtado, was not primarily a monetary disorder to be cured by austerity; it was the surface disturbance of these deeper rigidities, and austerity that ignored them would produce stagnation without stability.

Furtado deserves a sentence of his own in the formation story, because he carried structuralism into economic history. A Brazilian who worked with the commission in its early years and later directed development planning in his own country before exile took him to universities abroad, Furtado argued that underdevelopment was a historical formation with its own logic, not a phase through which all economies passed. His histories of Brazil treated the export economy's structures as creations of specific centuries and specific insertions into world markets. In that move, from bottleneck to history, structuralism approached the threshold the Marxists would cross: structures are made, they persist because interests sustain them, and they can be unmade only by changing the relations that reproduce them.

The method also travelled through people. The commission's training courses, its country missions, and the planning institute (ILPES) established in Santiago in the early nineteen sixties, multiplied the number of economists who thought in bottlenecks and balances. When the radical theorists later insisted that underdevelopment was produced and reproduced rather than original and natural, they were speaking a language whose grammar had been built inside the United Nations system. The dependency school's Marxism was laid over a structuralist formation, and the formation shows through at every joint.

## Import substitution: the programme the theory grew against

The policy programme that structuralism sponsored was import substitution industrialisation. Its logic followed directly from the diagnosis. If the periphery's trade position deteriorated because it exported primary products and imported manufactures, then the remedy was to produce at home what had been imported, beginning with the consumer goods whose markets already existed and moving, in intention, toward intermediate and capital goods. The instruments were the standard armoury of mid-century developmentalism: tariffs and import controls to shelter new industries, overvalued exchange rates to cheapen imported machinery, state enterprises in steel, energy, and transport, development banks to supply long-term credit that private capital markets would not. Across the region, from the nineteen thirties in the larger countries and with commission blessing from the nineteen fifties, the programme was actually attempted, which distinguishes it from most development doctrines: it had a record, and the record could be examined.

### Why did protection look different from Santiago than from Washington?

Because from Santiago protection was a reply to a diagnosed structural tendency, the long deterioration of the periphery's trading position, whereas from Washington it appeared as a distortion of an otherwise beneficent market order. The same tariff was, in one framework, a correction and, in the other, a sin against efficiency.

The first phase, the substitution of imported consumer goods by domestic production, was by the standards of the programme a success in the larger economies: factories rose, industrial employment grew, cities swelled. Its limits were structural, and the commission's own economists catalogued them with increasing candour through the nineteen fifties. Domestic markets in poor countries were small and skewed by unequal landholding and income, so consumer goods industries quickly saturated the demand available to them. Substitution at one stage created import demand at the next: the new factories needed imported machinery, components, and fuel, so the balance of payments constraint reappeared in a new form, often more rigid than before, since a factory starved of imported inputs stops entirely where a consumer merely goes without. Industrialisation proceeded, but it proceeded as an archipelago of protected enclaves, dependent on imported technology and inputs, employing fewer people than the cities attracted, and leaving the export structure, the original source of foreign exchange, largely unchanged.

One misreading must be corrected here, because it inverts the school's genealogy. Import substitution was not a dependency policy. It was the commission's structuralist programme, and dependency theory developed in substantial part from its failure: the radical theorists were formed by watching the programme they had been trained to administer reach its limits, and their central question was supplied by that experience. If industrialisation inside the existing international structure reproduced dependence in new forms, imported machinery, foreign patents, multinational subsidiaries, then perhaps the structure itself, and not merely the composition of trade, was the object that analysis had to seize. The theory was the programme's self-criticism, conducted by the programme's own graduates.

## The exhaustion of the easy phase

By the early nineteen sixties the vocabulary of exhaustion was common inside the commission's own publications. The easy phase of substitution, the replacement of imported consumer goods, was complete in the larger economies and had nowhere obvious to go. The harder phases, domestic production of intermediate and capital goods, demanded scales of investment, technology, and market size that strained against the region's fragmentation into national markets, which is why the commission also sponsored schemes of regional integration, hoping that a Latin American common market might supply the scale that single countries lacked. Meanwhile the external constraint tightened: export earnings from the traditional commodities stagnated, debt service grew, and the foreign exchange bottleneck that structuralism had identified at the outset returned as the binding limit on every plan.

The political temperature rose with the economic frustration. The Cuban Revolution of 1959 had demonstrated, ninety miles from Florida, that the hemisphere's order could be broken, and its example pressed a question on every Latin American economist: if reform within the structure had stalled, what followed? The commission's answer remained reformist, deeper substitution, integration, planning. But a younger cohort, reading Marx alongside the commission's surveys and watching military coups close the reformist road in Brazil in 1964 and elsewhere, drew a different conclusion. The stall was not a technical difficulty. It was the structure defending itself, through the domestic classes whose position the structure sustained and through the foreign interests to which those classes were bound. To say that was to cross from structuralism into dependency theory proper, and the crossing happened in specific rooms, to which the article now turns.

### What ran out first when substitution reached its limits?

Room to grow. The protected consumer goods industries had supplied the demand that existed at the prevailing distribution of income, and further expansion required either a redistribution the political order would not permit or an export capacity the structure had never built. Markets, not machinery, were the first scarcity.

It is worth pausing on the evidentiary character of this moment, because it distinguishes the school's formation from that of doctrines invented in libraries. The radicalisation was driven by a documented policy failure experienced at close quarters. The theorists of the research centre were not applying a foreign schema to a continent they had read about; several had administered, advised, or statistically chronicled the programme whose exhaustion they were explaining. Their Marxism acquired from that experience its distinctive concreteness about production, trade, and the state, and also its characteristic impatience with purely political explanations of underdevelopment. Whatever the later criticisms of their claims, and the criticisms are serious and will be given at full strength, the questions were earned in the region's own economic history.

Two features of the map deserve emphasis before the narrative resumes. The first is the concentration. For roughly a decade, the distance between the commission's structuralism, the planning institute's empiricism, and the research centre's Marxism was a matter of Santiago streets. A scholar could, and the record shows scholars did, move between these employers, argue in one building in the morning and teach in another in the afternoon. The second feature is the asymmetry of survival. The map's final column records one destruction among several survivals, and that one destruction fell precisely on the institution where the positions were being integrated into a programme. The school's archive, its seminar culture, and its collective memory were concentrated exactly where the blow landed.

## Inside the University of Chile's research centre

The Centre for Socio-Economic Studies, CESO in its Spanish initials, was founded in 1965 within the University of Chile, the country's great public university, and it became the radical school's home. Its character differed from the commission's in ways that shaped the work done there. It was small. It was academic rather than diplomatic, free to publish conclusions that a United Nations body would have had to phrase as recommendations. It was interdisciplinary by design, joining economists to sociologists and political scientists, and it worked in the charged atmosphere of a university whose students and faculty were participants in the country's political struggles. Above all, it was a place where the dependency argument was developed collectively: in seminars where drafts circulated, in a journal culture that rewarded polemic, and in daily proximity among scholars who disagreed about nearly everything except the question.

The question of the school's address can now be answered in summary form, at the centre of the map, as this article states it below.

### Where did the dependency theorists actually work?

principally in Santiago. A United Nations regional commission established there in 1948 produced the structuralist economics, and a research centre at the University of Chile housed the leading dependency theorists working together until the 1973 coup, after which they were exiled, mostly to Mexico.

The centre's Chilean setting mattered in a further way. Chile in the late nineteen sixties was conducting, in public, the argument the theorists were conducting in seminar: whether the country's structures could be transformed by electoral and legal means. The Christian Democratic government of Eduardo Frei, elected in 1964, attempted reforms, agrarian reform, the Chileanisation of copper, that read like the commission's programme translated into a presidency. Its frustrations, and the radicalisation of the electorate that followed, formed the lived background against which the centre's scholars wrote about the limits of reform inside dependence. When the Popular Unity coalition won the presidency in 1970 and Salvador Allende attempted the transition to socialism by constitutional means, the centre's researchers found themselves beside an experiment that was testing, at national scale, propositions adjacent to their own. The political history of those years is treated in the study of [Allende's Chile and the democratic road](/2018/12/01/allende-chile-democratic-road/); here it is enough to register that the seminar and the state were, for three years, in the same city and in conversation.

### What made a university research centre a different workplace from a United Nations commission?

Freedom of conclusion and poverty of protection. The centre could call dependence a structure of domination in print, where the commission had to speak of imbalances; but the centre existed at the pleasure of a national university in a polarised republic, and when the republic fell, nothing stood between it and destruction.

The centre also differed in its relation to language. The commission published in Spanish and English as a matter of course, its documents translated by an international secretariat. The centre published in Spanish, in Chilean and Mexican journals and with Latin American houses, and its working papers circulated in the region's Spanish and Portuguese. That linguistic fact, trivial while the school was intact, became decisive after the dispersal, because it determined which texts existed in a form the English-speaking academy could pick up, and when. The untranslated character of much of the centre's output is a theme this article returns to in its note on sources; the map should already suggest why translation, after 1973, was not a neutral conveyor belt but a filter with a politics of its own.

## The Brazilians in Santiago: exile before the exile

A striking share of the school's principal theorists were Brazilians, and their presence in Santiago was the product of an earlier catastrophe. The military coup that seized Brazil in 1964 purged the universities and drove a generation of social scientists abroad. Chile, with its open universities, its United Nations institutions, and its reputation as the region's asylum, received many of them. Fernando Henrique Cardoso, a young São Paulo sociologist whose university career the dictatorship had interrupted, worked in Santiago in the mid nineteen sixties at the planning institute, alongside the Chilean economist Enzo Faletto. Theotonio dos Santos, an economist from Minas Gerais formed in the debates of the Brazilian left, arrived by a similar road and became a central figure at the research centre. Ruy Mauro Marini, whose path ran first through Mexican exile, reached the centre in its final years. Santiago's school of dependence was thus, from the beginning, a school of the displaced, assembled by one dictatorship and destroyed by another.

### Why did Santiago fill with Brazilian social scientists in the nineteen sixties?

Because Brazil's military regime expelled them and Chile's institutions hired them. The Brazilian coup closed universities and careers at home, while the commission, the planning institute, the social science faculty, and the research centre offered posts, libraries, and students to scholars whose only disqualification was their politics.

The Brazilian formation of these men left its mark on the theory. Brazil was the region's largest economy and its most ambitious industrialiser, the country where import substitution had gone furthest and where its limits, the foreign-owned commanding heights, the imported technology, the exclusion of the mass of the population from the consumer market, were most visible. A theorist formed in São Paulo or Belo Horizonte had seen dependence from inside its most developed case, and the Brazilian experience supplied the school with its central puzzle in a sharpened form: not the absence of development, but development of a kind that deepened subordination while it proceeded. The Chilean experience, of an enclave mining economy and a radicalised democracy, supplied a second case with a different shape. The school's generality came from the friction between the two.

Exile also taught habits that the theory absorbed. The Brazilians in Santiago wrote for a continent because their national audience had been taken from them; they wrote historically because the present in their own country was censored; and they wrote with an acute sense that intellectual work depended on institutions that politics could withdraw, a sense the Chileans of the centre were about to acquire in their turn. When the second catastrophe came, the Brazilians recognised it earlier than most, having seen the sequence before: the lists, the raids, the closed institutes. Some of the centre's people escaped in 1973 because colleagues who had lived through 1964 in Brazil knew what the first days of a coup required of those it would hunt.

## The German-American in the seminar room

André Gunder Frank was the school's most famous member in the English-speaking world and its most atypical. Born in Berlin, trained as an economist at the University of Chicago, he had broken with the development orthodoxy of his training and arrived, by way of Mexico and Brazil, at the University of Chile in 1968, where he taught and worked in the centre's orbit. His celebrated essay on the development of underdevelopment, published in English in 1966, argued that underdevelopment was not an original state but a product, generated by the same capitalist process that generated development at the centre, through a chain of metropolis and satellite relations reaching back to the conquest. The essay's polemical force, its inversion of the modernisation story in a single formula, made it the school's calling card in the Anglophone world.

Frank's position inside the Santiago group is instructive precisely because it was argued with. His account stressed continuity: Latin America had been capitalist, and therefore exploited in a single mode, since the conquest, and underdevelopment was the satellite's immemorial condition. Colleagues with a stronger historical formation objected that this flattened the differences between colonial extraction, the export economies of the nineteenth century, and the industrial dependence of the twentieth, and that a mechanism unchanged across four centuries explained everything by explaining nothing in particular. The Argentinian historian's critique and the Brazilians' insistence on periodising dependence, distinguishing its colonial, export, and technological-industrial forms, were developed in the same seminar culture that Frank's provocation had helped to animate. The school's internal history is unintelligible if Frank is treated as its voice. He was its loudest voice in English, which is a different thing, and the difference is one of the reception distortions this article exists to correct.

His trajectory after the coup completed the pattern. Expelled from the Chile of the dictatorship, he rebuilt his career in European universities, writing prolifically in English, while the colleagues whose work had corrected his remained, for English readers, names in his footnotes. The accident of language and location, not any judgement of the field, determined that ordering. A reception history that begins from Frank and treats dos Santos and Marini as his respondents has the seminar room exactly backwards: in the room, the argument ran in all directions, and some of the strongest pressure on Frank's formulations came from the men whose books the English reader could not buy.

## Cardoso and Faletto: dependence written as history

The work that posterity has judged the school's most durable was written in Santiago in the mid nineteen sixties by Cardoso and Faletto and published in Spanish in Mexico in 1969, under the title Dependencia y desarrollo en América Latina. Its method announced its difference from both the commission's economics and Frank's sweeping continuity. Cardoso and Faletto refused to treat dependence as an external mechanism acting on passive societies. They analysed situations of dependency: historically specific configurations in which external structures and internal class alliances interlocked, so that the form dependence took in an export enclave differed from its form in an economy industrialising under foreign capital. Development and dependence, in their account, were not opposites. A dependent development was possible, and Brazil's industrialisation under multinational investment was its emerging case: growth that proceeded, dynamised sectors of the economy, and simultaneously deepened the subordination of the development pattern to decisions taken elsewhere.

### What did Cardoso mean by treating dependency as a situation rather than a mechanism?

That dependence names no single device, like a pump transferring surplus, which operates identically everywhere. It names a configuration, rebuilt in each period, between foreign capital, the local state, and domestic classes, so the analyst's task is historical: to show how a given configuration arose and what it permits.

The book's long delay in English, a full decade between the Spanish original and the English edition of 1979, is one of the cleanest illustrations of the dispersal thesis. The Spanish text circulated through the region's universities in the nineteen seventies, shaping a generation's understanding while the Anglophone debate proceeded without it, organised around Frank's earlier and cruder formulations. When the English edition finally appeared, reviewers treated its argument as a late moderation of a doctrine that had supposedly hardened without it, when in fact the moderation was contemporaneous with the doctrine's formation and had been present in the seminar room from the start. Sequence created the appearance of a school that began crude and grew subtle. The school had been subtle and crude at once, in argument with itself, from the beginning.

Cardoso's own later career, as a senator and then as president of Brazil in the nineteen nineties, carried the school's history into an irony its chroniclers cannot omit. The theorist of dependent development governed as a market reformer, and critics of the left treated the trajectory as apostasy while defenders treated it as the situation analysis applied to a new situation. For the formation story, the relevant point is narrower: the distance between the Santiago seminar and the presidential palace measures how completely the world that produced the school had vanished. The man had been formed by institutions, the commission's planning institute and the exile's São Paulo research centre he later helped to found, and the theory he wrote there belonged to those rooms in a way his later offices never could.

## Dos Santos and the structure of dependence

Theotonio dos Santos, who directed much of the research centre's work and edited its intellectual output, supplied the school with its most systematic statement. His essay on the structure of dependence, published in English in a leading American economics journal in 1970, defined dependence as a conditioning situation: an economy whose accumulation and expansion were shaped by, and subordinated to, the development of other economies to which it was tied. He distinguished historical forms of dependence, colonial, financial-export, and the new dependence that had emerged after the Second World War, in which multinational corporations invested inside the peripheral economies themselves, dominating their industrial sectors from within and binding their development to imported technology and to the remittance of profits, interest, and royalties.

### Why did dos Santos insist dependence had a structure rather than a single cause?

Because the subordination he observed worked through several channels at once, trade, finance, technology, and corporate ownership, and each channel reinforced the others. A single-cause account invited single remedies, export promotion or capital controls, that the structure would route around, leaving the conditioning situation intact beneath the policy.

The periodisation mattered as much as the definition. Against Frank's continuity, dos Santos argued that the new dependence differed in kind from the old: the foreign presence was no longer at the port and the mine but inside the factory, so that the periphery's most modern sectors were the most dependent, and industrialisation itself had become a form of the relation rather than an escape from it. That claim, aimed simultaneously at the commission's optimism about substitution and at Frank's undifferentiated history, shows the seminar culture at work: a concept refined under pressure from two directions at once, by a scholar who had read the commission's surveys and argued with Frank in person. It also shows why the school cannot be summarised as a single position. On the central historical question, whether dependence was one long condition or a sequence of distinct structures, its two most cited theorists stood on opposite sides, in print, while sharing an institution.

Dos Santos paid for the school's prominence with the school's fate. A Brazilian exile in Chile, he was doubly exposed in 1973, and his road after the coup led to Mexico, where he rebuilt an academic life at the National Autonomous University and continued to write the theory's history as one of its makers. His later work, surveying the tradition he had helped to found, performed an office the dispersal had made necessary: the school's memory, which an intact institution would have carried impersonally, had to be carried by its survivors in their own persons, in memoirs, interviews, and retrospective essays scattered across the journals of three continents.

## Marini: the strongest mechanism and the slowest journey

Ruy Mauro Marini was, by the judgement of many within the tradition, its most original economic theorist, and for English readers he was for decades its most inaccessible. A Brazilian formed in the revolutionary left of his own country, exiled after 1964 first to Mexico, he arrived in Chile as the nineteen seventies opened and worked at the research centre during the Popular Unity years. His principal work, Dialéctica de la dependencia, published in 1973, set out the mechanism of super-exploitation: the argument that capital in the dependent economy, unable to match the productivity of the centre and losing value through the exchange of its products, compensated by driving the exploitation of its own workers beyond the limits normal in the centre, lengthening the working day, intensifying labour, and paying wages below the cost of reproducing the worker's capacity to work. The mechanism's working, including his account of how it ruptures the circuit between production and consumption inside the dependent economy, belongs to the theory article in this series and is linked there rather than restated here. What belongs to this article is the mechanism's fate as a text.

That fate was determined by language and by the calendar. The book appeared in Spanish in the year of the coup, from a Mexican publisher, as its author fled a second dictatorship. It circulated in Spanish and Portuguese through the region's universities, was debated, excerpted, and taught, and became a foundation text of the Latin American Marxist tradition in its home languages. In English, for decades, it existed in fragments and summaries: an essay here, a chapter there, long quotations inside other men's books. The full argument, with its careful construction and its evidence, was unavailable to the reader who knew the school only in English, who therefore encountered super-exploitation as a slogan attributed to a name, rather than as a mechanism whose steps could be examined. No fact about the school's reception illustrates the dispersal thesis more cleanly. The argument that specialists judge the strongest was the argument the Anglophone world was least able to read.

### Why did Marini's book travel so much more slowly than Frank's essays?

Frank wrote in English, published with New York houses, and taught in North American and European universities, so his texts moved along the academy's main roads. Marini wrote in Spanish and Portuguese, published in Mexico in the year his workplace was destroyed, and spent his career in Latin American universities.

The contrast extends to scholarly standing. In the region, Marini's standing rose steadily as the tradition took stock of itself, and the reassessment of the school undertaken by Latin American scholars in later decades placed his work at its centre, as the point where dependency analysis became a theory of exploitation rather than a theory of trade. In the Anglophone literature of the same decades, he remained a minor citation, his name attached to a term whose argument few of the citers had read. Two reputations, diverging for half a century, for reasons that have nothing to do with the quality of the work and everything to do with which institutions survived to carry it and which languages it was imprisoned in. The institutional map explains the divergence; no account of the school's ideas, taken alone, can.

## A school arguing with itself

It is time to state directly the feature of the Santiago years that the reception most thoroughly lost: the school's positions were produced in argument among people who shared workplaces, journals, and a political catastrophe in the making. The debates were not retrospective constructions by historians. Cardoso's historical method was sharpened against Frank's continuities in real time. Dos Santos periodised dependence partly in reply to Frank and partly in reply to the commission. Marini's super-exploitation was framed against both the structuralist inheritance and the circulation-centred accounts that located dependence in trade alone. The Chilean economists of the commission and the university, men such as Osvaldo Sunkel, who moved between the commission's world and the university's and later helped to rebuild Chilean social science under the dictatorship's shadow, pressed the school's claims against the region's data and its environmental limits. To read the surviving texts as a shelf of separate doctrines is to mistake the debris for the building.

### What did the theorists gain from sharing a corridor that letters could not supply?

Speed and severity of correction. A claim made in a seminar met its strongest counterexample by Friday, from a colleague who knew the same statistics and the same countries, and weak formulations died young. Correspondence corrects slowly and politely; corridors correct at once, in public, before the claim hardens into a book.

The corridor also supplied a common evidentiary floor. Whatever divided the Santiago group, its members argued over the same surveys, the same trade series, the same Brazilian and Chilean cases, and a participant could not win a point by inventing a fact the room did not recognise. This discipline is invisible in the published record precisely because it worked: the wild claim unpublished, the statistic checked before the seminar ended. After the dispersal, the floor vanished. The exiles wrote from different countries, with different data and different audiences, and the school's later texts show the loss, growing more programmatic as the shared evidence thinned. Critics who charge the later dependency literature with assertion rather than demonstration are, without knowing it, describing the institutional loss: demonstration is a social practice before it is a logical one, and the society that practised it had been destroyed.

## The Popular Unity years: the seminar beside the state

Between 1970 and 1973 the research centre worked in the shadow of an experiment. The Popular Unity government of Salvador Allende was attempting to transform Chile's structure by constitutional means: nationalising the copper industry, accelerating agrarian reform, bringing banks and large enterprises under public control. The centre's scholars were not the government's economists, and the relation between seminar and state was one of proximity and argument rather than of office. But proximity changed the questions. Dependence was no longer an abstraction about trade patterns; it was the daily experience of a government whose room for manoeuvre was bounded by foreign credit, by the copper price, by the decisions of multinational boards, and by the hostility of the hemisphere's dominant power. The theorists could watch, from their own city, the mechanisms they had described operating as political facts.

The years beside the state also divided the school along lines that the coup would freeze. Some of the centre's milieu pressed the government to move faster, treating its legalism as the obstacle; others, formed by the Brazilian lesson of 1964, warned that a transformation which outran its social base would summon the military solution it feared. The argument was the Latin American left's deepest argument, between the road of accumulation of forces and the road of rupture, and it was conducted with the knowledge, available to the Brazilians from experience, of how such arguments end when the barracks decide. In September 1973 the barracks decided in Chile as they had in Brazil, and the seminar's unfinished argument was scattered into exile along with the seminar.

### How did proximity to a governing experiment change the questions the centre asked?

It converted dependence from a condition to be described into a constraint to be operated against, daily, by named officials facing credit refusals, supply blockades, and capital flight. The theory gained a practical tense: not only how the structure worked, but how much room a government inside it actually possessed.

For the formation story, the Popular Unity years complete the school's education. The commission had taught it statistics and the regional frame. The exhaustion of substitution had taught it that structures defend themselves. The years beside the Allende government taught it the tempo of structural power, the speed at which an economy's external connections could be mobilised against a government that challenged them. The lessons arrived together, and there was no time to write them into a synthesis before the institution that might have housed the synthesis was destroyed. The school's unfinished character, its quality of a programme interrupted rather than completed, dates from those years and from the morning that ended them.

## Santiago as a node: journals, publishers, and the roads texts travelled

An institution is also a set of channels, and the Santiago school's channels deserve a section of their own, because their geography prefigured the dispersal. The region's serious social science moved through a small number of journals and publishing houses, several of them Mexican: the Fondo de Cultura Económica, founded in the nineteen thirties, and Siglo XXI, founded in the nineteen sixties by an Argentine publisher exiled in Mexico, which issued Cardoso and Faletto's book and much of the school's Spanish-language canon. Journals in Santiago, Mexico City, and Buenos Aires carried the debates; the Monthly Review in New York carried Frank and a selection of others into English. The Latin American Council of Social Sciences, founded in 1967, stitched the region's institutes into a network of conferences and publications that survived, in attenuated form, the catastrophes of the nineteen seventies.

The channels were narrower than they looked. A text's journey from a Santiago seminar to an English-language syllabus required a translator, a willing publisher, and usually a champion inside the Anglophone academy, and each requirement filtered what passed. Short, quotable, programmatic texts travelled; long, technical, historically dense texts waited. Frank's essays were built for the journey. Cardoso and Faletto's book, dense with historical specification, waited a decade. Marini's treatise, the most technical of all, waited longest. The filter did not merely delay the school's reception; it selected a distorted sample of the school and presented the sample as the whole, so that the English-speaking world's dependency theory was, in significant measure, the dependency theory of its most portable texts. Teachers of the subject in London or New York in the nineteen seventies were not negligent. They taught what the channels had delivered, and the channels had delivered a fraction, skewed toward the slogan and away from the seminar.

## The morning the centre ended

On 11 September 1973 the Chilean armed forces overthrew the government of Salvador Allende, who died in the presidential palace as it was attacked. The military junta that took power moved at once against the institutions of the Chilean left and against the universities. The University of Chile was placed under a delegate rector appointed by the regime, its faculties were purged, and CESO ceased to exist as a working institution: its premises were taken over, its library and papers were dispersed or destroyed, and its researchers were dismissed. Across the university system, academics were expelled, detained, or driven abroad, and entire disciplines, sociology and political science among them, were closed or reduced for years. The school's Chilean home, the institution in which the radical dependency theorists had worked together, was destroyed in the coup's first phase, as an institution, before the longer work of repression had run its course.

The facts of the researchers' fates can be stated plainly, and this article states them without ornament. Some of the centre's scholars were detained in the coup's aftermath; the regime's prisons and camps held academics among their many thousands of prisoners. Some were expelled from the country by decree. Some left under the protection of foreign embassies, whose compounds sheltered hunted Chileans and foreigners in the coup's first weeks, and some simply fled across borders that were still passable. The Brazilians of the centre were in the gravest danger, being foreigners of the left in a country whose new rulers regarded them as agents of an international conspiracy, and several escaped through embassy asylum to third countries. Chileans of the school scattered through the same channels. Within months, a research community that had shared one building in Santiago was distributed among Mexico City, Caracas, Paris, Amsterdam, and a dozen other cities, and the building itself belonged to a university remade by decree.

### Which institutions received the exiles first, and why Mexico?

Mexico received the largest share, because its government maintained an asylum tradition, refused to recognise the junta for a period, and possessed universities and publishers able to absorb scholars quickly. The National Autonomous University, El Colegio de México, and new research centres took in Chileans, Brazilians, Argentines, and Uruguayans as the region's coups multiplied.

No figures for the numbers exiled, imprisoned, or killed are given in this article, and the omission follows the protocol that governs it. The counts are contested, the school's own losses are entangled with the far larger catastrophe of Chilean society, and the argument here requires only the institutional fact, which is not contested: the centre was destroyed, its people were scattered by force, and the work they had been doing together stopped. Precision about the mechanism of dispersal serves the history better than precision about numbers that the sources do not settle.

## Mexico City: the second workplace

The diaspora reassembled, partially and in a different shape, in Mexico. The Mexican state, pursuing its own tradition of asylum and its diplomatic quarrel with the Chilean junta, opened its universities to the southern exiles, and Mexico City in the mid nineteen seventies held an extraordinary concentration of displaced Latin American scholarship: Chileans from the destroyed centre and the purged universities, Brazilians on their second exile, Argentines and Uruguayans fleeing the coups that followed in their own countries. El Colegio de México expanded its social science programmes; the National Autonomous University absorbed scholars into its faculties and institutes; the Centre for Economic Research and Teaching was founded in 1974 and took in economists of the diaspora; and the great Mexican publishing houses issued the exiles' books to a Spanish-reading continent. The dependency debate continued in Mexico, in journals and seminars, and some of its most considered statements, retrospective and synthetic, were written there.

Yet the Mexican reassembly was not the Santiago school restored. The exiles were guests in institutions with their own agendas, dispersed across faculties and research centres rather than gathered in one corridor, and their audiences had changed: they wrote increasingly for a continent of dictatorships, about transitions, authoritarianism, and the state, as the questions of survival displaced the questions of dependence. The research programme that the centre had housed, the collective refinement of a theory of dependent capitalism, did not resume as a collective enterprise. Its members remained productive, several of them greatly so, but they were productive as individuals in other people's institutions, on questions the new settings posed. The dispersal thus worked twice: once in the destruction itself, and once in the redirection of the survivors' work by the institutions that sheltered them. What English readers later received as the school's fragmentation into individual positions was, in part, simply this: the positions had been individual since 1973, because the collectivity that had disciplined them no longer existed.

Mexico also preserved what Chile could not: the texts. The Spanish-language canon of the school, its books kept in print by Mexican houses, its journals maintained, its debates archived, survived the dictatorship decades in Mexican libraries and Mexican editions. When the region's democracies returned and the reassessment of the tradition began, it began from holdings that Mexico had kept. The institutional map's Mexican row is therefore not a footnote to the Santiago story. It is the reason there is a story to reassess.

## The dispersal as a publishing event

The school's passage into English can be dated like a series of arrivals at a port, each separated by years. Frank arrived first, in the nineteen sixties, writing in English from the start, published by the Monthly Review's press and reviewed in the journals of the New Left; for many Anglophone readers he simply was dependency theory. Dos Santos arrived partially, through journal articles, his 1970 statement reaching specialists while his larger works stayed in Spanish and Portuguese. Cardoso and Faletto arrived in 1979, a decade after their book had reorganised the Spanish-language debate, in an English edition that scholars welcomed and syllabuses absorbed slowly. Marini arrived last and least, in fragments, his major work untranslated in full for decades. Sunkel, Furtado, Pinto, and the commission's own vast output arrived hardly at all, known to English readers through summaries in other men's surveys.

Arrange those arrivals in their actual order and the standard Anglophone account of the school becomes explicable as an artefact. The account runs: a crude theory of stagnation, associated with Frank, was criticised and then moderated by a subtler theory of dependent development, associated with Cardoso, in a progression from dogma toward nuance. The progression is an illusion of sequence. In Santiago the subtlety and the crudity were contemporaries, argued out in the same years, and the man cast as the crude founder was being corrected in the corridor by the man cast as his later moderator while both were still in the building. The English reception mistook the order of translation for the order of thought. It is difficult to think of a clearer case, in the modern social sciences, of a reception determined by logistics: by who held a passport that let him teach in Amsterdam, who wrote in the academy's language, whose publisher had a New York distributor, and whose book appeared in the year his institute was destroyed.

### What feature of the English reception looks like disagreement but is really sequence?

The supposed evolution from a crude stagnationism to a sophisticated theory of dependent development. The two positions were formed simultaneously, in argument, inside one institution; they appear as successive stages of a debate only because translation delivered them to English readers a decade apart.

The artefact had consequences for the school's reputation that persist. Judged through its earliest translated sample, dependency theory was classified in the Anglophone academy as a political rhetoric rather than a research programme, and the classification stuck even as the fuller record became available, because classifications organise syllabuses and syllabuses reproduce themselves. Judged through the full Spanish and Portuguese record, as Latin American scholarship judges it, the school appears as what it was: a decade of collective work, uneven, argumentative, empirically ambitious, interrupted by force. The two reputations are of the same object. The difference between them is the dispersal, and the dispersal is a historical event with dates, agents, and mechanisms, not a mystery of national intellectual styles.

## The reception is an artefact of the dispersal: the claim stated

The claim can now be stated in its full form. Dependency theory was produced collaboratively by a group working in one city, and the destruction of that centre in 1973 meant it reached English-language audiences as individual translated works rather than as a school, which is why it is received as a set of competing positions rather than as a research programme. Every element of the claim has been documented in the sections above. The collaboration is documented by the institutional map and the seminar culture. The destruction is documented by the coup's treatment of the university. The staggered translation is documented by the publication record. The reception, a shelf of rival positions where a programme had been, is the observable fact the claim exists to explain.

The claim also disciplines judgement. If the reception is an artefact, then the school's reputation in English is evidence about publishing and politics, not straightforward evidence about the school's quality, and the school's arguments must be re-examined in their original collective setting before they are graded. That re-examination is what Latin American scholarship has undertaken in the decades since the dictatorships, recovering texts, re-editing classics, writing the biographies, and reconstructing the debates, with the result that the school's standing in its home region has risen as its archive has been reassembled. The English-language reassessment has followed, slowly, in the archive's wake, which is the claim's final confirmation: as the dispersal's effects are undone by editors and translators, the reception changes, although not one word of the underlying texts has altered.

None of this exempts the school from criticism, and the article turns next to the criticisms at their strongest, because a formation story that functioned as an acquittal would betray the standard the school itself was raised on. The critics charged the radical current with abandoning evidence for assertion, and charged its most sophisticated product, the theory of dependent development, with surrendering the theory's core. Both charges deserve their full force, named authors, and an honest answer that concedes what must be conceded.

## The critics at full strength: the charge of politics over evidence

The strongest general criticism of the radical current was made systematically by the American political scientist Robert A. Packenham, whose study of the dependency movement argued that the radical current had abandoned the commission's empirical method for a structural account that could not be tested. In Packenham's reading, the movement's central mechanisms were asserted rather than demonstrated: dependence explained underdevelopment, and underdevelopment evidenced dependence, in a circle that no finding could break. The school's influence in the region, he argued, owed more to its political usefulness during a period of radicalisation, when it supplied intellectuals and movements with a warrant for conclusions already reached, than to its analytical strength. Other critics pressed parts of the same case from other directions. The historian and economist Ian Roxborough charged the Latin American Marxist sociology of the period with substituting schema for investigation. Development economists objected that the school's claims about trade and stagnation dissolved under econometric scrutiny, and that the successful industrialisation of East Asian economies, achieved inside the world market the school treated as a trap, stood as a standing counterexample to its strong conclusions.

The charge should be felt at its full weight, because it identifies something real in the school's later manner. Texts of the radical current do exist in which the structure of dependence functions as a universal solvent, explaining stagnation where it occurs and, with adjustments, growth where that occurs instead, and a theory that predicts every outcome predicts none. The political convenience of the doctrine in the years of radicalisation is also a fact: it told a generation of militants that the obstacle was the system and the hour was late, and doctrines that flatter their moment travel for reasons unrelated to their truth. Packenham's error, where he errs, is one of scope: he grades the whole school by its weakest products, the programmatic texts of the dispersal years, and treats the empirical programme of the Santiago period, the commission's statistical inheritance, the case studies, the arguments conducted over shared data, as though it had never existed. The school he describes, pure assertion in a political idiom, is recognisably the school as the dispersal delivered it. The school as the centre housed it was a different and more disciplined thing, and the difference is the subject of this article.

### What evidence would have answered the charge of untestability?

Findings the theory's rivals could check and its friends could lose by: measurements of the channels through which dependence was said to operate, profit remittances, technology payments, price movements, assembled in series the commission's own statisticians would have recognised, with the theory staked on what the series showed.

The answer, then, has two parts. The components of the school's work that generated testable claims were real and identifiable. The terms of trade proposition was testable and was tested for decades. The claim that foreign direct investment in the periphery generated remittance flows exceeding its capital inflows over time was testable against balance of payments data. The proposition that the new dependence worked through imported technology and intra-firm trade generated claims about the structure of imports and the behaviour of subsidiaries that case research could and did examine. The super-exploitation mechanism implied observable consequences for wage shares, working time, and the composition of industrial output, which is why it could be argued with on evidence by the school's own members. A programme containing such claims is not untestable in principle. But the concession must be made plainly, as the brief for this article requires: the school's collapse as an institution prevented the empirical programme its founders envisaged. The testing of those claims, at the scale and with the continuity the programme needed, required exactly what was destroyed in 1973, a stable collective with data, students, and time. The claims were left to be tested piecemeal, by others, in other countries, often by scholars hostile to the school and working from its translated fragments. Some survived the testing in revised form and some did not, but the programme as a programme, the cumulative refinement of a shared account, died with the centre. The critics are right that the radical current's legacy contains more assertion than its founders intended. They are wrong about the cause. The cause was not a preference for assertion. It was the destruction of the institution in which demonstration had been a daily practice.

## The quarrel inside the school: associated development and its radical critics

The second criticism came from inside the school, and it was the sharper for being fraternal. When Cardoso and Faletto's account of dependent development, and Cardoso's subsequent essays on associated-dependent development, argued that development was possible within dependence, the radical current heard a surrender. Dos Santos, Marini, and Frank, in different idioms, pressed the charge: if development is possible within dependence, then the explanation of underdevelopment by dependence collapses. What remains, the critics said, is a theory of stages with foreign capital, in which the dependent economy passes through phases of growth like any other, its subordination reduced to a circumstance rather than a structure, and the revolutionary conclusion the theory existed to support, that dependence must be broken rather than managed, dissolves into a sociology of modernisation with a critical vocabulary. The charge was aimed at the school's most sophisticated product by the school's own members, and it split the tradition's memory: to one lineage, Cardoso is the school's maturity; to the other, he is its liquidation.

### What exactly did the radical critics say Cardoso had surrendered?

The theory's explanatory core. If the dependent economy can develop, they argued, dependence no longer explains why it does not, and the concept loses the necessity that made it a theory rather than a description: subordination becomes one influence among others, to be weighed case by case.

The answer begins by stating precisely what the associated-dependence claim asserts, because the claim is about the form development takes, not about development in the abstract. Cardoso's proposition was never that dependence is compatible with development as the commission or the modernisation theorists understood it, autonomous, diffused, and self-sustaining. It was that a specific configuration, the association of international capital, the local state, and domestic business, could generate industrial growth of a determinate kind: growth concentrated in dynamic sectors linked to foreign firms, excluding the mass of the population from its consumption, denationalising the commanding decisions, and reproducing subordination inside the growth itself. Development, on this account, does not refute dependence; it is one of dependence's historical forms, and the political question is transformed rather than abolished, from whether growth is possible to whom the growth belongs to and who commands it. The critics' logic, if development then not dependence, treats development as a single substance that either occurs or does not. The claim under attack treats it as a family of processes whose forms dependence selects among. Stated at that precision, the surrender charge misses its target.

But the criticism gets something right, and the concession belongs in the article's own voice. What the radical critics correctly perceived was the cost of the move for the research programme. The strong theory had staked everything on a single sharp claim, that dependence blocked development, and the claim's very sharpness had organised a decade of collective work: it told researchers what to measure, what would count against them, and why the work mattered politically. The associated-development revision, truer to the Brazilian evidence as Cardoso read it, dissolved that organising sharpness. In its place came the historical study of situations, each configuration analysed in its specificity, a method of great power in the hands of its inventors and of diminishing returns in the hands of their successors, for whom situations multiplied and the programme's centre of gravity thinned into case description. The critics named the destination wrongly; the destination was not modernisation theory. But they saw, earlier than the victors of the argument admitted, that the programme after the revision would have difficulty remaining a programme, and the dispersal completed what the revision began: deprived of both its sharp claim and its shared institution, the school's later work scattered into national histories and individual oeuvres. The quarrel inside the school is therefore the hinge of the whole formation story. It was an argument about Brazil's factories. It was also, without the participants quite saying so, an argument about whether the school could survive the loss of the proposition that had founded it, and history answered that question in the corridor's ashes.

## What was testable, what was asserted

A sober inventory of the school's claims, sorted by their relation to evidence, helps to fix what the criticisms established and what they missed. At the testable end stood the commission's inheritance: the terms of trade proposition, the balance of payments constraint, the bottleneck analyses, each tied to series and accounts that others could recompute. Beside them stood the school's mechanism claims of middle range: that profit remittances and technology payments structured the periphery's external accounts in determinate ways; that the industrialisation sponsored by multinational subsidiaries would exhibit specific patterns of import intensity, employment, and market concentration; that super-exploitation would appear in the wage relation's measurable deformations. These claims could be, and were, examined against data, and their fortunes varied, which is precisely what distinguishes a mechanism from a slogan: a mechanism can lose.

At the asserted end stood the large framings. The proposition that underdevelopment and development were two faces of a single process, true as an orientation, functioned in weaker hands as a licence to treat any evidence as confirmation. The continuity thesis, four centuries of one unchanging satellite condition, was asserted at a scale where the historical record's variations became embarrassments to be explained away. The political conclusions, that only a break with the world market could free the periphery, were presented as deductions from the analysis when they were in fact wagers upon it. The school's own best practitioners knew the difference and policed it in seminar; the school's popularisers, in the region and abroad, routinely did not. A reader reconstructing the tradition from its strongest texts will find the boundary observed. A reader reconstructing it from its pamphlets will find the boundary invisible, and both readers are describing real strata of the same formation.

The inventory also explains the school's strange double reputation among economists: dismissed as rhetoric by those who met its pamphlets, cited with respect, sometimes grudging, by those who worked through its mechanisms. The dispersal distributed the strata unevenly across languages. English received a disproportionate share of the framing and the pamphlet, Spanish retained the mechanisms and the cases, and the reputations sorted themselves by language accordingly. Once again the institutional story does the explanatory work that a history of ideas, unaided, cannot.

## Liberation theology borrowed the map

The school's influence escaped economics early, and its most consequential borrowing was theological. The Peruvian priest Gustavo Gutiérrez, whose Theology of Liberation appeared in 1971 and gave a movement its name, built his account of Latin American poverty on the dependency analysis: the poor were poor not because their societies lagged on a common road but because their societies occupied a position in a structure, and the theology of liberation accordingly spoke of dependence and liberation where an older charity had spoken of backwardness and aid. The bishops' conference at Medellín in 1968 had already moved the region's church toward the language of structural injustice, and the theologians who systematised that move reached, for their social analysis, to the shelf the Santiago school had stocked. The relationship between the two movements, their shared vocabulary, their different foundations, and the criticisms each attracted, is treated in the companion study of [liberation theology and Marxism](/2018/12/15/liberation-theology-and-marxism/); the formation point here is about channels. The theology travelled through seminaries, religious orders, and base communities, a distribution network denser than any university's, and it carried the dependency framework into parishes and villages that no economics journal reached. A theory formed in a United Nations commission and a university centre acquired, through the church's infrastructure, the largest popular audience any Latin American social theory has ever had.

The borrowing also fed back into the theory's survival. When the dictatorships closed the universities and burned the books, the theological carriers of the dependency vocabulary operated inside an institution the regimes, Catholic in their own self-description, could harass but not abolish. Fragments of the school's framework thus persisted in pastoral letters, in the documents of base communities, and in the formation of a generation of Latin American priests and lay workers, while the economics faculties were being purged. The institutional map of the theory's survival has a row that no secular historian would have predicted: the church as the archive of a Marxist-adjacent economics, keeping its central intuition, that poverty is produced by a structure and not by a deficiency, in circulation through the years when the structure's analysts were in exile.

### What did the theologians take from the commission's economics, and what did they leave?

They took the structural account of poverty: underdevelopment as a position produced within a world order, not a stage on the way out of it. They left the policy programme and the class analysis, translating dependence into a moral and pastoral key their own tradition could carry.

## Did the school govern anywhere?

The question of policy influence requires the same care as the question of origins, because the obvious answer inverts the record. Import substitution, the policy most associated in casual memory with the school, was the commission's programme and preceded the dependency theory that criticised it. The dependency theorists themselves held no ministries and designed no implemented programme; their influence ran through opposition movements, revolutionary organisations, and the climate of economic nationalism that shaped measures such as resource nationalisations and the Andean Pact's code on foreign capital, adopted by the Andean countries in the opening years of the nineteen seventies, which bore the marks of dependency reasoning about technology contracts and profit remittances. The Popular Unity government in Chile governed with economists formed in the structuralist world and under pressures the dependency framework described, but its programme was its own, and the centre's scholars were its critics and counsellors rather than its authors.

### Why is the school's policy record mostly a record of programmes it opposed?

Because its institutional homes were a commission that advised and a centre that analysed, while the programmes actually implemented in its lifetime belonged to governments, developmentalist, reformist, or military, whose premises the school existed to criticise. Its influence was diagnostic and oppositional, a map of the terrain on which others governed.

That pattern, influence without office, is commoner among consequential theories than the mythology of Keynes at the treasury suggests, and it had a specific consequence for the school's afterlife. Because the theory was never implemented, it was never refuted by implementation; no ministry's failure could be laid at its door, as the failures of actually administered doctrines are laid at theirs. Its critics were therefore driven to attack its logic and its evidence, as Packenham did, rather than its record, and its defenders could preserve it in a purity that governing would have compromised. The school's reputation in the region, where it is remembered as the road not taken as much as the theory once argued, owes something to that exemption. Roads not taken do not accumulate potholes, and the counterfactual dignity of the dependency programme, what the region might have become had its diagnosis been acted on, remains a live element in its regional afterlife precisely because the experiment was never run.

## The commission after the theorists

The commission outlived its radical children and revised itself in their absence. Through the nineteen eighties, the decade of debt crisis and structural adjustment, the Santiago institution defended a heterodox position against the Washington consensus, arguing in its documents for adjustment with growth and for attention to the social costs that the fund's programmes discounted, and it did so with the diminished authority of a body whose region was governed by its critics' pupils. At the start of the nineteen nineties it relaunched its doctrine under the name of a new structuralism, in a programme of changing production patterns with social equity, which proposed competitiveness through technical progress, openness managed rather than refused, and equity as a condition of development rather than its reward. The vocabulary had travelled far from Prebisch's manifesto: protection was out, systemic competitiveness was in, and the centre and periphery language survived mainly as a framing of asymmetries to be negotiated.

The later commission matters to the formation story for two reasons. It demonstrates the durability of the institutional form: a United Nations body can revise its doctrine across half a century, employ successive generations, and preserve its archive intact, none of which was available to the research centre or its exiles. And it supplied, in its own house journal and its conferences, one of the venues in which the reassessment of the dependency tradition was conducted, the parent institution hosting the stocktaking of the children who had rebelled against it. The school's history thus ends, institutionally, where it began, in the commission's Santiago headquarters, although the doctrine housed there now would be unrecognisable to the men of 1973 in everything except its conviction that the region's insertion into the world economy remains the master question of its economics.

## The decolonial turn: a successor that changed the question

From the nineteen nineties, a current formed that claimed the dependency inheritance while relocating its centre of gravity. Its founding figure was the Peruvian sociologist Aníbal Quijano, himself a veteran of the region's Marxist debates, who proposed the concept of the coloniality of power: the argument that the conquest of the Americas had constituted a pattern of power, organised around the racial classification of the world's population and the control of labour, authority, and knowledge, which survived the end of formal colonialism and structured the modern world system from its origin. Around Quijano gathered the modernity and coloniality group, including the Argentine philosopher Enrique Dussel and the North American based theorist Walter Mignolo, and the programme spread through the region's universities and into the Anglophone humanities with a success the dependency school's economics never achieved in that quarter.

### What did coloniality keep from dependency, and what did it discard?

It kept the founding intuition: that the Americas' subordination was constituted in the same process that constituted the modern world, not added to it later. It discarded the economic mechanism as the privileged level of analysis, relocating the structure from trade and production to the classification of peoples and the control of knowledge.

The decolonial turn is best described, for this article's purposes, as a partial successor. It inherited the school's refusal to treat underdevelopment as lateness, its insistence that the periphery's condition was made by the same history that made the centre's wealth, and its regional standpoint, the claim that Latin America could generate theory rather than merely receive it. It changed the object: where the dependency theorists had analysed an economic structure and its classes, the decolonial writers analysed a civilisational pattern and its hierarchies of race and knowledge. The change of object changed the institutional home as well, from economics faculties and planning institutes to literature departments, anthropology, and philosophy, and with the home changed the standards of evidence and the audience. Whether the succession represents the tradition's renewal or its dissolution into cultural theory is argued, vigorously, within the region's scholarship; what the institutional map records is the migration itself, the school's questions surviving by moving house, as they had survived the nineteen seventies by moving to Mexico.

## Neo-extractivism: the successor on material terrain

A second successor worked on the older, material terrain. When governments of the region's left won elections in the two thousands on the back of a commodity boom, critics within their own camp, the Uruguayan ecologist Eduardo Gudynas and the Argentine sociologist Maristella Svampa among the most cited, named the resulting pattern neo-extractivism: the intensification of primary commodity extraction, soy, oil, gas, minerals, by governments that used its rents for redistribution while deepening the economy's dependence on the export of nature. The debate reproduced, in a new idiom, the Santiago school's founding scene: a policy programme celebrated as development, an external structure quietly reproducing itself through the programme, and a radical criticism arguing that the structure, not the government's intentions, was the fact to be explained. That debate, and the governments it judged, belong to the companion study of [the pink tide and its limits](/2019/01/01/pink-tide-marxist-analysis/), and are linked rather than retold here. The formation point is the recurrence. Twice in half a century, Latin American attempts to develop within the world market's given structure generated, from inside the attempt, a theory of why the structure was reproducing itself. The dependency school was the first such generation. Neo-extractivism's critics were the second, and they knew their parentage, citing the Santiago texts as they wrote.

## The quantification revival: the old question with new instruments

A third afterlife, the one that most directly continues the school's empirical ambition, is a body of work that returned to the quantification of value transfer through trade. Its practitioners, economists and ecologists working with global input-output tables and material flow accounts, set out to measure what the structuralists had asserted: the net movement of value, embodied labour, and materials between the world economy's poles through the ordinary operations of exchange. Studies in this literature, among them work by the anthropologist Jason Hickel and his collaborators on the drain of value from the global South, and a wider body of ecological economics on unequal exchange, argue in the school's own grammar with instruments the commission's statisticians would have envied: not bilateral price series but the full interindustry structure of the world economy, traced commodity by commodity. The revival's contemporary extensions, into the analysis of value chains and the geography of production, connect with the questions pursued in the study of [imperialism and global supply chains](/2017/06/01/imperialism-and-global-supply-chains/).

### What can input-output tables show that price series could not?

The transfers hidden inside production itself: the labour and materials embodied in traded goods, valued at the prices of the countries that consume them, so that a transfer can be traced through chains of suppliers rather than inferred from the movement of an index between two ports.

This body of work should be described as what it is, a literature with methods, findings, and critics, not as a vindication ceremony. Its measurements are contested, as the terms of trade measurements were contested, on grounds of method and counterfactual: what prices would have ruled in a world without the transfer is a question no table answers by itself. Its significance for the formation story is independent of its eventual verdicts. The school's founding gesture, to take a moral intuition about the world economy's unfairness and convert it into a quantity that could be argued over, has been repeated by a generation with better data and faster machines, working, as the Santiago group worked, against an orthodoxy that treats exchange between unequals as harmony. The revival's practitioners cite the dependency tradition as ancestry, and the citation is accurate: they are the commission's methodological grandchildren, whether or not they share its politics. Consistent with the protocol governing this article, none of the revival's quantities are reproduced here; its claims are directional and attributed, and the reader who wants the numbers should go to the literature itself, where they can be examined with their methods attached.

## A note on sources: the untranslated archive

This is the article in the series where the note on sources carries the argument, and it is placed here, before the personas, so that no reader mistakes it for apparatus. The dependency school's archive is divided by language, and the division is not incidental to its history but constitutive of it. An English reader can obtain, in the original authors' words, the following: Frank's principal essays and books, written in English; dos Santos's statement of the structure of dependence in its journal form and a selection of later work; Cardoso and Faletto's book, in the English edition that appeared a decade after the original; Furtado's major history in translation; and a scattering of the commission's documents in official English versions. That is the accessible school, and it is a real school, worth reading in its own right.

What the English reader cannot obtain, except through summary, paraphrase, and the mediation of other scholars, is at least as significant. Marini's Dialéctica de la dependencia, the tradition's most rigorous economic work, circulated for decades in Spanish and Portuguese with only partial English renderings, so that its central mechanism was known in English largely at second hand. The research centre's working papers and seminar publications exist in Spanish, in Chilean and Mexican journals whose runs sit in few libraries outside the region. Much of the commission's internal production, the country studies and mission reports in which structuralism's method actually lived, was never Englished. The debates of the Mexican exile, conducted in journals such as the ones the diaspora founded and sustained, remain substantially untranslated. The rule for the reader follows directly: any English-language account of the school, this article included, is built disproportionately on the translated fraction, and the confidence of its judgements should be discounted accordingly. The imbalance also explains a standing puzzle of the reception, why the English literature debates Frank's formulations with an intensity the region finds disproportionate. Frank is what the English reader has. The archive's shape, not the argument's merit, assigned him the role.

### Which arguments can an English reader meet only in summary?

The full construction of super-exploitation, whose steps and evidence fill a book that spent decades untranslated in full; the centre's seminar debates, preserved in Spanish working papers; and the exile generation's syntheses, published in Mexican journals that Anglophone libraries rarely hold.

## The data as an institution

Behind the school's arguments stood an infrastructure so familiar to its members that they rarely mentioned it: the commission's statistics. Before CEPAL, Latin American economies were statistically opaque to one another and largely to themselves; national accounts were rudimentary, trade series inconsistent, and the region existed in the world's economic literature as a set of illustrations in other people's theories. The commission's surveys changed the region's relation to its own facts. Year by year its economists assembled comparable accounts of production, trade, and income, argued over definitions until the series could bear comparison, and published the results in volumes that sat on the desk of every finance ministry in the region. A Latin American economist of the nineteen sixties was the first of his profession who could argue about his continent from numbers the continent had produced about itself.

This infrastructure shaped the theory's character in ways that outlasted the theory. The dependency writers thought in balances because the surveys had taught them to: the external accounts, the transfer of resources abroad, the composition of imports, the share of wages in income. Even their most speculative claims were phrased as propositions about flows that a statistical office could, in principle, count. When the school's critics charged it with assertion, the fairest reply available to its survivors was to point at the desks they had been trained at. The assertion crept in later, in exile, when the flows could no longer be counted by the people theorising them, and the theory's centre of gravity shifted from the survey to the manifesto. The data infrastructure also explains the school's longevity in one specific respect: its questions were keyed to series that statistical offices still publish, so each new generation of regional economists, opening the same balances, finds the old questions waiting in the tables.

## The training network: how a doctrine becomes a profession

Institutions reproduce themselves through people, and the commission's most durable product may have been neither a book nor a doctrine but a profession. Its training courses in Santiago brought young officials from across the region to study together for months at a time; its missions sent Santiago economists into ministries from Mexico City to Buenos Aires; the planning institute extended the work into a standing school of planners. The graduates carried home a common technical language, a common set of suspicions about trade and foreign capital, and one another's addresses. By the time the dependency theorists radicalised the inheritance, the inheritance was already installed in the region's state apparatuses, in the heads of the men and women who computed the balances and drafted the plans.

The network explains a feature of the school's reception inside the region that outsiders often miss. In Latin America, dependency was never merely an academic doctrine to be believed or disbelieved; it was the house theory of a professional generation, learned in training courses alongside national accounting, and its critics within the region argued with it as insiders correcting a shared formation. The region's later economic debates, over debt, adjustment, and openness, were conducted between people many of whom had passed through the same Santiago classrooms on opposite sides of the argument. The Chicago-trained economists who staffed the Chilean dictatorship's economic team were the conspicuous exception, formed deliberately outside the network, and their exception proves the network's reach: to govern Chile against the cepalino consensus, the regime needed economists whom Santiago had not trained.

## Antecedents: the interwar sources of a postwar doctrine

The commission's economics had ancestors, and recovering them corrects the impression that structuralism sprang fully formed from the surveys of 1949. The historian Joseph L. Love, in his study of the origins of Latin American structuralism, traced a line from the Romanian economist Mihail Manoilescu, whose theory of protection, arguing that industrial labour's higher productivity justified sheltering industry in agrarian countries, circulated in Latin America in the nineteen thirties and was read in Buenos Aires. Prebisch's own formation ran through the Argentine debates of the depression, through the collapse of the export model his generation had administered, and through the wartime discovery that the periphery's industries, cut off from imports, could grow. The centre and periphery scheme gave those experiences a general form, but the experiences came first, in the central bank and the trade negotiations of the nineteen thirties.

The antecedents matter because they locate the school's deepest habit: the conversion of administrative experience into theory. Prebisch theorised what he had administered. Furtado theorised the Brazilian planning he had practised. The research centre's generation theorised the exhaustion of the programme they had been trained to run. At each stage the theory's authority in the region rested on the same claim, that its makers had operated the machine they described. The claim was not available to the school's Northern interpreters, who met the theory as text, and its absence from their reading is one more reason the Anglophone reception graded the school as ideology while the region graded it as experience. Neither grading is complete, but the difference between them is itself a datum of the formation story.

## The counter-institution: Santiago's other economics

No map of the school's Santiago is complete without the institution built to destroy its influence. In the mid nineteen fifties the Catholic University in Santiago entered an agreement with the University of Chicago under which Chilean economics students were trained in Chicago and returned to staff a reformed economics faculty at home. The programme was deliberate: it created, inside the same city, a cadre of economists formed in the neoclassical tradition, hostile to the commission's structuralism, and committed to the market order the cepalino consensus treated as the problem. For two decades the two economics coexisted in Santiago, arguing across a widening political divide, the commission's and the universities' structuralists on one side, the Catholic University's Chicago graduates on the other.

The coup of 1973 settled the argument by force. The Chicago-trained economists, organised and waiting with a programme prepared before the coup, took command of economic policy under the dictatorship and conducted the hemisphere's first thorough neoliberal experiment: privatisation, opening, deregulation, the dismantling of the protected industrial structure the commission's programme had built. The dependency school's institutions were destroyed in the same months in which its antithesis acquired a state. The juxtaposition is the sharpest illustration available of this article's method. The contest between structuralism and neoliberalism in Chile was not decided by the comparative merits of two theories in a seminar. It was decided by which theory's institution the military chose to arm with power and which it chose to abolish, and the consequences of that institutional decision, for Chile and for the world that later imported the Chilean experiment, ran for decades.

The counter-institution also clarifies the school's afterlife by contrast. Neoliberalism's Chilean cadre survived its regime, staffed the transition's economic management in moderated form, and wrote its memoirs from office. The dependency school's cadre wrote from exile and return, without office, and its memoirs are correspondingly the literature of a defeat. When later generations ask why the region's economic imagination remains marked by a theory that never governed, part of the answer is that the theory's rivals are remembered as administrators, with an administrator's record to defend, while the school is remembered as an interpretation, with an interpretation's freedom to be rediscovered.

## The second front of the destruction: books, publishers, and readers

The destruction of the school's world extended beyond its workplace to its print infrastructure in Chile. The state publishing house founded under the Popular Unity government, which had flooded the country with cheap editions of classics and contemporary writing, was raided and closed, its stocks destroyed. Books identified with the defeated order were burned in the coup's early days, in episodes photographed and remembered; private libraries were purged by their frightened owners; the import and sale of Marxist literature was suppressed. A theory is not only its authors. It is also its editions, its print runs, its presence in bookshops and school libraries, and the dictatorship understood this as clearly as it understood the universities. The Chilean reader of 1980 inhabited a country from which the school's texts had been physically withdrawn, even as the same texts circulated in Mexico City editions a flight away.

The print destruction had one consequence the regime did not intend. It made the Mexican editions, and the exile journals, into the tradition's ark, and it invested the surviving copies inside Chile with the aura of contraband, read in private and passed by hand. The school's Chilean afterlife was thus formed in samizdat conditions that prolonged its oppositional prestige long after its arguments had ceased to be the region's frontier. When democracy returned, the reassessment of the tradition in Chile was conducted by a generation for whom the texts carried the glamour of the forbidden, a reception condition as distorting, in its way, as the Anglophone one, and as fully explicable by the institutional history.

## The Chilean hosts: a national tradition inside the regional school

The school was regional in frame but Chilean in much of its daily life, and the Chilean contribution had its own lineage. Aníbal Pinto, the commission economist already named, had written in the nineteen fifties a celebrated diagnosis of his own country as a case of frustrated development, in which an early start, an educated population, and a functioning democracy had failed to produce sustained growth, and the failure was laid at the door of the country's insertion and its internal structures. Pinto's Chile was the school's home case: the enclave copper economy, the inflation that structuralists read as structural, the reformist governments testing the limits of change within the order. When the exiled Brazilians arrived, they entered a Chilean conversation already two decades old, and the dependency theory that resulted was a fusion, Brazilian industrial experience and Chilean enclave experience, argued out in a city where both were present as lived knowledge.

The fusion explains the theory's characteristic double focus, on the factory and on the mine, on industrial dependence and on the export enclave, which single-country theories rarely sustain together. It also explains the school's political temperature. Chile's democracy, with its Marxist parties legal, its unions organised, and its elections consequential, made the questions of the seminar into questions of the republic in a way that Brazil's dictatorship, which had closed those questions by force, did not. The Brazilians brought the analysis of a development that excluded; the Chileans brought the experience of a democracy that strained against its structure; the Popular Unity experiment then subjected both inheritances to the same test, and the coup subjected both to the same destruction. The school's unity, such as it was, was the unity of that shared trajectory, and it is no accident that its members, scattered afterward, continued to identify one another as the people who had been in Santiago when the questions were alive.

## The centre's daily life: seminars, students, and mimeographs

Institutions think through routines, and the centre's routines can be reconstructed from the record its survivors left. There were seminars in which drafts were read and dismantled. There were working papers, mimeographed in small runs, that carried arguments months before journals could print them, so that the debate's true chronology runs ahead of its publication dates and the historian who works from journals alone misdates the formation of positions. There were students, Chilean and foreign, who carried the centre's questions into theses and then into posts across the region, a second transmission network beneath the famous names. And there was the city itself as a routine: the commission's library, among the best economics collections in the southern hemisphere, open to the university's scholars; the bookshops of the centre of Santiago stocked from Buenos Aires and Mexico City; the cafés where the argument continued after the seminar ended.

The routines matter because they set the speed of the school's thought. A position staked in a working paper in March could be answered in a seminar in April, revised in a journal by the following year, and absorbed into a book the year after, with the whole cycle conducted among people who met weekly. Compare the school's later rhythm in exile: a book answered after five years by a review in another country's journal, its author meanwhile moved to a new post and a new question. The deceleration is not a biographical detail. It is the mechanism by which a research programme becomes a set of positions, each defended at the moment of its author's isolation, none revised under the pressure that had made the originals strong. The dispersal thesis, stated at the level of daily routine, is simply this: the school thought at corridor speed, and after 1973 it thought at postal speed, and the difference is legible in everything it produced afterward.

## The survivors who stayed: protected enclaves under the dictatorship

Not everyone left. A portion of Chile's critical social science survived the dictatorship inside the country, in new private research centres founded from the mid nineteen seventies onward, of which CIEPLAN, the Corporation for Latin American Economic Research, founded in 1976, became the best known. These centres lived in a legal and financial penumbra, funded from abroad, watched by the regime, and staffed by economists and sociologists whom the purged universities had expelled. Their work kept a Chilean tradition of empirical social research alive through the dictatorship's long night, and their studies of the neoliberal experiment's social costs supplied the democratic opposition with its evidence. Osvaldo Sunkel, the commission and university economist who had moved between the school's worlds, was part of the generation that sustained this enclave scholarship and later, from within the commission, helped to formulate the new structuralism of the nineteen nineties, carrying the tradition's questions into the era of openness in a revised idiom.

The enclaves complicate the map in a productive way. The destruction of 1973 was real, but it was not total, and the tradition's Chilean thread did not snap; it thinned, privatised, and changed its questions from dependence to authoritarianism, poverty, and transition, the questions the enclave scholars could work on under the regime's eye. When the exiles returned after the dictatorship, they found colleagues who had never left and who had kept a different part of the inheritance: less the theory of dependence than the practice of empirical research under hostile conditions. The reassessment of the school in democratic Chile was a reunion of the two threads, the returned and the remained, and its literature bears the marks of both, the exiles' memory of the programme and the enclaves' memory of the long defence.

## The school's American translation: world-systems and the sociology syllabus

The dependency school's passage into the North American academy ran through a second translation, conceptual rather than linguistic. Immanuel Wallerstein, an American sociologist formed in African studies, took the school's centre and periphery vocabulary, Frank's insistence that development and underdevelopment were one process, and the long historical reach of the Latin American debates, and rebuilt them into world-systems analysis, launched with the first volume of his modern world-system history in 1974. Wallerstein's version globalised the frame, added the semiperiphery as a third zone, and relocated the enterprise from economics to historical sociology. It travelled superbly: a research centre at Binghamton, a journal, a school with students and conferences, the institutional apparatus the Santiago group had lost, rebuilt in New York State under another name.

The world-systems succession is the reception story's most instructive chapter, because it shows what the Anglophone academy could absorb. It could absorb the vocabulary and the sweep, detached from the Santiago school's statistical formation and its policy formation alike, and housed in sociology rather than in economics, where the school's claims would have faced the profession's econometric tribunal. The Latin Americans noticed the migration and commented on it with mixed feelings: their framework had conquered a province of the Northern academy by ceasing to be theirs, its mechanisms generalised into a theory of everything since the long sixteenth century, its connection to the region's balances and bottlenecks attenuated to a founding citation. Frank himself, in his later career, moved toward the world-system frame and beyond it, to a history of a single world system centred for most of its life on Asia, so that the school's most translated member ended furthest from the seminar room where the argument began.

## The textbook codification: how the sample became the school

Between the specialist literature and the student stands the textbook, and the textbook is where the dispersal's distortions were frozen into common knowledge. Development sociology and politics textbooks of the nineteen seventies and eighties, written in English from the translated sample, presented dependency theory as a doctrine with a founder, Frank, a proposition, that trade causes underdevelopment, and a fate, refutation by the East Asian cases and by its own vagueness. Each element of the codification is traceable to the sample's skew. The founder was the man who wrote in English. The proposition was the most portable of the school's claims, shorn of the mechanisms, dos Santos's channels, Marini's wage relation, Cardoso's configurations, that had made the original a programme rather than a posture. The fate was the verdict of critics who had, for the most part, read the same sample. Generations of Anglophone students thus learned to dismiss a school they had never met, and learned the dismissal as a fact about the school rather than as a fact about the channels through which the school had reached them.

The codification also fixed the school's periodisation in the Northern mind as a nineteen seventies episode, a fashion of the radical decade, safely past. In the region the same decades look different: the school's categories remained the working language of economic argument, its texts stayed in print and in syllabuses, and its questions resurfaced in each subsequent crisis, debt, adjustment, commodity boom, in the idiom of the moment. A theory can be dead in one reception and alive in another for half a century, and the divergence is not a paradox to be resolved but a phenomenon to be explained, by the only variables that differ between the two cases: the institutions that carried the texts, the languages they were read in, and the presence or absence of the collective that had made them. The textbook chapter and the Santiago seminar are two receptions of one school, and the distance between them is the size of the dispersal.

## The debt decade: the framework in the wilderness

The nineteen eighties subjected the region to the debt crisis and to structural adjustment, and the school's framework, though its institutions were gone, supplied the crisis's first interpretations. The crisis itself read like a dependency case study written by events: a periphery that had borrowed the centre's recycled surpluses in the nineteen seventies found, when interest rates rose in the centre, that its development was hostage to decisions of the Federal Reserve, and the decade's net transfer of resources ran from the indebted periphery to its creditors, a flow the commission's statisticians computed and published while the adjustment programmes proceeded. The school's surviving members and its regional heirs argued, in those years, that the crisis vindicated the structural diagnosis: economies inserted as the region's were would have their policy made in Washington and New York whatever their governments intended. Their opponents replied that the crisis indicted the protected, state heavy model the cepalino tradition had built, and that the school's framework was not the crisis's explanation but one of its causes.

The argument was never settled, because the conditions for settling it, a shared institution with shared data and authority, no longer existed, and because both sides could muster the decade in their support. What the wilderness years show for the formation story is the framework's persistence as the region's default grammar of complaint and analysis, surviving its school. Politicians who had never read dos Santos spoke of the external constraint; finance ministers formed against the tradition negotiated with the fund in its shadow; and the commission, revising itself toward its new structuralism, quietly reabsorbed elements of the radical critique, on technology, on the quality of insertion, on equity, that its own children had pressed from outside. Traditions that lose their institutions do not necessarily lose their questions. They lose the power to discipline the answers, and the debt decade's debates, brilliant and inconclusive, conducted in a dozen countries at once with no corridor to settle them, display exactly that condition.

## The returns: late syntheses and the school's memory

The end of the dictatorships brought the exiles home in stages, Brazilians after the amnesty and the long abertura, Chileans after the plebiscite that ended the Pinochet regime, and the returned generation set about the work of memory. Dos Santos, in his later years divided between Venezuela and a Brazil that readmitted him, wrote the tradition's history from inside, in surveys and memoirs that named the rooms and the arguments. Cardoso wrote his own accounts of the Santiago years from the eminence of a presidency and then of an elder statesman's retirement, and his version, urbane and self distancing, competes in the literature with the radicals' versions, in which he figures as the brilliant defector. Marini, who died in 1997 after a career spent largely in Mexico and a final return to Brazil, left his papers and his students to make his case posthumously, and the case was made: Brazilian and Mexican scholars re edited his works, reconstructed his itinerary, and installed him, in the region's reassessment, at the centre from which the Anglophone reception had omitted him.

The memory work had an institutional form as well. The regional council of social sciences, the network founded in the school's own decade, built a digital library that put the tradition's classics, Marini prominently among them, into open circulation in Spanish and Portuguese, undoing at last the scarcity on which the old reception had rested. University programmes in the region created chairs and research lines in the history of Latin American economic thought. The reassessment literature, of which Cristóbal Kay's critical history of Latin American theories of development and underdevelopment, published in 1989 by a Chilean scholar working in Europe, was an early monument, grew into a field of its own. A school that had been destroyed as an institution was rebuilt as an object of study, which is not the same thing, but which preserved its texts, its questions, and its claim on the region's students for the moment when its questions returned to the centre of events, as they reliably do.

## The word dependencia: a term's career as evidence

The career of the school's central word carries the formation story in miniature. Dependencia began as a term of the region's political vocabulary, the dependence of the republics on foreign powers, and was converted by the Santiago writers into an analytical concept: not a complaint about foreign influence but the name of a structure, with forms, mechanisms, and a history. The conversion was the school's collective work, and each of its principals left the word altered. Prebisch's generation had spoken of centres and peripheries and of the periphery's vulnerability; dos Santos gave dependence its structural definition and its periodisation; Cardoso made it a situation to be analysed historically rather than a fate to be denounced; Marini anchored it in the wage relation. The word's precision was a seminar product, maintained by usage among people who corrected one another's usage weekly.

After the dispersal the word escaped its custodians. In the Anglophone reception it became a loose synonym for the condition of poor countries, then a label for a discarded doctrine, its internal distinctions, between dependence as conditioning situation and dependence as stagnation machine, lost with the collective that had policed them. In the region's political rhetoric it swelled into a general indictment, applied to any unwelcome external fact, until its analytical edge dulled there too. The decolonial writers, inheriting a word worn smooth by both receptions, coined a successor, coloniality, partly to recover by renaming the precision the old word had lost. A conceptual history of dependencia, from complaint to concept to slogan to discarded label, would be a history of the school itself, told through the one artifact every member shared and no member could protect once the corridor was gone. Concepts, like theories, are kept by institutions or they are not kept at all.

## What the map cannot show

The institutional method has limits, and candour requires a section for them. The map explains the school's formation, its discipline, its destruction, and its reception. It does not explain why these particular minds produced these particular ideas, and it cannot grade the ideas' truth. Prebisch might have written his manifesto in any city the commission had chosen; the choice of Santiago coloured the work without causing it. The centre's seminar sharpened Cardoso's method, but the method's power is Cardoso's. An institutional history that claimed more would commit the mirror image of the error it corrects, replacing the myth of the placeless idea with the myth of the placeless institution, as though buildings thought. Buildings do not think. They gather, protect, endanger, and disperse the people who do, and the gathering and the dispersing are historical facts with consequences as real as any in the history of thought.

The map also cannot show the school's interior weather: the ambitions, friendships, and vanities that decided who argued with whom and who stopped speaking. The record hints at them, in memoirs and in the polemics' temperature, but a responsible formation story leaves the weather largely unrecorded rather than invent it. What can be said is that the school's arguments were conducted by men who expected to go on arguing, in the same rooms, indefinitely, and that the expectation itself was an intellectual condition. Positions staked for a continuing seminar are staked differently from positions staked for posterity: more provisionally, more precisely, with the reply already forming. The coup converted every provisional position of the Santiago years into a final one, frozen at the moment of dispersal, and posterity has been grading the frozen positions ever since, as though the seminar had adjourned by consent. It did not adjourn. It was dispersed, and the difference is the whole of this article's claim.

## Havana: where the manifesto was first read aloud

The commission's founding text had a birthplace of its own. Prebisch prepared his study of Latin America's economic development and its principal problems for the commission's second session, held in Havana in 1949, and the document, published by the United Nations the following year, became the school's charter before the school existed. Its arguments, that the periphery's deterioration was structural, that industrialisation was the necessary reply, that the state must plan what the market would not spontaneously build, were delivered as an official paper to a diplomatic conference, in a Caribbean capital, by an Argentine central banker turned international civil servant. The setting is worth the emphasis this article gives such settings. The doctrine that would be denounced, a generation later, as a subversive import was first read aloud, as a conference document, to the region's assembled governments, most of whom received it as the programme they had been waiting for someone to write down.

The Havana session also fixed the doctrine's original audience: not students, not parties, but states. Structuralism was born as advice to governments, and it retained the advisory posture, the survey, the recommendation, the training course, through all its later transformations. Even the research centre's Marxists, who broke with the advisory posture in content, retained it in form: their texts address an implied policymaker or revolutionary government, a reader who might act, rather than the seminar alone. The dependency theory's practical tense, its perpetual orientation toward what is to be done, descends from the conference table in Havana as surely as its statistics descend from the Santiago surveys. Doctrines carry their first audience inside them for life, and this one's first audience wore the insignia of twenty republics.

## The Cuban shock and the age of planning

The Cuban Revolution of 1959 altered the school's environment as profoundly as any internal development. It demonstrated that the hemisphere's order could break, it terrified the United States into the Alliance for Progress, and it made the commission's gradualism look, to a radicalising generation, like an anaesthetic. The Alliance, launched in 1961, adopted the forms of the cepalino programme, planning requirements, agrarian and tax reform, regional integration, while intending their opposite, the forestalling of revolution; and the commission found itself, awkwardly, the technical auxiliary of a counterinsurgent developmentalism, its planning methods conscripted to certify reforms its own analysis suggested could not succeed inside the structures they left untouched. The experience educated the younger generation in the uses of their own doctrine. A framework that could be spoken, with equal fluency, by a United Nations commission and by an American president's anti-communist initiative was a framework whose political content lay not in its techniques but in the structures it was willing to name. The radicals concluded that the naming was the doctrine, and that the commission, which would not name class and empire in its documents, had built an engine it could not steer.

The Cuban example also supplied the radicals with their standing question about agency. Cuba had broken with the world market's discipline, or had exchanged one discipline for another within the socialist camp, and either way it had acted, while the continent's reformist governments, armed with the commission's plans, had administered and stalled. The dependency theory's impatience with gradualism, its insistence that the structure would defeat any programme that left the structure intact, was the seminar's transcription of the contrast between Havana and the Alliance capitals. The contrast was available to anyone in the region, but it was in Santiago, where the commission's headquarters stood a few streets from the university where its critics taught, that the contrast acquired its theoretical form, as an argument between colleagues about the meaning of the decade they were sharing.

## Furtado: structuralism becomes history

Celso Furtado deserves a fuller place in the formation story than the passing mention he received earlier, because his work shows the commission's economics turning into the region's historical self knowledge. A Brazilian from Paraíba who took his doctorate in Paris and joined the commission in its first years, Furtado wrote the economic formation of Brazil, published in 1959, as a history of structures: the sugar cycle, the gold cycle, the coffee economy, each analysed as a system that organised land, labour, and the state around an export, and each bequeathing the next its constraints. The book taught a generation of Latin Americans to read their national histories as the commission read the region's present, structurally, and it remains the point where structuralist economics and the region's historiography merge. Furtado then practised what he theorised, directing the development agency for Brazil's impoverished northeast and serving as a planning minister in the government overthrown in 1964, after which exile took him to Yale and to Paris, where he wrote through the dictatorship years on the theory of underdevelopment his career had embodied.

His trajectory is the school's in one life: formation in the commission, application in the state, radicalisation by the state's defeat, and production in exile. It also marks the boundary between structuralism and the Marxist school proper. Furtado never became a Marxist of the centre's kind; his underdevelopment remained a historical formation to be overcome by a development the state could still, in principle, organise, and his later work wrestled with the cultural and political conditions of that overcoming rather than with the wage relation. The centre's theorists read him as the inheritance's limit case, the most powerful mind the commission's tradition produced and the proof, by his stopping point, of where that tradition stopped. The argument between the Furtadian inheritance and the dependency radicalisation was, like the argument with Frank, an argument inside one formation, and it is misread when the textbooks sort the participants into separate schools with separate shelves.

## The Laclau intervention: the school's argument conducted in English

One episode of the reception deserves separate treatment because it occurred in English, early, and at the highest level of the period's Marxist debate. In 1971 the Argentine theorist Ernesto Laclau, then working in Britain, published in the New Left Review a critique of Frank's account of Latin America, arguing that Frank had conflated capitalism with participation in markets, so that regions producing for export with coerced labour appeared in his account as capitalist from the conquest, dissolving the specificity of modes of production and with it the analytical purchase of Marxist history. The exchange that followed, Frank replying, others joining, became for the Anglophone left the canonical form of the dependency debate: a controversy about feudalism and capitalism in the sixteenth century, conducted in a London journal.

The episode's interest here is what it selected. The Anglophone debate took from the school its most abstract and least empirical question, the classification of colonial economies, and left in Spanish the school's working questions about the contemporary structure: the channels of the new dependence, the wage relation under super-exploitation, the configurations of dependent development. Laclau's critique was serious and, on its chosen ground, damaging, and Frank's position in the exchange was weakened further by his isolation from the corridor, where the periodisation he resisted had already been supplied by dos Santos in a form that answered much of the objection. The English debate thus graded a version of the school that the school itself had already revised, in another language, in an institution the debaters in London had no occasion to visit. No malice is required to explain the pattern. The journals, the translations, and the conference circuits of the Anglophone left simply did not run through the University of Chile, and a debate is always about the texts its participants can read.

## Decision on foreign capital: the framework's one statute

If the school had a legislative monument, it was the Andean Pact's Decision 24, the common code on foreign capital adopted in 1970 by the Andean countries, which sought to discipline multinational investment across the subregion: screening entry, limiting profit remittances, requiring the eventual transfer of foreign firms to national or mixed ownership, and regulating technology contracts and the royalties they carried. The code's intellectual parentage was mixed, commission structuralism and dependency reasoning together, and its career was brief and embattled, diluted by amendments, abandoned by Chile after the coup, and eventually dismantled in the era of openness. Its place in the formation story is as evidence of the framework's reach at its height: by 1970 the proposition that foreign capital required collective discipline, because the individual peripheral state bargained from weakness, had passed from seminar papers into a treaty instrument signed by governments, the only moment at which the school's wider tradition held something like statutory form.

The code's fate also foreshadows the tradition's. It depended on the solidarity of governments whose interests diverged, it was administered by states whose own classes were entangled with the capital it regulated, and it collapsed when the political order that sustained it collapsed, first in Chile and then across the region. The dependency theorists who had criticised the code from the left, as a reform that left the structure intact, and those who had drafted in its spirit, as the attainable maximum of the moment, were overtaken together by a counterrevolution that abolished the question. The statute's papers sit in the archive beside the centre's working papers, two kinds of document from the same Santiago moment, each recording a way the structure might have been governed, each preserved by institutions other than the ones that wrote them.

## Sunkel and the ecological extension

The school's longest-lived internal development came from one of its most institutionally mobile members. Osvaldo Sunkel, formed in the commission and in the University of Chile, spent a career crossing the map this article has drawn: commission economist, university scholar, survivor of the dictatorship in the enclave institutes, and finally a formulator, from within the commission, of the new structuralism of the nineteen nineties. Along the way he pressed an extension of the tradition that its founders had not envisaged, joining the analysis of dependence to the analysis of the environment: the periphery's insertion, he argued, was also an ecological relation, a pattern of resource extraction and environmental cost whose accounting belonged inside the economics of development rather than in an appendix. His edited work on development from within, published as the nineteen nineties began, gathered the tradition's survivors and heirs around a programme that kept the structuralist core, the quality of a country's insertion into the world economy, while adding the sustainability of the natural base on which insertion rested.

The ecological extension matters to the afterlife because it is the bridge between the Santiago school and the neo-extractivism debate described earlier. When the critics of the commodity boom governments analysed the export of nature as the region's renewed dependence, they were working a seam that Sunkel's generation had opened inside the tradition itself, between the economics of insertion and the materiality of what is inserted. The line from the commission's surveys to the ecological economics of the revival literature runs through that seam. Traditions survive by such extensions, which is why the school's history cannot be told as a rise and a fall with dates at either end. It is better told as this article has told it: a formation, a destruction, and a set of questions that kept finding new institutions, and new vocabularies, in which to be asked.

## The Brazilian roots beneath the Brazilian exiles

The Brazilians who arrived in Santiago carried a national tradition of their own, and the school's formation is incomplete without it. Brazil had produced, a generation earlier, its own structural reading of its formation in Caio Prado Júnior's history of 1942, which treated the colonial economy as an enterprise organised for export, whose meaning lay outside itself, in the markets it supplied, a formulation that anticipated the dependency intuition by two decades. At the University of São Paulo, the sociologist Florestan Fernandes and his seminar had built, in the nineteen fifties and sixties, a rigorous empirical sociology of Brazilian class and race, from which Cardoso emerged and against which he defined his historical method. The São Paulo seminar, like the Santiago centre after it, was destroyed by its dictatorship, its principals expelled from the university after 1964 and again in the hardening of 1968 and 1969, and its survivors founded CEBRAP, the independent centre that appears in the institutional map, to continue the work outside the university's walls.

The Brazilian precedent gave the Santiago school two gifts. It gave it men formed in a seminar culture as demanding as its own, so that the centre's standards were, from the start, the standards of two destroyed or endangered institutions rather than one. And it gave it the CEBRAP model in reserve: when Chile's turn came, the Brazilians knew that a research centre could survive a dictatorship as a private institute under foreign funding and self censorship, because they had built one. The Chilean enclave centres of the later nineteen seventies, CIEPLAN and its fellows, reinvented the model in Santiago, with the Brazilian example available in the memories of the exiles who passed between the two countries' catastrophes. The school's history is, at this level, a history of institutional techniques for surviving authoritarianism, transmitted by the displaced from one national disaster to the next, and the techniques outlasted the theory they were built to shelter.

## The agrarian silence and the school's boundary

A formation story should record what a school did not do, and the dependency school's relative silence was the countryside. The commission's structuralism had treated agriculture chiefly as a bottleneck, the inelastic food supply and the latifundio's drag on the market, and the radical school, formed in cities and in the analysis of industry and trade, inherited the urban tilt. The region's deepest Marxist agrarian tradition, the Peruvian line that runs from Mariátegui, belonged to an earlier generation and a different institution, the party and the journal rather than the commission and the centre, and the Santiago writers engaged it only intermittently. Land reform, when it came to Chile and Peru in the school's own years, was analysed by the school's members more as a condition of industrialisation than as a question in its own right, and the peasantry figured in the theory largely as a labour reserve and a market, rather than as a historical subject with its own formations.

The silence had consequences the afterlife inherited. When the region's rural movements rose in later decades, indigenous and landless movements whose questions were of land, territory, and communal organisation, the dependency framework had less to say to them than its prestige promised, and the decolonial turn's relocation of the analysis, toward race, territory, and the colonial constitution of power, can be read in part as the correction of the school's urban political economy by the countryside it had bracketed. The institutional explanation offers itself one final time: a school housed in a trade commission and an urban university centre, staffed by economists of industry and balances, saw the region from the city and the port. The map of its institutions is also the map of its blind spots, and the blind spots, like the insights, were formed in specific rooms.

## The party the theorists did not have

The Santiago school's relation to political organisation was oblique, and the obliquity shaped its work. The commission's economists were international civil servants, barred by their posts from party militancy. The centre's Marxists were academics in a country whose great Marxist parties, the Chilean Communists and Socialists, had their own intellectual apparatuses and their own lines, with which the seminar's conclusions frequently collided; the parties of the Chilean left were notably cooler toward the dependency theorists than the theorists' later fame suggests, finding their economics heterodox and their politics impatient. The Brazilians among them had been formed, several of them, in a small revolutionary organisation of the Brazilian left, Política Operária, whose clandestine discipline and theoretical ambition left marks on their writing, but the organisation had been destroyed by the dictatorship that exiled them, and in Santiago they worked as scholars without a party's shelter or a party's instructions.

The absence of a party had intellectual consequences in both directions. It freed the school from the discipline that elsewhere in the Marxist world subordinated scholarship to line, and the freedom shows in the school's internal plurality, the corridor's tolerance of positions that a party institute would have settled by vote. It also deprived the school of the party's protective functions: no organisation owned its defence when the coup came, no apparatus spirited its archive abroad, no publishing house of a movement kept its books in print as a duty. What saved the texts was the exile network of universities and the Mexican houses, assembled by personal loyalty rather than by organisational command. A theory's survival, the case suggests, is likelier when some institution regards the survival as its business. The Santiago school had many institutions and, in the decisive month, none that could act, which is the precise institutional meaning of its dispersal.

## The other seminar: Havana's great debate and the Santiago audience

The Santiago school formed in earshot of another argument, conducted in Havana in the middle nineteen sixties, over the economics of socialist transition: the great debate between the partisans of the budgetary finance system associated with Ernesto Guevara and the partisans of economic calculus associated with Carlos Rafael Rodríguez, with European Marxists, Charles Bettelheim and Ernest Mandel, intervening from abroad. The Cuban debate asked how a poor country that had broken with the market should organise production and incentives; the Santiago debate asked why a poor country inside the market could not develop by the market's rules. The two arguments were cousins, both refusing the consolation that the existing order's mechanisms were neutral, and the participants read one another across the Caribbean. The dependency theorists' Cuba was not only an example of rupture; it was a working seminar on the sequel to rupture, whose difficulties, the persistence of the law of value, the weight of the inherited structure, confirmed the Santiago school's premise that structures outlast proclamations.

The comparison also fixes the Chilean school's distinctiveness within the region's Marxism. Havana's debate was conducted by ministers and their advisers, inside a revolution in power, and its texts are state documents as much as scholarship. Santiago's was conducted by professors and commission economists, outside power until the Popular Unity years brought power near, and its texts are scholarship with a policy accent. The two styles of Marxist economics, the ministerial and the academic, met in the region's journals and diverged in their fates: the ministerial debate was settled by administrative decision and archived by the state it served, while the academic debate was settled by a coup and archived by exile. The tradition's memory holds both, and the student who reads only one has half the region's economic Marxism.

## The print map completed: the journals that carried the argument

The region's argument ran through journals with the regularity of a nervous system, and a reader reconstructing the school needs their geography. The commission acquired its own journal, the Revista de la CEPAL, in 1976, a late formalisation of a documentary culture that had previously spoken through surveys and conference papers. Mexico's Comercio Exterior, published by the foreign trade bank since the early nineteen fifties, gave the region's economists a monthly forum in which cepalinos, dependency theorists, and their critics met in print across the decades, its pages a running archive of the tradition's public form. Buenos Aires contributed Desarrollo Económico, founded in 1961 by the institute where Aldo Ferrer and other Argentine structuralists worked, proof that the formation was not Chile's alone: Argentina's economists, formed in their own country's early industrialisation and its frustrations, sustained a parallel structuralism whose journals argued with Santiago's in a shared language. The exile years added the diaspora's journals in Mexico, and the return years added the reassessment's.

The print map explains a rhythm in the school's life that the book record hides. Books were events, years apart; journals were the medium of the argument's ordinary time, and a position's career can be tracked through them issue by issue, staked, answered, qualified, abandoned, in a tempo closer to the seminar than to the treatise. It also explains the archive's uneven survival. Journal runs are heavy, unglamorous, and housed in few libraries; the dictatorships' bonfires and the universities' pulping destroyed them more thoroughly than they destroyed the famous books, and the historian of the school works, even now, with gaps where a journal's Chilean run should be. The Mexican runs, preserved by the exile's librarians, fill many of the gaps, which is why the tradition's documentary centre of gravity sits, to this day, partly in Mexico City. A school is its paper, and the paper's map is the map this article has been drawing all along, at the level of the single issue.

## Three receptions compared

The school's afterlife can be summarised as three receptions of one body of work, each determined by the institutions that mediated it. The regional reception, conducted in Spanish and Portuguese by scholars who inherited the archive and the questions, treated the school as a research programme to be corrected, extended, and argued with: its verdict, delivered over decades, is that the programme's core intuition was sound, several of its mechanisms were real, its strong stagnation claim failed, and its most original economist was the one the North had not read. The Anglophone reception, conducted from the translated sample through textbooks and survey chapters, treated the school as a doctrine to be classified: its verdict, delivered quickly and rarely revised, is that dependency was a rhetoric of the radical decade, interesting as politics and negligible as economics. The decolonial reception, conducted in the humanities across both languages, treated the school as an ancestor to be surpassed: its verdict is that the intuition was foundational and the economics a limitation, to be replaced by an analysis of coloniality in culture and knowledge.

The three verdicts cannot all be descriptions of the texts, since the texts are the same. They are descriptions of three mediations: of an archive inherited whole, of a sample delivered by translation and frozen by textbooks, and of a framework absorbed through its most portable intuition by a neighbouring discipline. The institutional method does not ask the reader to pick a verdict by loyalty. It asks the reader to notice that the verdicts track the mediations with embarrassing fidelity, and to draw the practical conclusion: judgements about the school formed without reference to its formation are judgements about a delivery system, mistaken for judgements about a body of thought. The recovery of the formation, which this article has attempted, is the precondition for grading the school itself, and the grading, when the strongest criticisms have been given their full force as they have been here, leaves a programme that was interrupted in the middle of its work, neither vindicated nor refuted, but stopped.

## The second generation: students as the school's longest corridor

A school's least visible institution is its classroom. The centre's scholars taught, at the University of Chile and in the region's graduate programmes, and their students carried the formation into ministries, universities, and movements across the continent, often without the teachers' names attached to the transmission. The commission's courses did the same work at a larger scale and an earlier stage. By the time the famous books were being argued over in translation, the school's categories had already been installed, by teaching, in the professional reflexes of two cohorts of Latin American economists and sociologists, for whom centre and periphery, the external constraint, and the suspicion of the export model were not doctrines adopted but the furniture of the discipline as they had learned it. Teaching is the slowest of the channels this article has surveyed and the hardest to destroy: a coup can close a centre in a morning, but it cannot easily uneducate the educated, and the dictatorships' own economists complained, in their memoirs, of cepalino common sense surviving in the ministries they inherited.

The students also explain the tradition's capacity for revival. Each return of the school's questions, in the debt crisis, in the commodity boom, in the quantification literature, was staffed substantially by scholars who had been taught by the school's survivors or by their students, in the region's universities and in the exile institutions. The chain of teaching is the one institutional thread that no coup fully cut, because it ran through persons rather than premises. When this article's claim describes the reception as an artefact of the dispersal, the students are the counterweight inside the claim: what the dispersal scattered, the classroom partially regathered, cohort by cohort, in the region, which is why the regional reception never lost the programme's shape as completely as the translated reception did. The corridor was destroyed. The corridor's graduates were not, and they rebuilt shorter corridors wherever they landed.

## The map and the territory: a closing account

The formation story is now complete, and its elements can be gathered. A United Nations commission in Santiago built the statistics, the framework, and the profession from which a theory of dependence could grow. A policy programme, import substitution, supplied the theory's occasion by failing in the theory's sight. The University of Chile's centre gathered the programme's radical graduates, Brazilians exiled by one dictatorship and Chileans formed in their own republic's argument, into a corridor where the theory was refined at seminar speed. A coup destroyed the centre, exiled its people, and delivered its work to posterity through the filters of exile publishing and staggered translation, which together manufactured the reception the English-speaking world mistook for the school. Successors, decolonial, extractivist, and quantitative, kept the questions alive by moving them into new institutions, as the school itself had once moved the commission's questions into the centre.

What the story finally shows is a general proposition about how bodies of thought live. They live in institutions or they live as fragments. The dependency school's ideas were strong enough to survive their institution's destruction as fragments, in textbooks that diminished them, in theologies that translated them, in successor programmes that renamed them, and in a regional memory that preserved their shape. But the programme, the cumulative, self correcting enterprise the founders envisaged, did not survive, because programmes are not ideas. They are ideas plus a corridor, a library, a journal, a cohort of students, and time, and of those components the coup left only the ideas and the students. The reader who wants to judge dependency theory should therefore judge it as one judges an interrupted proof: the steps on the page are real, some are wrong, the conclusion was never reached, and the interruption is part of the evidence. The institutional map, with which this article began, is the record of the interruption, and it belongs at the front of any future edition of the school, before the propositions, where this article has tried to put it.

## Prebisch at Geneva: the framework goes global and leaves its author behind

The commission's founder carried its framework onto the world stage in the nineteen sixties, and the episode earns its place here because it shows the doctrine escaping its author's control even before the radicals escaped with it. As the first secretary general of the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development, convened at Geneva in 1964, Prebisch became the spokesman of the developing world's collective diplomacy: the Group of 77, formed at that conference, adopted the centre and periphery diagnosis as the charter of its demands for commodity agreements, preferential access, and a reformed trading order. The campaign culminated a decade later in the United Nations' declaration of a New International Economic Order, the high water mark of the periphery's institutional offensive. The vocabulary of Santiago had become the vocabulary of a global coalition, spoken by diplomats who had never opened the commission's surveys.

The globalisation of the framework diluted it in the manner this article has tracked at every scale. What travelled to Geneva was the diagnosis and the demand, not the method: the statistical discipline, the bottleneck analysis, the insistence that structures be specified country by country. What returned from the diplomatic circuit to the region was a rhetoric of North and South in which the school's heirs could barely recognise their formation. Prebisch himself, in his final works, wrestled with the transformation of his own doctrine into a slogan, arguing for a synthesis of centre and periphery analysis with the domestic reforms, of income distribution and of the state's role, that the slogans omitted. The radicals read his late work as the confession of the framework's limits; the diplomats read it not at all. The founder's fate prefigured the school's: to be quoted everywhere and read, in the original, almost nowhere, a fate that institutions exist to prevent and that the destruction of institutions guarantees.

## A canon without a single book: the collective form of the school's authority

One formal feature of the school follows directly from its collective production and explains much of its later vulnerability: it never produced a single canonical synthesis, a Capital or a General Theory in which the programme stood complete between two covers. Its authority was distributed across a shelf: the commission's surveys, Prebisch's manifesto, Furtado's history, Cardoso and Faletto's situations, dos Santos's structure, Marini's dialectic, Frank's provocations. In the corridor, the distribution was a strength, each text correcting the others in real time. After the dispersal, it was a fatal weakness, because a distributed canon cannot be translated, taught, or defended as a unit. The Anglophone world, needing a single book to classify, chose the most available one and classified the shelf by it. The region, which possessed the shelf, never made the mistake, and the difference between the two receptions is partly a difference of shelving: a programme read whole against a programme read by its most portable volume.

The missing synthesis was not an accident of temperament. Syntheses are written in the quiet after a programme's battles, by survivors with a library and a pension, and the school's survivors had neither quiet nor libraries of their own. Dos Santos's late surveys came closest to the genre and read, appropriately, as histories of the tradition rather than as its system, the programme become its own archive. There is a lesson in the absence for readers of other schools: when a body of work presents itself as a shelf rather than a book, ask what institution held the shelf together, and what happened to it. The answer, in this case, is the whole of the present article.

## The Brazilian miracle: the evidence over which the school split

The quarrel over associated development was not conducted over abstractions. Its evidence was the Brazilian economic miracle, the burst of rapid industrial growth achieved under the military dictatorship in the years around 1970, in alliance with multinational capital and on the basis of a severely compressed wage structure. For Cardoso, the miracle demonstrated the revision's thesis: development, of a determinate and exclusionary kind, was proceeding inside dependence, and a theory that denied the possibility could only avert its eyes. For Marini, the same years demonstrated the opposite mechanism at work: growth powered by super-exploitation, the wage squeeze not an accident of the dictatorship's politics but the condition of the accumulation itself, with the domestic market's narrowness, its orientation to luxury durables for a minority, following from the wage structure as his book had argued. For dos Santos, the miracle exhibited the new dependence in its pure form, the dynamic sectors owned from abroad, the technology imported, the surplus remitted, growth and subordination as a single process. Three readings of one boom, produced by three men who had shared a corridor, each claiming the evidence the others cited.

The miracle's evidentiary role explains the quarrel's bitterness and its inconclusiveness. All three readings were, in their own terms, responsive to the facts; what divided them was the theory of development each brought to the facts, whether development named a process that dependence blocked, a process that dependence deformed, or a process that dependence powered by intensified exploitation. The seminar might have settled the question's terms over years of shared work on the Brazilian data, and the settlement, whichever way it fell, would have been the school's mature achievement. The coups in both countries made the settlement impossible: the evidence was in Brazil, the theorists were in Chile and then in Mexico, the data were published by a dictatorship that had expelled the analysts, and the argument proceeded by book and review across borders, at postal speed, until the participants' paths diverged into presidencies, retrospectives, and posthumous reputations. The unfinished quarrel is the school's central text, and it is unfinished for institutional reasons before it is unfinished for intellectual ones.

## Copper: the school's home commodity

Chile's copper gave the school a home case in which every abstraction acquired a proper name. The great mines of the north, Chuquicamata and El Teniente among them, were owned by American corporations, Anaconda and Kennecott, whose position in the Chilean economy, the source of the bulk of its export earnings and a large share of its fiscal revenue, made the enclave a daily fact of national politics rather than a theorist's construction. The commission's economists computed the transfers; the Chilean debate over the metal's ownership ran through every election of the school's decades; the Frei government's Chileanisation, the negotiated purchase of majority stakes, and then the Allende government's full nationalisation of 1971, approved unanimously by the Chilean congress, enacted in sequence the region's repertoire of answers to the enclave problem. The dependency theorists wrote about foreign capital with the copper accounts open on the desk, and their readers in Chile read them the same way.

Copper also taught the school's characteristic lesson about the limits of ownership. Nationalisation changed the enclave's legal form; it did not change the metal's market, its prices formed in London and New York, its technology, or the economy's dependence on its earnings, and the Popular Unity government's fate was bound to the copper price and to the credit blockade that accompanied its quarrel with the dispossessed companies. The theorists who survived the coup drew the moral in their different idioms: that dependence was a structure of markets, technologies, and finance of which ownership was only the most visible strand, so that a revolution in ownership, however just, left the structure's other strands intact to strangle the revolution at leisure. The lesson, learned from the home commodity, is the school's most durable bequest to the region's political economy, and it was available in full only to those who had watched the accounts from Santiago.

## The missions: the commission in the field

The commission's influence ran through a channel the book record understates: the advisory mission. At the request of governments, Santiago economists travelled to the region's capitals to help construct national accounts, draft development plans, design tax reforms, and staff new planning offices, and the missions carried the structuralist method into the machinery of states that had never heard a lecture on the terms of trade. The method travelled as technique: how to build an input table, how to project the balance of payments, how to identify the bottleneck in a sector's supply. Technique is doctrine in working clothes, and a generation of officials across the continent learned to see their economies through the categories the missions installed, the external constraint, the structural heterogeneity, the insufficiency of the export sector, without necessarily adopting the politics that later grew from the same categories.

The missions also fed the centre. The economists who staffed them returned to Santiago with the region's economies in their notebooks, the Guyanese bauxite contract, the Bolivian tin collapse, the Central American common market's quarrels, and the seminar's generality was built from that accumulated casework. When the dependency theorists later insisted that their structure had forms, that dependence in an enclave differed from dependence in an industrialising economy, they were drawing on the mission generation's comparative knowledge, the school's unwritten archive of how the region's economies actually differed. The archive was never published as such. It existed in the heads of a network, and the network's destruction dispersed it irrecoverably, which is one reason the school's later statements, written from exile libraries, feel thinner than its first: the casework had been a collective possession, and collectivities cannot emigrate as cheaply as books.

## The paperback continent: cheap editions and the school's reading public

The school's texts reached a public that the Anglophone academy never imagines for works of political economy, because Latin America's mid-century publishers had built a mass market for serious books in Spanish. The Fondo issued its inexpensive Breviarios, pocket volumes of scholarship priced for students; Siglo XXI built a catalogue in which the dependency canon sat beside the region's literature; university presses and the exile houses kept the titles in print through the dictatorship decades. A student in Lima or Córdoba or Mexico City could buy Cardoso, dos Santos, or the young Marini in a paperback at a kiosk price, and did, in quantities that made the school's authors public intellectuals in their home languages, argued over in newspapers and cafés by readers who were not specialists. The dependency debate was, in the region, a public event conducted in cheap editions.

The contrast with the school's Anglophone material existence is total. In English the same texts appeared, when they appeared, as expensive library hardcovers and journal articles behind subscription walls, the material form of a specialist literature. A theory's public is made by its editions as surely as by its arguments, and the two publics, the kiosk public of the region and the library public of the North, formed two different schools in their minds: in one, a living inheritance argued by taxi drivers and presidents; in the other, a syllabus item of the nineteen seventies. The dispersal thesis, followed into the bookshops, explains even this. The cheap editions existed because the region's institutions, its publishers, universities, and reading habits, had been built across the same decades as the school, and they survived the school's destruction in Mexico and in the region's book trade, carrying the texts to readers the seminar never met. Where the infrastructure survived, the school remained a public presence. Where it had never existed, the school remained a rumour.

## Mexico, the exiles' city, becomes the crisis's first capital

The debt crisis that closed the school's second decade began, fittingly, in the city that had sheltered its survivors. In August 1982 Mexico announced that it could not service its external debt, and the announcement opened the region's lost decade. The Mexican state that had given the exiles their chairs now stood as the exemplary debtor, its oil financed borrowing of the nineteen seventies revealed as the new dependence's financial form, and the arguments of the Santiago years returned to Mexican public life in the mouths of a new generation: that the crisis was structural rather than a liquidity accident, that the transfer of resources to the creditors was the system's ordinary operation under new management, that adjustment on the creditors' terms would reproduce the subordination it claimed to cure. The exiles who remained in Mexico, and the Mexicans they had taught, staffed both sides of their host country's argument, some advising the governments that negotiated with the fund, others supplying the opposition's economics.

The symmetry is more than a chronicler's pleasure. It demonstrates the tradition's deepest mode of survival: not as a doctrine preserved in a text but as a formation reproduced in persons, who carry it into whatever institution will house them and apply it to whatever crisis arrives. The school's members and students were, by 1982, distributed across the region's universities, its international organisations, its parties, and its exile journals, and the crisis was interpreted, in the first instance, through categories they had placed there. A research programme whose centre had been destroyed a decade earlier nonetheless supplied the first draft of the region's understanding of its greatest economic catastrophe, because its graduates were the region's economists. Institutions can be abolished in a morning. Formations take longer to kill, and the dictatorships, for all their thoroughness, had settled for the morning's work.

## The reprimarisation debate: the school's questions in a new century's trade

The afterlife's most recent chapter, described here as the body of work it is, arose from the region's trade with China in the first decades of the new century. As Chinese demand raised the prices of the region's commodities and Chinese manufactures entered its markets, a literature formed, much of it housed at the commission itself, asking whether the pattern reproduced the old structure in a new geography: exports of soy, iron, copper, and oil concentrated and unprocessed, imports of manufactures rising, industrial sectors in the larger economies contracting under the competition, a process the literature named reprimarisation. The debate's terms were the school's own, the composition of trade as destiny, the quality of insertion as the master variable, the fear that a commodity boom was a structure reproducing itself through prosperity, and its participants, cepalino economists and their critics, argued the case with the region's trade statistics in explicit descent from the surveys of the nineteen fifties.

The episode closes the formation story's circle. The commission that Prebisch built to study the region's insertion into an Atlantic world economy now studies its insertion into a Pacific one, with methods descended from his and questions recognisably his great grandchildren's. The dependency school's radical heirs watch the debate with the ambivalence of ancestors: the questions are theirs, the institution is the one that housed and survived them, and the answers are being written, as the school's answers were written, by economists sitting in Santiago with the balances open. Whether the reprimarisation literature becomes a revival of the school or merely its longest echo depends on the variable this article has isolated throughout: whether an institution gathers its authors into a programme, corrects them at corridor speed, and survives long enough for the corrections to accumulate. The questions have never lacked for authors. It is the corridor that history keeps destroying.

## Why Santiago: the comparative geography of a school's home

The centre's location was not foreordained, and the comparison with the region's other capitals sharpens what Santiago supplied. Buenos Aires possessed older universities, a larger publishing industry, and its own structuralist milieu around the journal Desarrollo Económico, but Argentine intellectual life was organised by the Peronist and anti-Peronist divide, and its universities suffered their own destruction early: in 1966, after the military coup of that year, police invaded the University of Buenos Aires in the episode remembered as the Night of the Long Batons, beating professors and students and driving hundreds of academics into exile or private life. Argentina's social science survived, brilliantly and precariously, in private institutes, but the country's capacity to host a regional school in a public university was broken seven years before Chile's. Mexico City possessed the publishers, the great university, and the asylum tradition, and it duly became the school's refuge; but Mexico's own intellectual establishment, housed in a one party state's institutions, offered hospitality more readily than it offered the polemical freedom of the Chilean seminar, and Mexico's Marxists worked inside a national tradition with its own masters and its own questions.

Santiago's advantage was a conjunction that lasted barely a decade: a United Nations commission with regional money and diplomatic cover, a public university with a research centre free to be radical, a legal and competitive Marxist politics that made the seminar's questions the republic's questions, and a small country's intimacy, in which the commission's director, the university's professors, the parties' intellectuals, and the government's economists encountered one another in a handful of buildings and cafés. Conjunctions of that kind are the true units of intellectual history, rarer and more fragile than geniuses, and the dependency school is unintelligible as the product of anything else. The same comparison explains why the school could not simply relocate when its home was destroyed. Mexico could shelter the persons. It could not reproduce the conjunction, and the programme, which was the conjunction's product, ended where the conjunction did.

## The rival formation: modernisation theory's institutions

The school's great antagonist had an institutional map of its own, and the symmetry instructs. Modernisation theory, the doctrine that underdevelopment was a stage to be passed through with capital, technology, and the right values, was housed in the postwar American research university at its most confident: in centres at Harvard and MIT where economists and sociologists of development worked with foundation money and government contracts, and in the person of Walt Whitman Rostow, whose Stages of Economic Growth of 1960 announced itself as a non-communist manifesto and whose author carried the doctrine into the White House as an adviser through the Vietnam years. The antagonist's texts were written in English, published by university presses with global distribution, and taught wherever American influence built a syllabus. Its institutional base, unlike the Santiago school's, was never destroyed, because it was the intellectual apparatus of the hemisphere's dominant power rather than of a small republic's university.

Set the two maps side by side and the era's famous debate changes character. It was not a contest between two theories conducted on equal terms in a republic of letters. It was a contest between a doctrine with the American state's logistics behind it and a doctrine with a corridor in Santiago behind it, and the wonder is not that the first prevailed in the world's syllabuses but that the second survived at all, in its region, in its languages, through the destruction of its home. The survival had causes the map displays: the commission's diplomatic immortality, the Mexican publishers, the church's networks, the classroom chain. But the comparison should discipline any triumphalism in the telling. The school survived as a regional inheritance and a set of questions. The antagonist governed the world's development practice for a generation. Institutional history is not a consolation genre; it records what power does to thought, in both directions, and the record in this case is that the corridor thought better and the apparatus ruled longer.

## The enclave generation governs: the tradition inside the democratic transitions

When the dictatorships ended, the tradition's survivors and students entered the democratic governments in numbers that surprised the obituarists. In Chile, the economists of CIEPLAN, the enclave institute that had kept critical research alive under the regime, supplied the first democratic government's economic leadership, with the institute's founder Alejandro Foxley serving as finance minister in the administration that took office in 1990. The government they ran was not a dependency government; it kept the open economy the dictatorship had built and spent its proceeds on the social debt, a settlement its critics on the left called the administration of the model and its defenders called the only transition on offer. But the men who ran it had been formed in the tradition's institutions and argued in its categories, about the quality of insertion, the external constraint, and the social accounts, even as they governed against its conclusions. The school's afterlife inside the Chilean state was thus neither victory nor extinction but something harder to name: the formation of the governors by the tradition they had ceased to profess.

Brazil completed the pattern at the century's end. Cardoso, the seminar's subtlest mind, governed for two terms as a market reformer, privatising and stabilising, while his former comrades of the corridor, dos Santos among them, wrote the critiques of his government in the vocabulary they had built together in Santiago. The spectacle divided the tradition's memory of itself, as the quarrel section of this article recorded, but for the institutional historian it carries a plainer meaning. The school had been, among other things, the most effective training programme for Latin American statecraft of its generation, and its graduates governed the region's democracies in its aftermath, on both sides of every argument, because no rival formation had trained their equals. A theory can lose every battle for its conclusions and still win the battle for the personnel, and the personnel, in the end, are a large part of what a school is.

## The numbers that are not in this article

A word is owed about this article's own refusals, because they follow the school's history rather than any squeamishness. The text has given no figure for the movement of the terms of trade, none for industrial output under substitution, and none for the counts of the exiled, the imprisoned, and the dead after the coups. The school's own archive explains the discipline. Its founding claim was a number, and the number was fought over for decades by scholars who recomputed the series with different endpoints, different baskets, and different deflators, until the fight itself became the finding: that the periphery's statistical inheritance was too fragile to bear the weight the doctrine laid on it, and that a claim about a structure had been staked, by friend and foe alike, on the behaviour of an index. Its closing catastrophe was also a number, the count of the dictatorship's victims, and that count was fought over by regimes that minimised and movements that maximised, until the official commissions of the democratic years fixed floors that politics still contests. A formation history that sprinkled such figures through its pages would be repeating the school's founding error, the confusion of a structure with its most quotable measurement.

The discipline has a positive side. What survives the removal of the contested numbers is the part of the school's work that this article has argued was always its substance: the mechanisms, specified channel by channel; the configurations, described country by country; the institutions, mapped building by building. Those elements can be examined without an index and judged without a body count, and they are what the school's best practitioners, in the corridor, actually argued about when the seminars were at their best. The numbers will be fought over elsewhere, in the literatures that own them. The map is this article's contribution, and the map needs no figures to be checked: only the buildings, the dates, the names, and the roads the exiles took, all of which the record settles.

## The Portuguese circuit: the school's second language

The tradition lived in two languages, and its Portuguese life had a geography distinct from its Spanish one. Brazil's size made it a world to itself: its universities, its journals, its publishing houses in São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro sustained a dependency debate in Portuguese that ran parallel to the Spanish circuit, connected by the exiles who moved between them and by translations that lagged in both directions. Marini's work, written largely in Spanish during his Mexican and Chilean years, returned to Brazil as a rediscovery, its author repatriated by editors and students after his death into the canon of a country that had expelled him at twenty nine. The Brazilian reassessment of the school, conducted from the nineteen nineties in the universities that readmitted the exiles' generation and hired their students, became the tradition's most institutionally secure province, with research groups, collected editions, and a scholarly literature on the school's history that the Spanish circuit now reads in translation, the direction of the old flow reversed.

The two language circuits explain a final feature of the reception. The Anglophone world imagines a single Latin American conversation and wonders at its incoherence; the reality was two conversations, overlapping, mutually translating with delays of years, diverging in their judgements as their national experiences diverged, Brazil's miracle against Chile's experiment, Brazil's negotiated transition against Chile's plebiscitary one. The school's unity was always partly a retrospective courtesy. What held it together in life was not a language or a doctrine but the corridor and the network, the physical circulation of persons among Santiago, Mexico City, and São Paulo, and when the circulation stopped, the two circuits drifted into separate traditions that share a shelf of ancestors. The historian who reads only one circuit will misjudge the school's shape as surely as the historian who reads only its English sample, and the remedy is the same: follow the persons, the editions, and the institutions, in both languages, before grading the ideas.

## The open problems: what an interrupted programme bequeathed

An interrupted programme leaves a distinctive estate: not a set of answers but a set of problems whose formulation was its achievement, awaiting workers with the data and the institutions to finish them. The school's estate can be inventoried. The measurement of super-exploitation, the conversion of Marini's mechanism into series on wages, working time, and the reproduction of labour power that national accounts could sustain, was begun in the corridor and never completed. The specification of dependence's financial form, the analysis of a subordination worked through debt, portfolio flows, and the currency hierarchy rather than through trade and direct investment, was sketched by the debt crisis's interpreters and remains the tradition's largest unfinished construction. The historical programme, Cardoso's situations extended into a comparative archive of dependent formations, exists as exemplars rather than as the cumulative record its method promised. And the ecological extension, the accounting of nature's transfer alongside value's, has passed to the revival literature as the estate's most active file.

The inventory matters because it converts the article's elegy into an agenda. The problems are well formed; several are now tractable with data the founders lacked; and the literatures working on them, the quantification revival above all, are the school's continuation by other means, whether or not they fly its flag. What they lack, as the school's survivors lacked after 1973, is the corridor: a single institution where the mechanisms' specialists, the historians of the situations, and the statisticians of the transfers correct one another weekly. Should such an institution form, in the region or elsewhere, the interrupted programme could resume, and the reception this article has described, the school as a shelf of rival positions, would give way to the thing the shelf was always the debris of. The map ends, therefore, not at the destruction but at the vacancy: a set of rooms, described in these pages, that the tradition's questions are still waiting to reoccupy.

## The bridge from the North: Baran, Sweezy, and the Monthly Review circle

The Santiago school had one significant connection to the North that ran in its favour, and the connection's history refines the reception story. Paul Baran, a Russian born economist at Stanford, had argued in his Political Economy of Growth of 1957 that the economic surplus of poor countries was drained by the operations of monopoly capital and its local allies, and with Paul Sweezy he extended the analysis in Monopoly Capital in 1966. The Monthly Review, the independent socialist journal Sweezy co edited in New York, became the school's principal English language door: it published Frank's development of underdevelopment essay, reviewed the Latin Americans, and gave the tradition a North American readership formed in its own heterodox economics rather than in the development establishment's. Frank, who moved in the Monthly Review's orbit, was the human form of the bridge, which is another reason his version of the school became the North's version: the door admitted what resembled the house.

The bridge carried traffic in both directions, and the southbound flow mattered to the formation. Baran's surplus gave the Latin Americans a category that joined their trade analysis to a theory of monopoly; the Soviet and interwar debates on imperialism reached Santiago partly through the Monthly Review's translations and selections; and the Cuban Revolution's economics were reported to the region, admiringly and in detail, by the same journal's correspondents. The school's Marxism was therefore never a simple import from Europe. It was assembled in a triangular trade among European classics, North American heterodoxy, and the commission's statistics, with Santiago as the port where the three cargoes were unloaded into one warehouse. The coup closed the port. The bridge's northern end survived, and the Monthly Review's version of the tradition, Frank centred and surplus focused, continued to circulate in English long after the warehouse's fuller inventory had been scattered, one more filter in the long manufacture of the Anglophone school.

## What they read: the seminar's library as a formation

A school's reading is part of its institutional equipment, and the Santiago group's library can be reconstructed from its citations. Marx was read in the Spanish Capital issued in Mexico in Wenceslao Roces's translation, the edition through which the Spanish speaking world's Marxism largely passed, so that the school's Marx arrived with a Mexican imprint and a Spanish Republican exile's renderings. Lenin's imperialism pamphlet and the Russian debates stood behind the analysis of foreign capital. The commission's own surveys and the region's histories, Furtado's Brazil, Pinto's Chile, Caio Prado's colonial formation, supplied the empirical shelf. Baran and the Monthly Review supplied the bridge described above. And the group read one another, in draft, which no library catalogue records and which mattered most.

The library's composition explains the school's characteristic emphases and absences. It was strong on the economics of imperialism and on national economic history, weaker on the European Marxist philosophy that the Western Marxism of the same decades was elaborating in Frankfurt and Paris, a tradition with which the Santiago school had almost no commerce. The two Marxisms of the postwar world, the Latin American political economy and the European critical theory, formed in ignorance of one another, in different institutions, under different catastrophes, and their later encounters, in the world's syllabuses, have the awkwardness of relatives introduced as strangers. The series to which this article belongs treats the European line elsewhere; the formation point here is that the dependency school's celebrated concreteness, its balances and mechanisms, and its equally celebrated thinness on culture, ideology, and the state, are the marks of its library. Shelves, like corridors, are causes.

## Two late careers: the systematiser and the restless founder

The school's afterlife can be read in the two careers that bracket its range. Dos Santos, the corridor's systematiser, spent his exile decades doing what the dispersal had made necessary: restating the programme as a whole, in surveys of the dependency theory's history and in a late work on the world economy's transformation, where he argued that the globalisation of production had carried the new dependence to a planetary scale and required the theory's renewal rather than its abandonment. He worked in Venezuela's Central University and its centre for development studies, returned to Brazil as the dictatorship receded, and ended as the tradition's patriarch, interviewed, honoured, and cited as the living memory of the Santiago years. His late career performed the office of the missing institution in one person: he was the corridor's survivor as archive, and his retrospectives, for all their value, show the cost of the substitution, a programme's memory filtered through one participant's loyalties and wounds.

Frank, the corridor's provocateur, travelled the opposite road, away from the programme altogether. From the world-systems collaboration he moved, in his final decades, to a vast revision of world history itself, arguing in his book ReOrient of 1998 that Asia, not Europe, had stood at the centre of the world economy until the nineteenth century, and that the school's old categories, centre, periphery, dependence, required a five thousand year frame to be understood. The Latin Americanists received the late work with respect for its ambition and reserve about its relation to their evidence; the seminar's most famous member had become a world historian for whom Chile was an early episode. Between the patriarch who stayed with the programme and the founder who outgrew it, the school's other members distributed themselves, in presidencies, ministries, chairs, and memoirs, and the distribution is the dispersal's final form: a faculty scattered not only across countries but across the possible relations a thinker can bear to his own youth.

## The word entreguismo: the school's vocabulary in the street

The tradition's penetration of the region's political language can be measured by a single Brazilian word: entreguismo, the disposition to hand over the nation's patrimony to foreign interests, a term of abuse forged in the campaigns over oil and minerals in the nineteen fifties and generalised, in the school's decades, into the standing charge against any government that opened, privatised, or conceded. The word is the dependency framework compressed into an insult, structure and all: it presumes that the national economy has a patrimony, that foreign capital's entry is a transfer rather than an investment, and that the domestic agent of the transfer is a traitor to a collective interest that exists prior to the market. Politicians who had read nothing of Marini campaigned in his categories, because the categories had passed, through the cheap editions, the journals, and the classroom chain, into the region's political common sense.

The passage from analysis to slogan is the fate the school's critics predicted for it and its founders feared, and the street career of its vocabulary should be recorded with the same neutrality as its seminar career. A framework that becomes common sense gains a public and loses its precision, and the region's later debates were full of dependency's conclusions deployed without its mechanisms, the structure invoked as a curse rather than specified as a set of channels. The founders' own texts, read carefully, resist the slogans made from them; but founders do not control their receptions, which is, for the last time in this article, an institutional fact before it is a biographical one. What controls a reception is the presence of institutions that keep the careful version in circulation and in authority, the seminar, the journal, the school. In their absence the slogan and the textbook divide the estate between them, and the careful version waits in the archive for its next institution, as the dependency school's careful version waited, in Mexican libraries and Brazilian research groups, for the reassessment that this article has drawn upon.

## The region builds its own house: SELA and the institutional diaspora

The school's decades coincided with the region's one sustained attempt to build economic institutions of its own at the diplomatic level, and the attempt belongs to the map. The Latin American Economic System, SELA, founded in 1975 with its seat in Caracas, was created as the region's own forum for economic coordination and collective negotiation with the United States and Europe, an institutional child of the same impulse that had created the commission a generation earlier and of the New International Economic Order campaign that Prebisch's Geneva years had launched. Its staff and its consultancies drew on the cepalino network and on the exile generation, and its existence through the debt decade gave the region's heterodox economics a diplomatic address at the moment when its academic addresses were being closed. The body's practical achievements were modest, as its historians concede, but it testifies to the network's reach: the men and women formed in Santiago's institutions spent the dictatorship decades distributed across every regional body that would employ them, keeping the tradition's categories in official circulation while its books were burned in the country of its birth.

The diaspora of the network into the international organisations had a subtler consequence for the school's ideas. Inside the commissions and secretariats, the tradition's claims were translated into the dialect of official economics, hedged, quantified, and stripped of their Marxist signatures, until the structuralist core of the school's thought, the insistence that insertion determines possibility, circulated in documents whose authors would have disowned the school's name. Ideas survive their institutions by such launderings as well as by preservation in archives, and the dependency tradition's full afterlife includes this anonymous stratum: the official common sense of a region's worth of economic officials, formed by the network, applying its grammar, and innocent of its bibliography. The historian who counts only citations will miss it. The historian who follows the personnel, as this article has tried to do, finds the school still at work in the one place its founders always intended it to work, inside the region's machinery for thinking about itself.

## How the school should now be read: an order for the returning reader

The formation story implies a reading order, and stating it is a fitting close to the analytical sections. Begin with the commission, with Prebisch's manifesto and one of the early surveys, to acquire the questions in their original statistical form. Pass to Furtado's history of Brazil for the method turned into narrative, and to Pinto for the home case of frustrated development. Enter the corridor next: dos Santos's structure of dependence for the programme's systematic statement, Cardoso and Faletto for its historical method, Marini, in Spanish or in the fullest translation available, for its strongest mechanism, and Frank last among the principals, read as the corridor's provocation rather than its summary, with Laclau's critique beside him as the record of how the provocation fared abroad. Close with the afterlife: Quijano for the succession, the neo-extractivism debate for the recurrence, and the quantification literature for the programme's unfinished empirical business. Read in that order, the school appears as it was, a programme forming, arguing, and breaking off, and the reader is equipped to grade it, severely where severity is earned, without mistaking the delivery system for the thought.

The order also reverses the sequence in which the Anglophone world received the school, and the reversal is the point. Frank first and the commission never: that was the dispersal's syllabus, and a generation's judgement was formed on it. The archive now permits the true order to anyone willing to read across languages or to wait for the translators, whose work, proceeding title by title through the region's reassessment editions, continues to undo the dispersal's effects. Every newly translated text rearranges the school's reputation a little, as the belated arrival of Cardoso and Faletto rearranged it in 1979, and the process is not finished. The school's history is still being written by its editors, which is the final proof of this article's claim: a body of work whose reception is still being determined by the logistics of its transmission was never, at any point, received on its merits alone.

## The school's own diagnosis of its reception

The dispersal thesis of this article was stated, in embryo, by one of the school's principals within four years of the coup. In 1977 Fernando Henrique Cardoso published, in the Latin American Research Review, an essay on the consumption of dependency theory in the United States, in which he protested, with some asperity, at what the North American academy had made of the tradition: a formal theory of dependence, attributed to authors who had written historical analyses, formalised into propositions its supposed authors did not recognise, and then criticised for the formalisation's failures. The consumption he described, the conversion of situated analyses into a portable doctrine and the grading of the doctrine in place of the analyses, is the artefact this article has documented from the institutional side. Cardoso located the distortion in the academy's habits; the institutional account adds the mechanism, that the situated analyses were mostly unavailable in English, in their settings, at the time the formalisation was performed.

The essay's fate completes the demonstration. Published in English, in the North's own journal of Latin American studies, by the school's most sophisticated surviving voice, it was duly cited and duly ignored: the textbook codification proceeded through the following decade as though no correction had been entered. A correction, like a theory, requires an institution to keep it in force, a syllabus committee, a journal's standing controversy, a school. The corridor could have kept it in force, by teaching it, citing it, and making its point the price of entry to the debate. In the corridor's absence the correction became one more item in the literature, available to the conscientious and invisible to the codifiers. The reception of the school's self diagnosis thus replicated the reception of the school, which is the strongest evidence this article can offer that the phenomenon under study is real and structural, not a complaint about reviewers.

## The state steps forward: O'Donnell and the political science succession

The school's political science succession repaired its most obvious omission, the state, and did so from inside the same institutional world. Guillermo O'Donnell, an Argentine political scientist formed in Buenos Aires's milieu of private institutes, published in 1973 his study of modernisation and bureaucratic authoritarianism, which took the Southern Cone coups, Brazil's of 1964 and Argentina's of 1966, as its problem: why the region's most modern economies had produced its most systematic military regimes. The argument joined the dependency tradition's structuralism to a theory of the state and of the technocratic alliances that staffed the coups, and it founded a research programme, on authoritarianism and then on transitions, that dominated Latin American political science for two decades. O'Donnell's own institutional base, the Centre for State and Society Studies in Buenos Aires, founded in the mid nineteen seventies, was the enclave form again, critical scholarship housed privately under a dictatorship, and his later career, at universities in Brazil and the United States, traced the exile network's northern extension.

The succession matters here because it shows the tradition reproducing its method in a neighbouring discipline after its own centre was gone. The bureaucratic authoritarianism programme had the dependency school's signature: a structural account, historically specified, of the region's insertion and its domestic alliances, built by scholars in enclave institutions under authoritarian conditions, and received abroad, in translation and in summary, as a set of propositions detached from the institutes that made them. Its debates, over whether the coups expressed the needs of dependent accumulation or the fears of threatened elites, were the corridor's arguments continued by other means in other rooms. The school's deepest legacy may be this replicability of its form: wherever the region's scholars gathered under pressure, in enclaves, in exile, in the returned democracies, they rebuilt the same intellectual machine, a structural history of the region's insertion, staffed by the displaced, and aimed at the catastrophe in progress.

## The home country's reassessment: Chile reads its school again

The reassessment reached the school's home country last and most painfully, because in Chile the tradition's history was entangled with the national catastrophe in ways that scholarship could not separate from mourning and accusation. The rebuilt universities of the democratic era restored the closed disciplines, refounded the research centres, and gradually admitted the pre coup decades as an object of study rather than as a wound or a warning. Chilean historians and sociologists reconstructed the University of Chile's destroyed world from its survivors' testimony and its surviving papers; the commission's historians wrote the institution's own history; and the dependency school's Chilean members and students, Sunkel's generation and its heirs, took their places in the national account as the republic's economists rather than as the dictatorship's exiles. The process was slower than Mexico's preservation and Brazil's canonisation, because the Chilean reassessment had to pass through the memory politics of the transition, in which the years before the coup were long handled, by all sides, as prologue to the catastrophe rather than as a world with its own substance.

The delay had one scholarly benefit. The Chilean reassessment, arriving after the region's, could read the school whole, in both its languages and all its receptions, and its literature has the corresponding quality of a verdict delivered after the evidence closed. That verdict, in its broadest form, confirms this article's claim from the home side: the school is now studied in Chile as an institution and a moment, its texts edited with their settings restored, its members' biographies written, its seminar reconstructed, in short, as a formation rather than as a doctrine. The country that destroyed the centre has become the country that curates it, and the curation, like the destruction, is a political act performed by institutions, the universities and museums and commissions of a democracy deciding what its past contains. The school's Chilean afterlife is therefore in the keeping of the same kind of bodies that made it, which would not have surprised its founders, who never believed that ideas kept themselves.

## The land studies: the school's empirical conscience on the countryside

The agrarian silence recorded earlier had one large exception, and the exception was characteristically institutional. In the nineteen sixties the Inter-American Committee for Agricultural Development, working with the region's governments and the international agencies, conducted land tenure studies across the continent, country by country, documenting the concentration of land, the insecurity of tenants, and the stagnation of the latifundio with a statistical thoroughness the commission's own surveys had pioneered. The studies' director, the Canadian economist Solon Barraclough, became a figure of the Santiago world, and the land programme's findings fed the era's agrarian reform legislation, including Chile's, with the region's most authoritative account of its rural structure. The dependency school's members used the land data as they used the trade series, as the empirical floor of arguments about the internal allies of dependence, since the landholding oligarchy was, in their account, the domestic partner of the export structure.

The episode deserves its place because it complicates the school's portrait in a useful direction. The formation's empirical conscience was real but it was housed in programmes, the land studies, the surveys, the missions, that belonged to the official institutions the radical theorists criticised, and the traffic between the official empiricism and the radical theory ran through persons who moved between the buildings. When the coups destroyed the arrangement, the empiricism and the theory were separated again, the data programmes continuing under international auspices in muted form, the theory continuing in exile without its data. The school's posthumous reputation for assertion over evidence is, in part, the reputation of that separation, and the formation story restores what the reputation forgets: that in the Santiago decade, the men who theorised the structure of dependence worked streets away from the men who measured the region's land, and read their tables.

## The indigenous question: the school's second silence

The school's second silence was the indigenous peoples of the continent, and it was deeper than the agrarian one. The commission's framework treated the region's populations as factors and classes; the radical school, for all its talk of internal structures, analysed the Indian majority of the Andean and Mesoamerican countries principally as peasants, a labour reserve, and a market, categories that dissolved the specificity of peoples whose subordination was older than capitalism and organised through the racial classifications of the colonial order. The Peruvian tradition had known better: Mariátegui had placed the Indian and the land at the centre two generations earlier, from a party and journal formation outside the commission's world. The Santiago school, formed in trade and industry, largely did not follow him there, and the omission marked its analyses of the very countries, Bolivia, Peru, Guatemala, Ecuador, where the indigenous question was the national question.

The decolonial turn's founding gesture can be read as the return of this repressed. Quijano, who had worked inside the region's Marxist debates and knew the school's formation from within, built the coloniality concept precisely on the ground the school had bracketed: the racial classification of labour as the Americas' original and continuing structure. The succession thus appears, from the formation side, as a correction administered by the tradition to itself, across a generational interval, in new institutions. It also explains the decolonial literature's tone toward the dependency school, filial and exasperated at once, the tone of heirs who found the house well built and one wing missing. A formation story that recorded only the school's insights would flatter it; its silences were as institutional as its insights, the blind spots of a trade commission's economists and an urban seminar, and the afterlife's corrections were aimed at the rooms as much as at the texts.

## The numbers wars resumed: the long econometric afterlife of the founding claim

The school's founding empirical claim enjoyed an afterlife of its own in the econometric literature, and its fortunes there complete the evidence story. In 1988 two economists at the World Bank, Enzo Grilli and Maw Cheng Yang, published a long index of primary commodity prices reaching back to the start of the century, constructed to settle the question the commission had raised, and their series showed the long decline in the periphery's terms of trade that Prebisch had asserted, at a rate the subsequent literature then disputed, refined, and disputed again, through arguments over trends, breaks, and the composition of the basket that continue in the journals. The episode is the school's empiricism vindicated in form whatever the verdicts: the claim had been framed, by a United Nations commission in 1949, in a shape that the profession's most technical practitioners could still be profitably testing four decades later, with the argument conducted, as the founders would have wished, over the construction of the series itself.

The contrast with the radical school's other claims is instructive and should be drawn plainly. The propositions that entered the econometric afterlife were the ones the commission had stated as measurements. The propositions stated as structures entered the political and historical literatures instead, where they were judged by other standards and survive in other forms. A reader grading the tradition should therefore grade it in two currencies, and refuse the exchange rate, offered by both its detractors and its devotees, that converts all of its claims into one. The founders, who worked in a building where the surveys and the manifestos were written on different floors, understood the two currencies and their different futures. The dispersal, which mixed the floors' products into a single translated sample, is responsible for much of the confusion since, and the distinction, restored here at the end of the analytical sections, is among the most useful the formation story yields.

## The university reform: the centre's precondition inside its own walls

The research centre did not float free of its university's politics, and its existence is partly explained by the Chilean university reform of the late nineteen sixties. Beginning in 1967 at the Catholic University and spreading to the University of Chile, the reform movement, driven by students and younger faculty, democratised university government, expanded research as a university mission, and multiplied the institutes and centres in which new disciplines could organise themselves. CESO, founded on the reform's eve, was a creature of that expansion: an interdisciplinary unit of a kind the old faculty structure would not have housed, staffed by scholars whose appointments the reform's politics made possible, and enrolling the students whose radicalisation supplied its audience and its junior ranks. The dependency school's academic form, the centre rather than the chair, the seminar rather than the lecture, the working paper rather than the treatise, was the reform's form, and the school's history inside the university tracks the reform's history exactly, expansion, politicisation, and then destruction by the same coup that abolished the reform and restored the university to appointed authority.

The reform connection also explains the centre's exposure. An older, sleepier institute might have survived the dictatorship by insignificance, as some units did, purged of a few names and left to dwindle. The centre was instead identified, correctly, as a stronghold of the world the coup had come to abolish: the reform university, the Marxist seminar, the alliance of scholarship with the defeated political project. Its destruction was therefore not collateral but programmatic, an early exhibit in the regime's demonstration that the Chile of the reform would be unbuilt institution by institution. The school's fate and the reform's fate are one story, and the historian who separates the history of the theory from the history of the university that housed it will understand neither.

## The cultural twin: the Boom and the shared infrastructure of Spanish American letters

The school's formation had a cultural twin whose parallel career confirms the institutional argument from an unexpected quarter. The Latin American literary Boom of the nineteen sixties, the generation of García Márquez, Vargas Llosa, Cortázar, and Fuentes, was produced by an infrastructure strikingly similar to the school's: a handful of publishing houses in Mexico City, Buenos Aires, and Barcelona; a Havana institution, the Casa de las Américas, founded in 1959, whose prizes and journal organised a continental republic of letters; a Barcelona agent, Carmen Balcells, whose agency from 1960 professionalised the writers' relation to the world market; and a circuit of exile and expatriation that moved the authors among the same capitals the economists travelled. The Boom's writers and the dependency theorists were, in several cases, acquaintances in the same cities, published by the same houses, and read by the same students; the region experienced them as one event, the assertion that Latin America could produce the world's theory and the world's literature in the same decade.

The twin's fate diverged at the point of institutional protection, and the divergence is the lesson. Literature's institutions were commercial and dispersed across three continents, and no coup could abolish them; the Boom's canon survived the dictatorships intact, was translated promptly and completely, and entered the world's syllabuses as a school, with its internal differences read as the wealth of a movement rather than as rival doctrines. The economists' institutions were public, national, and concentrated in one purgeable university, and their canon arrived in the world as fragments. Two formations of the same generation, the same cities, and the same political weather met two receptions, and the variable that separates them is not talent, not politics, and not the quality of the work. It is the durability and dispersion of the institutions that carried the work, which is the proposition this article has maintained from its first section, here confirmed by the control case the cultural historians unknowingly provided.

## The building in Vitacura: the commission's architecture of permanence

The commission's durability has a physical form that the formation story should not omit. In 1966 the institution inaugurated its headquarters building in the Santiago district of Vitacura, a work of modernist architecture designed by the Chilean architect Emilio Duhart with his collaborators, its great curved volume and its celebrated circular assembly hall announcing, in concrete, that the region intended to think about itself in a house of its own. The building housed the surveys, the library, the training courses, and the conferences, and it performed, architecturally, the commission's claim to permanence: this was not a mission or a project but a seat. The theorists of the research centre worked across the city in humbler rooms, and the contrast between the two workplaces, the marble permanence of the international body and the rented precariousness of the university centre, is the institutional map drawn in building materials. One of the two buildings still houses the institution it was built for. The other's rooms were reassigned by decree to purposes their builders would not have recognised.

The building also became, after the coup, a small parable of the two fates. Protected by the United Nations' immunities, the commission's seat continued its work through the dictatorship years in a city whose universities were being remade by force, its library open, its surveys appearing, its staff travelling on diplomatic passports. Chilean economists of the opposition could not work in their own university, but the region's institution, a few kilometres away, continued to employ Latin Americans and to publish the statistics from which the dictatorship's record would later be judged. The enclave institutes of the later seventies consciously imitated the arrangement, seeking in foreign funding and legal forms the protection the commission enjoyed by treaty. Permanence, the building taught, is not a property of ideas. It is a property of charters, immunities, and endowments, and the dependency school's tragedy can be stated as the absence of all three at the address where its best work was done.

The archive that accumulated in Vitacura deserves the researcher's attention in its own right. The commission's library and document collections constitute the most complete surviving record of the region's economic self study, including the internal papers of the decades when the school was forming, and they survived because the institution that kept them could not be purged. The research centre's papers enjoyed no such keeper, and their dispersal, some saved by participants, some destroyed, some absorbed into other collections, is the archival form of the school's fate. A future historian of the tradition will work asymmetrically, rich in the commission's record and poor in the centre's, and should be warned that the asymmetry is not evidence about the two institutions' importance. It is evidence about their charters. Archives, like receptions, are artefacts of institutional survival, and the warning with which this article began, that the delivered record is a filtered sample of the whole, applies to the documents as strictly as to the doctrines.

## The francophone parallel: Amin and the tiers-monde circuit

The Santiago school had a contemporary in the francophone world whose parallel career tests the institutional argument on independent evidence. Samir Amin, an Egyptian economist formed in Paris, built his analysis of accumulation on a world scale from posts in Cairo, in the planning institutions of Mali, and above all in Dakar, where he directed the African Institute for Economic Development and Planning, a United Nations body for the African region. His great work of 1970 set out a theory of unequal development between centre and periphery that resembled the Latin Americans' in structure while differing in evidence and emphasis, and his later concept of delinking carried the policy argument further than the Santiago school had taken it. The resemblance was not imitation. It was the simultaneous production, in two United Nations regional institutes on two continents, of the same structural intuition by economists administering the same postwar development order and watching it fail in the same decades.

The parallel confirms the formation thesis and refines it. Where the intuition was housed in durable regional institutes with publishing circuits in a world language, as Amin's was in the francophone circuit of Paris and Dakar, it survived its author's displacements and entered the world's debates in something like its original shape. The Latin American version, housed one storey further down, in a national university centre rather than in the regional commission itself, was the one exposed to destruction. The comparison also locates the Santiago school's distinctiveness more exactly. Amin's frame was the world economy as a whole, theorised from Africa's insertion; the Santiago group's was a region, theorised from its own statistics, its own policy failure, and its own seminar. The regional concreteness, the balances, the named mines and ministries, is what the corridor added to the structural intuition the age produced in several places at once, and it is what the corridor's destruction put at risk, since concreteness lives in archives and casework rather than in portable propositions. The francophone circuit kept its theorist. The Spanish circuit kept its texts in Mexico. The Chilean corridor kept neither, and the difference among the three afterlives is a controlled comparison in the survival of schools.

## The bank across the river: the IDB and the network's Washington branch

The cepalino network had a Washington branch, and its existence complicates any simple geography of centre and periphery in the school's own world. The Inter-American Development Bank, founded in 1959 and seated in Washington, was the hemisphere's own development bank, capitalised by the region's governments with the United States as its largest shareholder, and its first president was Felipe Herrera, a Chilean economist of the commission's generation and formation. Under Herrera the bank lent for the integration, infrastructure, and social programmes the structuralist diagnosis prescribed, and its staff, drawn heavily from the region's universities and ministries, carried the network's categories into the one Washington institution the region partly owned. The school's theorists watched the experiment with the ambivalence they brought to all officialdom: the bank proved that the region could staff and steer an international financial institution, and it proved, in their reading, the limits of steering one whose largest shareholder's treasury held the effective veto.

The bank's place in the map is as the network's furthest outpost, the point where the formation's personnel reached into the antagonist's capital and worked there for a decade. Its later history, alignment with the adjustment era's consensus and the long presidencies of the market turn, belongs to the general history of the development banks; what the formation story keeps is the founding moment, when the men of the surveys attempted to build, inside the inter-American system, the financial instrument their doctrine required. The attempt's partial character, a regional bank under a hegemon's shareholding, was itself a dependency case study, and the theorists said so at the time. Institutions, once again, were the argument conducted by other means, and the school's members were as fluent in founding them as in criticising them, a fluency their purely academic reputation in the North has never credited.

## The labels: cepalino, dependentista, and the career of two adjectives

The tradition's members were known by adjectives before they were known by arguments, and the labels' careers condense the reception story into two words. Cepalino began as a description, an economist of the commission or of its school, and travelled through the region's ministries as a professional identity, half badge and half accusation, denoting the planner who distrusted the market's spontaneous order and reached for the survey and the tariff. Dependentista arrived later, coined for the radical school's adherents and used, from the start, more often by its critics than by its subjects, few of whom accepted the label for work they insisted was historical analysis rather than a doctrine of dependence. The asymmetry between the two words is diagnostic. The first named an institution's graduates and was worn, with whatever irony, by the graduates themselves. The second named a doctrine's supposed believers and was imposed from outside, a classifier's convenience that bundled the corridor's quarrels into a single adherence and made the bundle available for refutation.

The labels did the textbook codification's work in miniature. To call Cardoso a dependentista was to assign him a doctrine, dependence as fate, that his histories explicitly refused; to call the commission's economists cepalinos was, by contrast, to name their actual formation, and the word retains a descriptive innocence the other never had. The school's survivors policed the labels in their retrospectives, accepting the institutional adjective and refusing the doctrinal one, and the policing was not vanity. It was the claim of this article, conducted at the level of vocabulary: that the tradition is intelligible as a formation, a set of people made by institutions, and falsified when it is summarised as a creed. The reader who finishes this article with the two adjectives sorted, the one a biography and the other a caricature, has grasped the map's smallest and most portable lesson.

## What the exiles wrote first: the balance sheets of the defeated experiment

The diaspora's first literary product was not dependency theory. It was the balance sheet: the collective autopsy of the Popular Unity experiment, written in Mexico City, Caracas, Paris, and East Berlin in the years immediately after the coup, in journals founded for the purpose and in volumes whose titles announced their genre, lessons, balances, errors, and roads not taken. The exiles argued, with the intimacy of participants and the bitterness of the defeated, over the experiment's strategy: whether the government had moved too fast or too slowly, whether its legalism had been its honour or its fatal constraint, whether the middle classes had been lost by policy or by structure, and what the armed forces' decision revealed about the state the theory had undertheorised. The dependency school's members wrote their share of these texts, and the writing changed them. Men who had analysed trade structures found themselves analysing the coercive apparatus, the party system, and the tempo of mass mobilisation, the objects their formation had bracketed, because the catastrophe had occurred in the bracketed zone.

The balance sheets are the hinge between the school and its political science succession. Read in sequence, the exile literature of the nineteen seventies moves from the external constraint, the school's home variable, toward the internal architecture of power: the military as an institution with its own doctrine, the bourgeoisie as a political actor rather than a structural position, the state as the prize and the problem. O'Donnell's bureaucratic authoritarianism, the transitions literature that followed, and the region's democratic theory of the nineteen eighties all have their headwaters in those autopsies, written by displaced scholars in borrowed rooms. The dependency framework supplied the autopsies' economics; the autopsies supplied the succession's politics; and the corridor's absence is visible in the genre itself, for the balance sheet is the form a seminar takes when it can no longer meet, each participant publishing a verdict the others answer a year later in another country, the argument proceeding at the speed of exile mail toward conclusions no session ever ratified.

The genre also fixed the school's place in the region's memory of the experiment. Because the exiles wrote the first histories of the Popular Unity years, their categories framed the event for the generation that followed: the process as a structural transformation attempted against a dependent economy's constraints, defeated by the coalition those constraints sustained. Later historians, working from archives the exiles could not consult, have revised the frame in particulars, restoring contingency, error, and agency where the balance sheets saw structure. The revision is the normal metabolism of historiography, and it confirms, one last time, the article's method: the first account of an event is written by its survivors, in the institutions available to them, and every later account is a negotiation with the conditions under which the first was produced. The dependency school's own history has been negotiated in exactly that way throughout this article, and the negotiation, not any single verdict, is the tradition's true afterlife.

## Reading the hegemon: the school's Washington

The school's map had a capital it never visited as guests, and its analysis of the United States formed the shadow side of its institutional history. To the commission's economists, Washington was the treasury of the hemisphere's order, the seat of the fund and the bank whose conditionality bounded every plan, to be negotiated with in the language of programmes. To the centre's theorists, it was the centre's political form: the state whose corporations held the enclaves, whose credit decisions disciplined governments, and whose covert services, as the Chilean case laid bare, stood behind the local allies of dependence when the ballot threatened them. The documentary education of the seminar on this point was supplied by scandal. The publication in 1972 of internal memoranda of the International Telephone and Telegraph Corporation, a conglomerate with vast Chilean holdings, showed a corporation proposing to its government the economic strangulation of an elected administration, and the Church Committee's later investigations in Washington confirmed the covert programme the memoranda implied. The theorists required no instruction in the facts afterward. Their framework had predicted the pattern; the documents merely signed it.

The school's Washington matters to the formation story because it explains the tradition's tone toward the North's academy. The dependency writers did not experience the United States as a neutral republic of letters that happened to mistranslate them. They experienced it as the centre their theory described, whose universities' development doctrine arrived with its state's logistics and whose publishing industry filtered their work through the same asymmetries their work analysed. The bitterness in Cardoso's essay on the North American consumption of the theory, and the radicals' refusal of the textbook verdict, draw on that experience and cannot be understood as professional pique. A school formed in the periphery reads its reception at the centre as data, and the data, in this case, kept confirming the theory the reception dismissed. The circle was not lost on the participants, and it is the last of the formation's ironies this article records: the dispersal's distortions were, for the dispersed, the most persuasive evidence their school ever produced.

There is a discipline the reader can carry away from the whole account, applicable far beyond this school. Whenever a body of thought presents itself in a damaged or diminished form, a doctrine that seems cruder than its authors' reputations, a debate that seems frozen at a primitive stage, a shelf of positions that seem to contradict without engaging, the first question should be logistical before it is intellectual. Ask where the work was made and what became of the place. Ask in what language it was written and which of its texts crossed into the reader's language, in what order, carried by which publishers and which exiles. Ask what institution, if any, was charged with keeping the work whole, and whether that institution survived. The dependency school's history suggests that the answers to those questions will explain the damage more parsimoniously than any hypothesis about the authors' abilities, and will frequently acquit the work of the charges its reception preferred against it. Formations are fragile in exact proportion to their concentration, and the Santiago school was concentrated to the point of genius and of vulnerability in a single city, a single university, a single decade. What the concentration produced, no dispersal has yet succeeded in reproducing. What it cost, the map records. The reader who has followed the map to this point possesses the one thing the school's scattered texts, read alone, cannot give: the shape of the whole, and the knowledge of why the whole was never delivered.

That possession carries an obligation the school's survivors would have recognised, since it was the obligation their own seminar enforced. Judgement, when it comes, should be rendered on the work as its makers were able to make it, in the rooms where they made it, with the evidence they shared and the corrections they administered to one another, and not on the residue that a catastrophe and a translation market happened to deliver to a distant syllabus. Rendered on those terms, the verdict of this article is neither acquittal nor condemnation. It is the recognition of an interrupted programme whose strongest claims remain open, whose weakest were corrected in its own corridor before its enemies found them, and whose history, told whole, is the necessary preface to any honest grading of its ideas. The preface is now written; the grading belongs to the reader, equipped at last with the school entire rather than with its fragments, and with the means to test every claim the school advanced.

## For students and exam candidates

Your task with this material is to keep the formation and the theory separate, because examiners reward the separation and punish the conflation. Learn the theory itself, its propositions about trade, capital, and the structure of dependence, from the companion study of the theory elsewhere in this series, and learn from this article the story that examiners use to test whether you understand what you have learned: where the school worked, in the commission and the research centre; how it was formed, out of structuralism and the exhaustion of import substitution; and what happened to it, destruction and dispersal in 1973, with the reception distorted accordingly. When a question asks you to evaluate the school, the strongest answers deploy the institutional story as evidence, showing that the version of dependency theory graded in the textbooks is the translated sample, and that the programme in its setting was more empirical, more plural, and more unfinished than its reputation allows. Avoid them, name the institutions, date the destruction, and let your evaluation distinguish the programme from its fragments.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Teach this school as a formation before you teach it as a doctrine, because the formation is what makes the doctrine's shape intelligible and what protects students from the textbook codification. A productive sequence opens with the institutional map in this article, placing the research centre, the commission, the faculty of social sciences, and the planning institute in one city and one decade, then assigns the principals in the corridor's order rather than the translation order: Prebisch and the surveys, dos Santos, Cardoso and Faletto, Marini in the fullest available form, Frank with Laclau's critique as the record of the sample at work. Seminars profit from staging the school's internal quarrels as live arguments, the periodisation dispute and the associated development dispute, with students required to argue from the shared evidence, the Brazilian miracle and the Chilean enclave, as the corridor's members were required to do. The dispersal then lands as the course's analytical payoff: students who have felt the programme's collective discipline can measure what its destruction cost, and can be set the reception itself as an essay problem, comparing a textbook account with the record and explaining the difference institutionally.

Assessment should reward the same discriminations the article practises. Credit answers that distinguish the commission's programme from the centre's theory, that attribute criticisms to named critics rather than to a consensus, and that concede what the strongest objections establish, the assertion that crept in as the shared evidence thinned, without surrendering the programme's testable core. Penalise the conflation of the school with its most portable member, and the recitation of figures for the contested quantities, which this article has deliberately withheld and which students should learn to handle as contested rather than to quote. The school's own history is the best available teaching instrument for the difference between a research programme and its reputation.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this school is archival and biographical, and the map drawn in these pages is offered as its scaffolding. The open tasks include the reconstruction of the research centre's seminar record from surviving working papers and participants' papers, the prosopography of the commission's training cohorts and their placement across the region's states, the edition history of the canon in Spanish and Portuguese, print runs, translations, and reissues, and the recovery of the Portuguese circuit's debates, which the Spanish language literature itself treats partially. The untranslated source note in this article marks the sharpest gap: the centre's papers and the exile journals remain substantially unworked in English, and the scholar who reads them will find the school's reputation, in either language, a partial guide to their contents. Personal papers of the principals, where they survive, sit divided among the countries of the dispersal, and their cataloguing is uneven; interviews with the surviving second generation, the corridor's students, are a wasting asset and should be recorded while recording is possible.

Journalists covering the region's economic arguments will find the formation story a standing corrective. When a finance minister invokes the external constraint, when a commodity boom revives the vocabulary of reprimarisation, when a government blames the structure and its critics blame the slogan, the genealogy in this article identifies which stratum of the tradition is speaking, the commission's statistics, the centre's mechanisms, or the street's entreguismo, and what each stratum's claim is worth. The school's questions have outlived its institutions and will outlive several more news cycles; reporting them as a living inheritance with a documented history, rather than as a discarded doctrine of the nineteen seventies, is the accuracy this article exists to make easier.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Why did dependency theory emerge in Latin America?

Because the region's own economists, working in a United Nations commission in Santiago, watched their policy programme reach its limits. Structuralist analysis had explained the periphery's trade disadvantage and sponsored industrialisation by import substitution; when substitution stalled against small markets and the balance of payments, the programme's graduates asked whether the international structure itself, and the domestic classes bound to it, were the obstacle. The theory was that question systematised. It emerged in Latin America, rather than elsewhere, because the region uniquely combined the data, the institutions, the exiled scholars, and the lived experience of the programme's exhaustion in one city at one moment.

### Q: What was CEPAL and what did it argue?

CEPAL, the United Nations regional commission for Latin America, was established in Santiago in 1948. Under Raúl Prebisch, its executive secretary from 1950, it developed structuralist economics: the world economy divided into industrial centres and a primary exporting periphery; the terms of trade moving against the periphery over the long run, as the centres retained the fruits of technical progress while the periphery transferred its own abroad through prices; and underdevelopment treated as a structural position rather than an original backwardness. Its policy conclusion was planned industrialisation behind protection. The commission supplied dependency theory with its statistics, its regional frame, its professional network, and eventually its critics, since the radical school formed inside the commission's orbit.

### Q: What is import substitution industrialisation?

The strategy of replacing imported manufactures with domestic production, beginning with consumer goods and advancing, in intention, toward intermediate and capital goods. Its instruments were tariffs and import controls, overvalued exchange rates that cheapened imported machinery, state enterprises in heavy industry and infrastructure, and development banks supplying long term credit. Sponsored by the commission's structuralism and practised across the region from the nineteen fifties, it built real industrial capacity and then met structural limits: domestic markets narrowed by unequal income, and a new dependence on imported inputs and technology that reproduced the external constraint in a harder form. Dependency theory grew from analysing that exhaustion, and should never be confused with the programme itself, which belonged to the commission the theorists criticised.

### Q: Where did the dependency theorists actually work?

Principally in Santiago. The structuralist foundation was laid at the United Nations commission headquartered there, and the radical theory was produced at the Centre for Socio-Economic Studies of the University of Chile, where Theotonio dos Santos directed much of the work, Marini arrived for its closing years, André Gunder Frank taught from 1968, and Fernando Henrique Cardoso worked in the city's planning institute alongside Enzo Faletto. Around those poles stood the social science faculty, the planning institute, and the commission's library, a few streets containing a whole school. After the destruction of 1973 the work continued in exile, above all in Mexico City, but the single shared workplace, and the programme it sustained, belonged to Santiago's decade.

### Q: What happened to them after the coups?

The Brazilian coup of 1964 had already exiled the school's Brazilian founders to Chile; the Chilean coup of September 1973 destroyed their refuge. The research centre was abolished, the universities purged, and the theorists were detained, expelled, or driven into flight, many through embassy asylum. Most reassembled in Mexico City, where the great university, the Colegio, and the publishing house Siglo XXI gave them posts and print; others went to Venezuela and Europe. Cardoso had already returned to Brazil, where he built an independent research centre in São Paulo. None was able to resume the collective programme. They remained productive as individuals, in institutions that had sheltered them, on questions those institutions set.

### Q: How does Marini explain the rupture between production and consumption?

Marini's mechanism begins with super-exploitation: capital in the dependent economy, losing value through unequal exchange and unable to match the centre's productivity, compensates by paying labour below the cost of its reproduction and extending and intensifying work. A working class paid below reproduction cost cannot buy the goods its own industry produces, so production turns away from the mass market toward luxury goods for the high income minority and toward export. The circuit that in the centre joins wages to demand is thereby broken inside the dependent economy: its industry grows without its workers as consumers, and the internal market's deformity reproduces the dependence that produced it. The full argument belongs to his book of 1973, long available only in Spanish and Portuguese.

### Q: How did dependency theory relate to liberation theology?

As supplier and borrower of a framework. The theologians of liberation, systematised by Gustavo Gutiérrez in his book of 1971 and encouraged by the bishops' gathering at Medellín in 1968, needed a social analysis that treated poverty as produced rather than natural, and they took the dependency school's: underdevelopment as a position within a structure, not a stage on a common road. In return, the church's networks, parishes, religious orders, and base communities, carried the framework to audiences no economics journal reached, and sheltered its vocabulary through the dictatorship years when the universities were purged. The movements diverged in foundation, one Marxist and one theological, and each attracted its own critics, but their shared structural account of Latin American poverty is a documented case of a theory travelling through another movement's institutions.

### Q: What is the decolonial turn?

The relocation of the dependency inheritance, from the nineteen nineties, by the modernity and coloniality current associated with the Peruvian sociologist Aníbal Quijano. Its central concept, the coloniality of power, holds that the conquest of the Americas constituted a pattern of domination built on classifying peoples by race and on command over their labour, their authorities, and their knowledge, a pattern that outlived formal colonialism and still structures the modern world. The turn kept the school's founding intuition, that the region's subordination and the centre's wealth were products of a single process, while setting aside the primacy of the economic mechanism in favour of race, culture, and knowledge. It is best read as a partial successor: an heir that changed the question, moving the tradition's home from economics faculties to the humanities.

### Q: Did dependency theory influence policy?

Indirectly and mostly in opposition. The implemented programme of the era, import substitution, was the commission's, and the dependency theorists held no ministries. Their influence ran through the climate of economic nationalism that produced the nationalisation of resources and the Andean code that sought to discipline foreign capital in 1970, through the programmes of opposition movements, and through the formation of the officials who staffed the region's states. The Popular Unity government in Chile governed under pressures the framework described, advised by economists of the structuralist world, but its programme was its own. Because the theory was never administered, it was never refuted by administration, which partly explains its long regional afterlife as the road not taken, available for rediscovery at each subsequent crisis.

### Q: Is there a Latin American revival of dependency thinking?

A body of work, rather than a single movement, carries the school's questions forward. The neo-extractivism debate restated its founding scene for the commodity boom governments, arguing that extraction financed redistribution while deepening the old insertion. The reprimarisation literature, much of it housed at the commission, asks whether trade with China reproduces the centre and periphery pattern in a Pacific form. And the quantification literature, using global input-output tables to measure value and material transfers through trade, revives the founders' empirical gesture with instruments they lacked. These literatures cite the Santiago school as ancestry and argue with its claims on evidence, which is the form a revival takes when the original programme was interrupted rather than completed.

### Q: How does the reception is an artefact of the dispersal claim grade the school's reputation?

It instructs the reader to grade the delivery system before grading the school. The claim holds that the English language reputation, dependency as a shelf of rival doctrines, crude at the origin and vague in decline, was manufactured by the destruction of the producing institution and by translation that arrived in the wrong order over decades. Grading the reputation therefore requires two separate judgements: of the sample that was delivered, which deserves much of the criticism it received, and of the programme in its setting, which was more empirical, more internally critical, and more unfinished than the sample shows. The claim does not acquit the school. It relocates the trial to the correct evidence, the full record in its languages and institutions.

### Q: What should a student separate between the commission's empirical programme and the radical current's structural account?

Three things. The questions, which were shared: both asked why the periphery's insertion reproduced its disadvantage. The standards, which differed in practice: the commission's work was bound to surveys, series, and advice that governments could refuse, while the radical current's claims, framed in the seminar, were policed by colleagues who shared the same data, a discipline that weakened as exile scattered the policers. And the conclusions, which diverged: reform of the insertion from within, against the judgement that the structure itself was the obstacle. A student who keeps the three separations can see the radicalisation as an argument inside one formation, rather than as a new doctrine replacing an old one, and can grade each current by the standards it set itself.

### Q: Why did the school's institutional destruction shape what survived of it?

Because what an institution holds is not only its members' posts but the collective equipment of their work: the seminar that corrected claims weekly, the shared statistics, the journal deadlines, the students, and the memory of what had already been settled. The destruction of the centre in 1973 removed the equipment while sparing many of the persons, so what survived was what persons can carry, the portable formulations, the famous essays, the individual books already in print, and what perished was what only the collectivity could carry, the programme's cumulative discipline and its unwritten archive of cases. The reception's distortions follow from that selection with mechanical fidelity. Survival is a filter, and the filter's mesh was woven by the coup.

### Q: How does the institutional map order the tradition?

By workplace rather than by doctrine, which reverses the usual order of the textbooks. Read down the map, the tradition falls into its true sequence: a research centre where the framework was radicalised in collective argument; the planning and teaching bodies that multiplied its carriers; exile institutions that preserved the persons and the texts while dissolving the programme; later networks that inherited the questions without the corridor; and, before all of them, a commission that built the data and the framework. The map explains features that doctrinal order cannot, why the school's subtlety and crudity were contemporaneous, why its reputation differs by language, and why its afterlife took the forms it did. It is the article's central exhibit, and the tradition should be taught from it before it is taught from any summary of positions.

### Q: What separates a testable mechanism from an asserted one in this literature?

A mechanism specifies its channels and accepts the risk of the data: it says through which flows, prices, payments, or wage relations the subordination operates, so that a sceptic can measure the flows and the claim can lose. An assertion names the structure and treats every outcome as its confirmation, stagnation as dependence's effect and growth as dependence's disguise, so that no finding could count against it. The school's best work, the terms of trade proposition, the remittance and technology channels, the super-exploitation account of the wage relation, belongs to the first kind and was argued over on evidence by the school's own members. Its weakest work, and much of its popular afterlife, belongs to the second. The boundary between them is the boundary the seminar policed and the dispersal erased.
