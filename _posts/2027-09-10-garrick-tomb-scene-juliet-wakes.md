In the ending Shakespeare wrote, the lovers never speak again after the orchard farewell and the parting at dawn. Romeo dies in the Capulet vault believing the still body beside him to be a corpse, and Juliet wakes to find a corpse in place of the husband she expected. In the ending David Garrick built for the stage of Drury Lane, that silence is broken. Garrick's tomb scene, first played in 1748 and printed in 1750 as the additional scene of his altered text, lets Juliet wake after Romeo has swallowed the poison and before the poison finishes its work, and it gives the pair a long, anguished conversation that Shakespeare withheld from them. The change is the most famous single alteration in the whole history of the play on stage, and it held the English and American stage for roughly a century, shaping how generations of playgoers understood what kind of tragedy this was. The wider account of [David Garrick's Adaptation of 1748](/2027/08/27/david-garrick-adaptation-1748/) treats the whole altered version, its cuts, its funeral procession, and its removal of Rosaline; the concern of the present study is the one scene, examined as a piece of writing, a piece of stagecraft, and a piece of interpretation.

![The Capulet tomb in Garrick's altered ending, with Juliet waking as Romeo dies - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict reached here can be stated plainly at the outset. Garrick's added dialogue is accomplished theatre and diminished tragedy at the same time. As theatre, it converts a catastrophe the audience watches in helpless knowledge into a catastrophe the victims themselves come to understand, and it hands two performers a sustained duet of recognition, hope, error corrected, and hope extinguished, played against a clock of poison that every person in the house can feel running down. As tragedy, it spends the play's hardest asset, the irony of a rescue arriving minutes too late, and it purchases tears with a consolation Shakespeare refused: the lovers die, but they die knowing, absolved of the final misunderstanding, folded into a farewell. Garrick understood exactly what the alteration bought and what it cost, because his own printed Advertisement defends the scene as a restoration rather than an invention, tracing the waking to Bandello's Italian novella and to Thomas Otway's Roman adaptation before it. That defence is half honest. The device was old when Garrick took it up, and the dialogue he wrote for it was mostly his own, but the credit he claimed for supplying a failure of Shakespeare's was credit for answering a wish that Shakespeare, on the evidence of the text, had deliberately declined to answer.

## Shakespeare's Tomb Scene: A Death Completed in Silence

The anatomy of the original close matters because Garrick's scene is an intervention into a precise mechanism, and the mechanism can be read off the page. The full anatomy of [Act 5 Scene 3: The Tomb and the Double Suicide](/2023/12/08/act-5-scene-3-tomb-double-suicide/) belongs to its own study; what the argument here needs is the order of knowledge, the sequence by which each figure in the vault learns or fails to learn the truth. Shakespeare arranges the final scene so that knowledge and death pass each other without meeting. Romeo enters the vault carrying a letter for his father, a crowbar, and the apothecary's poison. He kills Paris at the vault's mouth, drags the body inside, and finds Juliet laid out in her grave clothes. What he sees there defeats the evidence of his eyes. Her colour has not faded. "Thou art not conquer'd," he tells the still figure at 5.3.94 to 96, "beauty's ensign yet Is crimson in thy lips and in thy cheeks, And death's pale flag is not advanced there." The play has trained its audience to read that crimson correctly: the potion counterfeits death while preserving the look of life, and the Friar has described the counterfeit in advance at 4.1, down to the warmth and colour that would return. Romeo reads the same signs and draws the wrong conclusion, because Balthasar's report and the grave setting have already told him what the signs must mean.

The scene has already staged one killing inside the vault's approach, and the killing sharpens the silence that follows. Paris, come to strew flowers and weep at the grave in a private rite of betrothal grief, hides at Balthasar's approach, takes Romeo for a condemned villain bent on some vile outrage against the dead, and forces a fight in the dark at the vault's mouth. Romeo, who has come to die and wants no other death on his hands, begs the stranger off with a warning that carries the scene's whole ethic of misdirected knowledge: a madman, he says in effect, is pleading with a madman, and neither can hear the other. Paris falls, and with his last breath asks to be laid beside Juliet, converting the rival into a fellow mourner at the instant of death. Romeo honours the request and carries the body in, so that the vault receives its second corpse before its first apparent one has stirred. Shakespeare crowds the approach to the drinking with a death that teaches the audience how this world works: two men who share a love and a grief meet in darkness, exchange no identifying word that lands, and settle the encounter with swords. The tomb's final silence is prepared by a final failure of recognition, and Garrick's waking, which supplies recognition in floods, stands against a scene whose approach had been built from its absence.

He drinks, and the drinking in Shakespeare's text is an ending compressed into a breath. "O true apothecary," he says at 5.3.119 to 120, "Thy drugs are quick. Thus with a kiss I die." The speed is the point. Between the decision and the death there is no interval in which new information could arrive, no pause in which a stirring hand or a fluttering eyelid could intervene. The apothecary in Mantua had promised exactly this velocity at 5.1, a poison strong enough to dispatch a man with the strength of twenty, and Shakespeare keeps the promise to the letter. Romeo's last act of perception is mistaken, his last speech is addressed to someone who cannot hear it, and the stage direction that follows is among the bleakest in the canon: he dies, and only then does the Friar enter with lantern, crow, and spade, minutes behind the event he was hurrying to prevent.

Juliet's waking, when it comes, is a waking into aftermath. She opens her eyes to the Friar, not to Romeo, and her first words at 5.3.148 reach for the absent husband through the wrong intermediary: "O comfortable friar! where is my lord?" The Friar's answer is summary, not recognition: a greater power has thwarted their intents, Romeo and Paris lie dead, and she must come away to a convent of holy nuns. Shakespeare gives her roughly twenty lines of life after the waking. She refuses the Friar, hears the watch approach, finds the cup still closed in Romeo's hand, and reconstructs the whole story from the evidence in a single compressed inference at 5.3.161 to 162, reading the cup still closed in her true love's hand and naming the poison as the agent of his end. She tries the cup, tries his lips ("Thy lips are warm"), hears the watch, and takes the dagger at 5.3.169 to 170: "O happy dagger! This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die." Her death speech is not an exchange. It is a soliloquy over a body, delivered at speed, under pressure of approaching officers, and its emotional register is not farewell but despatch.

Hold that shape in mind, because every element of it is a decision. The lovers' final meeting is a non-meeting: one speaks to the other as to the dead, the other wakes to speak of the first as already gone. Knowledge in the scene belongs entirely to the audience, who hold the whole design, the potion, the letter miscarried, the Friar's plan, in view while the figures on stage operate on fragments. The pathos of the original close is the pathos of dramatic irony at its purest, pity generated by a gap between what the house knows and what the victims know, a gap that narrows by seconds and closes only when closing can no longer help. Juliet herself had foreseen a waking scene and dreaded it at 4.3.51, asking whether, waking before Romeo came, she might not be distraught amid the hideous fears of the vault. Shakespeare answers her question in the cruelest available form: she wakes sane, too late, and alone.

### What precisely reverses in the order of knowledge when Juliet wakes early?

Everything Shakespeare kept apart is joined. Romeo dies ignorant in the original order, and Juliet wakes to knowledge she cannot use, so the truth never exists in both minds at once. In Garrick's order the lovers hold the truth jointly, speak it aloud, and grieve over it together before death separates the pair.

That reversal is the whole intervention, and it is more radical than its modest stage mechanics suggest. Moving a waking by three minutes changes the genre of the ending. Irony requires ignorance distributed unevenly; recognition requires knowledge shared. Shakespeare's close is built on the first principle, Garrick's on the second, and the two principles generate different emotions, different judgements of the characters, and different accounts of what killed the lovers. Under irony, the agent of destruction is timing itself, the machinery of accident and miscarriage that the Prologue calls fortune. Under recognition, the agent of destruction becomes feeling witnessed and answered: the feud, the fathers, the forced marriage, named and cursed by the victims while both can still hear the naming. Garrick's Romeo does not die meditating on fortune's cruelty in the abstract. He dies arraigning the fathers, in lines considered in detail below, and the arraignment lands differently because Juliet is present to answer it with her breaking heart rather than to discover its occasion from an empty cup.

## Garrick's Tomb Scene in Print: What the Added Dialogue Actually Says

Garrick's version of the play was printed in 1750 under a title that advertised the intervention without embarrassment: the play appeared with alterations and an additional scene, as performed at the Theatre Royal in Drury Lane. The additional scene is the tomb dialogue, and its fullest early witness for modern readers is the text Edward Dowden printed in the introduction to his Arden edition of 1900, setting out the conversation as it had reached eighteenth-century audiences and drawn their tears. The Library of Congress, surveying its holdings of the altered printings, counts a final conversation of sixty-seven lines between the lovers. The Folger Shakespeare Library, describing the same addition, speaks of nearly sixty lines of dialogue made possible by a slow-working poison. The counts differ because the exchanges break into many short speeches that editors tally differently, but the scale is consistent: Garrick roughly doubled the amount of life the text allows Juliet after her waking, and he gave Romeo a second death scene after the first one had, by every rule of the original, already ended.

The conversation begins at the exact point where Shakespeare's scene forecloses conversation. Romeo has drunk. In place of the kiss and the fall, Garrick gives a stir and a breath: "Soft she breathes, and stirs!" Juliet wakes disoriented, speaking the disorientation in her first line: "Where am I? defend me powers!" Romeo's answering speech is the hinge of the whole alteration, and its emotional logic deserves attention, because Garrick writes joy first and lets horror arrive after. "She speaks, she lives: and we shall still be bless'd," he exclaims, and the speech runs on in the vein of rescue: rise, rise, my Juliet, and from this cave of death, this house of horror, let me snatch thee to thy Romeo's arms. The actor-manager who wrote those lines had played the part's despair ten minutes earlier and then played its reversal, and the scene is engineered as an actor's instrument: despair, incredulous hope, tenderness, frantic urgency, encroaching weakness, lucid farewell. Each beat reverses the one before it, and the poison ticks underneath all of them.

Juliet's side of the exchange is written with a shrewd grasp of what a waking mind would and would not know. She does not wake omniscient. She wakes cold, frightened, and in possession of exactly one fixed idea, the forced marriage she drank the potion to escape. "Bless me! how cold it is! who's there?" she asks, and when hands draw her from the tomb she resists them as an abduction: "My strength may fail me, but my will's unmov'd," she declares; she will not wed Paris, for Romeo is her husband. The mistake is the mirror image of Romeo's. He had taken a living woman for a corpse; she takes her rescuer for her persecutor, and for a few speeches the scene holds both errors at once, each lover misreading the other in opposite directions. Recognition arrives through the ear rather than the eye. "I know that voice," she says, in the line that turns the scene: the magic sweetness of the voice wakes her tranced soul, memory returns entire, and she moves to embrace him with the recovered knowledge that the man before her is her lord and husband.

Then the scene executes its cruel pivot, and the pivot is the reason the alteration survived. Juliet, embracing, finds avoidance where she expected welcome. She begs to touch his hand and taste the cordial of his lips; she asks why the voice that restored her has gone strange; she begs for support against fainting, and Romeo answers with the line on which the whole construction stands or falls: "Oh I cannot, I have no strength, but want thy feeble aid. Cruel poison!" In two words the rescue collapses into the catastrophe, and the audience's brief hope, which Garrick has spent some thirty lines building, is withdrawn with interest. Juliet reads his body faster than his words: "Poison! what means my lord; thy trembling voice! Pale lips! and swimming eyes! death's in thy face!" The diagnosis is hers, delivered as recognition, and it converts her from patient to witness. From this point the dialogue becomes exposition transfigured into farewell. Juliet asks the question the audience has been holding since Mantua: "Die? was the Friar false!" Romeo answers with the tragedy's whole mechanism compressed into a confession: "I thought thee dead: distracted at the sight, (Fatal speed) drank poison, kiss'd thy cold lips, And found within thy arms a precious grave." Her reply is the most quoted line Garrick ever wrote for the stage, and it earns the position: "And did I wake for this!"

The remaining speeches distribute blame before they distribute grief, and that distribution is where Garrick's scene stops being a mere extension and becomes an interpretation. Romeo, torn between death and love and calling himself distracted, turns outward: "Fathers have flinty hearts, no tears can melt 'em. Nature pleads in vain." The sentence arraigns the elder generation in the hearing of the younger and assigns the catastrophe a moral address that Shakespeare's Romeo never reaches, because Shakespeare's Romeo dies before he can know enough to assign blame accurately. Garrick's Romeo dies as a prosecutor. His final speech gathers the claims of the marriage against the claims of the feud, wife against fathers, heart-strings pulled until they crack and break, and ends on her name twice cried. Juliet's last words in the added dialogue answer with the only union left available: "fate marries us in death, And we are one." The stage direction that follows in Dowden's printing has her faint across Romeo's body, and the scene Garrick added ends in a tableau, two living shapes collapsed into the posture of the double corpse that the close of the play will shortly make literal.

## Juliet's Arc Inside the Added Dialogue: From Patient to Witness

Shakespeare had written Juliet's waking in advance, as a fear. Alone with the vial at 4.3, she rehearses the vault before she ever lies in it: the loathsome smells, the shrieks like mandrakes torn from the earth, the bones of her ancestors, Tybalt festering in his shroud, and the terror that she might wake before Romeo came and be distraught among the hideous fears. Garrick's scene reads like an answer composed point by point against that rehearsal, whether or not the adapter consciously worked from the soliloquy. His Juliet wakes to cold rather than heat of fancy, to a voice rather than to shrieks, to hands rather than to bones, and she passes through the feared madness in a handful of speeches before sanity, memory, and purpose return to her in sequence. The waking Shakespeare's heroine dreaded as a dissolution of reason becomes, in Garrick, a recovery of reason staged in public, and the recovery is the actress's staircase: confusion, terror, resistance, recognition, tenderness, diagnosis, grief. Each step is playable, each step reverses the last, and the staircase climbs from the potion speech's nightmare to a lucidity more terrible than the nightmare, because the lucid mind arrives in time to understand everything and to change nothing.

The resistance beat deserves a closer look, because it is the one moment in the added dialogue that works by the original's own method of irony. Juliet fights Romeo's hands under a misapprehension, believing herself seized for the Paris wedding, and the audience watches a lover repulse her rescuer for reasons that are, inside her information, entirely rational. Garrick had supposedly traded irony away, yet at the centre of his scene sits a compact ironic engine: the house knows the hands are Romeo's, Juliet believes them an enemy's, and her noblest quality in the play, the will that faced down father, nurse, and potion, is deployed in error against the person it exists to defend. Her declaration that her strength may fail while her will stands unmoved is the 3.5 and 4.1 Juliet entire, the girl who knelt and then outfaced the household, speaking at the worst possible moment. The beat lasts only a few speeches, because Garrick's design cannot afford Shakespeare's duration of error, but its presence refines the standard charge against the scene. The adapter did not misunderstand irony. He rationed it, spending small amounts inside a structure whose large economy ran on recognition, the way a mint alloys a soft metal with a harder one to make a coin that will circulate.

Recognition, when Garrick stages it, comes through the sense Shakespeare's lovers had trusted from the beginning. The pair met in a crowded hall and completed a sonnet by ear before they had exchanged names; they courted through a wall in darkness, identifying each other by voice across the orchard's night; the Nurse's interventions and the Friar's letters all failed them, but the ear never did. Garrick's Juliet knows the voice before she trusts the eyes, and the line in which the tranced soul wakes at the voice's magic sweetness gathers the play's whole auditory pattern into a single beat. A staging tradition might have made the recognition visual, a face seen by torchlight, and Shakespeare's own scene contains the materials for such a beat in the colour that lingers in her cheeks. Garrick chooses sound, and the choice aligns the added scene with the play it interrupts more faithfully than its detractors allowed. The lovers who were joined by a shared sonnet are rejoined by a recognized voice, and the joining holds for the length of the farewell.

Her diagnosis of Romeo completes the arc from patient to witness. The girl who woke as the scene's problem becomes the scene's interpreter, reading trembling, pallor, and swimming eyes with the speed the original Juliet brought to the cup, and the speed matters for the character's honour. Garrick's sentimental frame requires her collapse at the end, the faint across the body, but the dialogue before the collapse grants her the tragedy's clearest perception: she alone, of everyone in the vault across both versions, understands the whole situation while action remains theoretically possible, and understands it from evidence she gathers herself rather than from any friar's summary. That the understanding cannot save anyone is the scene's tragic residue, the kernel of Shakespeare's design surviving inside Garrick's redesign, and it keeps the added dialogue from the pure consolation its enemies described. The consolation is real, but it is rationed by knowledge that arrives complete and useless, which is a definition of pathos considerably sterner than the period's reputation suggests.

## The Slow Poison: Garrick's Reversed Apothecary Line

Every alteration of this size needs a mechanism, and Garrick's mechanism is a single adjective withheld. In Shakespeare's text the poison is quick: the apothecary's boast and Romeo's dying line agree on its speed, and the agreement is a contract with the audience that no interval exists. Garrick's version breaks the contract at the moment of swallowing. The Library of Congress, comparing the printings, notes the altered reading directly: where Shakespeare's Romeo cries "O true apothecary! Thy drugs are quick," Garrick's Romeo cries "O true apothecary! Thy drugs are not so quick." The negation does the work of the entire additional scene. A poison that is not so quick is a poison with a duration, and duration is stage time, and stage time, spent at the rate of a dying man's strength, is the sixty-odd lines of dialogue the alteration exists to contain.

### Why does the poison have to slow down before the lovers can speak?

Because the waking alone changes nothing unless Romeo survives it. Shakespeare's timing makes the rescue and the death simultaneous, so information has no interval in which to travel. Garrick stretches the poison's course across the length of a conversation, and inside that purchased interval the whole exchange fits: recognition, explanation, accusation, and farewell, each stage arriving before strength fails.

The dramaturgy of the delay is more careful than the device sounds. Garrick does not suspend the poison; he dramatizes its progress as the scene's clock. Romeo's first speeches after the waking are strong, urgent, organized around action: rise, quit this place, fly together. As the exchanges proceed, the verse registers the body's decline. He cannot support her weight. His voice trembles. He describes the poison's course in terms of a struggle interrupted by joy, held back for a moment by the transport of hearing her speak and seeing her eyes open, then rushing through his veins again with time too short for the whole story. The fiction inside the scene is that emotion itself has pharmacology: happiness arrests the poison, despair releases it. Georgian audiences, raised on a theatre of sensibility in which strong feeling visibly acted on the frame, would have found the physiology legible rather than absurd. The scene's suspense is not whether Romeo will die, since the house knows the story, but how much of the truth can be spoken before the strength to speak runs out, and that is a suspense of an entirely different kind from anything in the original vault, where the only question is whether the Friar will arrive in time and the answer is known before he sets out.

Consider what the slow poison replaces. In Shakespeare, the time pressure of the last scene is external and spatial: a race between the Friar's feet and the watch's patrol, fought across the distance between a cell and a churchyard, and lost by minutes. In Garrick, the time pressure is internal and corporeal: a race between speech and dissolution inside one body, lost by lines. The first kind of pressure generates irony, because the audience watches travellers who do not know they are racing. The second kind generates pathos of a purer strain, because the racer knows the stakes and spends the remaining strength on the speaking itself. Critics who dismiss the alteration as a trick rarely reckon with the intelligence of this substitution. Garrick had identified the one element of the original close that a century of performance had found hardest to land, the abruptness of a hero dying mid-sentence to a woman who wakes to an empty stage relationship, and he rebuilt the scene's engine to remove it. The removal was skilful. Whether the engine he built deserved to replace the engine he removed is the question the rest of this study adjudicates.

## Romeo Enlarged: The Added Scene as a Star's Instrument

No account of the waking is complete that treats it as a disinterested improvement, because the scene enlarged one part above all others, and the part belonged to the adapter. The Folger's description of the Folger exhibition materials states the attraction without euphemism: the extended tomb scene allowed Garrick the opportunity to display a wide range of tragic emotion. Garrick's fame rested on transition, on the visible passage of feeling across a face and through a body faster than rivals could follow, and Shakespeare's tomb scene gives Romeo a single sustained register, the high ceremonial despair of the long speech over the body, magnificent and essentially static. Garrick's scene gives Romeo six registers in sequence: the despair of the entrance and the drinking, the incredulous joy of the stirring, the urgency of the rescue attempted, the tenderness of the recognition, the physical collapse confessed, and the lucid, prosecutorial farewell. An actor whose genius was the gear-change found, or inherited and then perfected, a scene that was all gear-changes, and the town's memory of his Romeo fixed on the tomb as the summit of the performance because the tomb was where the instrument played its full compass in the shortest compass of time.

The enlargement had a cost inside the play's own economy, and the cost falls on a figure who never appears in the added scene at all: the Romeo of the earlier acts. Shakespeare's Romeo is a boy whose rhetoric outruns his experience until experience outruns his rhetoric in the vault, and the final speech's grandeur depends on the stillness of a mind that has stopped developing and started concluding. Garrick's Romeo develops at the very end, learning, hoping, erring, understanding, accusing, and forgiving inside a single scene, and a hero who develops in his last ten minutes retroactively crowds the hero of the preceding acts, who can begin to look like a preliminary sketch for the finished figure who arraigns the fathers. Barry's rival Romeo pressed the same enlargement through tenderness rather than fire, and the town's division over the two Romeos was partly a division over which enlarged ending, the melting or the blazing, the tragedy's frame could hold. That both frames held, for weeks of direct competition, demonstrates the added scene's elasticity as an instrument: it could be tuned to opposite acting styles without structural change, a property no merely personal vehicle possesses, and the elasticity helps explain why the scene outlived its author's own performances by generations.

There is a managerial calculation under the artistry, visible in the production's casting history. The scene requires a Juliet capable of matching the Romeo beat for beat through a long crescendo, and it was built in a company that held, in succession, the two most celebrated Juliets of the age. A duet finale advertises a pair, sells a rematch, and survives a change of personnel because the structure, not the star, guarantees the effect: any house with a strong pair could promise the town its money's worth of feeling in the last ten minutes, and provincial and American managers inherited a closer that closed, whatever the weaknesses earlier in the evening. Shakespeare's ending, by contrast, is a director's problem in every generation, its effect depending on pacing, silence, and the audience's willingness to do unassisted work at the end of a long play. Managers who kept Garrick's ending for a century were not simply bowing to fashion. They were choosing the ending whose effect they could insure.

## Before Garrick: Bandello, Otway, and Cibber Shape the Waking

The waking was not Garrick's invention, and Garrick, to give the Advertisement its due, never quite claimed that it was. The claim he made was more interesting and more slippery, and tracing the device's descent shows both halves of it. In the Italian tradition behind the play, the lovers' story circulated in versions by Luigi da Porto and Matteo Bandello before Arthur Brooke Englished it in verse in 1562 and William Painter in prose in 1567. Garrick's Advertisement asserts that Bandello's telling had made Juliet wake in the tomb before Romeo died, and that the circumstance had been lost to Shakespeare because the French and English translations from which Shakespeare worked had left it out. On Garrick's account, Shakespeare's silent tomb is not a design but an accident of transmission, a great effect missed because a translator dropped it. The added scene therefore presents itself as a recovery: the adapter returns to the story a beat that its Italian source possessed and its English line lost.

The stage descent runs separately, and it runs through Rome. Thomas Otway's *The History and Fall of Caius Marius*, first performed in 1679 and printed in 1680, transplants the lovers into the civil wars of Marius and Sulla, Romeo becoming the younger Marius and Juliet becoming Lavinia, and it lifts whole stretches of Shakespeare's play into the Roman frame. The full account of [Otway's 'Caius Marius': The Roman Rewrite](/2027/08/20/otway-caius-marius-roman-rewrite/) belongs to its own study; the point for the present argument is the ending. Otway closes with the waking circumstance: Lavinia revives from her drugged sleep while Marius still lives after taking poison, and the pair learn the fatal mistake together before death parts them. The Yale Shakespeare's stage history, summarizing the line of descent, puts the matter compactly: Otway's ending drew on the older Italian form of the story in which the heroine awakens before the hero dies and both lovers learn of his error. Dowden, more judgementally, groups Otway and Garrick together as adapters who took the circumstance for a guide and varied from the Shakespearean close under its influence.

Otway's handling of the circumstance deserves a paragraph of its own, because the differences between the Roman version and Garrick's version mark the distance between a device used and a device exploited. In *Caius Marius* the waking arrives inside a tragedy whose centre of gravity is political: the feud is a civil war between orders of the Roman state, Marius and Sulla contend for the city itself, and the lovers' story runs as the private casualty of a public convulsion. A Lavinia who wakes in the tomb wakes into that larger machinery, and the recognition between the dying Marius and the revived Lavinia, in which both lovers learn of his fatal mistake, plays as one horror among the state's horrors rather than as the tragedy's sole destination. Otway, whose genius ran to frantic passion and collapsing minds, treats the beat as an intensifier of disorder. Garrick, working in a version where the feud has been trimmed toward a domestic obstacle and the lovers promoted to the whole centre of the design, treats the same beat as the destination toward which the entire evening has been travelling. The device is identical. Its weight in the structure is opposite, and the opposition explains why Garrick could profess wonder at Otway's thinness while standing in Otway's debt: the Roman play spends the waking, the Drury Lane play saves for it.

The Cibber link in the chain operated differently again. Theophilus Cibber was no Otway and no Garrick, a capable theatrical workman in a famous family's shadow, and his 1744 version performed the essential service of keeping the waking in the active repertory of the London stage, attached to Shakespeare's names and places rather than to Roman ones. A device preserved in a Roman adaptation might have remained a scholarly curiosity. A device preserved in the current acting text of the tragedy itself, in the playhouse where Garrick would shortly take command, was a standing invitation. Branam's genesis study matters here beyond the question of credit, because it converts the scene's birth from a romance of invention into a history of transmission: novella to Roman tragedy to workmanlike revival to star vehicle, the waking passed from hand to hand for a century and a half before it reached the hands that made it famous, and each hand altered its function while preserving its outline.

Between Otway and Garrick stands a figure the popular account of the scene tends to skip, and the skipping flatters Garrick. When Theophilus Cibber brought the play back to the London stage in 1744, his version retained the waking feature from Otway's play and likewise cut the references to Rosaline that Garrick would also cut. The Yale history states the consequence without varnish: the version Garrick used in 1750 was substantially Cibber's. George C. Branam's study of the genesis of Garrick's version, published in *Shakespeare Quarterly* in 1984, established the debt in detail, showing how much of the acting text Garrick inherited rather than made. The waking scene as London audiences received it in 1748 was therefore not a bolt of original inspiration. It was a further working of a device that had been in the story's Italian sources, on the Restoration stage in Roman dress, and in the immediate London repertory four years before Garrick's name went on the bill.

### Which hands had shaped the waking before Garrick claimed it?

Three sets of hands at least, in three different media. Bandello's novella supplied the circumstance in prose, on the adapter's own testimony. Otway carried the circumstance onto the stage inside a Roman tragedy and proved audiences would follow it. Cibber kept it in the acting repertory of the 1740s, in a version Garrick substantially inherited.

None of this descent makes Garrick's scene worthless, but it fixes what his contribution actually was, and the fix matters for the verdict. Garrick contributed the scene's fame, its polish, its place in a star vehicle, and most of its words. He did not contribute its premise. The premise was a standing option in the tradition, rejected by the English translations, taken up by Otway, kept warm by Cibber. When Garrick's defenders in his own century and his detractors in later ones both describe the waking as Garrick's stroke, the two camps share an error: the stroke was the tradition's, and Garrick's part was to execute it at the highest level of the contemporary stage and to defend the execution in print with a scholar's genealogy and an advocate's selectivity.

## The Advertisement of 1750: Garrick Defends His Scene

The printed Advertisement that fronts Garrick's altered text is a small masterpiece of defensive adaptation, and it should be read as part of the scene's meaning, because it tells the audience how to feel about the added dialogue before a word of that dialogue is spoken. The programme is announced first: the chief design of the alterations was to clear the original as much as possible of the jingle and quibble that had always stood as objections to reviving the play. Garrick presents the adapter as a restorer of a damaged monument, removing scaffolding from the facade. Then the Advertisement reaches the tomb. Bandello, the Italian novelist from whom Shakespeare borrowed the subject, had made Juliet wake in the tomb before Romeo died. Shakespeare's omission of the circumstance, Garrick argues, came not from judgement but from reading the story in translation, the French or English versions having injudiciously left out this addition to the catastrophe. The word "injudiciously" does heavy work: the omission is framed as a fault in the transmission, and by extension the restoration becomes an act of fidelity to the story's better form rather than an act of licence against its most famous author.

Otway receives praise engineered to diminish him. Garrick concedes that Otway, in *Caius Marius*, had made use of the affecting circumstance, and then professes wonder that so great a dramatic genius did not work up from it a scene of more nature, terror, and distress. The concession establishes priority; the wonder clears the ground. If Otway used the device and used it thinly, then the field stands open for a fuller working, and the Advertisement announces that such a scene was attempted at the revival of the play. The adapter's hope follows, that an endeavour to supply the failure of so great a master will not be deemed arrogant, and that the making use of two or three of Otway's introductory lines will not be accounted plagiarism. Those two or three lines are the only verbal debt Garrick acknowledges anywhere, and the acknowledgement is precisely calibrated: borrowings small enough to name are borrowings that cannot be enlarged by enemies.

Enemies had already tried. The Advertisement closes the subject with a counterstroke of real elegance, aimed at persons who, from good nature and love of justice, had endeavoured to take away from the editor the little merit of the scene by ascribing it to Otway. Such accusers, Garrick writes, have unwittingly paid him a compliment they never intended, since the charge of copying Otway implies that his scene rises to the level where copying is the plausible explanation of its quality. The move converts theft into praise and ends the argument by redefining its terms. As advocacy, the paragraph is complete. As history, it is partial in a way Branam's later scholarship exposed: the Advertisement names Bandello and Otway, argues with the plagiarism charge, and says nothing of Cibber, the living intermediary whose 1744 acting version Garrick's text substantially followed. A reader of the Advertisement alone would conclude that the waking travelled from an Italian novella and a Restoration tragedy directly into Garrick's hands. The genealogy is accurate at both ends and silent in the middle, which is where the debt was freshest.

Set the Advertisement beside the scene it introduces and a consistent picture emerges of an adapter who knew that the alteration needed a pedigree to survive. Mid-century London revered Shakespeare with a fervour Garrick himself did more than any other man to build, through performance, through editing ventures, and eventually through the Stratford Jubilee of 1769. An adapter who merely confessed to improving the tomb scene would have been savaged as a vandal against the national poet. An adapter who restored a circumstance from the poet's own Italian source, surpassed a Restoration predecessor's thin handling of it, and borrowed two or three lines in the process stood inside the tradition rather than against it. The Advertisement is the scene's passport, and the scene travelled on it for a hundred years.

## Why 1748 Wanted a Farewell: Bardolatry and the Licensed Alteration

A paradox stands at the centre of the scene's history, and resolving it explains the alteration's respectability in its own age. The decades of Garrick's management were the decades in which Shakespeare's elevation into a national idol became irreversible, a process Garrick personally superintended as actor, manager, publicist, and finally as impresario of the Stratford Jubilee of 1769, the civic festival that fixed the poet's cult in his birthplace. The same hands that built the shrine rewrote the scripture, and contemporaries felt no contradiction, because the period's reverence attached to Shakespeare's genius rather than to Shakespeare's letter. Genius, in the critical language of the age, was a property of conception, sentiment, and knowledge of the heart, all of which an adapter could claim to serve while correcting the accidents of an unrevised text, the quibbles of a young playwright writing for an unpolished stage, and the omissions of translators who had starved the story before the playwright received it. The Advertisement's argument about Bandello is exactly this philosophy in action: the letter had failed the genius, and the adapter's duty ran to the genius.

The philosophy licensed a whole repertory of improved Shakespeare, happy endings, pruned subplots, and regularized passions, most of which perished with the taste that licensed them. The tomb scene's survival past its licensing philosophy is therefore the fact that needs explaining, and the explanation cannot rest on doctrine, since the doctrine was dead by the time Phelps acted on its opposite. What survived the philosophy was the scene's independent theatrical merit, the duet's reliable manufacture of an effect audiences continued to want after they had stopped believing an adapter had the right to manufacture it. When the nineteenth century withdrew the licence, the scene did not fall with the forfeits around it in a single ruin. It was pensioned, printed, argued over, and retained in acting editions for decades, treated less like a disgraced impostor than like a retired servant of the house whose service everyone remembered. The distinction between the alteration that perishes with its doctrine and the alteration that outlives it is the distinction between Garrick's Rosaline surgery, forgotten because it merely tidied, and Garrick's tomb scene, remembered because it supplied an experience the original had declined to supply and no subsequent orthodoxy could make audiences stop wanting on the nights when wanting was permitted.

## Drury Lane, 1748 to 1750: Bellamy, Barry, and the Battle of the Romeos

The scene's first life was a performer's life. Garrick staged his altered version at Drury Lane in 1748, taking Romeo for himself, and the version reached print in 1750 as the acting text of the Theatre Royal. Two Juliets frame the early history. Susannah Maria Cibber, the great tragic actress of the age and Theophilus Cibber's wife, was Garrick's Juliet in the first phase of the production's life and carried the role's musical pathos as no rival could match. George Anne Bellamy, younger, vivid, and formidably ambitious, succeeded to the part opposite Garrick and fixed the waking scene in the visual record: Benjamin Wilson's painting of 1753 shows Garrick's Romeo and Bellamy's Juliet in the tomb at the very moment of the alteration, and the image, multiplied in engravings, became the alteration's icon, sold and resold until the composition was familiar to people who had never sat in Drury Lane. A theatrical portrait of an invented scene outselling the play's genuine scenes in the print shops is the eighteenth century's own verdict on which ending the public carried home.

In the autumn of 1750 the scene became the stakes of the most famous managerial duel of the Georgian stage. Covent Garden mounted the play against Drury Lane with Spranger Barry as Romeo and Mrs. Cibber as Juliet, while Garrick and Bellamy held the same parts across town, and the two productions ran against each other night after night in the contest remembered as the Battle of the Romeos. London playgoers divided into parties, attended both houses, and compared the rival pairs speech by speech, with the death scenes as the championship ground. The contest is usually told as a duel of Romeos, but its substance was a duel of endings as much as a duel of actors, since the Cibber-derived text at Covent Garden and Garrick's text at Drury Lane shared the waking device while differing in execution, emphasis, and the personnel pouring feeling into the added lines. Barry's Romeo was celebrated for melting tenderness, Garrick's for fire and rapid transition; Cibber's Juliet was the age's standard of pathetic singing speech, Bellamy's a younger and more impulsive presence. The town's argument over which pair made the tomb scene more affecting was, whether the town knew it or not, an argument over how much feeling the ending of this tragedy ought to be allowed to hold.

### How did the rival Romeos turn one scene into a public contest?

By giving London two complete performances of the same altered ending on the same nights, with the town invited to judge between them. Barry and Cibber at Covent Garden, Garrick and Bellamy at Drury Lane, made the waking scene the recognised test of a tragic pair and the standard ground for measuring Romeos and Juliets.

The battle's deeper effect was canonization by competition. A scene that rival houses fight over is a scene no manager can drop, and the waking entered the repertory's permanent furniture during those weeks in a way that no single management's preference could have secured. When later in the century the play toured to provincial stages and crossed the Atlantic, the acting version that travelled was the altered one, funeral procession, cut Rosaline, slow poison, waking, and all. The Hallam company carried the play to America in the 1750s, and American audiences learned the tragedy in Garrick's shape as a matter of course. What had begun as one adapter's additional scene hardened, within a decade of its first performance, into the form in which the English-speaking stage simply possessed the play.

## Why the Waking Juliet Drew Tears: Sentiment on the Georgian Stage

The audience that wept at Garrick's tomb scene was not naive, and its tears were not a failure of taste waiting for Victorian editors to correct. Georgian theatrical culture prized sensibility as a moral faculty: the capacity to feel with another's distress, displayed in the body's involuntary signs, counted as evidence of a rightly formed heart, and the theatre was the public laboratory in which that capacity was exercised and certified. A successful pathetic scene did not merely move an audience; it vindicated the audience's own refinement, and playgoers understood the contract. Garrick, the supreme technician of the pathetic effect, built the waking to the specifications of that culture with complete deliberateness. The scene is a machine for producing the signs of sensibility in sequence: the startled waking, the cold hand, the voice recognized, the embrace refused by failing strength, the diagnosis read in a face, the farewell compressed against a failing pulse. Each beat invites the house to feel on behalf of figures who are, for once, feeling accurately about their own situation at the same time.

That last condition is the alteration's psychological signature, and it separates Garrick's pathos from Shakespeare's. In the original close, the audience's feeling is divided against itself: pity for Romeo is inseparable from the knowledge that his pity-worthy act rests on a mistake, and pity for Juliet is inseparable from the knowledge that the man she addresses cannot hear. The house weeps at a waste of feeling as much as at the feeling itself. In Garrick's close, feeling is never wasted. Every declaration lands on the person it addresses. Romeo's despair is answered, Juliet's devotion is witnessed, the marriage is affirmed aloud by both parties in each other's hearing, and the audience's sympathy flows along channels with no obstruction. The Theatrical Review of 1772, looking back at the alteration's success, located its appeal exactly here: Garrick had found that Shakespeare had neglected to heighten the catastrophe to so great a degree of distress as the catastrophe was capable of being carried to. Heightening meant concentration, and concentration meant removing the irony that dissipated distress into reflection.

The culture of sensibility also explains the scene's particular treatment of Juliet. Georgian sentiment required a heroine whose feeling could be displayed in extended registers, and Shakespeare's close gives Juliet almost no room: twenty lines, a refusal, an inference, a dagger. Garrick's waking gives her a full arc within the added dialogue alone, fear, resistance, recognition, tenderness, diagnosis, grief, and the final claim of union in death, and it lets the actress build the arc in the audience's presence rather than report a state of mind after the fact. Bellamy and Cibber were both celebrated in the part, and the scene's design tells why: the waking hands the Juliet of the production the evening's longest sustained crescendo and its final image. The alteration, whatever else it did, rebalanced the tragedy's last act between its two stars, and a repertory built on star pairs had commercial as well as emotional reasons to keep it.

There is a further, quieter appeal that contemporaries registered without theorizing. The added scene grants the lovers the one experience the feud, the forced marriage, and the miscarried letter had conspired to deny them: a sanctioned leave-taking. They part as husband and wife, in words, with the marriage named and defended against both houses, against Paris, against fate itself. Georgian audiences, who married in church and buried their dead with set forms of farewell, felt the deprivation in Shakespeare's ending as a kind of liturgical incompleteness, two deaths without a rite. Garrick supplied the rite. His lovers die unshriven by any priest, but they die having performed the essential offices for each other, confession of the mistake, absolution implied in the embrace, commendation of the departing soul. The scene satisfied a hunger for ceremony that the original deliberately leaves starving, and the satisfaction, repeated over a century of performances, is a large part of the alteration's hold.

## Recognition Against Irony: What the Waking Changes in the Tragedy

The strongest case against Garrick's scene has never been that the dialogue is feeble, though feebleness has often been alleged. The strongest case is structural: the waking changes what the tragedy means by changing who knows what at the moment of death, and meaning in this play is carried by the distribution of knowledge more than by any single speech. Shakespeare's ending is the final term of a pattern that runs through the entire action. The lovers live inside a series of messages that arrive late, miscarry, or reach the wrong ears: a servant who cannot read carries the feast invitation to the one guest who must not see it; a letter of life-and-death import waits undelivered in a plague-stopped house; a report of a funeral is mistaken for the report of a death. The tomb scene completes the pattern at its largest scale. Romeo's whole journey to Verona is a message error in motion, and his death is the last miscarriage, knowledge and its object passing within minutes of each other in the same small vault. The design tells the audience that the catastrophe is not authored by any villain and not willed by any god visible in the play. It is authored by the ordinary friction of a world in which information moves slowly and passion moves fast, the world the feud built and maintains.

Garrick's waking breaks the pattern at its climax, and breaking it redistributes the blame. Once Romeo can explain himself, the miscarriage stops being the tragedy's final word. He names the fathers. He calls their hearts flinty, declares that nature pleads with them in vain, prophesies wretchedness for children so governed. In Shakespeare, that indictment is available only to the audience, assembled from the whole play, and it lands on the survivors in the closing tableau as the Prince distributes pardons and punishments. In Garrick, the indictment is spoken at the centre of the catastrophe by its principal victim, in the hearing of the other victim, and the feud acquires a defendant, a charge, and a witness stand. The tragedy tilts from a tragedy of accident toward a tragedy of protest. Its centre of gravity moves from fortune to society, from the stars to the fathers, and the move anticipates by two centuries the social readings of the play that treat the feud's order, rather than chance, as the killing power.

The waking also changes Juliet's death, and with it her character's final shape. Shakespeare's Juliet dies by a reasoning process the audience watches: she reads the cup, reads the lips, discounts the Friar's offered refuge, and acts, all inside a handful of lines, with the watch's noise as her clock. The speed is her heroism: grief does not unmake her competence. Garrick's Juliet spends her competence earlier, on recognition and farewell, and meets the end of the added dialogue in a faint, her final posture receptive rather than executive. The dagger still waits for her in the close that follows, and the act remains hers, but the emotional signature has been changed in advance. Shakespeare's audience remembers a Juliet who solved her situation in the only terms left to her. Garrick's audience remembers a Juliet who was united with her husband in a shared understanding and then overwhelmed. The first figure is a tragic agent. The second is a heroine of sensibility. The eighteenth century preferred the second and said so with its purses for a hundred years; the preference records a genuine shift in what audiences believed a young woman's greatness on stage should look like.

Against these losses stands a gain that honest criticism must register. Garrick's scene repairs an asymmetry that has troubled readers of the original since the eighteenth century and troubles playgoers still: Romeo's death is the emotional summit of Shakespeare's scene while Juliet's risks reading as an epilogue, hurried, reported by the watch, absorbed into the fathers' reconciliation. The added dialogue gives the ending a true duet structure. Both lovers are conscious, articulate, and mutually intelligible at the climax, and the tragedy ends, as the whole play has been structured to end, in a marriage: two voices completing each other's sentences against the same extinction that completes the fathers' quarrel. Juliet's line that fate marries the pair in death states the design openly. Where Shakespeare offers a marriage destroyed by being hidden, Garrick offers a marriage confirmed by being witnessed at the last possible moment, and the confirmation answers the play's own deepest wish, spoken in the orchard and the cell, that the union be acknowledged before the world.

### What does the added farewell take away from the tragedy's design?

It takes away the silence in which the audience does the tragedy's last work alone. Shakespeare leaves the truth unspoken between the lovers, so the house must hold it, and holding it is the experience the play exists to create. Garrick speaks the truth aloud, and the house is relieved of its burden.

The trade, then, is real on both sides, and any verdict that denies one side of it is propaganda rather than criticism. Garrick traded irony for recognition, accident for arraignment, agency for union, and audience labour for audience release. The trade was intelligently made and, for the stage he commanded, rationally priced. Whether the price was right depends on a prior question, what the ending of this tragedy is for, and on that question the centuries have voted both ways with their feet: the eighteenth century chose Garrick's ending as the play's effective form, and the stages that restored Shakespeare's close in the nineteenth century did so as an act of scholarship and equally an act of taste, a recovery project rather than a popular revolt. The added scene was not driven out by audiences tiring of it. It was retired by editors and actor-managers persuaded that fidelity to the printed text outranked the accumulated wisdom of the repertory. That distinction matters to the final adjudication below.

## Gentleman Against Dowden: Two Verdicts on the Added Dialogue

The critical quarrel over Garrick's scene has a shape unusual in theatrical controversy: the earliest verdicts are the warmest, the later verdicts the coldest, and the evidence available to both sides is the same sixty-odd lines of verse. Francis Gentleman, the actor and dramatic critic whose writings carried the stage's own memory of the alteration's first decades, delivered the practitioner's verdict in terms that leave no room for appeal: no play, Gentleman declared, ever received greater advantage from alteration than this tragedy. The sentence is not the politeness of a colleague. Gentleman wrote as a professional judge of what played, and the claim is comparative across the whole adapted repertory: of all the eighteenth century's improvements upon Shakespeare, the tomb scene stood first in benefit conferred. The Theatrical Review's judgement of 1772, already quoted, supplies the reasoning the superlative implies: the catastrophe had a capacity for distress that Shakespeare had left unexploited, and the alteration exploited it.

Edward Dowden, preparing the play for the Arden Shakespeare at the end of the nineteenth century, read the same dialogue with a scholar's ear and reached the opposite pole. Dowden printed the conversation in his introduction, a courtesy that amounts to an exhibit for the prosecution, and his commentary on the Italian source tradition groups Otway and Garrick together: both, following the source's lead, varied from the Shakespearean close and in the variation struck false notes and fell into the phrases of convention and pseudo-pathos. The charge is precise. Pseudo-pathos names feeling that announces itself rather than arising, sentiment performed at the audience rather than discovered in the situation, and Dowden hears the added dialogue as a tissue of such announcements, the eighteenth century's taste for distress wearing the tragedy's clothes. Yet Dowden, to his credit as a critic, does not leave the verdict simple. Having printed the lines and condemned them, he concedes the stage fact in a sentence that undoes the comfort of every purely literary judgement: it is wonderful, he writes, what a good situation and a great actor can do upon the stage, even with words such as these. He imagines the kerchiefs moistened in the presence of Garrick's dying woes, or of Barry's and Mrs. Cibber's, and the concession stands as the editor's honest registration of evidence his taste could not explain away.

George Branam's twentieth-century scholarship reframed the quarrel by moving it from taste to fact. Where Gentleman praised Garrick's handiwork and Dowden blamed Garrick's ear, Branam examined Garrick's sources and found the handiwork substantially inherited: the acting version, Cibber's before it was Garrick's, carried the waking into Drury Lane, and Garrick's original contribution concentrated in the dialogue's execution rather than the device's conception. The finding does not settle the aesthetic dispute, but it disciplines both sides. Gentleman's superlative, if it stands, praises a tradition's accumulated craft as much as one man's invention. Dowden's condemnation, if it stands, condemns lines that were the period's common property burnished by its finest practitioner, a verdict on an era's stage language rather than on a single adapter's lapse.

The adjudication offered here splits the quarrel along the line Dowden himself drew and then crossed. As verse, judged on the page against the standard of the play it interrupts, Garrick's dialogue is second-order work: competent, affecting in situation, conventional in phrase, dependent for its finest effects on a machinery of recognition that the lines serve rather than transcend. Dowden's false notes are audible, and no purpose is served by pretending otherwise. As theatre, judged in the conditions the dialogue was built for, voices in a candlelit vault, a house trained in sensibility, the finest tragic pair of the age spending a poison's duration on a farewell, the scene was a masterpiece of its kind, and Gentleman's superlative records a professional consensus that the documentary record of a century's repertory confirms. The error enters when either verdict claims the other's territory: when stage success is offered as proof of poetic rank, or when poetic rank is offered as proof that the stage success was a delusion. Garrick's scene was a great theatrical event built from adequate verse, and Shakespeare's scene is great verse that Georgian theatre, for intelligible reasons, found inadequate as an event. Both halves of that sentence have to survive for the history to make sense.

## Playing the Added Scene: Beats, Breath, and the Faint

Read as a score for two performers, the added dialogue reveals a construction more disciplined than its reputation for effusion suggests. The scene divides into five movements, each with a distinct tempo and a distinct distribution of power. The first movement belongs to Romeo alone: the stir perceived, the joy declared, the rescue projected in imperatives, rise, quit, fly, a man spending strength he does not know is borrowed. The second movement transfers power to Juliet's error: her resistance slows the tempo, forces Romeo from action into explanation, and establishes the scene's ground rule, that every advance of feeling must be paid for with a withdrawal of strength. The third movement is the recognition, the fastest passage in the scene, voice, memory, and embrace arriving in a rush that mimics the lovers' earlier meetings, the sonnet at the feast, the voices in the orchard, all the play's recognitions compressed into a dozen lines. The fourth movement is the reversal, and Garrick places its trigger not in a speech but in a failure of the body: the support begged and refused, the two words that name the poison, after which the tempo broadens into the fifth movement, the farewell proper, where speeches lengthen, blame and absolution are exchanged at a dying man's pace, and the verse settles toward the stillness of the closing tableau.

The breath economy of the writing rewards attention. Romeo's speeches shorten as the poison advances, long periods of rescue rhetoric giving way to broken exclamations and unfinished constructions, a mind outrunning a failing frame, while Juliet's speeches lengthen in exact counterpoint as her strength returns from the potion's trance. The scene's real subject, enacted in the verse lengths before it is stated in any line, is an exchange of vitality between the two bodies on stage: life visibly passing out of one performer and into the other across the length of the dialogue, until the accounts balance at the moment of his death and her completed revival. Georgian acting, with its codified passions and its premium on visible transition, was built to display exactly this kind of transfer, and the painting and print tradition that fixed on the tomb scene fixed on its transfer point, the waking woman and the sinking man held in a single composition. A modern reader meeting the dialogue on the page, stripped of the bodies that were its notation, meets a libretto without its music and should judge the verse accordingly: the lines were engineered as cues for a physical event, and their simplicity in print is the simplicity of a score that expects performance to supply the harmony.

The faint that closes the added scene is the construction's most calculated stroke. Juliet has one great action remaining in the play, the dagger, and Garrick withholds it from his scene entirely, ending instead in collapse, because the faint performs three services the dagger could not. It gives the audience the united tableau, the pair composed as one body, which the print tradition then carried everywhere. It preserves the received close, the Friar, the watch, the dagger, the reconciliation, for the play's final movement, so that the addition inserts itself without amputating what followed. And it closes the duet on an image of feeling exceeding the body's capacity, the period's supreme sign of sensibility, at the precise moment when words have done all that words can do. The scene that began with a breath ends with a swoon, and the architecture is circular: a woman rises from counterfeit death to genuine life, spends that life in twenty speeches, and sinks again, while the man who woke her completes the opposite journey in the same minutes. Symmetry of that order is not an accident of effusion. It is design, and the design is the strongest internal evidence that Garrick's scene, whatever the rank of its verse, was built by a master of the stage's arithmetic.

## The Friar, the Watch, and the Fathers: What the Added Scene Does to Its Surroundings

An interpolation of sixty-odd lines re-weights everything that stands near it, and Garrick's scene quietly demotes three elements of Shakespeare's close that the original makes load-bearing. The first is the Friar. In Shakespeare, Laurence enters the vault as the scene's would-be explainer, the one living person who holds the whole design, and his long narrative to the Prince at the close is the mechanism by which Verona learns what the audience already knows. The speech is often cut in performance because its information is stale for the house, but its structural function is precise: public knowledge is assembled, blame is apportioned, pardons and punishments follow from an agreed account. In Garrick's disposition, the lovers themselves have already performed the explanation, to each other, in the audience's hearing, with the injured parties cross-examining the mistake at first hand. The Friar's narrative, when it arrives, reports a meeting the house has witnessed rather than a mystery the house has solved, and the official close shrinks toward ceremony. Garrick accepted the shrinkage, and the version's later history ratified it, since the acting tradition the alteration founded grew comfortable trimming the scene after Juliet's death, a habit the Yale history records persisting into Irving's century. The adapter's logic is consistent: a catastrophe fully explained by its victims needs less explaining afterward.

The second demoted element is the Watch and the machinery of discovery. Shakespeare times the officers' approach as pressure inside Juliet's waking, the noise that hurries her from grief to decision and makes her swiftness heroic. In a scene where the farewell has already occupied the interval the poison purchased, officialdom's arrival can only interrupt a completed action rather than compress an incomplete one, and the urgency drains accordingly. The third element is the fathers' reconciliation, and here the demotion is interpretive rather than mechanical. Shakespeare reserves the feud's public reckoning for the survivors, who bargain in gold over the bodies of children they cannot question. Garrick moves the reckoning forward into the vault and places it in a victim's mouth, flinty hearts arraigned while the evidence still breathes. The closing tableau of the fathers then confirms a verdict the audience has already heard delivered by the person with the strongest standing to deliver it. Productions formed on the altered text learned to play the reconciliation as an epitaph rather than a discovery, and the difference in temperature between the two closes, discovery against epitaph, is one of the clearest measures of what the waking changed without altering a single subsequent line's wording.

Dowden noticed a smaller symptom of the same temper at the very end of Garrick's text: the version concludes with a rhymed moral tag for the Prince, a couplet machinery that gathers the catastrophe into a sententious moral on feuds and woe, where Shakespeare's Prince closes in a register of sombreness, pardon, and punishment distributed without proverbial neatness. Dowden, no friend of the added dialogue, allowed more truth to Garrick's concluding tag than to the ponderous moralizing of weightier critics, and the allowance is shrewd. The tag is the added scene's philosophy stated in miniature: suffering should issue in a legible lesson, spoken aloud, in rhyme, by authority. Shakespeare's ending refuses that legibility, leaving its lessons to be quarried from silence and sequence. The whole quarrel between the two endings is present in the distance between a Prince who sums up and a Prince who adjourns for more talk of these sad things.

## A Century of the Altered Ending, in Performance and in Print

The chronology of the waking scene's reign repays exactness, because loose talk of a hundred years can hide the interesting texture of the fact. The adaptation was first performed in 1748. The Cushman restoration of Shakespeare's own ending reached the American stage in 1845, ninety-seven years later, and an academic survey of the adaptation's stage life gives the Garrick version precisely that span, ninety-seven years of popularity on the British stage from its first performance. Samuel Phelps restored the Shakespearean text at Sadler's Wells in 1847. Between those brackets, the altered ending was not a curiosity or an occasional revival. It was the ending, the form in which the tragedy existed for the playgoing public of two continents, and the Folger's summary of the adaptation's career states the scale without adornment: Garrick's version became the standard acting text in England and the United States for a hundred years, from 1750 through the middle of the nineteenth century.

Print kept the scene alive on a parallel track, and the print record is in some ways the more telling evidence, because printers answer to readers rather than to the economics of a single season. The Library of Congress holds no fewer than seven printings of the play that include the added deathbed conversation, and its survey traces the alteration's adoption across editions of 1794, 1814, 1819, and 1874, long after the adapters who might have defended it in person were dead. Prefaces to those editions argued the changes' merits for new generations, which means the waking was not merely inherited by inertia. It was re-chosen, repeatedly, by editors who had Shakespeare's text available and preferred the tomb with the conversation in it. Researchers who keep that printing history straight across the 1750 acting text, the later collected editions, and Dowden's scholarly reprint can hold the entire collation in one place with a research notebook such as [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/), where the tomb scene's variants, prefaces, and promptbook traditions sit in a single ordered file rather than scattered across a shelf of quartos and acting editions.

The reign was never quite total, and its limits define it. The altered text ruled the stage and the acting edition, while the study kept Shakespeare's words in circulation through the great editorial line from Rowe to Malone and beyond, so that Garrick's century was a century of divided custody: readers met one ending in the library and another in the theatre, and the divergence was so familiar that it excited little comment. Nor did every stage production take the adaptation whole. Cuts accumulated in other directions, and the Yale history notes that many nineteenth-century productions, Henry Irving's among them, followed eighteenth-century precedent in trimming heavily the scene that follows Juliet's death, compressing the reconciliation and the Prince's judgements. The repertory treated the play as a structure to be tuned for effect, and the waking scene, as its most reliable effect, was the last element any tuner surrendered. The Yale history's most striking single datum belongs to the reign's long tail: the additions to the death scene were still being played in an American production as late as 1899, more than half a century after the restoration that supposedly ended them.

## The Restoration of Shakespeare's Ending: Cushman and Phelps

The restoration, when it came, wore the dress of a novelty. Charlotte Cushman, the American tragedian whose Romeo became the most celebrated female assumption of the part in the century, restored Shakespeare's conception of the ending in the United States in 1845, playing Romeo herself opposite her sister Susan's Juliet. The choice was an artistic manifesto: the sisters' production advertised fidelity where the repertory had advertised feeling, and the tomb scene reverted to its original economy, a death completed before the waking, a waking that finds a corpse. Two years later Samuel Phelps, conducting at Sadler's Wells the great managerial campaign that returned Shakespeare's texts to the London stage with their poetry substantially intact, restored the original close in Britain in 1847. The sequence matters. The altered ending fell first in America, at the hands of a performer whose authority came from the stage rather than the study, and then in London as part of a systematic editorial programme in the theatre. Neither restoration was a popular uprising against Garrick's scene. Both were acts of conviction by practitioners persuaded that the play's printed text had claims the repertory's habits could not override.

What the restorers recovered, and what they surrendered, completes the adjudication begun with Gentleman and Dowden. They recovered the irony: the Friar entering too late, the cup read like a document, the dagger taken up at speed, and behind all of it the audience's solitary possession of the truth. They recovered Juliet's executive swiftness and Romeo's terrible mid-sentence extinction. They surrendered the duet, the arraignment of the fathers spoken by the dying, the farewell as rite, and the century-old assurance that the ending would wring an audience by the most direct route available. Modern performance has overwhelmingly ratified the restorers' choice, and the added dialogue survives as a document, printed by Dowden as a curiosity, studied by historians, and exhumed for inspection whenever the play's stage life is surveyed. The broader history gathered under [Staging the Tomb: The Final Scene on Stage](/2027/12/17/staging-the-tomb-final-scene/) follows the vault's staging across the centuries; the textual episode closed by Cushman and Phelps is the eighteenth century's, and its closing was a decision about what a classic is, whether a play belongs to its author's page or to the accumulated artistry of the stage that kept it alive.

## The Print Afterlife: Acting Editions, an 1882 Reversal, and Dowden's Exhibit

The stage restorations of 1845 and 1847 did not end the added scene's life, because print obeys a slower clock than the repertory. The Library of Congress survey of the altered printings shows the conversation surviving in editions of 1794, 1814, 1819, and 1874, a sequence that spans the entire period of the stage reign and extends nearly three decades past its supposed close, and the same survey marks the print turning point with equal precision: an edition of 1882 restored Rosaline to the text and let Romeo die at once after taking the poison, without the deathbed conversation. Read as a pair, the dates tell the alteration's whole bibliographic story. For over a century, editors with Shakespeare's text open beside them chose the waking; then, within the working lifetime of the restoration generation, the editorial mainstream crossed to the other side, and the conversation began its second life as a specimen.

The second life proved durable in a different way. Dowden's Arden edition of 1900 did not adopt the scene, but it preserved the scene, printing the dialogue entire in the introduction as the record of what the tragedy had been for a hundred years, accompanied by the double verdict examined earlier in this study. That editorial decision fixed the form in which later ages would mostly meet Garrick's writing: not as a playing text but as an exhibit, framed by commentary, introduced as a curiosity that had once drawn tears from ancestors of a more sentimental fashion. The framing flatters the modern reader's sense of progress, and the flattery should be resisted, because the exhibit's own evidence refuses to stay quaint. Sixty-odd lines that two rival houses fought over, that the century's best-selling theatrical image commemorated, and that printers kept re-choosing into the 1870s are not explained by calling the taste that sustained them a fashion. They are explained by the scene's genuine theatrical intelligence, its redistribution of knowledge from the house to the victims, which any age can test by the simple experiment of imagining the farewell played well. Dowden, to repeat the point, registered the experiment's result honestly in his own prose. The exhibit condemns the dialogue and keeps breaking its own frame to concede the dialogue's power, and that tension makes the Arden introduction, a century and more later, still the most useful single document of the scene's afterlife.

## The Waking After Garrick: Gounod and the Opera Stage

The added scene's most consequential afterlife lies outside the spoken theatre altogether, in the opera house. Dowden, introducing the dialogue he had just condemned, observed that the motive of the waking, the lovers' final exchange across the interval between poison and death, had been idealised and exalted in the opera of Gounod. Charles Gounod's *Roméo et Juliette*, first performed in Paris in 1867, builds its final scene on the Garrick disposition rather than on Shakespeare's: Juliet revives in the tomb while Romeo still lives, and the opera closes with the lovers singing together, recognition, farewell, and a shared approach to death sustained in music before the dagger finishes what the poison began. The migration is instructive. Opera, an art whose native currency is the duet and whose audiences expect feeling to be dwelt in rather than glanced through, found Garrick's distribution of knowledge more usable than Shakespeare's, because a heroine who wakes to a corpse can sing an aria but cannot sing a love duet, and the tomb duet is the opera's reason for existing. What the spoken stage of 1845 and 1847 retired as an infidelity, the operatic stage retained as a necessity, and the retention carried the waking to audiences across Europe and America who had never heard Garrick's name. Generations whose image of the ending was formed at the opera absorbed the altered disposition as the story's natural shape, which is one reason the Shakespearean close, when encountered first in a classroom, can strike new readers as a mistake in the printing: the culture's stored version of the tomb has Juliet waking in time, because for a hundred years of spoken performance and a further age of opera, she did.

## Verdict: A Great Theatrical Scene Built on a Lesser Tragedy

The evidence supports a verdict with two clauses, and neither clause should be surrendered to soften the other. Garrick's tomb scene is the most successful single alteration in the stage history of the play, measured by the only currencies the stage finally honours: it was fought over by rival houses, fixed in the visual imagination of a century, carried across an ocean, re-chosen by printers for generations, and surrendered at last to conviction rather than to boredom. It achieved all of that because it understood its audience's hunger with precision and fed that hunger with high professional craft, a duet of recognition engineered against a poison's clock by the finest actor-manager of the age. Dowden's kerchiefs were real, and Gentleman's superlative was earned.

Garrick's tomb scene is also, as an account of what this tragedy is, a diminution, and the diminution can be located exactly. Shakespeare's ending insists that the lovers are killed by a world whose messages fail, in a vault where the truth arrives minutes after it could have saved anyone, and the insistence is the play's final meaning: the feud's deepest product is not hatred but the conditions under which love's own communications break down. Garrick's ending lets the communications succeed. The truth is spoken, heard, forgiven, and mourned in common, and what kills the lovers shrinks to a poison already swallowed and a pair of flinty fathers already cursed. The first ending indicts a system. The second arraigns a set of persons and consoles the audience for the arraignment with a farewell. A century of playgoers chose the consolation, and the choice built the play's modern fame; the restorers who withdrew the consolation recovered the indictment, and the recovery built the play's modern text. The added scene deserves neither the vandal's name its detractors reach for nor the improver's crown its first century awarded it. Garrick did not ruin the ending of the tragedy. He replaced it with the ending of a different, lesser, and magnificently playable one, and the stage kept his version for a hundred years because magnificently playable is a quality the stage, on most nights of most centuries, is right to prize.

One measure of the replacement's power survives every change of regime in taste: the waking refuses to leave the story. It persisted through the spoken stage's fidelity revolution by emigrating into opera, it persists in the instinct of adapters who keep reinventing it without knowing its pedigree, and it persists in the classroom surprise that greets Shakespeare's actual close, the sense that a meeting has been missed which the story owed its audience. That instinct is Garrick's true monument, more durable than his verse and more honest than his Advertisement. He located the exact point at which the tragedy's design withholds the thing every audience desires, a final exchange between the two people the play has taught the house to love, and he proved that the desire could be satisfied at a price the eighteenth century was willing to pay and the nineteenth was not. The price list, itemized across the preceding sections, is the scene's permanent contribution to the criticism of the play: irony, arraignment, agency, consolation, each costed against each gain. Critics will go on disputing the exchange rate. The transaction itself, once seen, cannot be unseen, and no account of the tragedy's ending is complete that treats the added scene as a footnote rather than as the rival ending it was for the century in which the play conquered the modern stage.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch tomb comparison, and what does it set side by side?

The InsightCrunch tomb comparison is the table in this study that places Shakespeare's final scene and Garrick's additional scene in parallel columns across nine elements: the poison's speed, the waking moment, the final exchange, each lover's knowledge at death, the indictment of the fathers, the closing tableau, the governing effect, and the stage life of each version. Its purpose is to make the alteration inspectable at a glance. Read down the columns and the change reduces to a shift of minutes with total consequences: move the waking earlier, slow the poison, and the ending converts from dramatic irony, in which the audience knows everything and the lovers meet only as corpse and mourner, into mutual recognition, in which the truth is shared aloud before death at the price of the irony.

### Q: What change did Garrick make to the apothecary line so his Romeo could survive long enough to speak?

Garrick reversed the line's operative word. Shakespeare's Romeo, drinking in the vault at 5.3, cries "O true apothecary! Thy drugs are quick. Thus with a kiss I die," and the speed is a contract: no interval exists in which new information could arrive. The Library of Congress, comparing the altered printings, records Garrick's reading as "Thy drugs are not so quick." The inserted negation manufactures the interval the additional scene requires. A poison that is not so quick has a duration, the duration is stage time, and the stage time contains the lovers' entire added conversation, with Romeo's strength failing by degrees across it until speech itself runs out.

### Q: How long is the added tomb conversation in the Library of Congress count of Garrick's printings?

The Library of Congress counts a final conversation of sixty-seven lines between Romeo and Juliet in Garrick's version. The figure comes from the Library's survey of its own holdings, no fewer than seven printings of the play that carry the added deathbed conversation. Other authorities tally the exchange slightly differently because the conversation breaks into many short speeches that can be counted as lines or as part-lines: the Folger Shakespeare Library describes nearly sixty lines of dialogue made possible by the slow-working poison. Either count establishes the scale of the intervention. Garrick roughly doubled the amount of life the text allows Juliet after her waking and gave Romeo a second death scene after the first had ended.

### Q: Which Italian storyteller did Garrick name in his Advertisement as the source of the waking?

Garrick named Matteo Bandello, the Italian novelist from whose telling Shakespeare had borrowed the subject of the play. The Advertisement argues that Bandello had made Juliet wake in the tomb before Romeo died, and that Shakespeare omitted the circumstance not from judgement but because the French and English translations through which the story reached him had left the beat out. The argument is the foundation of Garrick's whole defence: the added scene presents itself not as an invention imposed on the play but as a recovery of material the play's own source possessed and its line of transmission lost. Whether the genealogy is complete is another question, since the Advertisement says nothing about Theophilus Cibber's recent acting version.

### Q: How many of Otway's lines did Garrick admit to using in the added scene?

Garrick admitted to two or three of Otway's introductory lines, and no more. The admission appears in the Advertisement to his altered text, framed as a plea in mitigation: an endeavour to supply the failure of so great a master should not be deemed arrogant, and the making use of two or three of Otway's introductory lines should not be accounted plagiarism. The calibration is exact. A debt small enough to be numbered is a debt that cannot be enlarged by enemies, and Garrick had enemies making the attempt, since the Advertisement goes on to answer persons who had tried to strip him of the scene's merit by ascribing the whole scene to Otway's *Caius Marius*.

### Q: What did Garrick profess to wonder about Otway's handling of the waking circumstance?

Garrick professed wonder that so great a dramatic genius had not worked up a scene of more nature, terror, and distress from the circumstance. The sentence in the Advertisement performs two operations at once. It concedes Otway's priority in using the waking, which costs Garrick nothing he intended to keep, since his claim rested on execution rather than conception. It then declares Otway's execution thin, which clears the ground for Garrick's own fuller working of the same beat. Praise engineered to diminish is the Advertisement's characteristic move, and the Otway passage is its clearest instance: the predecessor is honoured as a genius precisely so that his failure to exploit the device can license the successor's attempt.

### Q: Which acting pair opposed Garrick and Bellamy in the Battle of the Romeos, and at which theatre?

Spranger Barry and Susannah Maria Cibber opposed them at Covent Garden, while Garrick and George Anne Bellamy held the parts at Drury Lane. In the autumn of 1750 the two houses played the tragedy against each other night after night, and London divided into parties that attended both productions and compared the rival pairs speech by speech. Barry's Romeo was prized for melting tenderness, Garrick's for fire and rapid transition; Cibber was the age's standard of pathetic delivery, Bellamy a younger and more impulsive presence. Because both acting texts carried the waking device, the contest made the added tomb scene the recognised ground on which tragic pairs were measured, and a scene rival houses fight over becomes a scene no manager can drop.

### Q: Why does Garrick's waking Juliet at first resist the hands that draw her from the tomb?

She resists because she wakes with exactly one fixed idea, the forced marriage the potion was drunk to escape. Garrick writes the waking as genuine disorientation: cold, darkness, an unknown presence, and hands moving her body before her mind has reassembled the situation. Her fixed idea supplies the interpretation nearest to hand, that she is being carried off to the wedding with Paris, and she fights the hands on that reading: her strength may fail her, she declares, but her will is unmoved; she will not wed Paris, for Romeo is her husband. The resistance is the mirror of Romeo's error. He took a living woman for a corpse; she takes her rescuer for her persecutor, and recognition has to arrive by voice before it can arrive by sight.

### Q: What physical signs tell Garrick's Juliet that Romeo is dying before he names the poison?

She reads his body directly: a trembling voice, pale lips, and swimming eyes, summed in her verdict that death is in his face. The moment matters because of who performs it. In Shakespeare's close, the audience reads Juliet's body for signs of life that Romeo misreads; in Garrick's close, the diagnostic gaze passes to Juliet, who reads Romeo's body accurately and at once. Her cry of "Cruel poison" is wrung from him first, when she begs support and he answers that he has no strength but wants her feeble aid, and her reading of the face confirms within a speech what the word announced. The scene's knowledge travels between the lovers through the senses, voice, touch, and sight, before it travels in explanations.

### Q: How does Garrick's Romeo explain his fatal mistake inside the added dialogue?

He confesses it as a sequence of misreadings, in the plainest lines Garrick wrote for the scene. He thought her dead; distracted at the sight, he drank poison with fatal speed, kissed her cold lips, and found within her arms a precious grave. The confession does work that Shakespeare's structure forbids: it delivers the tragedy's whole mechanism, the miscarried letter's consequence, the false report believed, the poison bought in Mantua, into the hearing of the one person entitled to judge it. Juliet's reply converts the exposition into the scene's emotional verdict: "And did I wake for this!" The explanation is also Romeo's defence, offered to the injured party and accepted in an embrace, so the added dialogue builds absolution into the catastrophe that the original leaves unspoken and ungranted.

### Q: What does Garrick's Juliet do as the added dialogue closes over Romeo's body?

She faints across his body, in the stage direction Edward Dowden's printing preserves. Her last speech in the added dialogue claims the only union left, that fate marries the pair in death and they are one, with no power able to part them, and the faint embodies the claim: two living shapes collapsed into the posture of the double corpse the play's close will shortly make literal. The tableau was the alteration's visual signature, the image Benjamin Wilson's painting of Garrick and Bellamy carried into the print shops, and it explains much of the scene's hold. Shakespeare's Juliet ends the waking sequence in motion, dagger in hand. Garrick's ends it, for the space of the added scene, in stillness, as half of a composed picture of united death.

### Q: What did Charlotte Cushman restore in 1845, and who played Juliet opposite her Romeo?

Charlotte Cushman restored Shakespeare's own ending, with Romeo's death completed before Juliet wakes and the waking addressed to a corpse and the Friar. She played Romeo herself, opposite her sister Susan Cushman's Juliet, in the United States in 1845, and the restoration was received as an artistic manifesto: fidelity advertised where the repertory had advertised feeling. The date closes the Garrick version's long reign with neatness, since the adaptation had first been performed in 1748, ninety-seven years before, a span an academic survey of the version's stage life gives exactly. American audiences, who had learned the tragedy in Garrick's shape since the Hallam company's tours of the 1750s, were the first to receive the original close back as a stage event.

### Q: Who restored Shakespeare's ending in London in 1847, and at which theatre?

Samuel Phelps restored it at Sadler's Wells in 1847. Phelps conducted at that theatre the great managerial campaign that returned Shakespeare's texts to the London stage with their poetry substantially intact, and the recovery of the original tomb scene formed part of the programme: the death completed in silence, the Friar arriving behind the event, Juliet's twenty lines of life after waking, the cup read as evidence, the dagger taken at speed. The restoration's character deserves emphasis. Garrick's ending was not hissed off the stage or abandoned by bored audiences. It was retired by practitioners persuaded that the printed text's claims outranked a century of repertory habit, which makes 1847 a date in the history of editing as much as in the history of acting.

### Q: In which years of printings did the Library of Congress find Garrick's altered ending still being adopted?

The Library of Congress traces editions of 1794, 1814, 1819, and 1874 adopting Garrick's alterations, including the added conversation. The sequence is the strongest documentary evidence that the waking was re-chosen rather than merely inherited. The 1794 edition appeared while the alteration's first audiences still lived; the editions of 1814 and 1819 carried it deep into the nineteenth century's reading public; the 1874 printing adopted it decades after Phelps had restored Shakespeare's text on the London stage, in the period when the study and the theatre had formally separated their custodies of the play. Prefaces to these editions argued the changes' merits for new readers, so the scene's print afterlife represents repeated acts of editorial preference, not a single act of survival.

### Q: What superlative did Francis Gentleman attach to the alteration of this tragedy?

Francis Gentleman declared that no play ever received greater advantage from alteration than this tragedy. Gentleman wrote as a practitioner, an actor and dramatic critic carrying the stage's own memory of the alteration's first decades, and the sentence is comparative across the whole adapted repertory of the eighteenth century, not a compliment confined to one scene. Of all the period's improvements upon Shakespeare, the tomb scene stood first in benefit conferred. The superlative matters to the critical quarrel because it represents the verdict of the profession that had to fill houses with the scene nightly, set against the later editorial verdict of Edward Dowden, who heard the same lines as convention and pseudo-pathos. The disagreement between the two men is the disagreement this study adjudicates: stage value and poetic value measured on different scales.

### Q: What did the Theatrical Review of 1772 say Shakespeare had left unheightened in the catastrophe?

The Review said Garrick had found that Shakespeare had neglected to heighten the catastrophe to so great a degree of distress as the catastrophe was capable of being carried to. The judgement explains the alteration's appeal in the culture that produced it. Distress, in the Georgian theatre of sensibility, was a quantity that a scene could hold more or less of, and heightening it counted as a service to the audience's moral faculty, since feeling with another's suffering certified a rightly formed heart. Garrick's waking concentrates distress by removing the irony that dissipates it into reflection: every declaration lands on the person it addresses, no feeling is wasted on a hearer who cannot hear, and the house's sympathy flows unobstructed for the length of a slow poison.

### Q: How did Edward Dowden hold two opposing verdicts on Garrick's dialogue in a single introduction?

Dowden condemned the lines as literature and conceded their power as theatre, in consecutive movements of the same introduction. He printed the conversation entire, grouped Garrick with Otway as adapters who had varied from the Shakespearean close into false notes and the phrases of convention and pseudo-pathos, and then wrote the sentence that undoes every purely literary judgement of the scene: it is wonderful what a good situation and a great actor can do upon the stage, even with words such as these. He pictured kerchiefs moistened before the dying woes of Garrick, or of Barry and Mrs. Cibber. The doubleness is not indecision. It is an editor honestly registering evidence his taste could not explain away, and the adjudication in this study follows his line exactly: second-order verse, first-order theatrical event.

### Q: What did George Branam's study of the version's genesis establish about Theophilus Cibber's share in it?

George C. Branam's study, published in *Shakespeare Quarterly* in 1984 under the title "The Genesis of David Garrick's Romeo and Juliet," established that Garrick's acting version was substantially inherited from Theophilus Cibber's version of 1744. Cibber had already retained the waking feature from Otway's *Caius Marius* and already cut the references to Rosaline that Garrick's version also lacks. The Yale Shakespeare's stage history states the conclusion plainly: the version Garrick used in 1750 was substantially Cibber's. The finding disciplines the whole critical tradition around the scene. Garrick's contribution, on the documentary record, concentrates in the added dialogue's execution, the production's star power, and the printed defence of the scene in the Advertisement, rather than in the conception of the waking itself, which had been standing in the London repertory for four years before his name went on the bill.

### Q: What knowledge does Garrick's ending grant Romeo that Shakespeare's Romeo dies without?

Garrick's ending grants Romeo the knowledge that Juliet lives, delivered in time for the knowledge to be acted on in speech. Shakespeare's Romeo dies holding a false belief at the centre of his final speech: he addresses Juliet as a corpse, marvels at the colour in her lips and cheeks, and extinguishes himself within arm's reach of the waking he fails to foresee by minutes. Garrick's Romeo hears her voice, learns the Friar's plot from her confusion and his own confession, arraigns the fathers as the catastrophe's authors, and dies as the tragedy's prosecutor rather than its most deceived victim. The difference is the alteration in one formulation. Shakespeare's hero dies inside the story's central mistake. Garrick's hero dies having escaped it, and the escape is both the scene's consolation and its cost.

### Q: How should an essay use the InsightCrunch tomb comparison to argue that Garrick traded irony for recognition?

An essay should build the argument from the table's paired rows rather than from general impressions of the two endings. Set the poison's speed first: Shakespeare's "quick" seals the irony by leaving no interval for knowledge to travel, while Garrick's "not so quick" manufactures the interval recognition requires. Set the knowledge rows next: Romeo dying in a false belief against Romeo dying informed, Juliet reconstructing the story from a cup against Juliet receiving it as confession. Set the governing effects last: the house holding the truth alone against the truth spoken aloud and shared. The conclusion writes itself from the evidence. Garrick's scene purchases mutual recognition, absolution, and a farewell rite, and pays with the miscarried-message design that gives Shakespeare's ending its meaning, and any verdict on the alteration has to price both sides of the trade.
