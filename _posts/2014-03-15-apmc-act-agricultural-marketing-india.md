There is no APMC Act. Anyone searching for a single central statute that governs how Indian farmers sell their produce is looking for something that has never existed, because the APMC Act is not one law but a family of state statutes, each enacted by a state legislature, each administered by that state's market committees, and each different from its neighbours in the fees it charges, the commodities it covers and the intermediaries it licences. That single fact explains more about Indian agricultural marketing than any other, and it is the fact most often got wrong. Commentators speak of "the APMC Act" being reformed, defied or abolished as though it were a law of Parliament. What they mean is one member of a family of more than two dozen state laws, and the member they mean changes with the state they are standing in.

![The APMC Acts and the mandi system in India, showing how state market laws, market committees and licences govern the sale of farm produce - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The family resemblance across these statutes is real, and it is strong enough that a reader who understands one member understands the design of all of them. Each state Act performs the same five moves. It empowers the state government to declare an area a market area and to specify the agricultural produce to which the Act applies there. It requires the first wholesale transaction in that notified produce to take place inside a market yard or sub-yard run under the Act. It constitutes a market committee, part elected and part nominated, to administer that yard, build and maintain its infrastructure, and enforce the rules of trade inside it. It confines the business of buying, selling and intermediating to persons the committee has licensed. And it funds the whole arrangement by a market fee levied on the transactions the statute has channelled into the yard. Around those five moves, states have built very different systems: the procurement-integrated mandis of Punjab, the large commodity yards of Maharashtra, the electronically unified markets of Karnataka. But the skeleton is the same everywhere, and the skeleton is where the argument lives.

For these laws were written as protection. The regulated market was invented, first in the colonial period and then across the states after independence, to rescue the farmer from a transaction in which he was systematically cheated: cheated on the weight of his produce, cheated by deductions he could not verify, cheated by prices quoted in a market where the buyers were few, organised and bound to him by his debts. The state answered that transaction with a place. Inside the notified yard, weighing would be supervised, sale would be by open auction in the presence of a committee official, the charges that could be taken from the farmer's price would be fixed and published, and no one could trade who had not taken a licence and submitted to the committee's discipline. The farmer would sell to the highest bidder in public, and the record of the sale would exist.

The reversal the reader has to understand is that the protection worked by compulsion, and compulsion created the thing it policed. Because the produce had to come to the yard, the yard's licensed population acquired a statutory position between every farmer and every buyer in the market area. Because the number of licences was finite and committees were cautious about adding to it, that position became scarce, and a scarce statutory position earns a return whether or not its holder adds anything to the transaction. Because the commission agent who stood at the centre of the yard also lent the farmer money for seed, fertiliser and family needs, the economic relationship outgrew the licence that described it, and a farmer in debt to his commission agent does not experience the auction as a free choice among bidders. The laws came to be described, by economists and by many farmers, as the main obstacle between the farmer and a fair price, and the description is not a paradox. It is the predictable consequence of a design that separated the place of sale from the freedom to sell, and then made the place compulsory.

This article treats the state APMC statutes as what they are: a statute family with a common architecture, a shared origin in farmer protection, and sharply different state lives. It explains the constitutional allocation that makes agricultural marketing a state subject and a central Act impossible without constitutional change. It works through the three state models that show the family's range, follows a consignment of produce through a mandi step by step, examines how the licence and the credit relationship interact, sets out what the levies actually are and who bears them, and then takes the reform record in sequence: the model Acts the Centre circulated in 2003, 2017 and 2018, the electronic market laid over the statutes from 2016, the one complete repeal, in Bihar in 2006, and the central legislation of 2020 that built a parallel trade area outside the mandis before being itself repealed. It closes with the two cases, for deregulation and for retention, stated as strongly as their serious advocates state them, and with the distinction on which the whole debate turns: the licence restriction and the price discovery infrastructure are two different functions that most reform proposals wrongly treat as one.

## The transaction the mandi was built to correct

To see why legislators reached for compulsion, the reader has to reconstruct the transaction the regulated market replaced. The cultivator who grew a surplus in the unregulated countryside sold it, most often, in the village or at a periodic rural market, to a trader who was frequently also his creditor. The grower had borrowed for the season's inputs, sometimes for a marriage or a funeral or a failed monsoon, and the loan was rarely a documented bank transaction. It was an advance from the same man who would buy the harvest, and the harvest was its security. When the crop was ready, the price was struck between a seller who had to sell, because the debt was due and the grain could not be stored, and a buyer who could wait, because waiting cost him nothing. Everything about the setting leaned the same way: the scales were the buyer's, the deductions for moisture, dirt and admixture were assessed by the buyer's eye, and the account into which the price was entered was kept in the buyer's book.

The catalogue of abuses that the early marketing enquiries compiled was concrete rather than rhetorical. Weights were manipulated by scales weighted against the seller and by practices such as taking a quantity from each bag as a perquisite. Deductions were taken under a vocabulary of customary charges that the farmer had never agreed to and could not audit: charges for the broker, for the weighman, for charity, for the upkeep of religious places, for the labour that loaded and unloaded. Prices were formed in private negotiation, often by a method designed to exclude the seller from knowledge of the price itself, the notorious practice of bargaining through fingers pressed under a cloth, so that the farmer learned what his produce had fetched only when the dealing was done. And behind every abuse stood the structural fact that the farmer could not take his produce elsewhere: the next market was far, transport was costly, the crop was perishable or the debt was pressing, and the local buyers knew one another and had no reason to bid against one another for a seller who could not leave.

Regulation attacked each element of that transaction by name. A notified yard with standard weights and supervised weighment answered the manipulated scale. A prescribed schedule of charges, displayed in the market and exhaustive of what could be deducted, answered the customary levies. Sale by open auction, called out in the hearing of the seller and of rival buyers, answered the cloth-covered bargain. Licensing answered the anonymous buyer: a trader or commission agent who traded in the yard held a permission the committee could suspend or cancel, which gave the committee a sanction against the man who short-paid or delayed payment, and the licence made the population of the yard knowable. The committee itself, with farmer representation in its composition, was meant to give the sellers a governing presence in the place where they sold. Each piece of the design corresponds to a documented abuse, and the correspondence is the reason the statutes cannot be understood as bureaucratic meddling in search of a purpose. They were a point by point reply to a market that had failed the weaker party in identifiable ways.

What the design also did, from the first day, was to decide where trade would happen and to police the boundary. Regulation of practices inside a market does not require that all trade be driven into it; the early legislators chose the stronger instrument anyway, because a yard that traders could ignore would be a yard without volume, and without volume the auction thins and the infrastructure goes unfunded. So the statutes made the yard a monopoly of a specific kind: not a monopoly seller or buyer, but a monopoly venue for the first wholesale transaction in notified produce across a defined territory. The market area of a committee could be a cluster of villages, a taluka, in some states an entire district or more, and within that area the farmer's lawful choices about where to sell narrowed to the yards the Act provided. The protection and the compulsion were the same clause. Every later argument about the APMC system is an argument about whether that identity still holds: whether the venue monopoly remains the price of the protection, or has become a burden that protects the intermediaries the yard was built to discipline.

## Cotton, Berar and the colonial origin of the regulated market

The regulated market in India began as an instrument of commodity supply before it became an instrument of farmer welfare, and the order matters because it explains the form. The first produce to attract official attention was raw cotton. The textile mills of Lancashire wanted Indian cotton of reliable quality at predictable prices, and what they received was cotton adulterated with dirt and moisture, mixed across varieties and traded through chains in which the grower's return bore little relation to the export price. The colonial state's response was to create, in the cotton tracts of Berar administered through the Hyderabad Residency, a market where sale would happen at a declared place under official supervision. The first such regulated market was established at Karanja in 1886 under an order of the Hyderabad Residency, and the experiment was given statutory form in the Berar Cotton and Grain Markets Act of 1897, which empowered the authorities to declare markets, constitute committees to supervise them and regulate the practices of those who traded in them. The committee, the declared place, the supervised transaction: the institutional DNA of every later APMC statute is present in that cotton law.

The idea spread slowly and commodity by commodity. Bombay legislated for cotton markets in the nineteen twenties, following the Indian Cotton Committee of 1917, and the decisive general endorsement came from the Royal Commission on Agriculture, which reported in 1928 and treated marketing as the missing half of agricultural policy. The Commission's reasoning was that improvement in production was wasted on a cultivator who could not sell to advantage, and that the remedy lay in regulated markets governed by proper rules, with standard weights, defined charges and representative committees. The Central Banking Enquiry Committee of 1931 supported the same direction. In 1938 the agricultural marketing department of the central government prepared a model bill on which provinces could base general legislation, and the Bombay Agricultural Produce Markets Act of 1939 became the first comprehensive provincial statute, extending regulation beyond cotton to agricultural produce generally and supplying the template on which the post-independence state Acts were drawn. The Madras Commercial Crops Markets Act of 1933 and similar provincial laws filled in the map unevenly, so that at independence the country had regulated markets in patches, mostly where an export crop had forced the issue.

The patchwork of the nineteen thirties and nineteen forties deserves a moment more, because it is the family's first draft. The Central Provinces and Berar legislated for agricultural markets in the nineteen thirties on the Berar foundation; Hyderabad, the princely state whose residency order began the story, enacted its own agricultural market legislation in the nineteen thirties for the territories it ruled directly; the Madras Commercial Crops Markets Act of 1933 took the narrower path of regulating commercial crops rather than produce generally; and Bombay's Act of 1939 took the broader one. When the reorganisation of states in 1956 redrew the map, whole districts carried these different inheritances into new states, which is why a consolidating Act like Karnataka's of 1966 had three predecessor statutes to repeal for three regions of one state, and why the family's members still differ in details that are, at root, the fingerprints of provinces that ceased to exist before independence.

After 1947 the purpose changed and the coverage generalised. The new state's concern was not the purity of an export staple but the condition of the food producer, and the planning era treated regulated markets as infrastructure of development, alongside irrigation and credit cooperatives. Most states enacted their marketing statutes in the nineteen sixties and nineteen seventies, converting the older patchwork into a near national system of state laws. The count of regulated markets grew from a few hundred at the start of the nineteen fifties to some two and a half thousand principal market yards with several thousand sub-yards in the counts maintained by the Directorate of Marketing and Inspection. The Green Revolution raised the stakes of the system it built: the high-yielding wheat and rice belts produced marketed surpluses on a scale the older countryside had never generated, and those surpluses moved, by statutory design, through the mandis, where the state's procurement agencies also came to buy. The yard became the junction at which the farmer, the trader, the commission agent and the government's food policy all met, and the statute that governed the yard became, by accumulation rather than intention, one of the most consequential pieces of economic legislation in the states that had it.

Two features of this origin story carry analytical weight for everything that follows. The first is that regulation preceded protection as the official motive and the two were never cleanly separated: a system designed partly to organise supply for buyers and the state will not behave as a pure farmers' charter, and the market fee, which funds the yard out of the value of what passes through it, was there from the Berar design onward. The second is that the post-independence expansion was a state by state construction undertaken over three decades, which is why the family exists at all. No constituent moment ever created an Indian law of agricultural marketing. Twenty odd legislatures, working from shared models at different times under different political pressures, created twenty odd laws whose similarity is the similarity of copies, and whose differences, in fees, coverage and the power of the intermediary, are the record of what each state's politics did to the common design.

## Why there is no central Act: the constitutional allocation

The absence of a central APMC Act is not an oversight that a determined Parliament could repair by ordinary legislation. It is a consequence of the Seventh Schedule, and any reader who wants to follow the politics of agricultural marketing reform has to begin with the entries. Agriculture itself, including agricultural education and research, protection against pests and prevention of plant diseases, is Entry 14 of the State List. Markets and fairs are Entry 28 of the State List. Trade and commerce within a state are Entry 26 of the State List, and the production, supply and distribution of goods are Entry 27, both expressly made subject to the Concurrent List entry on trade in specified commodities. The state legislature's competence over the place where produce is sold, the persons who may trade there and the charges levied on the transaction is therefore not inferred or residual. It is written into the constitutional text three times over, and a state APMC statute rests on the firmest ground the federal scheme offers. The division of legislative power between the Union and the states, entry by entry, is mapped in the series treatment of [the three lists of the Seventh Schedule](/2018/09/15/seventh-schedule-lists-explained/), and agricultural marketing is the field where that division bites hardest on economic policy.

Parliament's foothold in the field is real but differently shaped. Entry 33 of the Concurrent List covers trade and commerce in, and the production, supply and distribution of, the products of industries Parliament has declared it expedient for the Union to control, and then a named set of commodities: foodstuffs including edible oilseeds and oils, cattle fodder including oilcakes and other concentrates, raw cotton whether ginned or unginned and cotton seed, and raw jute. Entry 42 of the Union List gives Parliament inter-state trade and commerce. Between them, these entries let the Centre legislate about trade in foodstuffs across the country and about the movement of produce between states, which is a power over the commodity and its journey. They do not straightforwardly give the Centre the state subject of the market as a place: the constitution of market committees, the licensing of the yard's population, the levy of the fee that funds the yard. The distinction between regulating a market and regulating trade in the thing sold in the market is the constitutional fault line of the whole subject, and it was the line along which the central legislation of 2020 was attacked and defended, as the next cluster article examines in detail.

Two practical consequences follow from the allocation and both shape the reform story told later in this article. The first is that central reform of agricultural marketing has historically had to proceed by persuasion: the Centre drafts a model law, circulates it to the states, offers plan funds and scheme benefits to states that adopt its features, and counts adoption state by state and provision by provision, with results that arrive over decades and never completely. The model Acts of 2003, 2017 and 2018 are all instruments of that persuasion strategy, and their fate, partial adoption braided with local amendment, is what persuasion in a federal system looks like. The second consequence is that the states guard the field. Market fees are state and committee revenue, market committees are patronage and representation in rural districts, and the licensing power touches organised commercial interests in every market town. A state government asked to dilute its APMC statute is being asked to surrender revenue, appointments and a lever over rural trade at once, which is why the same reform proposals recur across twenty years of committee reports with adoption always incomplete.

A second constitutional conversation runs beneath the first and concerns movement rather than markets. Part XIII of the Constitution declares the freedom of trade, commerce and intercourse throughout the territory of India, and subjects it to a scheme of permitted restrictions in the public interest. A state marketing system touches that freedom at two points: its confinement of trade to notified yards is a regulation of where commerce happens, and its fee, charged on produce that may be bound for another state, is a burden on commerce in motion. The jurisprudence of the freedom, built in the tax cases of the nineteen sixties in which the Supreme Court worked out when a charge is a barrier to trade and when it is a compensatory payment for facilities the state provides, supplies the frame in which a market fee is defended: a charge that pays for the roads, yards and services the trade uses regulates commerce rather than restricting it, while a charge unconnected to any facility begins to look like a toll on movement itself. The reader will recognise the Kewal Krishan Puri logic arriving by a second road. The two doctrines, the fee's correlation with services under the law of fees, and the trade freedom's tolerance of compensatory charges, are cousins, and together they explain the family's fiscal constitution: the mandi may charge for what it builds, and its difficulties begin where the charge outruns the building. Inter-state movement adds the practical grievance the e-NAM reforms targeted, since produce crossing a state line historically met a second state's committee, licence expectations and fee at the border of the system, so that the legal map of twenty state markets sat across a national trade the Constitution had declared free, a friction no single state could remove because no single state had created it.

### Who fixes the market fee in a mandi?

The market fee is fixed under the state Act: the statute names the rate, caps it for the government or committee to levy up to, or leaves it to rules and notifications. The committee collects and spends it through its market fund, but it does not invent the rate, and the Centre has no power to set it.

The rate is set under the state Act, not by the committee acting alone and not by the Centre. Each state's statute either names the rate, caps it as a maximum the state government or the committee may levy up to, or leaves it to rules and notifications issued under the Act. The committee collects the fee on transactions in its yard and applies it to its market fund, out of which the yard is staffed, maintained and developed. Because the power to fix the rate sits in state law, the fee differs from state to state and is one of the clearest expressions of the federal character of the whole system: there is no Indian market fee, there are only state market fees.

The fee's legal character matters to one recurring misunderstanding. The market fee is a fee for the services of the regulated market, levied under the state's marketing statute on the transaction, generally collected from the buyer side of the trade, and distinct both from a tax on the produce and from the commission the seller's agent earns. States that have capped the fee in the statute itself, as Punjab has, produce a number that can be read off the Act; states that have left the rate to notification produce a number that lives in their rules and gazette notifications and changes without any amendment of the statute. Either way, the mandi does not invent its charge, and neither does the trader: the charge is a creature of state law, which is why arguments about its level are always, in the end, arguments addressed to state legislatures.

## The common architecture of the state APMC Acts

Strip any state marketing statute to its working parts and the same machine appears. The machine begins with two declarations by the state government. The first declares a market area, the territory within which the Act's discipline will run; the second specifies the agricultural produce to which the Act applies in that area. The second declaration is as important as the first and easier to overlook. The Acts define agricultural produce widely, covering produce of agriculture, horticulture, animal husbandry and allied activities, but the operative coverage in a given market area is the notified list, and the list is a schedule that can be amended by notification. A commodity not notified for an area is outside the compulsion for that area, which is why the delisting of a commodity, as happened with fruits and vegetables in several states, is a complete if partial deregulation: the statute remains, the yard remains, but that commodity is free of both.

Inside the declared area, the Act establishes the market proper: a principal market yard and, under it, sub-yards at smaller settlements, each a defined place where the regulated transaction occurs. The statute then attaches its central prohibition to that geography. Produce notified for the area may be bought and sold, in the wholesale transaction the Act targets, only in the yard or sub-yard or at another place the Act permits, and trading functions within the market, as trader, commission agent, broker, weighman, measurer, warehouseman and the rest, may be performed only under a licence from the market committee. The exemptions matter and are commonly misunderstood. The farmer selling his own produce is regulated as to the place and method of the wholesale sale, but the Acts are built around licensing the trade side of the yard, and a producer selling at retail to a consumer, a cultivator selling to a neighbour, or produce moving under an exemption the Act or rules recognise, stands differently in the scheme. The compulsion is on the assembling wholesale market, the point at which the produce of many farms is gathered for onward sale, and it is there that the statute concentrates the auction, the licence and the fee.

The market committee is the machine's governor. Committees are constituted under the Act for each market area, with membership drawn from the constituencies of the yard: agriculturists of the area form the largest bloc in the standard design, joined by representatives of traders and commission agents, of cooperative marketing societies, of local bodies such as the panchayat and the municipality, and by officials and nominees of the state government. Members are partly elected and partly nominated, terms are fixed by the Act, and state governments retain powers of supervision that in practice have often extended to superseding an elected committee and running the market through an administrator. Above the committees stands, in most states, a state agricultural marketing board, a statutory body under the Act that coordinates the committees, channels development funds, maintains a marketing development fund fed by contributions from committee income, and gives the state a single institutional hand on the system. The committee maintains its own market fund: the fees it collects, licence charges, rents of shops and godowns in the yard, and composition money for offences, applied to the yard's establishment, works and services.

The transaction rules are the part of the machine a farmer actually touches. Sale in the yard is to be by open auction or another method the Act prescribes, conducted under the supervision of committee staff, with the bids called openly and the highest bid recorded. The charges that may be taken are the charges the Act and rules schedule: the market fee, the commission of the agent through whom the sale is routed, and the functional charges for weighing, handling, cleaning and loading where those are permitted, and the scheduled list is meant to be exhaustive, so that the informal deductions of the unregulated market have no lawful counterpart. Payment discipline is written into the design: the buyer is to pay the seller on the day of sale or within the short period the rules allow, and the commission agent through whom the produce moved is made responsible in the scheme for ensuring the farmer receives the price. Weighment is to be by licensed weighmen using the yard's scales. The committee's staff record arrivals and sales, and the record is not clerical decoration: it is the raw material of the price reporting system through which mandi prices reach the newspapers, the government and, in time, the farmer's phone.

Enforcement completes the machine, and its teeth show the statute's priorities. Trading without a licence, buying or selling notified produce outside the permitted places, and evading the fee are offences or breaches attracting penalties that in most Acts are modest fines, often with the committee empowered to compound the offence on payment, and with suspension or cancellation of the licence as the sanction that actually disciplines the yard's population. The committee may stop and check vehicles carrying notified produce, a power that turns the market area's boundary into something real at its check posts. Appeals against committee decisions run to the board or to a state authority the Act designates. The reader should notice what this enforcement pattern implies: the system's sanctions are administrative and fiscal rather than criminal in any serious degree, because the statute is not principally aimed at punishing trade but at confining it, and the offence it cares about is not fraud on the farmer but escape from the yard.

Over this common machine the states have made characteristic choices. Some Acts cover the whole state in market areas and others leave pockets outside. Some permit the committee to license a single trader for a whole area's specialised trade, others have opened windows, for direct purchase by processors, for farmer consumer markets, for private yards, each by amendment in the direction the model Acts pointed. The composition and election of committees, the level of the fee, the list of notified commodities and the strength of the state board vary enough that the answers to practical questions, can I sell outside the yard, what will be deducted from my price, who do I complain to, are always state answers. But the machine's logic does not vary: notify, confine, license, auction, levy, record. A reader who holds those six verbs holds the APMC system, and can read any state's variation as a setting of the same dials.

## Who the Acts bind, and where the compulsion stops

A statute family's edges teach as much as its centre, and the APMC Acts repay attention to the persons and transactions they do not reach. The producer selling his own crop stands at the scheme's centre as its protected person, not as its licensee: the Acts license the trade side of the yard, and a farmer needs no licence to bring and sell his produce, though he must sell it in the manner and place the Act prescribes when the sale is of the wholesale kind the Act governs. His position changes the moment he trades in what he did not grow. Several state Acts distinguish the producer seller from the producer trader and from the trader pure and simple, and a farmer who buys produce to resell enters the licensing net like any other dealer, a boundary that matters in villages where the larger grower is also the locality's first assembler. The exemptions that soften the compulsion are as much a part of the design. Retail sales in small quantities to consumers, sales by a producer to a consumer for personal consumption, produce sold by government or its agencies under their own schemes, and transactions the state government exempts by notification for a class of persons or a place, stand outside the offence in most Acts, though the list and its width are state specific and the fee's treatment of exempt trade has generated its own litigation.

The compulsion also stops at the commodity's form and movement in ways trade feels daily. Produce brought into a market area for processing, or in transit through it to another state, has repeatedly raised the question whether passage or processing is a transaction in the area at all, and states have answered it differently, some exempting produce that enters an area solely for processing by a licensed processor on intimation, others charging the fee and leaving the processor to complain. The Punjab complaint recorded during the farm law debates, that fee was payable on notified produce at a buyer's own premises which had never used the yard, is the extreme of one answer; the processing exemption is the moderate form of the other. Beneath the variations sits the recurring judicial question of what the fee is for. Courts examining marketing levies have had to decide whether a charge is a fee, which requires a broad correlation with services rendered to the class that pays, or a tax in fee's clothing, which a market committee has no competence to impose; the distinction has disciplined some levies and sustained others, and it explains why the statutes are careful to tie the fee to the market fund and the fund to the yard's works. The leading examination is the Supreme Court's decision in Kewal Krishan Puri v State of Punjab, in 1980, on a challenge to Punjab's enhancement of its market fee: the Court treated the levy as a fee rather than a tax, applied the test of a broad correlation between the charge and the services the marketing system renders to the trade that pays it, and upheld the enhancement on the strength of the market development the fund was shown to finance. The case stands for the proposition on which every committee's finance quietly rests, that the fee is defensible while it is visibly buying the market and vulnerable in proportion as it becomes general revenue. A charge visibly spent on the yard defends itself; a charge swept into general purposes defends itself badly, and the long drift of some state levies toward development cesses of general application is where the family's legal vulnerability concentrates.

One more boundary defines the Acts' daily reach: they govern a transaction, not a crop's life. Seed retained by the farmer, produce consumed on the farm, barter in the village below the wholesale threshold, and the farmer's contracts for growing, other than where a state has brought contract farming into its Act or a separate law, proceed under the general law of contract and property, untouched by the committee. The mandi system is therefore best pictured not as a blanket over agricultural trade but as a gate across its busiest road: the great flow of marketed surplus passes through it and is taxed and recorded there, while a countryside of smaller transactions flows around it, lawfully or by neglect, in quantities no committee has ever counted.

## Three state models: Punjab, Maharashtra and Karnataka

The family's range is best seen in three states whose statutes share the skeleton and diverge in almost everything else. Punjab shows the system fused with the state's food procurement and with the commission agent at its centre. Maharashtra shows the classic large-state statute, amended late and partially toward deregulation. Karnataka shows the statute amended early toward electronic unification, and in doing so shows that the family's reform did not have to wait for the Centre's models to be adopted whole.

The Punjab Agricultural Produce Markets Act of 1961, Punjab Act 23 of 1961, which received the President's assent on 18 May 1961, consolidated the law for the then undivided Punjab and continues to frame marketing in both Punjab and Haryana, each of which works with its own state agricultural marketing board established under the Act, the Punjab board being known everywhere as the Punjab Mandi Board. Punjab's distinction is not its text but its load. The wheat and paddy surpluses of the Green Revolution made its mandis the collection points for one of the largest public procurement operations in the world, in which the Food Corporation of India and state agencies buy at the minimum support price through the yards, using the commission agents as their procurement intermediaries and paying them commission for the service. The mandi in Punjab is therefore not merely a place of private trade supervised by a committee. It is the physical plant of the food security system, and its fee income, drawn on the enormous value of grain that passes through, funds a rural infrastructure of market yards and link roads that the state points to whenever the system is criticised. The Punjab charges also mark the family's high end. Section 23 of the 1961 Act caps the market fee a committee may levy at three rupees for every hundred rupees of the value of the produce, with an additional fee of up to one rupee on sales by a producer to a licensee, and Punjab has levied at a level that, with the rural development fee of three per cent charged alongside the market fee of three per cent and the commission agent's two and a half per cent, brings the total statutory take on a grain transaction to about eight and a half per cent of value, a figure the state itself placed on record when rejecting the Centre's draft national marketing policy framework, which had proposed cutting the two fees to one per cent each.

The Maharashtra Agricultural Produce Marketing (Development and Regulation) Act of 1963, Maharashtra Act XX of 1964, assented to on 30 April 1964, is the western member of the family: the original short title spoke of regulation alone, and the word development entered it only through an amendment of 2005, a small textual fossil of the shift this article traces across the whole family. Maharashtra's system is one of scale and of commodity breadth, with a few hundred market committees, the great terminal yards at Mumbai's Vashi among them, and a notified list that has historically reached across an unusually wide range of produce. Its market fee, a cess of one per cent of trade value in the reporting of its own markets, sits far below the Punjab level, which is why comparisons of "the" APMC burden that begin from Punjab mislead: the same architecture carries a light load in one state and a heavy one in another. Maharashtra also supplies the family's clearest episode of deregulation by delisting. By an ordinance of 5 July 2016, later enacted, the state removed fruits and vegetables from the regulated channel, so that their wholesale trade need not pass through the committee yards, and coupled the change with a single point levy of the fee within the state. The episode was instructive in both directions. Farmer groups selling directly in cities reported keeping margins that the yard had absorbed, while the committees, which continued to claim their fee in contested cases even on trade outside the yard, demonstrated how a levy attached to a territory rather than to a service survives the withdrawal of the service. A later attempt to extend delisting to all produce, moved by ordinance in 2021, met organised resistance from the market interest and did not settle into law, leaving Maharashtra with the characteristic family outcome: reform real, bounded, and contested at the boundary.

Karnataka's statute, the Karnataka Agricultural Produce Marketing (Regulation and Development) Act of 1966, Karnataka Act 27 of 1966, which received the President's assent on 19 August 1966 and was first published the following month, began as a unification measure, replacing for the new state of Mysore the Bombay Act of 1939 in its northern districts, the Madras Commercial Crops Markets Act of 1933 in its southern districts and Bellary, and the Coorg Act of 1956, a reminder that several members of the family are themselves consolidations of older provincial laws. Its later life is the point. Following amendments in 2013, Karnataka built a unified market platform over its mandis: a single trading licence usable across the state, electronic auctions in which buyers beyond the yard could bid, and a special purpose vehicle, Rashtriya eMarket Services, created with a national commodity exchange as partner, to operate the platform. The state agricultural marketing policy of 2013 framed the change as reducing barriers to participation and treating the state as one market supported by digitisation, which is in substance the programme the Centre's model Acts preached, achieved inside a state Act by a state that chose to amend. Karnataka's experience is the standing reply to two opposite exaggerations: that the APMC statute makes electronic integration impossible, which it plainly does not, and that a platform dissolves the underlying interests, which it does not either, since the licence, the fee and the committee all survived the platform's arrival and the bidding widened within the statute rather than outside it.

### What is the difference between a principal market yard and a sub-yard?

The principal yard is the market area's market place, with the committee's office, auction platforms, weighbridges, godowns and staff. A sub-yard is a smaller notified place in the same area, closer to the producing villages, where notified produce may also be sold. Both work under the same Act and committee; they differ in scale and facilities, not in legal character.

A principal market yard is the main regulated market place for a market area, where the committee maintains its office, its auction platforms, weighbridges, godowns and staff, and where the bulk of the area's wholesale trade is meant to concentrate. A sub-yard is a smaller notified place within the same market area, established by the committee nearer to clusters of villages, where notified produce may lawfully be brought, auctioned and charged under the same Act and the same committee. The distinction is one of scale and facilities, not of legal character: the licence requirement, the prescribed method of sale and the market fee apply in the sub-yard as they do in the principal yard.

The two tier design is the statutes' answer to distance. A market area drawn widely enough to sustain one good yard would otherwise require every farmer in it to haul produce to a single point, and the sub-yard pulls the regulated transaction closer to the farm while keeping it inside the system. In practice the tiers also sort by volume and commodity: bulk grain assembles at the principal yards, smaller and more local trade uses the sub-yards, and the quality of infrastructure, storage, covered auction space and banking presence falls off sharply between the two, a gradient reformers cite when they argue that the network's coverage on paper overstates the service it delivers at the periphery.

## The wider family: variations the three models do not show

Beyond the three models, the family's other members mark the range of what a state can do with the common design. Uttar Pradesh runs one of the largest systems by count of markets, under its own post-independence statute and a state mandi parishad, and its yards handle the assembling trade of the country's most populous agrarian state with fee levels well below the Punjab peak. Delhi, a city state that grows almost nothing, maintains APMC markets of national significance for the produce of other states, Azadpur for fruit and vegetables above all, so that its committee regulates a terminal trade whose producers vote in other states' elections, an inversion of the design's assumption that the yard serves its own hinterland. Gujarat amended toward the model programme comparatively early and combined its mandis with a strong cooperative and processing sector, which is why its reform record reads differently from states where the private alternative the model imagined did not exist on the ground. West Bengal's regulated markets never acquired the fiscal or procurement weight of the northern system, and its trade remained substantially village based despite the statute on its book.

The family's absences are as instructive as its members. Kerala never enacted an APMC statute of the standard pattern, and its agricultural marketing runs through other institutions and laws, a standing demonstration that the absence of the Act does not produce a vacuum but a different, older organisation of trade, with its own intermediaries operating under general law rather than committee licence. Several north eastern states adopted frameworks that remained weak in operation, with few notified markets and thin committee structures, reflecting small marketed surpluses and difficult terrain rather than a considered rejection of regulation. Jammu and Kashmir stood outside the family's ordinary history for constitutional reasons of its own. The comparative point completes the constitutional one: a State List subject in a diverse federation yields not one system with local accents but a spectrum, from Punjab's tax heavy procurement machine through Maharashtra's broad middling model to states where the statute is closer to a framework awaiting use, and any sentence about "the APMC system" that does not survive that spectrum should not be written.

The variation extends to the committee's own species. In the procurement states the committee is a substantial public body with works programmes and political weight; in states where the marketed surplus is thin, committees exist on paper with yards that meet weekly and funds that barely keep a clerk. The state marketing board's strength tracks the same gradient. Reform instruments addressed to the family as a whole, a model Act, a platform's admission criteria, a national policy framework, land on these different soils and grow differently, which is the practical reason the Centre's two decades of uniform prescription produced a patchwork: the prescription was uniform, and nothing else was.

## How a farmer's produce actually moves

Abstractions about monopoly and protection resolve, on the ground, into a sequence of physical steps that a consignment of grain or vegetables passes through between the field and the buyer's godown, and the statutes touch every step. Follow a wheat farmer of the Punjab or Haryana belt bringing a trolley of grain to the mandi in the procurement season, and the same sequence, with local variations of name and charge, will be found in a Maharashtra onion yard or a Karnataka ragi market. The harvest is threshed and bagged at the farm, and the first decision, where to sell, has in the regulated commodities already been made by the Act: the trolley goes to the yard of the market committee for the area, or to the purchase centre the committee and the procurement agencies operate in season. The farmer rarely deals with the yard directly. His produce moves through his commission agent, the arhtiya, whose shop in the yard is his point of contact, whose labourers unload and clean the grain, who presents the lot at auction or, in procurement season, to the purchasing agency, and who receives and passes on the payment. The table below sets out the sequence with the statutory hook at each step, the charge the step carries and the complaint it characteristically generates.

| Step in the journey | What happens | Statutory basis | Fee or commission charged | The commonest grievance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Harvest and the decision to sell | The farmer threshes, bags and loads the produce for the notified market of his area | The state Act's declaration of the market area and of the notified produce | None at this step, but the choice of venue has been made by the Act | The nearest yard may be far, and for a regulated commodity there is no lawful nearer buyer |
| Transport to the yard | The trolley or truck carries the lot to the principal yard, sub-yard or seasonal purchase centre | The Act's confinement of wholesale trade in notified produce to the notified places | Transport cost borne by the farmer | Check posts and fee demands on produce moving between market areas |
| Arrival, unloading and cleaning | The lot is unloaded at the commission agent's shop, cleaned, heaped and displayed for inspection | Committee bye-laws and the scheduled charges for handling functions | Hamali and cleaning charges within the schedule the Act permits | Deductions for moisture and admixture assessed against the seller without a test he can see |
| Lot formation and display | Buyers inspect heaps or bags and form their view of quality before the sale opens | The Act's requirement of sale by open auction or the prescribed method | None directly | Absence of assaying and grading, so quality is priced by eye and the eye belongs to the buyer |
| The auction or the procurement purchase | Bids are called openly, or the lot is taken by the procurement agency at the support price | The state Act's method of sale provisions and committee supervision | Market fee levied on the transaction value | Few bidders, understood ceilings among them, and bidding that closes before distant buyers can enter |
| Weighing and bagging | The sold lot is weighed on the yard's scales and bagged for the buyer | The Act's licensing of weighmen and prescription of standard weights | Weighing charges within the schedule | Disputed weighments and shortages discovered after the lot has left the yard |
| The sale record | The transaction is recorded, in Punjab as the J-form issued through the commission agent, and arrivals and prices enter the committee's returns | The Act's record and reporting requirements | None directly | A record the farmer cannot easily obtain or use, though his payment and credit history turn on it |
| Payment and deductions | The buyer pays, the commission agent settles with the farmer after deducting his commission, the charges and any advance he had given | The Act's payment discipline and the scheduled list of permissible charges | Commission, market fee where passed on, handling charges, and set-off of the season's advances | Payment delayed beyond the day the Act promises, and a settlement statement in which old debt swallows the price |

Read down the grievance column and the whole reform debate is present in miniature. Notice, though, what the sequence also shows: at every step a real service exists. Grain is cleaned, weighed, aggregated, bid for, financed and moved, and someone must do and be paid for each of those things. The complaint that survives scrutiny is rarely that the steps exist. It is that the farmer cannot choose who performs them, cannot test the price of performing them against a competitor, and cannot leave the sequence at the point where it stops serving him, because the statute has made the sequence the only lawful road from his field to the wholesale market.

## The licence at the centre of the system

Every account of the APMC system's failure returns, sooner or later, to the licence, and the reader should understand precisely what kind of instrument it is. A licence under a state marketing Act is a permission to carry on a defined function, trader, commission agent, broker, weighman, processor and others as the Act lists them, within a defined market area, granted by the market committee on conditions, renewable periodically, and liable to suspension or cancellation for breach of the Act, the rules or the bye-laws. As a regulatory device it is unremarkable; food premises and transport operators are licensed everywhere. What makes the APMC licence analytically interesting is the conjunction of three features: the trade it licenses is compulsory, since notified produce must be traded in the yard; the territory it covers is exclusive, since the committee's area is a defined monopoly of venue; and the number of licences in issue has in many markets grown slowly or not at all relative to the trade, because committees, on which sitting licensees are represented, have little reason to multiply their own competitors and because the physical yard, its shops and platforms, is finite. Put the three together and the licence stops being a mere registration of fitness and becomes an asset: a claim on a stream of transactions that the law guarantees will keep arriving.

The consequences are visible in the yard's sociology. Licences acquire value beyond their fee and pass within families and firms as part of the goodwill of the business. The population of buyers at any auction is the licensed population and no one else, so the competitiveness of the auction is decided in advance by the committee's licensing history. Inquiries into the working of regulated markets, and the expert committees that preceded the model Acts, recorded the pattern in which traders and commission agents of a yard, few in number, known to one another, and linked by credit and kinship and caste networks of the market town, bid in ways that divided the lots rather than contesting them. The cartel claim must be stated with care: collusion at auction is an empirical matter that varies yard by yard and season by season, and some yards with many active licensees and heavy arrivals are genuinely competitive. But the structure needs no conspiracy to disappoint the farmer. It needs only a small number of buyers, each aware that the seller cannot withdraw his produce to another venue and must sell into the day's bidding because his trolley cannot wait and his debt cannot either. Thin bidding is not a scandal superimposed on the system; it is the system's predictable output where licences are scarce and exit is barred.

The commission agent is the figure through whom this structure is lived, and the statutes' treatment of him is where the design's age shows most. The Act contemplates the arhtiya as a functionary of the sale: he receives the farmer's produce, arranges its display and auction, guarantees or effects payment, and earns the commission the schedule fixes. What the Act did not contemplate, because it belongs to the countryside's credit structure rather than to marketing law, is that the same man is the farmer's banker. Entry 30 of the State List, money-lending and money-lenders, sits in the same legislature's hands as Entry 28, but the two statutes were never integrated, and the mandi's credit relationship grew up in the space between them. The commission agent advances money and inputs across the season, interest free in the form the accounts take in some regions and at interest in others, and the advance is repaid at the only moment it can be: out of the price, at his shop, on the day of sale. The interlinkage of credit and sale is the strongest single explanation of the mandi's durability, stronger than any affection for the institution, because it means that for the indebted farmer the auction's freedom is formal. He sells through his creditor not because the Act leaves him no alternative venue, though it does, but because his crop was spoken for before it was harvested.

### Why do farmers borrow from the same commission agent they sell through?

Because he lends on terms no institution matches: money across the season against the coming crop and a relationship, without land records, credit histories or delay, and for consumption and ceremony as readily as for seed and fertiliser. The advance is repaid out of the price at his shop on sale day. Debt and sale are one act.

Because for a large part of the countryside he is the only lender whose terms fit the borrower's life. The commission agent lends on the security of the coming crop and of a personal relationship maintained across seasons, without land documents, credit histories or the procedural delay of institutional lending, and he lends for consumption and ceremony as readily as for seed and fertiliser, which formal crop credit will not do. The price of that convenience is the obligation, enforced by custom rather than by any clause the marketing Act contains, to bring the harvest to his shop and to settle the account out of the price before taking the balance home.

Reform proposals that treat the arhtiya as a pure extraction device fail against this fact, and proposals that treat him as a benign village institution fail against its twin: the same relationship that supplies working capital also suppresses the borrower's freedom at the one moment freedom would pay. The honest analysis has to hold both halves. Where institutional credit has deepened, through cooperative banks, self-help groups, kisan credit cards and the interest subvention the Centre runs on crop loans, the farmer's dependence on the agent's money has thinned, and with it the agent's hold on the sale; where it has not, abolishing the intermediary's statutory position without replacing his credit function would leave the farmer selling alone, earlier in the season, to the same man in a different legal costume. This is why the Punjab and Haryana arhtiya commands a political defence that puzzles outside observers: within the procurement system he is also the machinery that assembles, cleans, bags and finances the state's grain purchase, and his associations can present him, with some evidence, as load-bearing.

## Distance, lot size and the smallholder's arithmetic

The statutes speak of the farmer in general, but the system's costs and protections fall very unevenly across the class the word names, and the distribution follows two variables: how far the farm is from the yard, and how much the farmer brings. The market area is a territory, often a district or a group of talukas, served by one principal yard and a scattering of sub-yards of lesser equipment. For the grower near the yard, the statutory channel is a short haul to a place with buyers, scales and a bank; for the grower at the area's edge it is a day's hiring of transport to reach the same door, and the transport charge, which the Act does not regulate because it arises before the market begins, can exceed every charge the Act does. The model Act of 2017 wrote the complaint into its design when it proposed that a regulated wholesale market should be available within a radius of eighty kilometres, a standard large parts of the network do not meet, and the sub-yard system, which was the original design's answer, has thinned in many states as committees concentrated investment where volume already was. Distance is thus a regressive feature built into geography rather than text: the farmer least able to pay for haulage is the one the map charges most.

Lot size works the same way at the auction itself. The buyer at a mandi is purchasing in truckloads and godown lots; the produce of a one acre holding arrives as a fraction of a lot, and its price is struck in a process calibrated to volume. Small lots wait longer, are handled last, attract the eye of fewer bidders, and carry the fixed costs of the journey, the handling and the day's lost labour spread over a few quintals, so that the smallholder's effective deduction rate, counting every cost the Act permits and several it does not reach, is the highest in the yard. His dependence compounds the arithmetic: he is the farmer most likely to owe the commission agent, least able to store and wait for a better day's bidding, and least able to refuse the price struck and haul his produce home. The protective design was drawn with him in mind, and he is the seller it serves least completely, a pattern that explains why the system's defenders and its angriest critics can both be describing real mandis. The large grower of the procurement belt, selling many quintals through an agent who courts his custom, in a yard where the support price floors the auction, experiences the institution as it was drawn. The small grower at the district's edge experiences its costs with its protections attenuated by distance and scale.

Perishability sharpens every term of the arithmetic. A grain grower who dislikes the day's price can, in principle, store; a vegetable grower cannot, and his produce enters the yard on a fuse measured in hours. For perishables the auction's defects are at their worst, because the seller's refusal right, formal in the Acts, is worthless for a crop wilting on the trolley, and the buyer's knowledge that the lot must move that day is worth more than any bid he makes. This is why fruits and vegetables led the deregulation queue in state after state: their growers had the most to gain from alternative channels, their produce could reach a city buyer without the yard's storage, and the political cost of freeing them was lowest because the procurement system does not run on tomatoes. It is also why the delisting results, encouraging as they were, cannot be read across to the grains, where storage, credit and procurement bind the farmer to the yard's calendar in ways a vegetable grower escapes. The family's hardest cases are its storable ones.

The institutional answers that have actually moved this arithmetic are worth naming, because they operate inside the statutes rather than against them. Aggregation is the first: a farmer producer organisation or a cooperative marketing society that pools the lots of fifty smallholders sells one lot of consequence, hires one truck, waits one day instead of fifty, and can hold produce for a price in a way none of its members could alone, and the Acts have always had room for cooperative societies as licensees and committee constituents, even where the room was little used. Aggregation is also what the platforms reward: an electronic auction prices a graded, assayed, aggregated lot from a distant buyer far better than it prices a trolley of ungraded grain, so the gains of e-NAM and of Karnataka's unified platform have flowed disproportionately to organised sellers, and the same will be true of any successor arrangement. The second answer is the purchase centre: in procurement season the agencies carry the yard to the village, opening temporary centres where grain is weighed, bought at the support price and lifted, collapsing the distance variable for the season's main crop in the states that run procurement seriously. Neither answer required repealing anything. Both required the state to operate its own statute with the smallholder's arithmetic in view, which is a choice about administration, not about the Act, and the states differ in that choice as much as in their texts.

## The committee as government: elections, funds and local power

To understand why the statutes are so hard to amend, the reader has to see the market committee as what it also is: a unit of rural government. A committee controls a yard that may be the largest commercial site in its district, employs staff, lets shops and godowns, awards works contracts, and spends a market fund fed by a percentage of every substantial transaction in its territory, a revenue no panchayat and few municipalities in the same countryside can match. Its chairmanship and membership are therefore prizes. The trader and commission agent members arrive by election among licensees, a constituency with money and organisation; the agriculturist members arrive by election among the area's producers or through cooperative institutions, a constituency that in practice is often mobilised by the same rural leadership that contests panchayat and assembly elections; and the state government's nominees and its power to post the committee's secretary ensure that the ruling party of the state is present in the yard whichever way the elections run. Market committees have served for decades as the second rung of political careers in the agrarian states, and the web of obligation between committee office holders, licensees and legislators is the transmission belt by which a state Act's amendment, however technocratic in Delhi's drafting, is priced in every market town before it is voted on.

The supervisory powers tell the same story from the other side. State governments may call for records, give directions, and in most Acts supersede a committee that in their judgment is not functioning properly, appointing an administrator to run the market in its place. Supersession was designed as a discipline for defaulting bodies; its recorded use across the states has been frequent and long, with committees in several states spending years under administrators between elections, so that the democratic design of the Acts, elected farmers governing their own market, has coexisted with a practice in which the state executive governs the market directly and indefinitely. The consequences for the reform debate are concrete. A proposal to cap the fee is a proposal to cut the income of a body whose office holders sit in the state's political class. A proposal to multiply licences is a proposal to dilute the electorate and the clientele of sitting members. A proposal to admit private yards is a proposal to license a competitor to an institution that local legislators may chair. None of this makes reform wrong; it makes reform legible, and it explains why the successful changes this article records came either from states whose governments chose to spend political capital on their own statutes, as Karnataka and Maharashtra did, or from a central scheme that paid states to qualify, as e-NAM did, rather than from the circulation of a model text, however well drafted.

The fund's expenditure pattern completes the picture and cuts both ways in argument. In the strong systems the market fund has visibly built the countryside's commercial skeleton: the yards themselves with their auction platforms and sheds, approach roads and link roads, rest houses, drinking water, lighting, and the purchase centres that open in the villages at harvest. Punjab's defence of its high fees rests substantially on this record, and the roads are real. In the weak systems the same fund can be absorbed by establishment costs, salaries and the maintenance of a small yard's staff, leaving little capital works to show against the percentage taken from the farmer's price. The Kewal Krishan Puri test, correlation between the charge and the service, is thus not only a legal doctrine but the right audit question for any given mandi: the fee is justified yard by yard, and a national debate conducted in averages misses both the yards that earn their fee and the yards that do not.

## The money: market fees, cesses and commission

Three different charges are habitually run together in criticism of the mandis, and they must be separated before any of them can be judged. The market fee is the committee's charge, levied under the state Act on the value of the transaction, collected generally from the buyer or through the commission agent, and credited to the market fund that pays for the yard. The commission is the agent's remuneration, fixed in the schedule of charges or by the Act's rules, paid in the grain trade of the north as a percentage of value and in some trades and regions as a rate per bag or per quintal, and earned on the sale the agent intermediated. Around these two, states have layered cesses that belong to other policies: Punjab's rural development fee, charged to finance rural infrastructure, is the largest, and various states have at times added development cesses and charges for specific funds. Only the first of the three is the price of the market's services in any strict sense. The commission is the price of intermediation, and the cesses are taxation wearing the mandi as a collection point, a distinction that matters because abolishing the yard would abolish only the first, while the political defence of the whole bundle usually argues as though all three buy the same infrastructure.

The levels differ so widely that national generalisation fails. At one end stands Punjab, where, as the state's own figures record, the market fee of three per cent and the rural development fee of three per cent, with the commission agent's two and a half per cent, take about eight and a half per cent of the value of a grain transaction before handling charges, a burden the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices has repeatedly criticised in its price policy reports as raising the cost of food, distorting trade and inflating the food subsidy bill, since the same levies fall on grain the state itself procures. Haryana's combined charges stand somewhat lower, in the region of six and a half per cent on the same published comparisons. At the other end, Maharashtra's one per cent cess and the still lower effective fees of several southern and eastern states show the same architecture carrying a fraction of the Punjab load, and a scattering of states have exempted particular commodities or trades entirely. The variation is the federal system expressing itself in price: because the fee is state law, the cost of selling a quintal of wheat depends on which side of a state border the mandi stands, and traders price the difference into their bids, so that a high fee state taxes its own farmers' realisations even when the fee is nominally collected from the buyer. Incidence follows the market, not the schedule, and it is the farmer in the high fee state who pays for the yard through a thinner bid, a point the defenders of high fees rarely meet directly.

Two features of the fee deserve separate notice because they recur in every reform design. The first is that the fee in many states attaches to the transaction in notified produce within the market area, not strictly to the use of the yard, so that trade the Act permits outside the yard's fence can still be charged for, as Maharashtra's committees demonstrated after the 2016 delisting and as buyers in Punjab have long complained when purchasing at their own premises. A fee for a venue becomes, at that point, a tax on a territory, and the legal basis and the economic character part company. The second is the multiplication of the fee along the chain: where produce is assembled in one market area, traded onward in a second and processed in a third, an unamended Act can charge at each passage, which is why the single point levy, one fee for the state, collected once, became a standard item of the reform menu from the model Act of 2003 onward and a precondition the electronic market later imposed. The fee is small at each step and the steps are many, and the arithmetic of the chain, several small percentages compounding across assembly, wholesale and processing, is how a system of modest individual charges reaches the consumer's price carrying a load the farmer never sees as a gain.

## Grading, assaying and the price the system publishes

For a statute family so concerned with the fairness of price, the APMC Acts are strikingly incurious about quality, and the gap explains several of their failures. Price discovery at auction is only as honest as the information in the room. Where a lot is ungraded, a heap of grain or a stack of onion bags whose interior no buyer has seen, the price struck is a price for the average the buyer assumes, discounted for his risk; the farmer whose produce is genuinely superior receives the average less the discount, and the farmer whose produce is poor receives more than its worth, so the yard's pricing compresses quality differences and removes the incentive to produce quality at all. Grading before sale, the sorting of produce into recognised grades under the national AGMARK standards tradition, and assaying, the laboratory or rapid testing of a sample for moisture, admixture and variety, are the instruments that break the compression, and their scarcity in the yards is the recurring finding of every review of the system. The statutes empower committees to provide grading services and the national grading law exists beside them, but the service was never made the centre of the transaction as weighment was, partly because grading benefits the seller of good produce at the visible expense of the seller of bad, and a committee has no constituency for that redistribution.

The information the system does produce has nevertheless become a public good of real value. Committees report arrivals and the prices struck, minimum, maximum and modal, and those reports, aggregated through the marketing directorates and the national AGMARKNET portal, constitute the country's basic price intelligence for agricultural commodities: the mandi prices quoted in newspapers, used in the government's own market interventions, consulted by researchers, and fed back to farmers through radio, applications and the e-NAM platform itself. The record function deserves emphasis in the balance sheet of the institution, because it is easy to overlook: a countryside in which the day's prices in the district's yards are published and archived is a countryside in which a farmer can test his buyer's story, and the disappearance of that record in Bihar after repeal, where trade became statistically invisible just as it became free, is one of the quiet costs the repeal's evaluators noted. Price information is also where the electronic turn has been unambiguous gain: whatever e-NAM's limits as an auction, as a price reporting and payment system it tightened a record the paper system kept loosely. The assessment that follows from this section is the same separability the article argues elsewhere. Grading, assaying and price publication are services; nothing in them requires a compulsory venue or a scarce licence; and a reform that funded them as public infrastructure, charged to the fee the Kewal Krishan Puri test can defend, would attack the information asymmetry that was half the original case for regulation, by means the original design neglected.

## What the APMC Acts do not govern

The mandi statutes are one layer of a thicker legal stack, and a reader who loads every grievance about agricultural trade onto them will misdiagnose most of them. Movement and stocking of produce, the power to limit how much a trader may hold and to direct where grain may move, belong to a different statute altogether, the Essential Commodities Act of 1955, whose control orders have historically restricted the very trade the marketing Acts organise; the relationship between the two regimes, one building the venue and the other policing the commodity, is traced in the series article on [the Essential Commodities Act and its control architecture](/2014/03/01/essential-commodities-act-1955-guide/). Price support is likewise not the mandi's creation. The minimum support price is an executive policy announced on the recommendations of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, not a statutory entitlement the APMC Acts confer, and procurement at that price is an operation of the Food Corporation of India and state agencies that uses the mandi as its buying counter because the statute has gathered the trade there; the legal framework of support prices and procurement, and the long argument for giving the support price a statutory form, belong to the series treatment of [the MSP and procurement framework](/2014/07/01/msp-procurement-legal-framework-india/). What the marketing Acts themselves supply to both neighbours is the venue, the licensed population and the record: the auction at which the support price becomes a floor under open bidding where procurement is active, the sale slips from which arrivals and prices are known, and the physical plant without which a procurement season in the surplus states could not be run at its scale.

The boundary cuts the other way as well. Because the Acts govern the first wholesale transaction in notified produce, they barely touch the long chain after the mandi gate, the assemblers, processors, wholesalers, transporters and retailers through whom the produce reaches the consumer, and they say almost nothing about the farmer's other transactions: his purchase of inputs, his credit from sources other than his agent, his tenancy or his wages as a labourer in the same countryside. The retail price of food in a city and the farm gate price in its hinterland are connected by that unregulated chain, and a reform confined to the mandi statute can at best change the first link. This is the analytical answer to a common rhetorical move in the debate, in which the spread between what the farmer receives and what the consumer pays is laid entire at the mandi's door. The mandi's charges and margins are a real and measurable slice of that spread, and in the high fee states a heavy one. The rest is transport, handling, processing, wastage, finance and the margins of a chain the marketing Acts were never written to govern, and which deregulating the first sale leaves exactly as it was.

## Disputes and enforcement in the yard's daily life

Between the statute's architecture and the farmer's experience stands a layer of small adjudication that the Acts create and that largely determines whether the protection is felt. The characteristic disputes of a mandi are not constitutional; they are about a weighment short by a quintal, a deduction the farmer did not expect, a payment two days late, a bid withdrawn, a lot damaged in the yard. The design's answer is to keep these quarrels inside the market's own institutions in the first instance: the committee's officers supervise the sale, the committee or its designated authority hears complaints about weighment and charges, and the commission agent's responsibility for the farmer's payment gives the farmer a solvent, licensed, findable defendant rather than a vanished buyer. Appeals and references from committee decisions run upward to the state board or the director of agricultural marketing as the Act provides, and the civil courts stand behind the whole structure for what escapes it. The system's virtue is speed and locality; its vice is the obvious one, that the first forum is the institution whose licensees are the other party, and a farmer complaining about the yard to the yard requires a faith in committee neutrality that the committee's composition, part licensees by election, does not always earn. Serious accounts of mandi governance return to this point: the protection works where the committee's farmer majority is real and its staff honest, and degenerates into procedure where it is not, which is to say the statute supplies a forum and politics decides whether it is one.

Enforcement against the trade follows the same administrative logic. The Acts arm the committee with inspection of premises, books and vehicles, stop and search of produce in movement for fee evasion, prosecution for unlicensed trading with fines that are small beside the value of a season's trade, and the compounding of offences for a money payment. The realistic sanction remains the licence itself: suspension stops a business, cancellation ends it, and both lie close to the committee's hand. The enforcement record has a shape the reader should register, because it qualifies the monopoly's reputation. A committee whose income rises with the volume of trade has an interest in policing escape from the yard, and the check post on the road is diligence in defence of revenue; the same committee has a weaker interest in policing conduct inside the yard that does not threaten volume, the quiet understanding among bidders, the deduction dressed as custom, and its record against those is the thinner half of most yard level studies. The monopoly, in short, is enforced hardest at its boundary and softest at its centre, and both the farmer's grievance about the cartel and his grievance about being stopped on the road come from the same incentive, correctly read.

## Bihar 2006: the repeal that settled nothing

One state has run the experiment the debaters argue from, and its discipline is that it proves less than either side claims. Bihar enacted its marketing statute as the Bihar Agricultural Produce Markets Act of 1960 and then, in 2006, under the government of Nitish Kumar, repealed it outright by the Bihar Agriculture Produce Market (Repeal) Act of 2006, effective from 1 September 2006. The repeal was complete in form: the market committees and the state marketing board stood abolished, their assets and liabilities vested in the state under the repeal Act, pending proceedings for fee and penalty were saved and carried on before designated officers, and the employees of the dissolved bodies were held in service pending a scheme of absorption or retirement. The Patna High Court upheld the repeal against challenge, accepting the state's stated purpose of encouraging private and cooperative markets trading directly with farmers. No other state has followed Bihar all the way, and Bihar itself did not replace the system with a successor statute: the regulated market simply ended, and trade continued in the yards' physical shells and in the village, without licence, fee or committee.

What happened next is genuinely contested, and the contest is instructive because the studies do not line up by ideology. The expectation of the repeal's authors was that private capital, freed of the licence and the fee, would build modern markets and compete for the farmer's produce. The National Council of Applied Economic Research, in its 2019 diagnostic study of Bihar agriculture, recorded that the investment did not come: private creation of new markets and strengthening of the old ones did not take place, market density stayed low, and by its estimate some ninety per cent of the state's crops were sold within the village to traders and commission agents, the very intermediaries the regulated system had at least licensed and watched. Prices rose for major crops across the period, as they did nationally, but their volatility rose with them, and farm harvest prices in Bihar ran persistently below the support prices that procurement states could deliver, because the procurement machinery that works through mandis had no counterpart once the mandis were gone; the state later built procurement through primary agricultural credit societies, with its own record of delay and exclusion. Econometric work on the repeal tells a cooler story in the other direction: a difference in differences study of farm harvest prices found the repeal associated with a fall in the paddy price and a rise in the maize price, and an assessment published by Ideas for India, testing prices, arrivals and investment together, found no statistically significant effect of the repeal on most outcomes it measured, Bihar's farmers ending neither clearly better nor clearly worse off than the trends in comparable states would predict.

Read together, the record supports a narrow conclusion and forbids two broad ones. It forbids the deregulator's conclusion, because removing the statute did not summon the competing private market of the theory: where marketing infrastructure is a collective good and the countryside is poor, the private yard did not replace the public one, and the farmer's counterparty remained the local trader, left unlicensed and unrecorded. It equally forbids the retentionist's conclusion, because the sky did not fall: trade continued, production responded to other signals, maize in particular expanded and found its prices, and a careful reading of the price evidence does not show the systematic collapse of realisations that abolition was predicted to cause. Sukhpal Singh of the Indian Institute of Management Ahmedabad, who has studied the state's marketing for decades, put the institutional point in terms both sides would do well to keep: abolishing the committees without a ready alternative worsened the situation of farmers who had depended on what the markets did provide, and the remedy was to improve and expand the network rather than to end it. The repeal's legal afterlife is itself a small lesson in what abolishing an institution involves. Because the committees and the board had employed staff, held property, let shops and godowns and accumulated fee claims across decades, the repeal Act's savings provisions kept a skeleton of the old system alive for years: fee and assessment proceedings pending at repeal continued before the special officers and administrators the Act substituted, the assets vested in the state had to be administered and in places litigated over, and the absorption, retirement and service conditions of the dissolved bodies' employees generated litigation in the Patna High Court long after the yards had ceased to be regulated, with the court in the course of it recording and upholding the repeal's validity. A legislature can end a market system in a section. It cannot end the system's accrued rights and obligations in the same breath, and any future repeal, in Bihar's pattern or another's, will spend a decade paying the transition costs that the repeal debate rarely prices.

What Bihar demonstrates is the separability thesis of this article in reverse. The state removed the licence restriction and the price discovery infrastructure together, as one bundle, and discovered that the two had been doing different work: the first's departure changed little, and the second's departure is the part its farmers felt.

## Reform by model: the Acts of 2003, 2017 and 2018

The Centre's answer to its constitutional position was the model Act, and the sequence of models is the cleanest record of how official thinking about the mandis changed across two decades. An expert committee on agricultural marketing reforms reported in 2001 and an inter-ministerial task force on agricultural marketing reforms followed in 2002, both recommending that the state Acts be amended to admit competition to the committee yard: direct marketing by processors, exporters and bulk buyers purchasing from farmers outside the yard, markets established in the private and cooperative sectors, and contract farming brought inside the legal frame. Acting on that programme, the Ministry of Agriculture formulated a model Agricultural Produce Marketing (Development and Regulation) Act in 2003 and circulated it to every state and Union Territory for adoption into its own law, a fact the ministry placed on the record of the Lok Sabha in those terms. The 2003 model's design is worth pausing on, because it did not propose to abolish the regulated market. It proposed to pluralise it: the committee yard would remain, joined by licensed private yards and farmer consumer markets, direct purchase centres would be permitted under licence, contract farming agreements would be registered with the committee or a designated authority, the market fee would be levied at a single point in the state, and the committee's duties would be rewritten to include ensuring transparency of pricing, publicising arrivals and prices, and ensuring payment to the farmer on the day of sale. The monopoly being attacked was the monopoly of venue, not the idea of a regulated market.

Adoption was the slow, partial, braided process the federal allocation predicts. Over the following decade and a half, most states amended their Acts in the model's direction and almost none adopted it whole: one state took private markets and not contract farming registration, another took direct marketing and kept its fee structure untouched, several took the provisions on paper and notified the supporting rules late or never, and the Union government tracked the reform state by state and clause by clause, a scoreboard that itself concedes the model's nature. The states' reasons for selectivity were not all obstructionist. A private yard duplicates fixed costs in thin markets; direct purchase by a processor is valuable in a processing belt and irrelevant in a subsistence one; contract farming raised, in states with strong peasant movements, fears that registration would legitimate unequal bargains. But the pattern of adoption also maps the interests this article has described: the provisions that cost the committee and the licensees least were taken first, and the provisions that multiplied buyers at the auction, the heart of the matter, were taken slowest. The model Act strategy thus produced a country of amended statutes whose family resemblance to the 2003 text is genuine and whose distance from it is the measure of each state's politics.

By 2017 the diagnosis had hardened and the instrument changed character. The Agricultural Produce and Livestock Marketing (Promotion and Facilitation) Act of 2017, released on 24 April 2017 in a meeting of state agriculture marketing ministers, was drafted under a committee headed by Ashok Dalwai and was framed, from its title onward, as a replacement for the state Acts rather than a patch upon them. Its principal moves were to treat the whole state as a single unified market area, to provide for a single point levy of market fee and a unified single trading licence valid across the state, to declare warehouses, silos and cold stores to be market sub-yards where trade could lawfully occur, to deregulate fruits and vegetables from the notified channel, to admit e-trading as a statutory mode, to cap market fee and commission charges, to separate the regulatory function from the market operating function by vesting licensing in the state's director of agricultural marketing, and to require payment to the farmer on the day of sale, with a penalty on the buyer for each day of delay borrowed from the Rajasthan statute. Read as a drafting exercise, the 2017 model is the 2003 programme completed and systematised. Read politically, it met the same fate by the same route: states were urged to adopt it, responded with compliments and committees, and enacted from it selectively or not at all, so that its largest service has been as a quarry of provisions for state amendments and as the stated template against which the Centre measured the states when it later chose to legislate itself. A companion instrument, the model State and Union Territory Agricultural Produce and Livestock Contract Farming and Services (Promotion and Facilitation) Act of 2018, released on 22 May 2018 and announced in the Union Budget for 2017-18, took contract farming out of the marketing statute altogether into a dedicated facilitative law under which contracts would be registered, the farmer's title to his land expressly protected, farmer producer organisations enabled to contract on members' behalf, and disputes sent to a local settlement mechanism; it too travelled the road from release to selective adoption.

### What changed in Maharashtra when fruits and vegetables were delisted in 2016?

Delisting removed fruits and vegetables from produce notified under the Maharashtra Act, so their sale no longer had to pass through the committee yards. Farmers could sell at the farm gate, in private markets or to buyers. The committees' claim to a fee on that trade stayed contested, and much trade remained in the yards for their facilities and buyers.

Delisting removed fruits and vegetables from the list of produce notified under the Maharashtra Act, so the legal compulsion to route their wholesale sale through the committee yards fell away for those commodities. A farmer could sell to a trader, a retailer, a processor or a consumer group at the farm gate, in a private market or in the farmers' markets the state promoted, without the sale being an offence and without a licence being needed for the venue. What did not fall away was the committee's claim to its fee in contested cases and the physical advantages of the yards, their location, their labour and their buyer concentration, which is why much of the trade stayed where it had always been even after the law let it leave.

The episode is the cleanest available test of what the notified list does. Fruits and vegetables are perishable, high value and sold by farmers who can reach a city market within hours, so they were the commodities for which the yard's monopoly was most expensive and an alternative channel easiest to build, and the results matched that logic: direct marketing channels and weekly farmers' markets grew, some growers kept margins the yard had taken, and the terminal markets adapted rather than emptied. The same episode warns against generalising from it. A grain farmer two hundred kilometres from a bulk buyer, selling a storable crop into a procurement season, faces a different structure of choices, and a delisting that liberates the peri-urban vegetable grower does not by itself build him a market.

## e-NAM: a platform laid over the statutes

The electronic National Agriculture Market, e-NAM, is the one central intervention in this field that did not require any state to surrender its statute, and its design is a study in working within the allocation the Seventh Schedule imposes. Launched on 14 April 2016 with a pilot of twenty one mandis in eight states trading twenty four commodities, and implemented by the Small Farmers' Agribusiness Consortium as the lead agency of the Ministry of Agriculture, e-NAM is a common electronic trading platform into which existing APMC mandis are integrated. The produce still comes to a physical yard; it is still weighed, and increasingly assayed, there; the sale still occurs under the state's Act, through the state's licensees, and the market fee is still levied under state law. What the platform changes is the width of the auction: a lot listed in a mandi in one district can attract bids from licensed buyers in other mandis and, from January 2019, when the first inter-state trade on the platform was recorded between a mandi in Telangana and a buyer in Andhra Pradesh, from other states as well. The statute built the room; the platform widened the door, without touching the room's ownership.

The conditions of entry disclose the strategy. A state could bring its mandis onto the platform only by amending its Act in three respects the Centre specified: a single trading licence valid across the state, a single point levy of the market fee, and provision for electronic auction or trading in its markets. The model Act reforms of 2003, which states had adopted à la carte for a decade, were thus converted into admission criteria for a funded central scheme, and states amended their laws to qualify in numbers the persuasion of the model alone had not achieved. Integration then proceeded mandi by mandi, reaching five hundred and eighty five regulated markets in sixteen states and two Union Territories by 2020, with the physical prerequisites, assaying laboratories, electronic weighing, gate entry systems and bank payment rails, installed yard by yard alongside the software.

The platform also quietly changed the politics of the fee. A state that joined accepted a ceiling in practice on what its committees could do with charges on platform trade, since a lot offered to buyers across a network invites comparison of the total deductions at each yard of origin, and a yard whose bundle of fee and commission is visibly the heaviest in the network prices its own farmers' lots down. Transparency of charges, which committees had published on notice boards to an audience of the already captive, acquired an audience of potential buyers elsewhere, and the discipline that followed is the kind no statute in the family had ever imposed on a committee from outside. In the same movement, farmer producer organisations gained a statutory-adjacent route to aggregate lots and offer them on the platform at a scale a single smallholder's trolley cannot reach, which answered, partially, the aggregation problem that had always made the small lot the worst priced lot in the yard.

The honest assessment of the platform is that its constraint was never the software. An electronic bid is a promise about a physical lot, and the promise is only as good as the assaying behind it: a distant buyer bidding on a description of moisture, admixture and grade requires laboratories and graders he can trust in a yard he will never visit, and in much of the network the assaying capacity remained thin, so a large share of trade on the platform stayed local in substance, with lots listed online and bought by the men standing in the yard. Payment moved online and quickened where banking reached; logistics, the aggregation of small lots into truckloads and their movement between states, remained outside the platform's gift. None of this diminishes the design's importance to the legal story. e-NAM demonstrated that the APMC statute, so often described as a wall, functioned in this instance as a socket: because every participating market already had a legal identity, a licensed population and a fee system, a national platform could be plugged into it state by state, and the integration that a central Act could not have imposed was achieved by a standard the states chose to meet.

## 2020: the parallel trade area and its repeal

The APMC statutes themselves were not amended by the central legislation of 2020, and precision on this point matters because most public description got it wrong. What the Farmers' Produce Trade and Commerce (Promotion and Facilitation) Act of 2020 did was to define a trade area consisting of every place outside the physical premises of the market yards and deemed markets notified under the state Acts, and to give farmers and traders freedom to conduct inter-state and intra-state trade in that area, with state governments barred from levying any market fee, cess or levy on trade conducted there. The mandis were left standing, their Acts unrepealed, their fees intact on trade within them; a parallel channel was legislated into existence beside them, resting on Parliament's Concurrent and Union List entries on trade in foodstuffs and inter-state commerce rather than on the State List subject of markets. The architecture of that Act, the constitutional contest over it, and its companion statutes are owned by the series article on [the farm laws of 2020 and what they attempted](/2014/04/01/farm-laws-2020-explained/), and the political convulsion that followed, the year long protest at Delhi's borders and the repeal of all three farm laws by the Farm Laws Repeal Act of 2021, passed by both Houses on 29 November 2021 and assented to within days, belongs with the series account of [the farmers' protest and its legislative lessons](/2014/05/01/farmers-protest-legislative-lessons/). For this article's purpose, the episode is the limiting case of the federal problem: persuasion by model Act had moved slowly for two decades, legislation around the state Acts moved in a season, and the political cost of the second route restored the first. The state statutes emerged from the episode formally untouched, which is not the same as unchanged, since every state government in the surplus belt had been taught the price of appearing to threaten the mandi system and its fee income. The states had also been heard inside the episode itself: several state assemblies resolved against the central laws, Punjab and others moved their own legislation asserting the state position on agricultural marketing while the central Acts stood, and the constitutional argument from Entries 14 and 28 was pressed in the political arena with a completeness that spared the courts the final word on it, since the repeal withdrew the question before any authoritative adjudication of the boundary between Entry 33 and the market entries could settle the law. The boundary therefore remains where the Constitution left it, argued but unadjudicated, available to the next Parliament that tests it and to the next state that resists.

## The two cases, stated at full strength

The deregulation case, in the form its serious advocates make it, is an argument about freedom and incidence. A farmer who may not lawfully sell his produce to the buyer offering the highest price, at the place of his choosing, is not being protected by the rule that confines him; he is being taxed by it. The licensed population of the yard is small because the state keeps it small; the auction is thin because the statute has emptied every competing venue; the fee and commission are deducted from a price the farmer had no hand in forming. Ashok Gulati, agricultural economist and a former chairman of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices, has argued across decades that Indian farmers are net taxed by a policy regime that suppresses what they receive while professing to support them, and that marketing law is a principal instrument of the suppression: free the farmer to sell anywhere, to processors, exporters, retailers and other states, admit private markets and direct purchase, and competition among buyers will do for his price what no committee has done. Ramesh Chand of NITI Aayog has made the companion empirical point about the charges: levies of the Punjab order, charged even on transactions that never use the mandi's infrastructure, function as a tax on trade that raises food prices, deters private investment in marketing and loads the food subsidy, and should be cut to the cost of services actually rendered. On this view, the mandi's undoubted services, aggregation, weighing, auction, payment, are real but do not need a monopoly to exist; they will be supplied by whoever wants the farmer's custom, at prices competition will discipline.

The retention case, made at its strongest by the farmer organisations of the procurement belt and by scholars of the state's agrarian institutions, begins by denying that the alternative to the mandi is a competitive market. For the smallholder with two acres, no storage, a debt falling due and a perishable or bulky crop, the alternative to selling in a yard where many buyers are gathered by law and an auction is supervised is selling alone, at the farm gate, to the one buyer who comes, who is frequently the same trader the statute licensed, freed of the licence's discipline, the schedule of charges and the committee's record. Monopsony, one buyer facing many sellers, is the countryside's default condition, and the mandi is the only institution that has ever reliably converted it into something resembling an auction. The retentionists add the system's second function, which the deregulators' framework omits: the mandi network is the delivery mechanism of the support price, and procurement at the minimum support price through the yards is what puts a floor under the open market price across the surplus states, a floor whose removal would be felt by farmers who never sell to the government at all. The Samyukt Kisan Morcha, the coalition that sustained the protest of 2020 and 2021, framed its defence of the mandis in exactly these terms, not as nostalgia but as the protection of the only price formation process in which the farmer is not alone.

Between these poles stands an institutional middle position that the official committees themselves mostly occupied, and it should be recorded because it is where policy, when it has moved, has actually gone. The committee on doubling farmers' income, whose work under Ashok Dalwai framed the 2017 and 2018 models, treated the mandi neither as sacred nor as scrap: its programme was to retain and modernise the regulated market, multiply the channels around it until the farmer's choice was real, unify the state as one market for licence and fee, and build the grading and assaying infrastructure that lets quality speak. The National Commission on Farmers had earlier pressed the same direction from the farmer's side, recommending that markets be brought within reach of producers and that the regulated system's services be extended rather than its compulsion. The distance between this position and the deregulators' is the distance between licence as registration and licence as abolition; the distance between it and the retentionists' is the distance between defending the yard and defending the yard's monopoly. Most of the durable change in the family's history sits in that middle ground.

Both cases are right about the system the other defends. The deregulators are right that the licence restriction manufactures scarcity and that the fee bundle in the high levy states is indefensible as a charge for services. The retentionists are right that an unbuilt alternative is not an alternative, that Bihar's missing private markets are data and not anecdote, and that price discovery and procurement are public goods the yard supplies and a purely private chain underprovides. The confusion that sustains the debate is the treatment of the mandi as one thing. It is two: a restriction, the compulsory channel and the scarce licence, and an infrastructure, the physical yard, the supervised auction, the assaying and record, the procurement counter. The restriction can be relaxed without demolishing the infrastructure, as Karnataka's widening of the auction and Maharashtra's delisting each show in their different ways, and the infrastructure can be retained and funded without the restriction, as the single point levy and the capped fee of the 2017 model contemplate. Reform fails when it is drafted as a verdict on the bundle: repeal that takes the infrastructure with the restriction, or defence that protects the restriction as though it were the infrastructure. The question the brief for this article set, whether the licence should be scarce, is the right question because it separates the two: a licence that registers fitness and disciplines conduct can be issued to all comers who qualify, in numbers the trade itself determines, and nothing in the protective purpose of 1897 or 1963 requires it to be rationed.

## The amendment arc: how the family's texts have drifted

Read across the family's amendment histories and a common drift appears, legible even in the short titles. The original statutes spoke the language of regulation and control: their objects were to regulate buying and selling, to constitute committees and to discipline practices, and the Punjab Act of 1961 states that purpose without decoration. The first drift was verbal and then substantive: development entered the titles, in Maharashtra by the amendment of 2005 that wrote the word into the Act's name, in Karnataka by the substitution of 2007 that recast its preamble around improved regulation, development of an efficient marketing system, agri-processing and export, as though the statute had been re-founded as an instrument of growth rather than of order. The second drift admitted the channels the model Act of 2003 proposed, state by state and clause by clause, direct marketing licences, private and cooperative markets, contract farming provisions, single point levy, each amendment an argument won or lost in that state's politics. The third drift was the electronic one: between 2016 and the end of that decade, state after state amended to recognise a single statewide licence, electronic trading and the single point levy, not from conversion to the model philosophy but because the platform's funding stood behind those clauses, which is why those three provisions are the most uniformly adopted in the family's whole reform history. The fourth movement was reactive: the state amendments and counter resolutions of 2020 and 2021, passed to assert the state position against the central farm laws, and the fee and cess revisions with which states defended their revenue as the central pressure rose.

The drift's direction is consistent enough to support a prediction the reader can test against any state's next amendment. The texts move, decade by decade, from control toward facilitation in their language, from monopoly of venue toward plurality of channels in their structure, and from the committee as operator toward the state as licensor of operators in their institutional design, while the fee survives every redrafting and the committee survives every facilitation. Nothing in the record suggests the family's end by repeal; Bihar stands alone after two decades with its example unimitated, and the 2021 repeal of the central alternative closed the other road. What the record suggests is continued amendment along the gradient the texts already show, fastest where a state government spends its own capital, slowest where the amendment's cost falls on the committee and its licensees, which is to say that the family's future will be written, as its past was, in twenty odd state legislatures and not in New Delhi.

## How to read a state APMC Act

A reader opening one of the family's statutes for the first time, whether to advise, to study or to argue, will read it faster for knowing where the weight sits. The definitions chapter repays slow reading, because the Act's reach is decided there: the definition of agricultural produce and its schedule, of market and market area, of the functionaries from trader to weighman, and of the producer, whose protected status the whole scheme assumes, are the provisions on which coverage questions turn. The notification provisions come next, since the Act in the book is only a frame until the government has declared areas and specified produce, and the operative law for any real field or yard is the Act as notified. The committee chapters, constitution, elections, supersession, the market fund and its audit, state the institutional facts that predict how the yard will actually behave. The licensing and fee chapters carry the system's economics, and the offences chapter, short and modest in every member of the family, tells the reader what the statute fears, which is escape, not exploitation. Amendments in this family are frequent and often made by ordinance in the first instance, so the text to trust is the text as amended, read with the state's rules and the committee's bye-laws, which is where the scheduled charges and the auction's procedure live in enforceable form. Two cautions complete the method. The short title's year is the year of the parent Act and says nothing about the vintage of the provision in hand, which may be a substitution decades younger; and the family's shared vocabulary conceals different content, so that market fee, commission and even market area must be re-read in each state's definitions rather than carried across a border in the reader's head.

## Assessment: what the record supports

The verdict this article defends follows from the structure it has traced. The APMC statutes solved the problem they were written against. Measured against the unregulated transaction of the moneylender's book and the cloth covered bargain, the regulated market delivered supervised weighment, published charges, open bidding and a payment discipline enforceable through the licence, and the growth of the marketed surplus through the Green Revolution was handled by yards the system built and maintained out of its own fee. Any honest account has to begin there, because the fashionable summary, that the mandis were a mistake, cannot explain their adoption by twenty legislatures or their defence by the farmers of the states where they work hardest. What the record equally shows is that the solution carried its cost in its mechanism. A compulsory venue with a rationed licence population will, over decades, convert a protective institution into a toll on the transaction it protects, and the conversion does not require corruption: it requires only that licences stay scarce, that committees answer to the yard's population as much as to its hinterland, and that fees drift from the cost of services toward the capacity of the trade to bear them. Punjab's eight and a half per cent and the thin auctions of the small yards are not aberrations of the design. They are the design, aged.

One fiscal question should be answered before the verdict, because it decides whether the separation proposed below is practical: if the licence is opened and the fee capped at the cost of services, who pays for the yard? The honest answer is that the yard's legitimate services, platforms, weighbridges, assaying, price reporting, sanitation and the procurement counter's physical plant, are cheaper than the bundle the high fee states collect, and that a fee set at the cost of those services across the volume of a working yard is a modest charge of the Maharashtra order rather than the Punjab one. What would lose funding is the surplus the mandi bundle carries for other purposes, above all the rural development spending Punjab finances from grain. That spending is defensible or not as taxation policy; what it cannot be is a reason to keep a marketing charge at a level its own governing test, correlation with the service, cannot carry. A state that wishes to tax agricultural trade for rural roads has legislatures and finance Acts with which to do it openly, and the candour would improve both the tax and the market.

The reform direction the record supports is therefore neither of the slogans. It is the separation this article has argued for: keep the market, end the scarcity. The licence should become a registration of fitness available to every qualified applicant, the venue should multiply under licence until the committee yard has competitors it must answer to, the fee should be capped at the cost of the service and levied once in the state, and the auction should be widened by platform and assaying until a bid from three districts away is as good as a bid from the front row. Each element exists somewhere in the family's own practice: Karnataka's unified licence and platform, Maharashtra's delisting and single point levy, Punjab's demonstration, at a price, of what a funded yard network can do for procurement. The states that prosper their farmers best in this field have been the states that amended their own statutes in this direction without waiting to be rescued or abolished from outside, which is what a State List subject means when it works. This article is legal information and analysis of a statute family, not advice on any particular sale, licence or dispute, and a reader facing a live question under a state Act should take the position from that state's text and rules, which differ in the particulars that decide cases.

For the reader who intends to work on this material, whether as an aspirant organising the federalism of Entries 14, 26, 27 and 28 against Entry 33, as a researcher comparing the fee schedules and committee compositions of three states, or as a farmer organisation documenting what happens to a season's consignments between the farm gate and the settlement statement, the working method is the one the statutes themselves suggest: keep the record. A private, durable file of one's own notes, sale slips, state notifications and comparisons, maintained state by state and season by season, is the difference between arguing about the mandi system from slogans and arguing from evidence, and readers who build such records will find the practice of [keeping working files private and protected in the way journalists use VaultBook to protect their sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) directly suited to material of this kind. The statutes will continue to change state by state, notification by notification; the reader with a record will see the changes first.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is an APMC mandi and how does it work?

An APMC mandi is a regulated wholesale market for agricultural produce, established under a state Agricultural Produce Marketing Act and administered by a market committee, the committee being what the letters APMC expand to. The state government notifies a market area and the produce the Act covers there, and the first wholesale sale of that notified produce must take place in the committee's market yard or sub-yard. Farmers bring their produce to the yard, usually through a licensed commission agent; the lot is displayed, sold by open auction or the prescribed method under committee supervision, weighed on the yard's scales and paid for under the Act's payment discipline. The committee charges a market fee on the transaction, licenses the traders and agents who operate, and maintains the yard's infrastructure out of its market fund.

### Q: Why are APMC laws made by states?

Because the Constitution gives the subject to them. Agriculture is Entry 14 of the State List of the Seventh Schedule, markets and fairs are Entry 28, and trade and commerce within a state and the production, supply and distribution of goods are Entries 26 and 27, each subject to the Concurrent List. A state legislature is therefore the competent legislature for the constitution of markets, the licensing of those who trade in them and the levying of market fees, and every APMC law in force is a state law enacted under those entries. Parliament's entries are differently shaped: Entry 33 of the Concurrent List covers trade and commerce in foodstuffs and certain other commodities, and Entry 42 of the Union List covers inter-state trade, which is why the Centre has historically reformed agricultural marketing by circulating model Acts for states to adopt rather than by enacting a central APMC Act of its own.

### Q: What is a market fee and who pays it?

A market fee is the charge a market committee levies under its state Act on transactions in notified produce within its market area, calculated as a percentage of the value of the produce bought or sold. It is the financial foundation of the regulated market: the fee is credited to the committee's market fund, out of which the yard's staff, auction platforms, weighbridges, godowns, roads and other services are paid for. In legal form the fee is generally levied on and collected from the buyer side of the transaction, often collected through the commission agent who handled the sale, though the economic burden is shared through the price the buyer is willing to bid. It is distinct from the commission agent's remuneration and from state cesses such as Punjab's rural development fee, and its rate is fixed or capped by each state's law, which is why it differs sharply across the country.

### Q: Who is an arhtiya and what role do they play?

An arhtiya is a commission agent licensed under a state marketing Act who intermediates the sale of a farmer's produce in the mandi. The farmer delivers his lot to the arhtiya's shop in the yard; the arhtiya's establishment unloads, cleans and displays it, presents it at the auction or to the procurement agency, supervises weighing, receives the price from the buyer and settles with the farmer after deducting his commission, the market charges and any advance outstanding. In the northern grain belt the arhtiya is also the farmer's creditor, financing seed, fertiliser and household needs across the season against the coming harvest, which makes him a banker as much as a broker. In Punjab and Haryana the arhtiyas are also the procurement machinery of the state, assembling and processing grain bought at the support price on behalf of government agencies, for which they receive commission.

### Q: Can a farmer sell produce outside the mandi?

It depends on the state, the commodity and the kind of sale, which is why the question has no single Indian answer. Under the classic form of a state Act, the wholesale sale of produce notified for a market area must take place in the committee's yard or sub-yard or at another place the Act permits, and a farmer who sells notified produce to a trader outside that channel exposes the buyer, and in some Acts himself, to penalty. But the channel has been widened in many states: commodities delisted from notification, as fruits and vegetables were in Maharashtra in 2016, can be sold anywhere; states that adopted the model Act reforms permit direct marketing to processors and bulk buyers under licence, farmer consumer markets and private yards; and retail sale by a farmer of his own produce to a consumer stands differently from the wholesale transaction the Acts target. The state Act and its notified list decide.

### Q: What did the Model APMC Act 2003 propose?

The Model Agricultural Produce Marketing (Development and Regulation) Act of 2003, formulated by the Union Ministry of Agriculture and circulated to all states for adoption, proposed to end the market committee's monopoly of venue while keeping the regulated market. It provided for markets to be established in the private and cooperative sectors alongside committee yards, for direct marketing under which processors, exporters and bulk buyers could purchase from farmers outside the yard under licence, for contract farming agreements registered under the Act, for farmer consumer markets where producers sell directly to consumers, and for the market fee to be levied at a single point in the state rather than at each passage of the produce. It also rewrote the committee's duties toward transparency, requiring publication of arrivals and prices and payment to the farmer on the day of sale. States adopted its provisions selectively over the following decade and a half rather than enacting it whole.

### Q: What is e-NAM and has it worked?

e-NAM, the electronic National Agriculture Market, is a common online trading platform into which existing APMC mandis are integrated, launched on 14 April 2016 with twenty one mandis in eight states and implemented by the Small Farmers' Agribusiness Consortium. It does not replace the state Acts: produce is still brought to a physical mandi, weighed and sold under state law, but the lot is listed electronically so that licensed buyers in other mandis and other states can bid, widening the auction beyond the yard. States could join only after amending their Acts to provide a single statewide trading licence, a single point levy of market fee and electronic trading. Its record is mixed. Integration reached five hundred and eighty five mandis by 2020 and online payment and price information improved where it operates, but trade remained largely local in practice because assaying and grading facilities, on which a distant buyer's confidence depends, were thin in many yards.

### Q: What happened when Bihar abolished its APMC Act?

Bihar repealed its Bihar Agricultural Produce Markets Act of 1960 by a repeal Act effective from 1 September 2006, abolishing its market committees and its state marketing board, vesting their assets in the state and saving only pending fee and penalty proceedings. The Patna High Court upheld the repeal. Trade did not stop: it continued in the old yards and, increasingly, within villages, but the expected wave of private investment in new markets did not arrive. A 2019 diagnostic study by the National Council of Applied Economic Research found market density low and most produce sold to traders and commission agents inside the village, while econometric studies of prices found mixed or statistically insignificant effects, with paddy prices lower and maize prices higher after repeal. Bihar is therefore cited by both sides of the debate and settles neither: the licence vanished and changed little, and the infrastructure vanished and was missed.

### Q: What is the difference between a market fee and a commission?

The market fee is a statutory charge levied by the market committee under the state Act on the value of a transaction in notified produce, and it belongs to the committee's market fund, which pays for the yard's staff, works and services. The commission is the remuneration of the commission agent who intermediated the sale, fixed by the Act's schedule of charges or its rules, and it belongs to the agent as his earnings. The two differ in who imposes them, who receives them and what they buy: the fee is public revenue for market infrastructure, the commission is private payment for the service of receiving, presenting, selling and settling the farmer's lot. In the farmer's settlement both appear as deductions from his price, which is why they are confused, but a reform can cut one and leave the other untouched.

### Q: Who sits on a market committee and how are its members chosen?

A market committee is constituted under the state Act for each market area, and its composition is designed to seat the constituencies of the yard around one table. Agriculturists of the market area form the largest bloc in the standard design; traders and commission agents licensed in the market elect their own representatives; cooperative marketing societies, local bodies such as the panchayat and the municipality, and the interests of consumers or labour find places in varying state patterns; and the state government nominates members and officials and appoints the committee's secretary. The farmer and trader members are chosen by election from electoral rolls of their constituencies under the Act and rules, for terms the statute fixes, while nominated members hold office at the government's instance. State governments also retain supervisory powers, including, in many Acts, the power to supersede an elected committee and appoint an administrator, a power whose use has often made committee elections intermittent in practice.

### Q: What does it mean for a crop to be notified under an APMC Act?

Notification is the act by which a commodity is brought inside the regulated system for a market area. The state Act defines agricultural produce in wide terms, but its compulsion bites only on the produce specified for the area in the notification or schedule issued under the Act. Once a crop is notified for an area, its wholesale purchase and sale in that area must follow the Act: the transaction belongs in the yard or another permitted place, the parties trading must be licensed, the prescribed method of sale applies, and the market fee is leviable on the transaction. A crop that is not notified is outside that discipline and may be traded freely. Because coverage is created and removed by notification, a state can deregulate a single commodity without touching its Act, which is what states did when they delisted fruits and vegetables: the statute stood, the yard stood, and those commodities walked out of both.

### Q: What is a direct marketing licence under the model Acts?

A direct marketing licence is a permission, introduced by the Model APMC Act of 2003 and taken into the laws of states that adopted that reform, under which a processor, exporter, bulk buyer or other approved purchaser may buy agricultural produce directly from farmers outside the market yard, at a farm gate collection centre or the buyer's own premises, without routing the purchase through the committee's auction. The licence is granted by the market committee or the state authority the Act designates, on conditions that typically include recording the purchases, paying the market fee at the single point the law prescribes, and paying the farmer within the period the rules fix. Its purpose is to shorten the chain for produce destined for processing or export, where the yard's aggregation service adds cost without adding value, while keeping the transaction inside a recorded and fee bearing frame. The farmer gains a second channel; he is not obliged to use it.

### Q: What is a private market yard and who can set one up?

A private market yard is a wholesale market for agricultural produce established and operated by a private person, company, cooperative or farmer producer organisation under licence, as an alternative to the market committee's yard. The idea entered the reform programme with the Model APMC Act of 2003, which proposed that the private and cooperative sectors be allowed to set up markets, and it was carried further by the model Act of 2017, which would let warehouses, silos and cold stores be declared market sub-yards. Under state laws that have adopted the provision, the applicant must satisfy the licensing authority about land, infrastructure, auction and storage facilities, weighment and the services the yard will provide, and the yard then operates under the Act's discipline: licensed trade, prescribed charges and the market fee or a user charge in its place. The point of the device is competition of venue: a committee yard that can lose trade to a better run yard nearby has a reason to improve.

### Q: How is the price discovered in an open auction at a mandi?

The lot is first made visible: the produce is unloaded at the commission agent's premises, cleaned and heaped or stacked in bags, and buyers walk the lots and judge quality by eye, handful and experience, since formal grading and assaying are available in only some yards. When the sale opens, an auctioneer, a committee official or the commission agent calls for bids lot by lot; licensed traders bid openly against one another in the hearing of the seller, the bid rises by small increments, and the highest bid is recorded as the price, subject in many yards to the seller's formal right to refuse and take his produce home, a right whose practical value depends on his storage, his transport and his debts. The committee records the prices struck, and those records feed the daily price reporting through which mandi prices are published. Where procurement agencies are buying at the support price, that price acts as a floor under the bidding for the grades they accept.

### Q: What happens if a trader buys notified produce without a licence?

He commits the central offence of the marketing Acts. Trading in a market area in notified produce without a licence from the market committee, or buying such produce outside the places the Act permits, exposes the trader to the Act's penalties, which are typically fines that are modest in amount, recovery of the market fee evaded, and proceedings before the committee or the magistrate as the state Act provides; many Acts let the committee compound the offence on payment of a composition fee. The sanctions with real weight are administrative rather than criminal: a licensee found trading outside his licence risks its suspension or cancellation, which expels him from the lawful trade of the yard, and an unlicensed buyer's purchase can be treated as no sale the system will recognise or record. In practice enforcement concentrates on the fee and the venue, through check posts on roads out of the market area and inspection of traders' premises and books, rather than on prosecuting individual transactions.

### Q: What is a state agricultural marketing board and what does it do?

A state agricultural marketing board is the statutory apex body that most state marketing Acts establish above the market committees, known in Punjab simply as the Punjab Mandi Board and elsewhere by similar names. The board coordinates and supervises the committees of the state, approves their budgets and works, administers a marketing development fund built from contributions out of committee income, finances the construction and modernisation of market yards, runs market information and training, and advises the state government on marketing policy. It is the institution that turns a hundred local committees into a system: a farmer's fee is collected by his committee, but the board is where the surplus of rich markets can be directed to the development of poor ones and where the state's grading, research and infrastructure programmes are housed. Boards are constituted under the Act with official and non-official members, and their chairmanship is frequently a political appointment, which is one reason the boards figure in every account of the system's politics as well as its administration.

### Q: What must a state change in its law before its mandis can join e-NAM?

Three amendments were made the condition of integration. The state Act had to provide a single trading licence valid across the whole state, so that a buyer licensed anywhere in the state could bid in any integrated mandi. It had to provide for a single point levy of the market fee, so that produce traded onward within the state is not charged again at each market it passes. And it had to admit electronic auction or e-trading as a lawful mode of sale in its markets, so that a bid made on the platform is a bid in law and not merely in software. These were the three reforms the electronic market borrowed from the model Act programme and converted into admission criteria, and states amended their statutes to meet them in order to qualify. Beyond the statute, a mandi needs the physical apparatus the platform presumes: assaying facilities, electronic weighing, gate entry recording and payment systems, without which its lots cannot honestly be offered to distant bidders.

### Q: Why do market fees differ so much between states?

Because the fee is a creature of state law and the states have made different fiscal choices inside the same architecture. One legislature caps its fee in the statute itself, as Punjab does at three rupees for every hundred rupees of value in its Act of 1961, while another leaves the rate to rules and notifications that can be revised without amending the Act. States also differ in what they load onto the mandi transaction: Punjab charges a rural development fee of three per cent beside its market fee, financing rural roads and infrastructure out of grain trade, while Maharashtra's markets report a cess of one per cent of trade value and several states charge less still or exempt commodities outright. The differences reflect what each state asks the mandi to pay for, the strength of the interests that receive the money, and the politics of the farm sector in that state. Since the fee is priced into buyers' bids, the variation is also a variation in the cost of selling the same crop on opposite sides of a state border.

### Q: Do APMC Acts apply to livestock and processed produce?

Generally yes, within limits set by each state's definition and notification. The state Acts define agricultural produce widely, typically as all produce, whether processed or not, of agriculture, horticulture, animal husbandry, fisheries and forests that the Act's schedule specifies, and many states notify livestock and livestock products, so that cattle markets and trades in commodities such as milk products, hides or wool can stand inside the system where they are notified. Processed forms are included in the same way: the definition in several Acts expressly covers produce in processed form, which is how commodities such as gur, oilseeds and their oils, or cotton and its ginned forms, appear in notified lists. The model Act of 2017 carried the logic into its title, addressing agricultural produce and livestock together. But coverage remains notification specific and state specific: a processed product or a head of livestock is regulated in a given market area only if the state's notification for that area names it, and much livestock trade in practice moves through traditional fairs and private sales that the Acts reach unevenly.

### Q: What is single point levy of market fee?

Single point levy means that the market fee on a consignment of produce is charged once, at one point in the state, and not again when the same produce is traded onward. Under the unreformed working of many state Acts, produce assembled in one market area, sold to a trader who carried it to a second market, and sold again to a processor could attract the fee at each passage, so that several small percentages compounded along the chain and the final price carried a load no single committee had voted for. The Model APMC Act of 2003 proposed the single point levy as a standard reform, the electronic market later made it a condition of a state's joining, and the model Act of 2017 adopted it as a design principle. Its mechanism is documentary: proof that the fee has been paid on the consignment, carried with the produce or recorded against the trader's licence, entitles it to pass subsequent markets without a second charge.
