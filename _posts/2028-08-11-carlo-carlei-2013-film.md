## The Verdict First: A Handsome Film Built on a Broken Bargain

Carlo Carlei's Romeo and Juliet, released in October 2013 with Douglas Booth as Romeo and Hailee Steinfeld as Juliet, sold itself as the traditional version of the play for a generation raised on Baz Luhrmann's guns and Franco Zeffirelli's memory. The costumes were Renaissance, the streets were the real streets of Verona and Mantua, the faces were young, and the marketing promised a return to roots. What the film actually delivered was something no major screen version had attempted before: a period production, dressed as fidelity, whose dialogue had been quietly rewritten by its screenwriter, Julian Fellowes, who cut Shakespeare's text hard, paraphrased passages he judged difficult, and composed new lines in an imitation of the original manner. The verdict of this study is plain. The Carlei film is the most instructive failure among the famous screen versions of the play, because its failure was structural rather than accidental. A film may legitimately cut Shakespeare, as Zeffirelli cut him, or rehouse Shakespeare, as Luhrmann rehoused him, and survive on the strength of what remains. A film that keeps the play's plot, the play's period and the play's most famous speeches, while silently replacing the connective verse with pastiche, has kept the one element audiences could have supplied for themselves, the story, and discarded the one element that makes the story worth filming, the language in which it thinks.

A note on sources belongs at the threshold, since the film's literature is unusually contaminated by its own publicity. The account that follows rests on the trade record of the production, Screen Daily and The Hollywood Reporter and Variety reporting from Cannes and from the release; on the review file of October 2013, from the notices quoted by name throughout; on the aggregators' tallies, which are cited as tallies and not as judgements; on the interviews in which Fellowes and Carlei explained themselves, in Entertainment Weekly, the BBC and the Canadian press; and on the scholarly responses, Levenson's above all, that tested the makers' claims against the play in the Oxford edition. Where the sources divide, on the budget's exact composition or the dialogue's exact percentage, the division is reported rather than resolved. The film itself, at 118 minutes, is short enough that every claim made here about its contents can be checked against a single viewing, and readers disposed to check are encouraged toward exactly that scepticism, since the study's whole argument is that Shakespeare's audiences, like this film's, deserve material they can verify.

The evidence for that verdict runs through every department of the production. The trade press framed the film as a prestige object: the first feature backed by Swarovski Entertainment, the crystal company's new production arm, budgeted at seventeen million dollars and shot on location in Italy. The reviews that followed were among the harshest any Shakespeare film of its decade received. Justin Chang, writing in Variety, called it a desultory version, streamlined and simplified by Fellowes, offering a throwback to classicism while standing in little danger of being mistaken for a classic, and shorn of eroticism, intensity or purpose. The aggregate record agreed in substance if not in wording: a Metacritic score of 41 from thirty reviews, a Rotten Tomatoes approval figure in the mid-twenties, and an American theatrical life so brief that the film earned barely more than a million dollars in the market it was designed to conquer. Fellowes defended the rewrite on the ground that an unchanged Shakespeare requires, in his phrase to the BBC, a kind of Shakespearian scholarship that his own expensive Cambridge education had given him and that other intelligent people lacked. That defence, and the fury it provoked from actors and from the Oxford editor of the play, Jill Levenson, is the centre of the case. The film asked to be judged as the accessible Shakespeare. It is examined here as exactly that, and found to have misunderstood where the difficulty, and the accessibility, of Shakespeare actually live.

None of this makes the film negligible. Its cast list reads like a deliberate bridge between generations, with Paul Giamatti as Friar Laurence, Damian Lewis as Lord Capulet, Lesley Manville as the Nurse and Stellan Skarsgard as the Prince supporting two leads who were, for once, close to the ages Shakespeare imagined. Its score, written at short notice by Abel Korzeniowski after a completed score by James Horner had been set aside, won the International Film Music Critics Association's Film Score of the Year award, an honour no other element of the production approached. Its locations, from the loggia at Caprarola that served as Juliet's balcony to the monastery of San Benedetto at Subiaco, gave it a material authenticity that studio-built Veronas cannot match. A fair account has to hold these assets in view while explaining why they could not save the enterprise. The explanation offered across the sections that follow is that each asset was subordinated to the same mistaken theory: that the audience for this play needed protection from its words. The scenery trusted the eye, the casting trusted the face, the score trusted the ear, and the screenplay trusted nobody.

The film's place in the screen history of the play frames the whole question. Four versions dominate that history, and each arrived as a settlement between the text and its moment. George Cukor's MGM production of 1936 embalmed the play in studio prestige, with adult stars and a reverence that treated the verse as a museum label. Castellani's Anglo-Italian version of 1954 took the play back to Italian locations and won the Golden Lion at Venice, proving that place could carry meaning the studio set could not. Zeffirelli in 1968 cast teenagers, cut hard and made the version by which classrooms still measure the rest. Luhrmann in 1996 changed everything except the words. Carlei's production, arriving seventeen years after Luhrmann's, defined itself against all four inheritances at once: Cukor's prestige without Cukor's stars, Castellani's locations without Castellani's festival, Zeffirelli's teenagers without Zeffirelli's text discipline, and an anti-Luhrmann posture that kept the period while abandoning the language Luhrmann had kept. A film negotiating four inheritances simultaneously needed a clear principle of selection. The principle it chose, accessibility through paraphrase, satisfied none of the four traditions and armed all of their partisans against it.

## How the Carlei Film Was Made: Money, Casting and the Italian Shoot

The production began as a vehicle for a screenwriter rather than for its director, and the anomaly shaped everything that followed. Julian Fellowes came to the project with an Academy Award for Gosford Park and with Downton Abbey remaking his public identity week by week; Carlo Carlei came with a respectable Italian career, headed by The Flight of the Innocent, and a Hollywood reputation light enough that many American reviews reached for his least representative credit, the 1995 fantasy Fluke, when introducing him. Producer Ileen Maisel developed the film through Amber Entertainment, with Echo Lake Entertainment and, decisively, Swarovski Entertainment, the production arm launched by the Austrian crystal house under its chairwoman Nadja Swarovski, who took a producer's credit alongside Maisel and Fellowes. Screen Daily reported from Cannes in May 2012 that Swarovski had made a substantial backing of the film's seventeen million dollar overall budget, that the company planned to work in the ten to twenty million dollar range, and that a related line of jewellery would accompany the release. The Hollywood Reporter recorded Nadja Swarovski describing the commitment as substantial, far outweighing any other single commitment the company had made in that period. A Shakespeare tragedy was thus financed as a brand extension, and the brand logic matters to the later history: the film had to look luxurious, had to photograph beautifully, and had to offend nobody, because its first function was to gleam.

The financing was assembled in the open market and reported in detail, which allows the production's economics to be reconstructed with unusual completeness. At Cannes in May 2012 the producers screened four minutes of footage for selected buyers, with Nadja Swarovski, Fellowes and Maisel presenting in person. Pre-sales were announced to Entertainment Film Distributors for Britain, to Icon for Australia and to Rai for Italy, with further territories including Russia, South Korea, Iceland and the Middle East, while the American rights were held back under the sales agent Rena Ronson in the hope of a major deal. Trade reporting from the same market spoke of a British opening the following February, on Valentine's Day, a date that announced the intended positioning as plainly as any poster. The American deal that eventually materialised was of a different kind from the one hoped for, as the commercial section records, and the February date slipped to October. The gap between the Cannes presentation, confident, pre-sold and brand-led, and the eventual release, limited, service-financed and quarrel-shadowed, is the production's biography in miniature.

Carlei's own position in the enterprise deserves a clarifying paragraph, because the reviews treated him as an unknown and the record is more interesting. His reputation rested on The Flight of the Innocent of 1992, a kidnapping drama that had travelled internationally and established him as a director of children in peril, a specialism directly relevant to a film carried by a fifteen-year-old Juliet. His Hollywood credit, the 1995 fantasy Fluke, was unrepresentative enough that hostile American reviewers wielded it as a joke at his expense. He was, in substance, an Italian director of proven competence with young performers, hired to give an English screenwriter's script an Italian body. The division of labour was visible in the finished film: the visual conception is coherent, Italianate and often beautiful, while the verbal conception belongs to another sensibility and another country. Productions in which the director and the screenwriter pursue different films rarely satisfy either intention, and the Carlei film's two intentions, spectacle and simplification, proceeded in parallel without ever meeting. Ed Westwick, who played Tybalt, was reported to have been the first actor to read the script. In April 2011 Hailee Steinfeld, fresh from an Academy Award nomination for True Grit, entered talks for Juliet, and in June 2011 Douglas Booth was cast as Romeo after a search said to have considered three hundred actors. Paul Wesley was offered Paris before the part settled, in February 2012, on Tom Wisdom. Around the young leads the production assembled a supporting company of unusual weight: Giamatti's Friar, Lewis's Capulet, Natascha McElhone as Lady Capulet, Manville's Nurse, Christian Cooke as Mercutio, Kodi Smit-McPhee as Benvolio, Laura Morante as Lady Montague, Tomas Arana as Lord Montague, and Leon Vitali, Stanley Kubrick's former assistant, as the Apothecary. The arithmetic of the leads deserves its own section below; the structural point here is that the film was cast like a prestige literary adaptation of the older Hollywood kind, in which established actors guarantee the minor architecture while beautiful unknowns carry the romance.

### Why was the film's love scene removed before shooting began?

The love scene was removed because the screenplay had been written for a Juliet of twenty, and the actress cast was fourteen. Carlei explained the excision to Entertainment Weekly in June 2011: the drafted lovemaking scene included nudity, and once Steinfeld was cast, all nudity and lovemaking were cut, leaving a version he called romantic and age-appropriate for a fourteen-year-old.

That single production decision exposes the gap between the film's marketing and its materials. The screenplay Fellowes delivered had been composed with an adult Juliet in mind, and its wedding-night sequence belonged to the Zeffirelli inheritance, in which the marriage is consummated on screen. Casting a child of fourteen, fifteen by the time cameras turned, converted that sequence from an artistic choice into an impossibility, and the production solved the problem by subtraction. Reviewers who later found the romance chaste to the point of bloodlessness were, without knowing the mechanism, registering this excision: Susan Wloszczyna, reviewing the film for RogerEbert.com, noted the deleted nudity in the post-wedding bedroom scene and treated the resulting restraint as part of the film's broader refusal of passion. The episode also fixed the film's double bind. Its Juliet was young enough to silence any charge of miscast age, and too young to play the desire that the balcony scene, the wedding and the tomb require an audience to believe. Shakespeare's Juliet is thirteen in the text and written with a rhetorical maturity that has challenged adult actresses for four centuries; the film cast the age and inherited the challenge in its sharpest form.

Principal photography began on 3 February 2012 in Italy and the shoot became the film's strongest claim on posterity. The Capulet palace was built around the Villa Farnese at Caprarola, not in the great villa itself, which is a museum, but in the Casino in its gardens, the Casina del Piacere, whose loggia, dressed with artificial greenery and roses on supports that Booth could climb, served as the balcony. The garden's southern staircase, with its chain of water and the fountain at its foot, staged the lovers' separation at dawn. The Friar's monastery scenes were filmed at the monastery of San Benedetto at Subiaco. Further work took the company to Mantua, where the local newspaper Gazzetta di Mantova published the first set photographs in mid-February 2012, to Verona itself, and to the studios at Cinecitta in Rome. David Tattersall photographed the film in a widescreen format that reviewers across the spectrum conceded was beautiful, Tonino Zera designed the production, Carlo Poggioli designed the costumes, and Paolo Antonini choreographed the fights. The material record, in short, was honourable: real Renaissance fabric, real Italian light, a real city standing where the play says the city stands.

The locations themselves carry a history that the production used without examining, and the history rewards a paragraph. The Villa Farnese at Caprarola is no generic palace: it is the great pentagon fortress-palace built for Cardinal Alessandro Farnese in the sixteenth century, with the Casino in its gardens raised as a pleasure house within the grounds, a retreat inside a retreat, designed for the exact mixture of formality and intimacy that a balcony scene performs. The monastery of San Benedetto at Subiaco stands at the fountainhead of Benedictine monasticism, above the cave of Saint Benedict, its chapels frescoed across the centuries, so the Friar's scenes play inside a sanctity that no art department could counterfeit. Mantua, the third location, is the city to which the play's geography assigns exile, the seat of the Gonzaga, whose courtly splendour the feuding families of Verona could only imitate. A production alert to its assets might have made this archaeology part of its meaning, since the tragedy unfolds within the real inheritance of the Renaissance city states whose violence it abstracts into a brawl and a ball. The film used the three places as backgrounds of increasing loveliness, functionally indistinguishable from the spreads of a Tuscan calendar. The stones were eloquent; the production asked them only to be picturesque, and the distinction between eloquence and picturesqueness is the distinction the whole enterprise failed to draw, between the play's words on the one hand and everything that can be photographed on the other.

### Where did the Capulet ball and the balcony actually stand?

They stood at Caprarola, in the Casino of the Villa Farnese gardens rather than in Verona. The Casina del Piacere supplied the Capulet palace interiors and grounds, and its loggia, dressed with artificial greenery and roses fixed to supports that Douglas Booth could climb, served as Juliet's balcony, while the garden's water staircase staged the lovers' parting at dawn.

The money also explains the film's cautious shape. A first feature carrying a luxury brand cannot be violent, cannot be erotic, cannot be strange; it must be, in the word the trade reached for, lavish. Lavishness is visible in every frame of the finished film and legible in its certificate, PG-13 in the United States, and the production succeeded completely at the level of the object. Where it failed was at the level of the text it had bought. That failure was not Carlei's cinematography or Zera's streets. It was written into the screenplay before a camera turned, and the screenplay is where the film must be confronted.

The music carries a smaller version of the same story of replacement. James Horner, one of the most commercially successful film composers of his era, wrote and recorded a complete score for the film months before release; the producers set it aside and engaged Abel Korzeniowski, whose replacement score, all swooning strings and piano in the ears of hostile reviewers, divided opinion as sharply as any element of the production. Jonathan Broxton, reviewing the score for Movie Music UK, called it a sweeping, soaring romantic achievement and one of the finest musical accomplishments of its year, and the International Film Music Critics Association agreed in institutional form, naming it Film Score of the Year. The soundtrack album also carried a song by Zola Jesus alongside period pieces performed by Ensemble La Rossignol. A production that replaced its Shakespeare and replaced its composer had, in the event, more luck with the second replacement than with the first, and the disparity is diagnostic: new music can honour an old story because music does not pretend to be the original. New dialogue, offered under the original's name, labours under no such freedom.

Film studies has a name for the enterprise the producers thought they were making, and the name explains both the budget and the blind spot. The heritage film, the category critics built around the British period productions of the 1980s and 1990s, sells the past as a beautifully lit museum of itself: country houses, canonical literature, exquisite objects, an air of quality that flatters the spectator's taste while asking nothing difficult of it. Fellowes was the heritage mode's defining screenwriter, its Oscar winner and its laureate, and the Carlei production is best understood as a heritage Romeo and Juliet made a generation late, with the tradition's resources, an international co-production, a television aristocrat's script, a crystal company's objets d'art sensibility, intact, and its central evasion intact as well. Heritage cinema had always treated literature as furnishing, a source of plots and prestige whose language could be quietly modernised for the drawing room. Against Shakespeare that evasion becomes visible, because Shakespeare's language is not the furnishing of the play; it is the play. The film's failure is thus a generic failure before it is a personal one: a mode of filmmaking organised around not frightening its audience met the one canonical author whose audience has never needed the reassurance, and the mode, rather than the author, broke.

## The Fellowes Script Against the Play: Cut, Paraphrase and Invention

Any judgement of the screenplay has to begin with the adapter's own arithmetic, because Fellowes offered a statistic and the statistic does not survive inspection. Interviewed around the release, he claimed, in words preserved by Jill Levenson from The Globe and Mail of 11 October 2013: "Seventy-five per cent of the film is Shakespeare. We've tightened the narrative, we've made certain things clearer that were difficult to understand, but the great speeches are all still in." Levenson, the editor of the Oxford Shakespeare edition of the play and the scholar whose performance history the sister studies in this series cite throughout, answered with a professional's caution: although statistics are difficult to confirm from viewing rather than from analysis of the screenplay, it seems apparent that Shakespeare's dialogue has been cut by well more than twenty-five per cent. The distinction embedded in that reply is the whole argument. Fellowes's figure conflates two different operations, retaining lines and retaining share of text, and trades on the audience's inability to audit either. A film can indeed keep three quarters of the lines it uses while discarding far more than a quarter of the play, provided the lines it discards are long and the lines it keeps are famous. What the claim cannot survive is the third operation, the one Fellowes's sentence omits entirely: substitution, the replacement of Shakespeare's words with his own.

### Did Fellowes invent whole lines, or only trim Shakespeare's?

Fellowes invented whole lines and whole beats of dialogue. Reviewers transcribing the released film recorded Romeo telling Juliet, "Juliet, if your heart like mine is full, then tell the joy that awaits us this night," and Romeo, over Tybalt's body, asking, "What have I done but murdered my tomorrow?" Neither sentence exists in any edition of the play.

The invented matter has a consistent profile, and the profile is more damning than any single line. Shakespeare is retained where retention is safest: the Prologue's outline, the balcony scene's famous exchanges, Mercutio's death curse, "A plague o' both your houses" (3.1), the potion scene's machinery, the tomb's broad shape. Fellowes's hand falls on everything between those peaks, the connective tissue in which Shakespeare's characters actually reason. The Nurse's scenes with Juliet were rewritten into brisk, maternal, modern-feeling dialogue; one contemporary reviewer in Dallas singled out the Nurse congratulating Juliet on her taste in men as the moment the substitution became unmistakable, and another noticed the Friar's great rebuke in 3.3, in which the thrice-repeated sentence "There art thou happy" tolls through a long anatomy of Romeo's luck, compressed toward a bare summary of its conclusion. The pattern is a highlights edition with the gaps plastered over in matching plaster. Audiences who knew the play heard the joins; audiences who did not know the play could not hear them, and were therefore the very people the enterprise claimed to serve and the people it most thoroughly deceived, since what reached them under Shakespeare's name was, in passage after passage, the work of a competent television dramatist writing in fancy dress.

The inventions extend beyond dialogue into structure, and two of them frame the film. It opens with a joust, a mounted tournament between the houses that Shakespeare never wrote, over which a rewritten Chorus delivers an updated introduction; Wloszczyna called it a needless bout of jousting and took it as the first evidence of a director in over his head, and the instinct was sound, because the device announces the film's method in miniature: spectacle first, text approximated. Within the fiction, Rosaline, whom Shakespeare keeps entirely offstage as the unseen object of Romeo's first infatuation, is embodied in the film, credited to Nathalie Rapti Gomez, and placed at the Capulet ball as a visible presence. The change looks harmless and is not. Shakespeare's Rosaline functions as pure report, the measure of Romeo's self-dramatising melancholy; a visible Rosaline converts a diagnosis into a love triangle and shifts the balcony scene's meaning, since Romeo's "Did my heart love till now?" (1.5) answers a reported fancy in the play and a witnessed encounter in the film. Small substitutions of this kind are the screenplay's signature: each is defensible in a story conference, each dilutes the specific mechanism it touches, and their accumulation is the film.

A passage-by-passage audit sharpens the picture further, because the screenplay's three operations distribute themselves unevenly across the play, and the distribution is a map of its values. The street brawl of 1.1, in which Shakespeare builds the feud from servants' banter upward, disappears into the joust. The Queen Mab speech, Mercutio's great set piece in 1.4 and the play's first test of any cast, survives in compressed form, since even this screenplay flinched from paraphrasing the most anthologised bravura in the text. The lovers' shared sonnet at the ball in 1.5 is retained, as it had to be; a Romeo and Juliet that rewrote the sonnet would have announced itself. The balcony scene stands largely intact at its centre and thins at its edges, where the vows and the parting arrangements pass into Fellowes's idiom. Juliet's epithalamium in 3.2, the "Gallop apace" speech in which her desire speaks with a frankness that still startles, was reduced, as one reviewer noted with regret, to a voice-over beneath images, a demotion that tells its own story about a production nervous of its heroine's wanting. The Friar's scenes bear the heaviest paraphrase after the Nurse's, with the 3.3 rebuke compressed and the 4.1 potion exposition, Shakespeare's masterclass in dramatic instruction, streamlined into clarity. The apothecary scene in 5.1 survives, with Leon Vitali gaunt in the part, and constitutes a genuine restoration beside Zeffirelli's film, which cut it. Read as a whole, the audit shows a screenplay that trusted Shakespeare exactly where the audience could check the trust, and replaced him exactly where it could not. That is not an accessibility strategy. It is a risk-management strategy, and the distinction explains the particular anger of the informed: the film was careful in proportion to the likelihood of detection.

The film's handling of the catastrophe's machinery shows the same priorities working at the level of plot, where simplification had its strongest case and still managed a loss. Shakespeare's fifth act turns on a double failure of communication: Friar John's letter is stopped by plague quarantine in 5.2, a scene of pure exposition that explains the disaster's mechanism, while Balthasar carries the false report to Mantua. The Carlei film streamlines the machinery: the Friar's letter simply fails to arrive, and Benvolio, having witnessed the funeral, carries the news to Romeo himself, replacing Shakespeare's servant with the friend and deleting the quarantine scene's bleak bureaucracy of fate. The change is defensible as economy and was barely remarked in the reviews, yet it alters the tragedy's metaphysics in the direction the whole screenplay prefers. Shakespeare's mechanism makes the catastrophe a failure of systems, of plague houses and sealed doors and letters entrusted to a friar who could not leave the city; the blame diffuses into the world's arrangements until the Prince can close the play by punishing all. The film's mechanism makes the catastrophe a failure of errands, one letter lost and one friend too swift, a matter of bad luck between individuals. A story about a society whose structures kill its children becomes a story about a message that went astray. The compression is small, the running time saved is a few minutes, and the thematic price is the largest the screenplay pays after the tomb scene itself, larger than any single paraphrased speech, because it rewires the meaning of the ending the paraphrase then decorates.

The Chorus deserves separate notice within the audit, because the prologue is where the play announces its own terms and the film rewrote those terms most freely. Shakespeare's Chorus speaks a sonnet that discloses the ending in advance, "a pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life", and thereby converts the entire performance into dramatic irony: no spectator in the Globe was permitted to hope, and every tenderness on stage is shadowed by the knowledge the Chorus has imposed. The form is the meaning. A sonnet, the period's instrument of private lyric feeling, is conscripted to deliver public doom, and the tension between the form and the news is the play in miniature. Fellowes's rewritten Chorus keeps the foreknowledge and discards the sonnet, folding the prophecy into the narration that carries the audience toward the invented tournament. The information survives; the irony does not, because irony requires the audience to hold the Chorus's sentence in the mind as a made object, a fourteen-line warning framed and hung at the door, rather than as a voice-over's scene-setting. Critics who called the opening a trailer for the film were responding, without necessarily knowing it, to the loss of that framing: a play that begins by renouncing suspense has been converted into a film that begins by manufacturing it. The conversion is the accessibility programme in one stroke, assistance offered for a difficulty the author had deliberately abolished, at the price of the abolition's meaning.

The textual baseline against which all of this is measured is the Second Quarto tradition as modern editions present it, and the contrast of texture is available to any reader who sets the screenplay's reported lines beside the play's. Take the single most famous sentence in the balcony scene: "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" (2.2). The line is brief, metrically exact, and built on a word, wherefore, that means why and not where, so that the speech becomes an argument about names rather than a search for a hiding place. Fellowes retained the line, as he retained most of the anthology pieces. What he removed, across the evening, was the argumentative tissue of which such lines are the peaks: the reasoning in verse by which Juliet dismantles the feud's logic, by which the Friar weighs his potion, by which Romeo talks himself from Rosaline to Juliet and from despair to action. Abridgement that kept the arguments and dropped the decoration would have served accessibility better, and several stage productions have proved as much. The Carlei screenplay did the reverse, keeping the decoration and replacing the arguments with summaries of their conclusions. Audiences followed the story perfectly and understood the characters hardly at all, which is the precise inversion of the difficulty the makers claimed to be solving.

The largest substitution is reserved for the ending, and it is also the one with the longest pedigree, which the film's publicity never acknowledged. In Shakespeare, Romeo drinks the poison and dies beside a Juliet who has not yet woken; she wakes to find him dead, and the irony is absolute: "Thy lips are warm" (5.3) spoken over a corpse. In Carlei's film, Juliet wakes while Romeo still lives; they kiss, and he collapses and dies in her arms. The device is not a modern invention and it is not Fellowes's. It descends from Thomas Otway's Caius Marius of 1679, passed through Theophilus Cibber's version of 1744, and was expanded by David Garrick into a long waking conversation that held the stage for the better part of a century, until nineteenth-century taste restored Shakespeare's harsher timing. The film thus ends with the most famous interpolation of the eighteenth-century stage while advertising itself as a return to the play's roots. The irony compounds: the one moment at which the screenplay joins a genuine tradition of adaptation is the moment it departs furthest from the text, and the tradition it joins is precisely the sentimental stage tradition that two centuries of scholarship laboured to displace. Fellowes and Carlei were, whether they knew it or not, Garrick's heirs; the difference is that Garrick's audiences knew they were watching Garrick.

What, then, is the fair measure of the screenplay? Adaptation studies, the discipline this series applies to every screen version, distinguishes three familiar operations. Abridgement shortens the text and is as old as the play's stage life. Transposition moves the text into a new world and is the method of Luhrmann and of every modern-dress staging. Substitution replaces the text while keeping its costume on, and it is the rarest operation in respectable Shakespeare filming for a reason: it breaks the contract under which the audience bought the ticket. The Carlei screenplay performs all three operations at once, abridging heavily, transposing nothing, and substituting in the seams, and it markets the result as the first two. Levenson's gentle statistic and Fellowes's confident one frame the dispute exactly. On the evidence of the released film, the verdict must go against the screenplay's own account of itself: the cuts exceed the claim, the paraphrase reaches the famous scenes' edges and occasionally their centres, and the invention is structural as well as verbal. None of this would matter if the substituted material were strong. Judged as original writing, Fellowes's additions are fluent, speakable and entirely without pressure; they explain what Shakespeare's lines enact. The play thinks in metaphor, in the compressed logic of "It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2). The screenplay thinks in summary. A summary of Romeo and Juliet is a synopsis, and a synopsis photographed in Verona at seventeen million dollars remains a synopsis.

## Two Teenagers Amid a Wardrobe of Adults: Steinfeld, Booth and the Acting

The casting of the leads was the film's answer to the oldest embarrassment in the play's screen history, and on paper the answer was impeccable. The 1936 MGM version had starred Leslie Howard at forty-three and Norma Shearer in her mid-thirties; Zeffirelli had corrected the error with actual teenagers and made the correction his legend. Steinfeld was fourteen when she was cast in the spring of 2011, fifteen during the Italian shoot of early 2012, and sixteen when the film opened. Booth, born in July 1992, was nineteen during photography. Between them they made the youngest pairing of any major English-language film of the play, and the only one besides Zeffirelli's to put a genuinely mid-adolescent Juliet before the camera. The long debate over [how old Romeo and Juliet are meant to be](/2022/02/18/how-old-are-romeo-and-juliet-age-question/) is usually conducted against the Nurse's arithmetic, "Come Lammas Eve at night shall she be fourteen" (1.3), and against the stage tradition of grown women in the part. Carlei's production is the rare case in which the arithmetic was honoured almost to the letter, and the honouring became its own problem, since a Juliet of fifteen can look the part completely while lacking the vocal and rhetorical armoury the part demands.

The reviews divide over the leads with unusual symmetry, and the division is itself evidence. Wloszczyna wrote that Steinfeld duly and dully mumbles the famous phrase during the balcony scene, and that both leads struggle with the poetic rhythms. The Arizona Republic, in the warmest major notice the film received, answered that Booth and Steinfeld prove capable in the title roles and that the film is well staged and well acted throughout. Manohla Dargis in The New York Times occupied the documented middle: a sufficiently entertaining, adamantly old-fashioned adaptation that follows the play's general outline without ever rising to the passionate intensity of its star-crossed lovers. Adjudicating among these positions requires noticing what each critic was measuring. The hostile notices measure verse-speaking and find it wanting; the friendly notice measures presence and narrative clarity and finds them adequate; the Times measures the whole against the tragedy it adapts and finds the outline present and the fire absent. All three can be true of the same footage, and the synthesis they support is unflattering to the production rather than to the children in it: two young performers of real ability, one of them an Academy Award nominee at fourteen, were given a screenplay whose flattened verse offered nothing to climb, in a production whose camera preferred their faces to their speeches. Booth's clarity of diction was conceded even by hostile reviewers. Steinfeld's scenes with Manville and Lewis, where Fellowes's contemporary-feeling dialogue suited her naturalistic gifts, were repeatedly singled out as her strongest. The balcony scene, where Shakespeare's own words survive in bulk and the film had nowhere to hide, was named by Wloszczyna as the production's greatest disappointment. The conclusion writes itself: the closer the film came to Shakespeare, the worse its young leads were made to look, which is an indictment of the preparation, the direction and the script, in that order.

The wherefore problem concentrates the whole question of what preparation means. Juliet's "wherefore art thou Romeo?" is the most misread sentence in the play, heard by generations of schoolchildren as a question about location, and answered by the speech itself, which proceeds to argue that a name is an accident and a rose would smell as sweet by any other. An actor who understands the argument delivers the line as the opening of a proof; an actor who has been handed the line as an anthology piece delivers it as a sigh. Critics who accused Steinfeld of mumbling were, at their most precise, accusing the production of leaving its Juliet at the sigh. The craft Antony Sher later described, the actor's labour of conveying meaning, is not an optional refinement on top of the text; it is the mechanism by which the text reaches an audience at all, and it cannot be delegated to a screenplay. Where that labour was supplied, by Giamatti above all, the film's dialogue, Shakespearean and Fellowesian alike, landed. Where it was not supplied, no simplification could rescue the scene, because the difficulty had never been lexical. The two leads were failed not by their years but by an enterprise that had decided the words were the obstacle and therefore spent its preparation everywhere except on the words.

Booth's case deserves separate statement, since his notices mixed the film's harshest condescension with its most consistent concession. Fashion coverage had circulated his face for years before the film, through modelling work that included Burberry campaigns, and several reviewers treated the casting as a magazine decision, a Romeo chosen from a poster. The condescension overshot. Booth spoke the verse with a clarity that even hostile notices conceded, and his Romeo improves scene by scene in a manner that suggests a performer learning the assignment in public. If the film failed him, it failed him through the paraphrase, which gave his character summaries where Shakespeare gives soliloquies of self-persuasion, and through a camera that kept interrupting his acting to admire his profile. The open-shirted first entrance, remarked by Wloszczyna with open derision, is the emblem: a production that introduces its Romeo as a torso has told the audience how to watch him, and no subsequent verse-speaking, however clear, fully recovers the instruction.

The supporting company, by contrast, collected the praise that crossed every factional line, and the pattern of that praise carries the film's acting verdict. Giamatti's Friar Laurence was commended by reviewers who detested the enterprise and by reviewers who defended it, a bumbling, lovable, wholly authoritative performance that made the character's plotting legible without flattening his culpability. Manville's Nurse, granted a larger share of the surviving text than most of the cast, registered warmly even in notices that regretted the proportion. Lewis, who had himself played Romeo on the British stage some twenty years before the film, brought to Capulet a coiled, frightening plausibility in the disowning scene that several critics ranked as the film's finest acting. Skarsgard's Prince supplied the civic weight the production's Verona needed. The effect of this distribution is paradoxical and was visible to audiences at the time: the adults act Shakespeare, or act Fellowes as if he were Shakespeare, while the lovers pose inside the scenery. A Romeo and Juliet in which the Friar, the Nurse and the heroine's father give the performances is a film whose centre has failed, and the centre failed for reasons no casting director could have repaired. Desire at fifteen, photographed chastely, spoken in paraphrase and directed toward the camera rather than toward the other actor, becomes portraiture. The film is full of beautiful portraits. It contains almost no scenes of two people changing each other, which is what the play, at any age of casting, is for.

Zeffirelli's example haunts this section and should be faced directly, because the comparison is the film's own chosen ground. His teenagers, Leonard Whiting and Olivia Hussey, were also novices, also beautiful, also directed within a lush Italianate production; what they had, and what Booth and Steinfeld were denied, was Shakespeare's text spoken whole in the scenes that matter and a director whose instincts ran to the verse's rhythms rather than to the picturesque. The bedroom scene that made Zeffirelli's film notorious, and which the companion study of the controversy treats in full, has its counterpart here only as an absence, the sequence deleted when a child was cast. The younger supporting cast divides along the same line as the leads, between performers the screenplay armed and performers it disarmed. Kodi Smit-McPhee's Benvolio drew some of the warmest notices given to any younger player in the film; one American review called the performance scene-stealing, heartbreaking and reserved, and the judgement identifies the reason. Benvolio's function survives paraphrase because the function is structural, the friend who reports, restrains and finally carries the fatal news, and an actor of Smit-McPhee's stillness could build a performance out of errands. Christian Cooke's Mercutio laboured under the opposite condition: Mercutio is nothing but language, a character made of puns, fantasies and aggression conducted in wordplay, and a screenplay that trims the Queen Mab speech and flattens the prose duels leaves the actor with the outline of a wit. Westwick's Tybalt, played at a single temperature of menace, was found by one Tampa notice so perpetually ill-tempered as to verge on the comic, and the fault is again legible as a textual one, since Shakespeare's Tybalt is courteous in form and murderous in content, a duellist whose politeness is the threat. Strip the courtesy and the menace has nowhere to live except the face. The pattern completes the section's argument: wherever a role's essence is action, the film's young company serves it well; wherever a role's essence is speech, the screenplay had removed the evidence in advance, and the actors were reviewed for an absence they did not create.

Where Zeffirelli risked scandal to insist that the marriage was real, Carlei's production, through no one's malice and everyone's caution, insinuates a marriage the film is not permitted to show. The leads' celebrated lack of chemistry, remarked by reviewer after reviewer from Variety downward, is better understood as a lack of occasion: the screenplay and the certificate between them removed most of the scenes in which chemistry could have been demonstrated, and left the kisses.

## Between Zeffirelli and Luhrmann: The Traditional Film That Changed the Words

The film's own publicity placed it in a lineage, and the placement deserves scrutiny because it conceals the film's actual novelty. Chang's Variety review reported the billing precisely: the production presented itself as the first picture since Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 film to return Shakespeare's tragedy to its Veronese roots. New York magazine, surveying the autumn releases, framed the same positioning from the other side, remarking that Fellowes was attempting to de-Luhrmann-ize the classic. Between those two sentences the whole marketing strategy stands exposed. The film promised Zeffirelli's world without Zeffirelli's forty years of classroom familiarity, and Luhrmann's youth audience without Luhrmann's modern dress, guns, and pop soundtrack. What it could not promise, because the promise would have been false, was either predecessor's relationship to the text. [Franco Zeffirelli's 1968 Romeo and Juliet](/2028/07/21/franco-zeffirelli-1968-film/) cut the play severely, by some scholarly counts to a fraction of its lines, but every line that survived the cutting was Shakespeare's, and the film's authority rests on that discipline. [Baz Luhrmann's Romeo + Juliet](/2028/08/04/baz-luhrmann-romeo-plus-juliet-1996/) moved the play to a fictional Verona Beach of limousines and handguns, yet kept the language with a fidelity that disarmed many early sceptics, trusting editing, design and performance to carry a modern audience across the verse rather than translating the verse down to the audience. The Carlei film inverted both bargains. It kept the world and changed the words.

The comparison can be sharpened into a table of three contracts. Zeffirelli's contract runs: the story shortened, the setting enriched, the words authentic. Luhrmann's runs: the story shortened, the setting transformed, the words authentic. Carlei's runs: the story complete in outline, the setting authentic, the words approximated. Each contract finds its audience. Zeffirelli's film became the classroom standard for two generations; Luhrmann's became a commercial hit and the defining Shakespeare film of its decade; Carlei's found neither audience, and the commercial section below gives the figures. The reason is not mysterious and was identified at the time by critics as different as Chang and Dawn Monique Williams, the director and Shakespeare practitioner whose essay on the film remains its most searching practitioner critique. Williams's charge is precise: radical adaptation is an honourable form, from ballet to Gnomeo and Juliet, but Fellowes's version presents itself as the original while behaving as an adaptation, a bait and switch in which newcomers may believe they are receiving Shakespeare when they are not. Against that charge the film's defenders could plead its beauty, its cast and its intentions; they could not plead its label, because the label was the complaint. The wider question of [which film Romeo and Juliet is best](/2029/05/25/which-film-version-is-best/) is argued in its own study in this series; for the Carlei film the narrower point suffices, that a version must first be honest about what it is before it can compete to be best at being it.

One further comparison disciplines the whole discussion, and it comes from the stage rather than the screen. The play's performance history is a four-century record of adaptation far more radical than anything Fellowes attempted: Otway rewrote it into a Roman tragedy, Garrick added a funeral procession and a waking heroine, nineteenth-century managers restored and re-cut it by turns. Nobody in that tradition, however, sold the result as the unchanged original. Acting editions printed their adapters' names; Garrick's prefaces defended his alterations as alterations. The ethical line in Shakespeare adaptation has never run between changing and not changing. It runs between declaring the change and concealing it. Carlei's film is a minor work that stumbled across a major line, and the stumble, more than the film's modest artistic failings, is why it remains worth sixteen thousand words of anybody's time: it is the cleanest modern demonstration that the audience's trust, once spent on a false label, cannot be recovered by scenery.

The commercial histories of the two models complete the contrast, and they matter here because Carlei's backers had read them as encouragement. Zeffirelli's production, shot for a reported sum under a million dollars, returned tens of millions in its first life and stands among the most profitable Shakespeare films ever released, its returns compounded by classroom immortality. Luhrmann's, on 14.5 million, returned more than 147 million worldwide and carried its soundtrack to the top of charts on several continents. The lesson the trade drew across those decades was that Romeo and Juliet, filmed with conviction, prints money at any budget: the title is pre-sold, the story requires no explanation, and each generation supplies a fresh audience of exactly the lovers' age. The Carlei production applied the lesson's arithmetic, a mid-range budget against a pre-sold title, while discarding the lesson's conditions, which were conviction of opposite kinds, Zeffirelli's in place and casting, Luhrmann's in style and language. Three million dollars worldwide against 17 million of budget is therefore not a refutation of the title's commercial magic. It is a measurement of how much of that magic resided in the two things the production chose to economise on, the author's words and the courage of its own convictions.

The decade's other Shakespeare films supply the control experiment, and the comparison removes any suspicion that the Carlei film merely suffered from its moment. Ralph Fiennes's Coriolanus of 2011 transposed its play to a modern Balkan war, cut the text with a soldier's economy and trusted the surviving verse to carry audiences through thickets far denser than anything in Romeo and Juliet; the film was honoured across the festival circuit. Joss Whedon's Much Ado About Nothing, shot in twelve days at the director's house in black and white and released in the same season as Carlei's film, kept its text essentially intact in a modern Californian setting and was received as a delight. Neither production commanded a fraction of the Carlei budget. Both proceeded from the opposite premise: that the audience's intelligence is the asset and the words are the attraction. Set beside them, the Fellowes experiment reads less like an answer to a real problem than like a solution in search of one, mounted by a producer-screenwriter whose true subject, in Gosford Park and Downton Abbey alike, had always been the intelligibility of a social world, and who mistook Shakespeare's Verona for another country house whose inhabitants needed their dialogue tidied.

## The Beauty Defence: Costume, Stone and the Case for the Eye

Every sympathetic account of the film, and several hostile ones, retreats at last to the same ground: whatever the screenplay did, the picture is beautiful. The defence deserves a full hearing, because the beauty is real and its components are documented. Poggioli's costumes clothed the feud in velvets and brocades that reviewers across the spectrum called exquisite. Zera's production design used authentic Renaissance fabric rather than studio imitation, from the Caprarola Casino to the cloisters at Subiaco, and Tattersall's widescreen photography held those spaces in a golden register that flattered faces and stone alike. Antonini's fight choreography gave the street violence a drilled, processional quality that one strand of reviewing praised as visceral and competent. The Capulet ball was staged as a formal dance of genuine splendour. Even the score's defenders and detractors agreed that the film sounded expensive. A minority of dissenting details survives in the record: one reviewer excepted a computer-generated wedding chapel from the general praise of the design, calling it an intruder from a different kind of film, and another strand of notices found the beauty inert, a succession of glossy tableaux in which the lovers are posed like figures in an advertisement. The dissent and the praise are not as opposed as they look. Both describe a cinema of surfaces, and the disagreement is only over whether surfaces suffice.

The adjudication turns on what beauty can and cannot do for this particular play. Romeo and Juliet is, among Shakespeare's tragedies, the one most dependent on language as event: its lovers fall in love inside a shared sonnet, its heroine reasons her way to the potion speech, its catastrophe is a failure of messages and timing that only words can make intelligible. Spectacle can frame those events; it cannot substitute for them, and when the verbal events are thinned the spectacle is left holding narrative weight it cannot bear, which is why the film's beauty so often registered as slowness. The famous comparison is again Zeffirelli, whose locations were as lush and whose camera as besotted, but whose spectacle served speeches delivered whole: the eye feasted because the ear was feasting first. In Carlei's film the feast is single-course. The verdict on the beauty defence is therefore a limited acquittal. The film is beautiful, and the beauty is the strongest reason to watch it still, as a moving record of Verona, Caprarola and Mantua photographed at a generous budget. Beauty of that order is a documentary virtue. The production's error was to offer it as a dramatic one, as though the treat for the eye could discharge the debt owed to the ear, and the audience's refusal of the exchange is the final form of the accessibility argument: the public had not asked to be spared the words, and it declined the sparing.

The film's failure cannot be laid at the door of indulgence. Every departmental economy was competently managed, the cast was assembled before the money ran short, and the production delivered its locations, costumes and crowds on schedule. What failed was a single line item that cost nothing at all, the decision to trust the words on the page, and no budget in the world compensates for refusing the one asset that arrives free.

## The Defence and the Rebuttal: Fellowes, the Actors and the Oxford Editor

Fellowes made his case in public and in his own words, and fairness requires the case to be stated before it is judged. Promoting the film, he told the BBC: "When people say we should have filmed the original, I don't attack them for that point of view, but to see the original in its absolutely unchanged form, you require a kind of Shakespearian scholarship and you need to understand the language and analyse it and so on. I can do that because I had a very expensive education, I went to Cambridge. Not everyone did that and there are plenty of perfectly intelligent people out there who have not been trained in Shakespeare's language choices." A second statement, preserved by Book Riot from the same promotional round, sets out the positive aim: the makers wanted a wider audience "to enjoy a version of Romeo and Juliet much more than they would expect to," allowing them "to enjoy the great beauty of the speeches, but at the same time to tell the story in a way they could follow and wouldn't feel alienated or left out." The two statements together form a complete position. Shakespeare undiluted is a scholarly object; the film offers the story, plus the beauty of the famous speeches, in a form the untrained can follow; the screenwriter's own training certifies the translation. It is the position of a man who believes the barrier is vocabulary, the remedy is simplification, and the qualification for simplifying is having once been on the other side of the barrier.

### What did Fellowes mean by an expensive education?

He meant that his Cambridge training let him read Shakespeare without help, and that audiences lacking such training needed his screenplay as a bridge. The remark, made to the BBC while promoting the film, named his expensive education as the credential for rewriting the play on behalf of people he described as perfectly intelligent but untrained in Shakespeare's language choices.

The rebuttal came from three directions at once, and its unanimity is the story. From the acting profession, Janet Suzman, a classical actress of long standing, called the remarks patent piffle in The Guardian's report, and Antony Sher later dismissed the underlying idea as nonsense in a BBC Four interview, adding the craftsman's counter-thesis: "I never went to university but my job as a Shakespeare actor, and I have done a lot of them now, is to work hard on conveying the meaning. It's not a university degree you need, it's the craft of speaking Shakespeare." From the institution charged with popular access, Fiona Banks of Shakespeare's Globe answered in a sentence: "Shakespeare's for everybody. We can all understand him." From scholarship, Levenson's objection struck not at the philosophy but at the fact: the seventy-five per cent figure was wrong on a viewing, and the great speeches, while broadly present, stood in a text cut and supplemented well beyond the advertised margin. Fellowes subsequently retracted the ill-judged formulation, as The Shakespeare Blog recorded when the quarrel resurfaced in 2015, but the film itself could not be retracted, and the quarrel fixed its identity before most of its audience had seen a frame.

The adjudication this study reaches favours the rebuttal on every count, and the grounds are empirical rather than sentimental. First, the premise that the unchanged text requires scholarship is refuted by the play's own commercial history, which includes four centuries of audiences who encountered the words in performance, without editions, and by the two screen predecessors the film defined itself against, both of which trusted the original language to do its own work on untrained audiences and were rewarded for the trust. Second, the remedy was misaimed: the difficulties a first-time audience meets in this play are difficulties of plot density, of doubled names and compressed time, which cutting can genuinely ease, rather than of individual words, which performance glosses in passing. Fellowes simplified the stratum that needed no simplification and retained, in the famous speeches, the stratum his theory called forbidding. Third, the credential argument inverted the actual division of labour. The people professionally charged with making Shakespeare intelligible, the actors, acquire the skill Sher named, the craft of conveying meaning; a screenplay that replaces the meaning's vehicle relieves actors of that craft and replaces it with nothing. The film's own evidence convicts it: Giamatti, speaking substantially Shakespearean material prepared by a lifetime's technique, was intelligible to every reviewer; the young leads, speaking Fellowes, were accused of mumbling. The barrier was never in the audience. It was in a screenplay that had decided, in advance and on the audience's behalf, how much poetry the audience could bear.

The quarrel also had a prehistory in print that shaped the release. On 1 September 2013, six weeks before the opening, The Telegraph published Claire Duffin's report under the headline "Romeo, Romeo, what's Julian Fellowes done to you?", setting the screenplay's alterations before the newspaper's readers and gathering the objections that the release coverage would repeat. The film thus arrived pre-judged in a specific and damaging way: not as a failure, which audiences might have forgiven, but as a deception, which they would not. Howard Sherman, the American arts administrator and commentator, framed the institutional lesson with a marketer's bluntness after the release: the producers had attended to the Romeo and Juliet brand, its story, its period design and its casting, while failing to attend to the most powerful brand in the enterprise, William Shakespeare himself, and the first trailers, offering language that sounded old-fashioned without being Shakespearean, had advertised the omission to anyone listening. Sherman's point converts the artistic quarrel into a commercial diagnosis, and the box office section below shows the diagnosis to have been exact. A production may survive the charge of dullness. A production launched on a famous name cannot survive the charge that the name is the one thing it left out.

Suzman's fuller case, as the Guardian reported the exchange, reached past the insult to the history beneath it, and the history is unanswerable. Shakespeare's first audiences were not Cambridge graduates awaiting the assistance of a screenwriter; they were the mixed crowd of the Globe, apprentices and merchants and groundlings standing in the yard, who followed plays of greater verbal density than this one in real time, without texts, in a noisy open-air theatre, and whose comprehension was the playwright's commercial lifeblood. The plays were built for that comprehension, with the meaning carried in the action of the verse, in repetition, image and rhetorical shape, and four centuries of schoolchildren, translation audiences and unselected playgoers have confirmed the engineering ever since. A theory of the audience that requires expensive schooling to precede understanding does not merely insult the modern spectator; it describes a playwright who could never have survived his own box office. Suzman's "patent piffle" was thus not a debating point but a correction of fact, and the correction has never been answered, only abandoned, as the later softening of Fellowes's own position tacitly admitted.

A word of fairness belongs at the end of the adjudication, because the position Fellowes defended has an honourable ancestry he never invoked. The eighteenth-century adapters who rebuilt this play did so from a sincere conviction that their audiences needed a different ending, a clearer morality, a singable pathos; Garrick's version was the work of the greatest Shakespearean of his age, and it held the stage for a century. The twentieth century produced Christopher Fry's and others' verse translations for foreign stages and a long line of prose renderings for schools. Simplification is a real genre with real uses, and Tasha Robinson, reviewing the film for The Dissolve, located its genuine if modest place with more precision than either camp: an abbreviated version that preserves the high points and moves past the rest, tasteful and polished to a fault, never approaching the depth of the full text. Had the film been sold in those terms, as a gateway abridgement for the youngest viewers, much of the fury would have been disarmed, and what remained would have been a debate about quality rather than about honesty. The producers chose the grander label and the grander claim, and the rebuttal they provoked was the price of the choice. It is the film's misfortune, and its permanent lesson, that the argument about its screenplay became more interesting, more distinguished and better written than the screenplay.

## Translation, Paraphrase and the Honest Label: The Accessibility Question in Full

The strongest version of Fellowes's position deserves a section of its own, because the position is not absurd and its defeat is more instructive than its victory would have been. Begin with the fact that most of the world meets Shakespeare in replacement words. The play exists in dozens of languages, and every foreign production in history has spoken a translator's text, often a great poet's text, without the smallest stain on its legitimacy. The German Shakespeare of Schlegel and Tieck is a national classic in its own right. Translation is replacement, total and unapologetic, and no one calls it betrayal, because translation announces itself: the title page names the translator, the audience knows the transaction, and the new words labour openly to deserve the old ones. Cross-media adaptation tells the same story. Prokofiev's ballet discards every word and keeps the title; West Side Story discards the words, the city and the names; both declare their distance and are honoured for the declaration. Even within English, the paraphrase has an honest home: the Lambs' Tales from Shakespeare retold the plots for children in 1807 and said so on the cover, and the modern educational market sustains parallel-text editions that print a plain rendering opposite the original, where the rendering's status as a crib is the entire point of the format. Accessibility, in every one of these traditions, is a technology of declared mediation. The reader or spectator always knows where Shakespeare ends and the mediator begins.

The Carlei screenplay is anomalous within that honourable family tree at exactly one joint: it is a crib that declines to identify itself, interleaved with the original at a ratio its makers understated, in a film whose credits, title and marketing assert the original's presence. The anomaly explains the otherwise puzzling disproportion between the offence and the reaction. Directors have cut this play to ninety minutes without scandal; Garrick rewrote its ending and was celebrated for two centuries; Luhrmann set it among handguns and was forgiven by all but the fringes. The fury at Fellowes was never proportioned to the quantity of his alterations, which Garrick's exceeded. It was proportioned to the concealment, and to the justification offered when the concealment was discovered, which blamed the audience's education rather than the producers' label. Sher's rebuttal is decisive precisely because it comes from inside the transaction: the actor is the play's licensed mediator, the person whose whole craft consists of making the original words land in untrained ears, and a screenplay that pre-empts that mediation with paraphrase has not assisted the audience; it has dismissed the mediator. Banks's sentence from the Globe makes the institutional version of the same point. The Globe's entire enterprise rests on the empirical finding, renewed at every performance, that an unselected modern audience follows the original language when it is spoken as action. The finding is not a theory. It is a box office record.

What, then, could an honest accessibility version of this film have been? The counterfactual is worth stating because it shows how narrow the failure was. The same budget, the same locations, the same young cast, with Fellowes's name above a declared adaptation, "freely adapted from William Shakespeare" in the credits and the marketing, would have disarmed the central charge entirely, and the film would have been judged, as Robinson judged it in The Dissolve, on the modest terms it could actually meet: a tasteful abridgement for first-timers, high points preserved, depth forgone. Alternatively, the same production with Shakespeare's text abridged but never paraphrased, the Luhrmann and Whedon discipline, would have met the fidelity charge and very likely served its young leads better, since verse gives young actors a rhythm to ride where paraphrase leaves them exposed. Either film was available to the producers at no additional cost. The version chosen instead spent the production's considerable assets to defend an indefensible middle position, traditional in appearance and revisionist in substance, and the quarrel consumed the release. The accessibility question, examined in full, therefore acquits accessibility itself. Simplification honestly labelled has served Shakespeare for two centuries. What failed in 2013 was not the wish to open the play to new audiences, a wish every party to the quarrel shared, but the decision to open it by quietly replacing the thing the new audiences had come for.

## The Commercial Reckoning: 461 Screens Against a Seventeen Million Dollar Budget

The market delivered its verdict quickly and in numbers that require no interpretation. The film premiered on 24 September 2013 at the ArcLight cinema in Hollywood and opened commercially on 11 October 2013 in Britain and the United States. In America it was handled by Relativity Media under an arrangement that itself signalled the producers' difficulty: as The Hollywood Reporter documented, Relativity was paid by the producers to release the film in North America, a service deal of the kind struck when a finished picture cannot command an ordinary distribution advance. The opening placed the film on 461 screens, a limited release for a production carrying international stars and a famous title, and returned 520,116 dollars across the opening weekend, an average of little more than eleven hundred dollars per screen. The theatrical life that followed was brief. Box Office Mojo's tally closes at 1,162,635 dollars in North America and three million dollars worldwide, against a reported budget of seventeen million dollars before prints and advertising. Even allowing for pre-sales to Britain, Australia, Italy and other territories, and for the home entertainment release that followed in February 2014, the arithmetic describes a substantial loss on the most frequently filmed love story in the canon, released into a market that had made Luhrmann's version a fortune seventeen years earlier.

### What was unusual about the film's American distribution deal?

The producers paid Relativity Media to distribute the film rather than selling it to the company in the ordinary way. The arrangement, reported by The Hollywood Reporter, put the commercial risk on the producers and helps explain the limited scale of the release: no distributor had bought in, so no distributor's fortune depended on opening the picture wide.

The geography of the release completes the picture. Italy, the film's setting, its director's homeland and the source of its locations and much of its crew, did not receive the film until 12 February 2015, sixteen months after the Anglo-American opening, through Good Films. A production marketed on its authentic Verona was, in the event, nearly invisible in Verona's own country during its moment of publicity. The delay is a small fact with a large meaning: whatever the film was for, it was not for the Italians whose streets it borrowed.

The release pattern in the primary markets carries a quieter confession of the same kind. The Cannes planning had pointed toward a British opening on Valentine's Day, the calendar position that announces a romance confident of its audience; the film in the event opened in October, in the crowded corridor of the autumn prestige season, where a quiet costume picture competes against award contenders with larger stars and louder campaigns. Whether the move was forced by the American arrangements or chosen for the schools market, its effect was to surrender the one date on which the title sells itself, and to ask reviews to do the work that the calendar would have done free. The reviews, for the reasons set out in the preceding sections, declined. A production history is often legible in its dates alone, and this one's dates tell a continuous story: conceived for Cannes, dressed for Valentine's Day, released in October under a fee-paid distributor, and shelved in Italy for sixteen months. Each postponement and displacement records a fresh discovery, by someone with money at stake, that the film could not do what its label promised. It was for the Anglophone youth market and the Anglophone classroom, the two audiences its makers named in interview after interview, and both audiences declined it in their different ways. The youth market stayed away from a period film without stars it owned; the classroom market, as the next section argues, could not safely adopt a text that teachers would have to correct.

Responsibility for the commercial failure should be apportioned with the same care as responsibility for the artistic one, because the film's defenders made one true point that the record supports. The autumn of 2013 was a hard corridor for a quiet costume picture, the title had been filmed often, and the leads, for all their promise, had no drawing power of their own in the American market. A better screenplay would not have guaranteed a hit. But the counterfactual the producers themselves invited was the comparison with Luhrmann, whose film had cost less and returned more than a hundred and forty million dollars worldwide on the strength of exactly the audience Fellowes courted, and with Zeffirelli, whose version had earned its budget back many times over in an era of far smaller grosses. The audience for this play on screen existed and had twice demonstrated its size. What it would not buy, at any scale the producers could reach, was a version whose advance publicity had been consumed by a quarrel about whether the words were genuine. Marketing can survive a bad review. It cannot survive the suspicion that the product has been mislabelled, and the Fellowes controversy converted every subsequent advertisement into a reminder of the complaint. The film's commercial history is therefore not a separate misfortune from its textual history. It is the textual history, expressed in receipts.

Two release details round out the commercial record. Emanuel Levy, in an otherwise dismissive review, remarked on the critics' embargo Relativity had placed on the picture, reading it as the distributor's own forecast of the notices; embargoes are routine in the industry, but on a film sold as a prestige event the reading stuck, and it coloured the opening weekend's coverage with a narrative of concealment that rhymed, unfortunately for the producers, with the screenplay's own alleged concealment. The Australian opening followed in February 2014 through Icon, the pre-sale announced at Cannes, and the home entertainment release arrived in the same month from 20th Century Fox Home Entertainment, closing a theatrical biography that had lasted, in most markets, a matter of weeks. The film that was to relaunch a production company and inaugurate a slate concluded its first life as a catalogue title, kept in circulation chiefly by the classroom and streaming afterlife examined next.

## What Survives: The Score, the Streets and the Classroom Problem

An honest reckoning ends with the residue, and the Carlei film leaves more residue than its reputation admits. The score is the first item. Korzeniowski's music, commissioned as a rescue and delivered against the memory of a discarded Horner score, became the production's most decorated element, winning the International Film Music Critics Association's Film Score of the Year and sustaining an afterlife on record that the film itself never achieved. The second item is visual. Tattersall's photography of Caprarola, Subiaco, Mantua and Verona fixed a material Verona of genuine Renaissance fabric, and for readers of the play the film remains a useful visual footnote: the Casina's loggia is a defensible answer to the balcony problem, the water staircase a beautiful solution to the dawn parting, and the monastery interiors a credible Friar's world. The marketing craft forms a smaller item in the same ledger: the film's romantic poster was nominated for a Golden Trailer Award, a reminder that the packaging departments, at least, had known their work, and that the failure lay in the distance between the package and the contents rather than in any want of skill around the production. The third item is documentary. The film preserves early performances by two actors whose careers continued upward, a late flowering of Giamatti's classical technique in a substantial role, and Manville's Nurse, the most complete screen reading of the part since Pat Heywood's for Zeffirelli, played in a register the paraphrased script accidentally suited.

The classroom, though, is where the film's story refuses to end happily, and the classroom was always its intended destination. The Dissolve's judgement, that the film is an abbreviated version preserving the high points, implies a classroom use, and other reviewers named the use with less kindness, calling the film a cheat sheet for students who would rather watch than read. The distinction between the two descriptions is the whole pedagogical question. A film that abridges Shakespeare can serve a classroom honestly, as Zeffirelli's does, because a teacher can state exactly what has been cut and a student who later reads the scene finds the film's lines waiting in the text. A film that paraphrases without signalling the paraphrase cannot serve without supervision, because the student who quotes it quotes Fellowes under Shakespeare's name. The production that set out to lower the barrier between schoolchildren and the play thus produced the one screen version a teacher must actively warn against, and the warning has become its standard classroom function. The classroom stakes are concrete rather than rhetorical. In Britain, Shakespeare is compulsory in the national curriculum and this play is among the most frequently set of all his works for the examinations taken at sixteen, with the WJEC Eduqas specification among those prescribing it by name; in the United States the play anchors the ninth grade year in state after state. The market for a definitive school film of Romeo and Juliet is therefore one of the most reliable in all of educational publishing, renewed by every cohort, immune to fashion, and already served for two generations by Zeffirelli's version, whose excerpts structure countless first encounters with the balcony scene. A new version that displaced it would have earned for decades. A new version that could not be trusted to quote the set text accurately forfeited the displacement at the precise point of sale, which is why the textual quarrel was never a critics' parlour game: it was the difference between a perpetual annuity and a footnote, between the film on the classroom shelf and the film in the cautionary lecture. There are worse fates for a film than to be taught as a cautionary example, but it is not the fate its backers bought.

The scholarly afterlife has been kinder in one respect: the film gave the discipline of adaptation studies a perfect specimen. Levenson's OUP essay, written in the month of release, already treats the production as the limiting case of the fidelity debate, the point at which tightening a narrative becomes replacing it, and her observation that the film was received entirely through its two predecessors, as Zeffirelli's heir and Luhrmann's antidote, has governed discussion since. Williams's practitioner essay extended the specimen's use, making the film the standard classroom illustration of the difference between adaptation and impersonation. Academic syllabi on Shakespeare and film routinely programme the picture, not beside its rivals as a competitor but beneath them as a warning label, and there is a sense in which that programming completes the producers' educational ambition by inversion: the film reaches students reliably, every year, as the demonstration of how the play should not be served to them.

The final verdict can be stated in the terms the film itself supplied. Fellowes promised a version that untrained audiences could follow without feeling alienated or left out, and the promise was kept at the level of plot: nobody leaves this film unsure who loves whom, who dies, or in what order. What the promise cost was the thing the plot exists to carry. Shakespeare's play is not difficult because its words are old; it is demanding because its words are dense with the thought they perform, and every simplification that preserves the sense while releasing the density releases the thought with it. Carlei's Romeo and Juliet is a beautiful, well-cast, honourably photographed film of a summary of a masterpiece, released as the masterpiece, and its history, the Cambridge defence, the actors' rebuttal, the editor's arithmetic, the 461 screens, is the record of an audience declining the substitution. Accessibility was the stated goal. The play had been accessible all along, to the Globe's groundlings, to Zeffirelli's teenagers, to Luhrmann's, through the unglamorous craft of actors speaking the words as if they meant them. The lesson the film teaches, in production meetings and classrooms for as long as its story is told, is that the barrier was never where its screenwriter said it was.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Carlei profile, and what does it record about the film and its screenplay?

The InsightCrunch Carlei profile is the quick-reference record of the production set out in the table within this study. It assembles the film's credits, budget, locations, casting and ages, its release pattern and box office, its critical aggregates and honours, and, at its centre, the two rival accounts of its dialogue: Julian Fellowes's claim that seventy-five per cent of the film is Shakespeare, and the Oxford editor Jill Levenson's judgement that the dialogue was cut by well more than a quarter. No other screen version of the play requires its profile to carry a disputed statistic as a main entry, and that necessity is the profile's point: the film and the controversy over its screenplay are a single subject, and the record keeps them together.

### Q: Who directed the film, and why does its screenwriter draw more attention than its director?

Carlo Carlei directed the film; Julian Fellowes wrote the screenplay and took a producer's credit. The screenwriter draws the greater attention because the film's identity, its publicity and its controversy all attach to the script rather than to the direction. Carlei, an Italian director known for The Flight of the Innocent, delivered a handsome and largely conventional staging whose virtues reviewers conceded across the spectrum. Fellowes arrived with an Academy Award for Gosford Park and the fame of Downton Abbey, rewrote portions of Shakespeare's dialogue in his own imitation of the style, and then defended the rewriting in interviews, most damagingly in a BBC appearance that contrasted his expensive Cambridge education with the training of his audience. The quarrel that followed made the screenwriter the story.

### Q: Why did critics object so strongly to a screenplay that kept the plot intact?

Critics objected because keeping the plot was never in dispute, and the objection concerned the words. The play's story survives any competent summary, as centuries of adaptation prove; its distinction lives in the language, in speeches whose metaphors perform the thinking of the characters. Fellowes cut that language hard, paraphrased passages he judged difficult and inserted lines of his own in Shakespearean dress, while the production marketed the result as the traditional version. Reviewers therefore treated the screenplay not as an abridgement, which has always been legitimate, but as a substitution offered under a false description. Justin Chang's Variety notice gave the charge its durable form: a classicism that was in little danger of being mistaken for a classic.

### Q: What did Fellowes claim about the share of the film that is Shakespeare, and who challenged the arithmetic?

Fellowes claimed that seventy-five per cent of the film is Shakespeare, that the narrative had been tightened, that difficult matters had been made clearer, and that the great speeches were all still present. Jill Levenson challenged the arithmetic. Levenson, editor of the Oxford Shakespeare edition of the play, answered that statistics are hard to confirm from viewing alone, but that Shakespeare's dialogue seems cut by well more than twenty-five per cent. The challenge matters because it came from the scholar professionally responsible for the text, and because it exposed a slippage in the claim itself: retaining three quarters of the lines a film uses is compatible with discarding far more than a quarter of the play, once long speeches fall and short famous ones remain.

### Q: Why does the Nurse sound more modern than the lovers in this film?

The Nurse sounds more modern because Fellowes rewrote the domestic scenes most freely while protecting the famous speeches. The balcony exchange, the ball sonnet and Mercutio's death were retained in recognisable form, since those were the passages audiences could audit from memory. The scenes between Juliet and her Nurse carried no such protection, and Fellowes recast them as brisk, warm, contemporary-feeling exchanges, the register his television work had perfected. Contemporary reviewers noticed the seam at exactly this joint: one Dallas notice singled out the Nurse congratulating Juliet on her taste in men as the moment the substitution became unmistakable. The effect divides the film's world in two, a ceremonial Verona in which the lovers quote, and a household Verona in which the servants chat.

### Q: How old was Hailee Steinfeld at casting, at filming and at release?

Hailee Steinfeld was fourteen at casting, fifteen at filming and sixteen at release. She entered talks for Juliet in April 2011, four months past her fourteenth birthday, and the screenplay had to be altered around her, since its wedding-night sequence had been written for a Juliet of twenty. Principal photography ran from February 2012, when she was fifteen. The film opened in October 2013, when she was sixteen. Douglas Booth, her Romeo, was nineteen during the shoot. The pairing was the youngest in any major English-language film of the play and the closest to Shakespeare's own arithmetic, since the Nurse fixes Juliet's age at thirteen, a few weeks short of fourteen, in 1.3.

### Q: What does the invented jousting tournament accomplish in the film's design?

The joust stands at the head of the film because the production wanted an immediate spectacle of the feud and a showcase for its locations and costumes before any verse could slow the entry. Shakespeare opens with a street brawl between servants, a deliberately low and comic beginning that the Prince's sentence then raises into law. Carlei's film replaces the brawl with a mounted tournament between the houses, over which a rewritten Chorus delivers an updated introduction. The substitution announces the film's method in its first minutes: pageantry promoted, text approximated. Susan Wloszczyna, reviewing for RogerEbert.com, called the joust needless and treated it as the first sign of a production entranced by its own wardrobe, and the sequence has few defenders in the review file.

### Q: How does the film change the lovers' final minutes in the tomb?

The film lets Juliet wake before Romeo dies. In Shakespeare, Romeo drinks the poison and dies beside a wife still locked in her feigned sleep; Juliet wakes to a corpse and speaks her last lines over it, ending with the dagger and the sentence "There rust, and let me die" (5.3). In Carlei's version Juliet opens her eyes while Romeo lives, the two kiss, and he collapses dead in her arms before she takes his dagger. The change restores a device from the old stage tradition, descending from Otway's Caius Marius through Garrick's expanded waking scene, and it converts Shakespeare's absolute irony, two deaths in mutual ignorance, into a farewell the lovers are permitted to share.

### Q: How does the film treat Rosaline, who never appears in Shakespeare's play?

The film brings Rosaline on screen. Shakespeare keeps her entirely offstage: she exists as report, the unseen Capulet niece whose refusal feeds Romeo's opening melancholy and against whose absence the balcony scene measures his conversion. The production cast Nathalie Rapti Gomez in the part and placed her at the Capulet ball as a visible presence in Romeo's evening. The change redistributes the psychology of the first act. In the play, Romeo's "Did my heart love till now?" convicts his earlier passion as self-dramatisation, love rehearsed on an idea. In the film, with Rosaline present and witnessed, the same turn becomes a simpler transfer of attention between two women in one room, and the diagnosis Shakespeare built into the absence is lost.

### Q: Why did Italian cinemas not show the film until February 2015?

Italian cinemas waited because the release strategy privileged the Anglophone markets and the Italian opening simply trailed far behind. The film premiered in Hollywood in September 2013 and opened in Britain and the United States that October, yet its Italian release, through Good Films, did not arrive until 12 February 2015, sixteen months later. The delay is striking because the production's claims rested on Italy: an Italian director, Italian locations in Verona, Mantua, Caprarola and Subiaco, an Italian crew, and marketing built on a return to the play's Veronese roots. The country that supplied the film's authenticity was the last major market asked to pay for it, and the episode underlines where the producers believed the audience lived.

### Q: Why did the film open on only 461 American screens?

The film opened on 461 American screens because its distribution was a limited release conducted on the producers' own account. Relativity Media handled the North American release under an arrangement in which, as The Hollywood Reporter documented, the producers paid Relativity to distribute the picture rather than selling the film to the company. No studio had committed its own money to the launch, and the rollout matched that caution: 461 screens, concentrated in markets judged friendly to costume drama. The strategy capped the opening before a single review appeared, and the returns justified nothing wider, 520,116 dollars across the first weekend and a North American total of 1,162,635 dollars. The counterfactual remains unprovable, but a platform built for a phenomenon behaved instead like an apology.

### Q: Why was James Horner's score replaced after it had been recorded?

The production replaced Horner's score because the finished film was judged to need a different musical register. James Horner, among the most bankable film composers of his generation, wrote and recorded a complete score for the picture months before its release; the producers set that work aside and engaged Abel Korzeniowski to compose a replacement at short notice. Score replacements of this completeness are rare and expensive, and the episode belongs to the production's broader pattern of substitutions made late and defended lightly. Jonathan Broxton of Movie Music UK documented the change while praising the replacement as a sweeping romantic achievement. The discarded Horner score went unheard in the film and survives only as a credit in accounts of the production.

### Q: What honour did Abel Korzeniowski's replacement score go on to win?

Korzeniowski's score won Film Score of the Year from the International Film Music Critics Association. The award, announced in February 2014, made the replacement score the production's most decorated element, outstripping anything won by the direction, the screenplay or the performances. The soundtrack also carried period pieces performed by Ensemble La Rossignol and a song by Zola Jesus, and the score has sustained an independent life among film-music listeners that the film itself never matched. The outcome carries an irony the production cannot have intended: the one wholesale substitution in the enterprise that posterity ratified was the musical one, undertaken quietly, credited honestly, and judged on its merits rather than on a famous name.

### Q: How did actors and Shakespeare's Globe answer Fellowes's defence of the rewrite?

Actors and the Globe answered that the audience needed no such protection. Janet Suzman called Fellowes's remarks patent piffle in The Guardian's report of the exchange. Fiona Banks, speaking for Shakespeare's Globe, answered in a sentence: "Shakespeare's for everybody. We can all understand him." Antony Sher later called the underlying idea nonsense and supplied the professional counter-thesis, that the intelligibility of Shakespeare is a craft learned by actors rather than a scholarship conferred by universities, in his case without any degree at all. The replies share a structure: each locates the play's accessibility in performance, where Fellowes had located its difficulty in the audience. Fellowes subsequently retracted the formulation that had caused the offence, though the film bearing it could not be withdrawn.

### Q: Why did the production shoot in Mantua, the city of Romeo's exile?

The production shot in Mantua because the city gave the exile act a real geography and the company a second authentic Renaissance setting. Shakespeare sends Romeo to Mantua in banishment, and Friar Laurence's plan depends on reuniting the lovers there; most filmed versions treat the city as a studio afterthought. Carlei's company photographed in Mantua during February 2012, when the Gazzetta di Mantova published the first pictures from the set, and the location allowed Romeo's banishment to be played among streets as old as Verona's own. The choice belongs to the production's strongest instinct, its material honesty about place, and it throws the screenplay's opposite instinct into relief: a film willing to travel to the real Mantua was unwilling to trust the real words for what happened there.

### Q: Which performances earned praise from reviewers who disliked the film?

Paul Giamatti's Friar Laurence and Lesley Manville's Nurse earned praise across the whole range of notices. Giamatti was commended by hostile and friendly reviewers alike for a humane, authoritative reading that made the Friar's plotting legible, and Manville registered warmly in almost every account, helped by the screenplay's decision to preserve more of the Nurse than of most roles. Damian Lewis's Lord Capulet, frightening and plausible in the scene of Juliet's disowning, was ranked by several critics as the film's finest acting, and Stellan Skarsgard's Prince drew consistent respect. The pattern is diagnostic: the praise pools in the adult supporting roles while the complaints gather on the young leads, reversing the distribution a Romeo and Juliet exists to produce.

### Q: What role did the Swarovski company play in bringing the film into being?

Swarovski financed the film as the founding project of a new production company. The Austrian crystal house launched Swarovski Entertainment with this production, took producer credits for its chairwoman Nadja Swarovski, and made what Screen Daily reported as a substantial backing of the seventeen million dollar budget, the largest single commitment the company had undertaken in that period, on its own account to The Hollywood Reporter. A related jewellery line accompanied the release, and the company spoke at Cannes of building a slate in the ten to twenty million dollar range. The arrangement shaped the film's character: a luxury brand's first picture required splendour, respectability and a PG-13 surface, and the production delivered all three at the expense of danger.

### Q: How was the film marketed, and why did the marketing become part of the complaint?

The film was marketed as the traditional version, the first picture since Zeffirelli's 1968 film to return the tragedy to its Veronese roots, as Justin Chang reported the billing in Variety. That framing became part of the complaint because it described the one thing the film had not done. Audiences were promised the authentic article, period, place and text, and received period and place with a partially rewritten text whose rewriting had not been announced. The director and practitioner Dawn Monique Williams gave the objection its sharpest form: the version presents itself as the original while behaving as an adaptation, so newcomers risk believing they have received Shakespeare when they have not. Once the Fellowes interviews made the rewrite famous, every advertisement repeated the misdescription to an audience by then equipped to resent it.

### Q: Can the film replace the play for students meeting the tragedy for the first time?

The film cannot safely replace the play, and the reason is the paraphrase rather than the abridgement. A cut film serves a classroom honestly because a teacher can name the omissions and a student who opens the text finds the film's surviving lines in place. Carlei's film interleaves Shakespeare's lines with Fellowes's imitations without marking the join, so a student quoting the film may quote the screenwriter under the playwright's name. Reviewers at The Dissolve and elsewhere foresaw a cheat-sheet afterlife for exactly this reason, an abbreviated version preserving the high points. Used with supervision, as a study in adaptation, the film teaches a great deal. Used as a substitute text, it miseducates with complete confidence.
