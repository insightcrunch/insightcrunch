Every earlier unit of calculus runs in one direction. You are handed a function, perhaps a position or a cost or a temperature, and the machinery of derivatives tells you how fast that function changes. Differential equations reverse the traffic. Here the rate law arrives first, stated as a relationship involving a derivative, and the unknown is the function itself. Recovering a whole curve from instructions about its steepness is the central puzzle of the unit, and it changes how the entire subject feels, because the answer to a problem is no longer a number or a slope but a family of possible worlds.

The territory in AP Calculus AB is deliberately narrow, and that narrowness is a gift. The course asks you to solve separable equations, to read and sketch slope fields, to pin down particular solutions with initial conditions, and to build exponential growth and decay models. Two companion ideas round out the picture: the logistic model, which bends growth toward a ceiling, and Euler's method, which approximates a solution numerically when algebra runs out. Nothing in the unit is computationally savage. The difficulty lives in interpretation, in knowing what each symbol claims about a changing quantity, and in keeping the bookkeeping of constants and domains honest.

One habit carries a student further here than any formula sheet. Read every equation as a rate model before touching a pencil to its algebra. A differential equation describes how something changes, so each term has a meaning in the story the problem tells: a population reproducing, a cup cooling, a tank filling, a rumor spreading. When the meaning stays in view, separation of variables stops being a ritual and becomes the obvious move, because the steps mirror the structure of the model. The [AP Calculus AB complete guide](/2008/08/10/ap-calculus-ab-complete-guide/) places this unit inside the full arc of the course, and the pages below work it from first reading to final answer.

## Reading a Differential Equation as a Rate Law

An equation like dy/dx = x + y makes a claim at every point of the plane. Pick any coordinates, say x = 2 and y = 3, and the equation announces that a solution curve passing through that point climbs with slope 5 there. Nothing about the claim requires knowing the whole curve in advance. The equation is a local weather report repeated everywhere: wherever a solution happens to be, this is how steeply it must be heading. That local character explains why one equation owns infinitely many solutions, since a curve may pass through any starting point and then obey the reported slopes as it travels.

Units discipline the reading. If y measures grams of a substance and x measures hours, then dy/dx carries units of grams per hour, and every term on the right side must make sense in those units. A term like 0.04y works because 0.04 carries units of per hour, converting grams into grams per hour. A bare x added to y would be nonsense in that story, because hours cannot be added to grams. Graders reward students who keep units attached to their interpretation sentences, and the habit also catches setup errors early, since a mismatched unit almost always signals a misread problem.

Sign analysis is the fastest form of interpretation. Where the right side is positive, solutions rise; where negative, they fall; where zero, a solution levels off momentarily. Consider dy/dx = 0.5y - 20, a crude model of a savings account earning interest while fees drain it. Setting the right side to zero gives y = 40, the balance at which growth pauses. At a balance of 30, the rate equals 15 - 20, which is -5, so the account shrinks by about five dollars per period. At a balance of 60, the rate equals 30 - 20, which is 10, so the account grows. One line of arithmetic has already sketched the fate of every possible account: balances below 40 decay toward zero, balances above 40 grow without bound, and exactly 40 stays fixed forever.

That fixed value deserves its name. An equilibrium solution is a constant function that satisfies the equation, found by asking where the rate could vanish identically. With dy/dx = (y - 2)(y + 5), the constants y = 2 and y = -5 both work, because substituting either makes the derivative zero and the right side zero as well. Equilibria organize the long-term behavior of a model the way shores organize a map. A population equation with an equilibrium at zero and another at some carrying capacity is telling you, before any solving begins, which states persist and which states merely pass through.

Some equations depend only on the independent variable, some only on the dependent one, and many on both. An equation whose right side involves y alone is called autonomous, and autonomy has a visual consequence you will exploit later: along any horizontal line, every point shares the same y value, so every slope repeats across that line. The equation dy/dx = y - 2 assigns slope 0 to the whole line y = 2, slope 1 to the whole line y = 3, and slope -1 to the whole line y = 1. Recognizing autonomy lets you predict the striped structure of a slope field before drawing a single segment.

Interpretation questions on the exam often stop at this level. A typical prompt hands you dy/dt = 4 - 0.2A for the amount A of a drug in the bloodstream and asks what the 4 and the 0.2 mean. The 4 is an inflow, four milligrams per hour arriving from a drip, constant regardless of the amount present. The term 0.2A is an outflow proportional to the amount, an elimination rate of twenty percent of the current amount per hour. Two numbers, two mechanisms, and the whole pharmacology of the model sits inside one short equation. Students who practice translating in both directions, from story to symbols and from symbols back to story, find these questions nearly free.

Teachers compress this unit into a single reference page so often that the page deserves a name. The InsightCrunch differential equations guide, separable equations and slope fields worked through side by side, gathers the four moves the exam repeats most: separate and integrate, read a field, impose an initial condition, and recognize the standard growth forms on sight. The table below is the skeleton of that guide. Each row pairs one equation with the method it calls for and the result the method delivers, and the paragraphs after the table slow two of the rows down to full working speed.

| InsightCrunch guide entry | Differential equation | Method | Result |
| Growth law through a known start | dy/dt = 0.04y with y(0) = 250 | Separation, then exponentiation | y = 250e^(0.04t) |
| Curve through a chosen point | dy/dx = x/y with y(1) = 2 | Separation, then the initial condition | y = the square root of (x squared + 3) |

Take the second row at full speed. Here dy/dx = x/y says the slope at any point is the ratio of its coordinates, so the curve steepens as x grows and flattens as y grows. Separation gathers the y factors on one side and the x factors on the other, producing y dy = x dx. Integrating both sides gives y squared over 2 on the left and x squared over 2 plus a constant on the right. Multiplying through by 2 and renaming the doubled constant yields y squared = x squared + C, a family of hyperbolas in disguise. The initial condition y(1) = 2 selects one member: substituting gives 4 = 1 + C, so C = 3, and the particular solution is y squared = x squared + 3. Because the starting value 2 is positive, the solution takes the positive root, y = the square root of (x squared + 3), and at x = 2 it predicts y = the square root of 7, about 2.646.

Notice what the example teaches about the general workflow. Separation is the engine, the constant is the memory of the family, and the initial condition is a filter that keeps exactly one curve. The same three beats appear in nearly every analytic problem in the unit, whether the story involves cooling coffee or compounding interest. Students who narrate the beats to themselves, family first, member second, rarely lose the constant, and losing the constant is the single most expensive habit in this territory.

The guide's other analytic showcase runs in the opposite emotional direction, because it feels almost too clean. Consider dy/dx = 2xy, whose slope at a point doubles the product of the coordinates. Separating gives dy/y = 2x dx, and integrating gives the natural logarithm of the absolute value of y equal to x squared plus a constant. Exponentiating both sides turns that into y = Ce^(x squared), where the new constant C absorbs the exponential of the old one and carries either sign. A quick check confirms the family: differentiating Ce^(x squared) produces 2x times Ce^(x squared), which is exactly 2xy. One line of verification closes the loop, and verification is a skill the exam tests directly, as a later section shows.

Slope fields occupy the other half of the guide because they answer a different kind of question. Algebra hands you formulas; a field hands you behavior. Given only a picture of short segments, you can watch solutions bend toward equilibria, flee from them, or thread between them, all without solving anything. Pairing the two halves matters for scoring: many free-response questions open with a field to sketch or read, then pivot to separation for an exact solution, then close by asking how the two views agree. The guide keeps them adjacent so the pivot feels natural.

Keep the guide's rows in mind as templates rather than answers. An exam equation will rarely match a row exactly, but it will almost always rhyme with one: a rate proportional to the amount present, a ratio of coordinates, a product of factors with visible zeros, a bounded growth law with the ceiling showing. Naming the rhyme is the first scored insight in most solutions, and it costs nothing but attention.

## Separation of Variables, Taken Apart Slowly

A separable equation is one whose rate law factors into a part depending only on x multiplied by a part depending only on y. Written as dy/dx = g(x) times h(y), it invites a single rearrangement: gather every y factor beside dy, gather every x factor beside dx, and integrate the two sides independently. The rearrangement looks like algebra on a fraction, and treating dy/dx as a separable quotient is a licensed shortcut here because the chain rule guarantees the outcome. What licenses it is not notation but structure: after rearrangement, each side is a perfect differential of one variable, so antidifferentiation can proceed without the variables interfering.

Watch the full machine run on dy/dx = 3x squared over y, with the initial condition y(0) = 2. Multiplying both sides by y moves the dependent variable home, giving y dy = 3x squared dx. Integration produces y squared over 2 on the left and x cubed plus a constant on the right. Doubling everything and absorbing the factor into the constant leaves y squared = 2x cubed + C. The condition y(0) = 2 turns the family into one curve: 4 = 0 + C, so C = 4, and the solution reads y = the square root of (2x cubed + 4). The positive root is mandatory because the curve starts at a positive height and a continuous solution cannot jump between branches. At x = 1 the model predicts the square root of 6, about 2.449.

The domain of that answer is part of the answer. The radicand 2x cubed + 4 stays positive only when x exceeds the negative cube root of 2, roughly -1.26, so the solution lives on that interval and nowhere else. Exam solutions that ignore the interval still earn most points, yet the restriction explains a subtlety graders love: a formula and a solution are not the same object. The formula is ink; the solution is the ink together with the interval on which it obeys the equation and honors the initial condition.

### Why does the plus C belong to the whole family and not to one curve?

Separation ends with two antiderivatives, and each side could carry its own constant. Subtracting one constant from the other leaves a single net constant, and that lone survivor labels the family: every value draws one member curve, so the plus C belongs to all solutions at once, not to any individual.

Constant bookkeeping provokes more lost points than any hard integral in the unit. Three conventions keep the ledger clean. First, write one constant only, on the side with the independent variable, since two constants always merge into one. Second, rename freely when algebra dresses the constant in new clothes: if exponentiation turns C into e raised to C, call the result A and move on, because the family is unchanged. Third, remember that after exponentiation the new constant may be positive, negative, or zero, because it represents a signed scale factor rather than a logarithm. The solution y = Ce^(4x) includes the equilibrium y = 0 at C = 0, even though the logarithm step seemed to exclude it.

Division during separation carries a quieter risk. Splitting dy/dx = y(y - 3) requires dividing by y(y - 3), and division discards any solution hiding at the zeros of that product. The constants y = 0 and y = 3 satisfy the original equation directly, as equilibria, and a complete solution names them even though the separated computation never sees them. The same check applies whenever the y factor can vanish. Growth equations like dy/dx = 4y escape the trap because C = 0 resurrects the zero solution inside the family, but a factor with a nonzero root, like y - 3, keeps its equilibrium outside the general form, and only a deliberate glance recovers it.

Not every problem in the unit needs the full ceremony. When the right side involves x alone, as in dy/dx = 2x/(1 + x squared), separation is already complete and the task reduces to one antiderivative, here the natural logarithm of (1 + x squared) plus a constant. That reduction is the Fundamental Theorem wearing work clothes: the solution accumulates the rate, exactly the viewpoint developed in the treatment of [Riemann sums and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus](/2006/09/12/ap-calculus-ab-riemann-sums-ftc/). Recognizing which problems are pure accumulation and which demand genuine separation saves minutes that the harder questions happily consume.

Implicit answers deserve respect as well. Separation of dy/dx = e^x over y squared yields y cubed over 3 = e^x + C, and imposing y(0) = 2 gives 8/3 = 1 + C, so C = 5/3 and the solution is y cubed = 3e^x + 5. Extracting the cube root is legal, producing y = the cube root of (3e^x + 5), which indeed returns 2 at x = 0 because the cube root of 8 is 2. When extraction is uglier than this, an implicit relation with the constant evaluated earns full credit, provided the work labels it as the solution and states the condition it satisfies. Graders grade mathematics, not tidiness.

A final structural warning concerns the order of operations. Variables must be fully separated before any integration begins; integrating an unseparated equation term by term produces confident nonsense. The tell is simple: before writing an integral sign, scan each side and confirm that only one letter appears. If a stray y survives beside dx, the rearrangement is unfinished, and every line after it inherits the error. Students who build that two-second scan into their routine almost never produce the classic separation blunder.

## Slope Fields: Seeing the Equation

A slope field converts an equation into a landscape. Choose a lattice of points, evaluate the derivative at each one, and draw a short segment whose steepness matches that value. No solution has been computed, yet the drawing already behaves like a map of every solution at once, because any genuine curve must run tangent to the segments it passes. The construction is humble arithmetic repeated patiently, and its power comes from accumulation: one segment says little, but hundreds together reveal rivers of flow, barriers, and resting places that algebra alone tends to hide.

Build a fragment of the field for dy/dx = x + y to feel the mechanics. At the origin the slope is 0, so the segment lies flat. At (1, 0) the slope is 1, a diagonal climbing at forty-five degrees, and (0, 1) gives the same value, an early hint that slope here depends on the sum of the coordinates. At (1, 1) the slope reaches 2, noticeably steeper, while at (-1, 0) it drops to -1, tilting downward. Along the whole line y = -x, every sum vanishes, so a chain of horizontal segments runs diagonally across the field, marking where solutions pause their climb before turning. Curves starting below that chain fall away; curves starting above it rise ever faster, bending upward as both coordinates feed the slope.

### What does one short segment in a slope field actually record?

A segment records a single promise: at its center point, every solution curve passing through must travel with exactly that steepness. The segment says nothing about which curve, nothing about position beyond its point, and nothing about the future beyond the local direction the equation enforces there.

Patterns in a field betray the form of its equation, and reading them is a scored skill. When every segment in a horizontal row matches its neighbors, the rate ignores x entirely, which means the equation is autonomous: dy/dx = y - 2 draws identical rows, flat along y = 2 and steepening as rows move away from that line. When every segment in a vertical column matches instead, the rate ignores y, as in dy/dx = 2x, where the column at x = 0 stands vertical-flat and columns tilt harder with distance from the axis. Diagonal repetition signals dependence on a sum or difference of the variables. A field with vertical segments scattered along a line is confessing a denominator that vanishes there, as dy/dx = x/y does along the x-axis, where slopes blow up and curves cross the axis at right angles to it.

### Which feature of a slope field reveals an equilibrium solution?

An equilibrium appears as an unbroken horizontal row: at every point along one y value, the segments lie flat, so a solution starting there never leaves. Rows above and below tilt toward or away from that line, revealing whether nearby solutions drift back or escape over time.

Sketching a particular solution through a marked point is the second scored skill, and it resembles floating a leaf on a stream. Place the pencil at the given point, then draw a smooth curve that stays tangent to every segment it meets, never crossing another solution and never fighting the local tilt. Uniqueness guarantees the discipline of the drawing: two solution curves of the same equation cannot intersect, because at an intersection both would need the single slope the equation assigns that point yet head toward different futures. Your drawn curve should extend across the whole printed window, easing through segments rather than hopping between them, and it should approach equilibria asymptotically instead of crashing into them at an angle.

Reading values off a field trades precision for speed. Asked to estimate the solution at x = 3 for the curve through (0, 1), you trace the flow from the starting point and read the height where your curve crosses the vertical line x = 3. The estimate inherits the drawing's roughness, and the exam knows it; answers within a sensible band earn the point. The same tracing answers qualitative questions that algebra answers slowly: whether the solution through a point is increasing or concave up, where it peaks, and whether it levels toward an equilibrium or escapes the window entirely.

Matching a field to its equation among several candidates is a detective exercise with reliable clues. Test the origin first, since many candidates disagree there. Then test a point on each axis, then a point where signs differ, until only one candidate survives every probe. Sign regions do heavy work: the set of points where a candidate predicts positive slope must match the region where the drawn segments climb. Zero-slope loci are equally decisive, because each candidate flattens along a different curve. Students who probe systematically eliminate three wrong answers in under a minute, while students who squint at overall vibes often talk themselves into the prettiest wrong field.

One more phenomenon deserves attention because it surprises students every year. Solutions can approach an equilibrium without ever reaching it, sliding closer across the whole window while the gap shrinks toward nothing. That asymptotic approach is not sloppy drawing; it mirrors the mathematics, since a solution that actually touched the equilibrium would merge with it and violate uniqueness. When your traced curve seems to melt into a horizontal row, trust the picture. The field is showing you stability, the tendency of nearby states to settle, and stability language earns interpretation points whenever a prompt asks for long-term behavior.

## Particular Solutions and the Discipline of Domains

A general solution is a family portrait; a particular solution is one face in it, chosen by an initial condition. The condition might be a point the curve must pass through, a starting amount at time zero, or a temperature at the moment observation begins. Mechanically, you substitute the pair into the general form and solve the small algebra problem that remains for the constant. Conceptually, you are answering a modeling question: of all the futures this rate law permits, which one began here? The exam pairs the two steps so reliably that separating them in your mind, family first and member second, prevents the most common structural error, which is substituting the condition before the constant exists.

A dramatic example shows why the interval matters as much as the formula. Separate dy/dx = y squared, an equation claiming that growth accelerates with the square of the amount. Division gives dy over y squared = dx, integration gives -1/y = x + C, and the initial condition y(0) = 1 forces C = -1. The solution simplifies to y = 1/(1 - x). At x = 0 the value is 1, at x = 0.5 it is 2, at x = 0.9 it is 10, and as x approaches 1 the values rocket beyond every bound. The solution exists only for x less than 1, a finite lifetime ending in a vertical asymptote, because the equation's own feedback destroys the quantity it describes. No repair extends the curve past the blow-up; the model simply stops.

Contrast that violence with the calm of dy/dx = y/x under the condition y(-2) = 4. Separation produces the natural logarithm of the absolute value of y equal to the natural logarithm of the absolute value of x plus a constant, which exponentiates to y = Cx. The condition gives C = -2, hence y = -2x. Yet writing the answer without a domain would be careless, because the original equation is undefined at x = 0, and the initial point sits on the negative side of that barrier. The solution is y = -2x for negative x only. On the positive side, the same formula belongs to a different solution with a different constant, and the two never meet. A single equation can own disconnected territories, and an initial condition claims exactly one of them.

The square root example from the guide carries the same lesson in softer form. The solution y = the square root of (x squared + 3) happens to live everywhere, since the radicand never vanishes, but its sibling from the separation section, y = the square root of (2x cubed + 4), dies where the radicand turns negative. Branch selection interacts with domains too: the negative root of the same relation is a separate solution, chosen whenever the initial value is negative, and mixing branches mid-solution is a contradiction the graders notice. Continuous solutions do not teleport between branches.

Existence and uniqueness hover behind these rules without needing their formal statements. Roughly, when the rate law and its partial derivative with respect to y behave continuously near a point, exactly one solution passes through that point, which is why slope field curves never cross and why an initial condition is a genuine selection rather than a suggestion. The exam never asks you to prove the theorem, but it constantly asks you to use its consequences: one point, one curve, one domain bounded by the nearest discontinuity of the equation or the solution.

Initial conditions also arrive disguised. A problem may say a tank holds 40 gallons at the moment observation starts, or a sample registers 12 grams when the clock starts, or a population is censused at 800 in the initial year. Each statement is an initial condition wearing a story's clothes, and translating it into function notation, A(0) = 40 or P(0) = 800, is the scored first step. Notice that the independent variable need not be zero at the start; a condition like y(3) = 7 anchors the curve at x = 3 and shifts every domain statement accordingly. The constant does not care where the anchor sits, and neither should you.

## Exponential Growth and Decay: The One-Parameter Universe

The equation dy/dt = ky is the hardest-working sentence in applied mathematics. It claims the rate of change is proportional to the amount present, with k the constant of proportionality, and that single claim governs bacteria in a dish, money in an account, heat leaving a roast, and atoms disintegrating in a rock. Separation takes one breath: dy/y = k dt, integrate to get the logarithm of the absolute value of y equal to kt plus a constant, exponentiate to get y = Ce^(kt). The initial amount fixes C, so every such story ends at the same address: the amount at time t equals the starting amount multiplied by e raised to kt.

The sign of k decides the genre. Positive k grows, negative k decays, and the magnitude sets the tempo. With k = 0.04 and a start of 250, the population P(t) = 250e^(0.04t) reaches about 373 at t = 10, since e raised to 0.4 is about 1.4918 and 250 times 1.4918 is 372.96. Doubling time comes from solving 2 = e^(0.04t), giving t = the natural logarithm of 2 divided by 0.04, about 17.33 time units. That independence is worth pausing on: the doubling time ignores the starting amount entirely, because proportionality scales every starting point identically. A colony of 250 and a colony of 250,000 double on the same schedule.

Decay tells its story in half-lives instead. A sample governed by dA/dt = kA with k = the natural logarithm of 2 divided by 8, taken negatively, about -0.0866 per day, halves every 8 days. Starting from 100 grams, three half-lives consume 24 days and leave 100 times one eighth, exactly 12.5 grams. The two dialects, k and half-life, translate through k = ln 2 over the half-life, with a negative sign for decay. Fluency in both matters because problems choose whichever dialect suits their data: a stated percentage rate hands you k after a decimal conversion, while a stated halving period hands you the base one-half form A = A0 times (1/2) raised to t over the period.

A subtlety separates continuous models from the discrete compounding of a bank statement. Interest compounded yearly at 5 percent multiplies a balance by 1.05 annually, a staircase. The differential equation dB/dt = 0.05B describes interest flowing continuously, and its solution e^(0.05t) outruns the staircase slightly, since e raised to 0.05 is about 1.0513. The exam model is always the continuous one, because only the continuous law is a differential equation at all. When a problem says a quantity grows continuously at a rate proportional to its size, it is naming this equation in prose, and the translation should be reflexive.

Cooling wears the same mathematics with a shifted center. Newton's law of cooling states that temperature changes at a rate proportional to the gap between the object and its surroundings: dT/dt = k(T - 70) for a room at 70 degrees, with k negative. Substituting u = T - 70 converts the law into du/dt = ku, pure decay of the gap. A liquid starting at 200 degrees with k = -0.05 follows T(t) = 70 + 130e^(-0.05t), and after ten minutes the gap has shrunk by the factor e raised to -0.5, about 0.6065, leaving T = 70 + 78.85, about 149 degrees. The object never quite reaches room temperature in the model; the gap decays toward zero asymptotically, which matches the physical intuition that the last degree takes forever.

Growth and decay also model elimination and accumulation in the body. A drug cleared by kidneys often obeys dA/dt = -kA between doses, so the amount falls exponentially and clinicians speak of the drug's half-life exactly as physicists do. A reservoir filling at a steady inflow while leaking proportionally to its level obeys dA/dt = r - kA, a hybrid whose solution bends toward the equilibrium r/k. That hybrid deserves its own family name in your recognition gallery: rate equals constant minus proportional term, equilibrium where the two balance, approach that slows as the gap closes. It reappears in the modeling section below wearing a salt tank's clothes.

Reading k from data is the final skill in this family. Given two observations, the ratio of amounts determines e raised to k times the elapsed time, and one logarithm recovers k. Given a graph of the amount, k is the slope of the tangent divided by the height at that instant, because the equation literally equates the two. Given a slope field for dy/dt = ky, k is the slope along the horizontal line y = 1. Each route exploits the same proportionality from a different window, and exam questions rotate among the windows to see whether the understanding is structural or memorized.

## A Gallery of Standard Forms

Fluency in this unit is largely recognition speed, the ability to name an equation's family before computing anything. Five forms recur so often that each deserves a portrait. The first is the proportional law dy/dt = ky, solved above, whose exponential answer you should be able to write before the separation steps finish. The second is the approach law dy/dx = k(a - y), where a is a target level and the rate is proportional to the remaining gap. Substituting u = a - y turns the gap into the variable, and since du/dx = -dy/dx, the equation becomes du/dx = -ku, pure decay. The solution y = a - Ce^(-kx) climbs or falls toward a and lingers there asymptotically, the signature of cooling, charging, and learning curves across the sciences.

The third form is pure accumulation, dy/dx = g(x), where the rate never mentions y. Separation is vacuous; the solution is one antiderivative plus a constant, and the initial condition merely chooses the accumulation's starting height. This form quietly includes every definite-integral-as-net-change problem from earlier units, now reframed: the solution at a later input equals the initial value plus the accumulated rate between the two inputs. Students sometimes hunt for a y to separate where none exists. The absence is the diagnosis.

The fourth form is the product of an x factor and a y factor with internal structure, like dy/dx = x(y - 2) or dy/dx = (x + 1)(y squared). Separation still works mechanically, placing 1/(y - 2) or 1/(y squared) beside dy, but the portraits add equilibria at the y factor's roots and, for the squared factor, the finite-lifetime blow-up behavior already met in the particular solutions section. Products also produce the richest slope fields, because zeros along both a vertical line and a horizontal line partition the plane into regions of opposing flow.

The fifth form is a ratio, dy/dx = g(x)/h(y) or its mirror, and the only new idea is care with the denominator's zeros, where slopes turn vertical and solutions may fail to be functions over any interval containing the bad line. The guide's second row, dy/dx = x/y, is the emblem: separation is trivial, yet the geometry, with its vertical tangents along the x-axis, needs the field to be seen properly.

Just as valuable is recognizing what separation cannot touch. The equation dy/dx = x + y, the star of the slope field section, does not factor into an x part times a y part, because a sum refuses to split into a product. No rearrangement isolates the variables, and AB students meet this equation only through fields and numerical approximation, not through a closed-form hunt. A neighboring sum like dy/dx = x + y squared fails the same way. The exam includes such equations precisely to test whether you reach for the right tool: when separation stalls at the factoring step, the problem is steering you toward a picture or a table of approximations, and the sooner you notice, the more time you keep.

There is a deeper unity beneath the gallery. Every solvable AB form reduces, after one substitution or rearrangement, to the statement that some logarithm or power of y equals some antiderivative in x. The exponential family comes from the logarithm of y equaling a linear function. The approach family comes from the logarithm of the gap. Power families come from y raised to a negative power equaling a polynomial. Seeing the reduction in advance lets you predict the solution's shape, growth, ceiling, blow-up, or calm, before the algebra confirms it, and prediction is the difference between checking an answer and merely having one.

## The Logistic Model: Growth with a Ceiling

Exponential growth is a young model's optimism. Real populations meet walls: food runs short, territory fills, susceptible hosts for a rumor or a virus grow scarce. The logistic equation encodes the wall by multiplying the proportional law by a braking factor that fades as the population nears its carrying capacity. In its most readable dress, dP/dt = kP(1 - P/L), where L is the capacity. When P is tiny beside L, the parenthesis hovers near 1 and growth is nearly exponential at rate k. As P approaches L, the parenthesis collapses toward zero and growth strangles itself, no matter how large the population stands.

Read the equation before solving it, because the exam's logistic questions are mostly reading questions. The rate vanishes at P = 0 and at P = L, the two equilibria, one trivial and one crowning. Between them the rate is positive, so populations starting below capacity always increase. Starting above capacity flips the sign: the parenthesis turns negative, the rate turns negative, and the population declines toward L from above. Capacity attracts from both directions, which is why ecologists treat it as the landscape's verdict on a species rather than a suggestion.

The growth rate itself peaks halfway up. Treating the rate as a function of P, the product P(1 - P/L) is a downward parabola with its vertex at P = L/2, so the population grows fastest at half capacity, where the rate equals kL/4. With the guide's numbers, k = 0.2 and L = 600, the fastest growth hits 30 individuals per unit time at P = 300. That halfway point is also the inflection point of the solution curve: below it the curve is concave up, accelerating; above it concave down, braking. A graph question that asks where growth is fastest, or where the curve changes concavity, is asking for L/2 in two different costumes, and spotting the costume change is pure profit.

The solution, when a problem supplies or requests it, is P(t) = L divided by (1 + Ae^(-kt)), where A = (L - P0)/P0 measures how much headroom the start enjoys relative to the start itself. For L = 600, P0 = 50, and k = 0.2, the headroom ratio is 550/50, so A = 11, and the solution is P(t) = 600/(1 + 11e^(-0.2t)). At t = 10, the exponential e^(-2) is about 0.1353, the denominator is about 1 + 1.4887, or 2.4887, and P is about 241, still below the halfway mark, consistent with a curve that has not yet reached its fastest day. As t grows without bound the exponential dies and P settles onto 600, tracing the famous S shape: slow dawn, steep noon, long dusk against the ceiling.

Slope fields draw the same story in stripes. Because the logistic equation is autonomous, each horizontal row repeats its segments, flat at the two equilibrium rows and steepest along the row P = L/2. A solution sketched through a small starting value hugs the floor, tilts upward through the steep band, then flattens against the ceiling row without touching it. The picture explains a frequent conceptual answer: a logistic population never exceeds capacity when starting below it, because crossing would require passing through the equilibrium solution, and uniqueness forbids the meeting.

Honesty about scope helps here. Deriving the logistic solution from the equation demands partial fractions, an integration technique beyond the AB core, so AB prompts emphasize interpretation: identify the capacity from the equation's form, locate the fastest growth, predict long-term behavior, and use a supplied solution formula to evaluate or to find when the population reaches a stated level. Meeting the model on those terms is not a lesser mastery. It is the mastery the course actually certifies, and it transfers directly to reading any bounded-growth claim in biology, marketing, or epidemiology.

## Euler's Method: Walking the Field with a Ruler

Some equations refuse closed-form solutions, and some exam questions simply prefer numbers to formulas. Euler's method answers with a walk. Begin at a point where the solution's value is known. Read the slope the equation assigns there. Travel along that slope for one short step of width h, arriving at a new point whose height is the old height plus h times the old slope. Then repeat: fresh point, fresh slope, fresh step. The recipe y new = y old + h times f(x old, y old) is the entire algorithm, a tangent-line approximation reapplied until the target x is reached.

Run it on the slope field star, dy/dx = x + y with y(0) = 1, using two steps of width 0.5 to estimate the value at x = 1. At the start, the slope is 0 + 1, which is 1, so the first step lands at y = 1 + 0.5 times 1, giving 1.5 at x = 0.5. The equation now assigns slope 0.5 + 1.5, which is 2, at the new point, and the second step lands at y = 1.5 + 0.5 times 2, giving 2.5 at x = 1. The true solution, y = 2e^x - x - 1, gives 2e - 2, about 3.437, at x = 1, so the walk undershot by nearly a full unit. The shortfall is no accident: the solution bends upward throughout, each tangent line sags beneath the arc, and every step inherits the sag.

A finer walk on a simpler equation shows the method's character more delicately. For dy/dx = y with y(0) = 1 and step width 0.25, the four steps multiply the height by 1.25 each time, since the update adds a quarter of the current height. The heights run 1, then 1.25, then 1.5625, then 1.953125, and finally 2.44140625 at x = 1. The exact value is e, about 2.71828, so the estimate is low by about 0.277. Halving the step would roughly halve that error, because Euler's method is first-order: its total error scales in proportion to the step width. Doubling the arithmetic buys double the accuracy, a trade the exam exploits by choosing step counts you can finish by hand.

### When does an Euler estimate land above the true curve?

Euler steps follow tangent lines, so the error's sign follows the bending. Where the solution curves upward, each tangent trails below the arc and the estimate finishes low. Where the solution bends downward, tangents overshoot the arc, and the same procedure finishes high instead.

Concavity decides the direction of error, and the equation itself reports the concavity if you differentiate it. For dy/dx = x + y, differentiating gives the second derivative as 1 + dy/dx, which is positive wherever the slope exceeds -1, confirming the upward bend behind the underestimate. Exam questions ask for the over-or-under verdict explicitly, and the justification they want is exactly this: name the concavity on the interval, then state that tangent lines lie below a concave-up graph and above a concave-down one. The verdict without the reason earns nothing; the reason is the point.

Procedural discipline matters as much as insight, because Euler arithmetic fails in predictable places. The classic slip is updating x while recycling the original slope, which flattens the walk into a single tangent line. The second classic slip is computing the new slope at the old point after updating, a half-step confusion that scrambles every subsequent row. A tidy table with columns for x, y, slope, and the product h times slope quarantines both errors. Write the table even when the prompt does not demand one; the columns force each quantity to be recomputed at the correct address, and graders scanning a table can award method points even when one arithmetic slip poisons a late row.

## Modeling: Translating Stories into Rate Laws

Every applied problem in this unit is a translation exercise before it is a calculus exercise. English phrases map onto mathematical structures with unusual fidelity, and building the dictionary explicitly pays off. The phrase "proportional to the amount present" writes dy/dt = ky with no negotiation. "Proportional to the difference between the temperature and the room" writes dT/dt = k(T - M), where M is the ambient level and the sign of k usually runs negative. "Increases at a rate proportional to the product of the number who know and the number who do not yet know" writes the logistic law for a rumor, because both factors throttle the spread. "Decreases at a rate proportional to the square root of the height" writes dh/dt = -k times the square root of h, the draining law for a tank with a hole in its floor.

The draining law rewards a full solve. Separate dh/dt = -0.5 times the square root of h by dividing both sides by the root: h raised to the negative one-half, dh, equals -0.5 dt. Integrating gives 2 times the square root of h equal to -0.5t plus a constant. An initial height of 16 sets the constant at 2 times 4, which is 8, so the square root of h equals 4 - 0.25t and h itself equals (4 - 0.25t) squared. Differentiate to confirm: the derivative is 2(4 - 0.25t)(-0.25), which is -0.5(4 - 0.25t), exactly -0.5 times the square root of h. The tank empties when the root reaches zero, at t = 16, and unlike the exponential stories, this model truly ends; the solution's domain closes at the emptying moment, after which the equation no longer describes anything physical.

Mixing problems add a second mechanism and with it a second term. Picture a tank holding 100 gallons of brine, volume held constant because inflow matches outflow at 3 gallons per minute. Fresh brine entering at 2 pounds of salt per gallon delivers salt at 6 pounds per minute. The outflow carries the tank's own mixture, whose concentration is A/100 pounds per gallon when A pounds are dissolved, so salt leaves at 3 times A/100, or 0.03A, pounds per minute. The law writes itself from the bookkeeping: dA/dt = 6 - 0.03A, rate in minus rate out. This is the approach family from the gallery, since 6 - 0.03A equals 0.03 times (200 - A), with equilibrium at A = 200 pounds, the concentration the inflow would eventually impose. Solving with A(0) = 0 gives A(t) = 200(1 - e^(-0.03t)), and at t = 20 the salt content is 200 times (1 - e^(-0.6)), about 200 times 0.4512, roughly 90.2 pounds. The derivative check closes the loop: differentiating gives 6e^(-0.03t), while 6 - 0.03 times the solution equals 6 - 6(1 - e^(-0.03t)), the same thing.

The translation habit generalizes beyond tanks. Any problem that narrates a rate, inflow against outflow, births against deaths, learning against forgetting, is asking for a difference of mechanisms, and [related rates and optimization](/2006/09/08/ap-calculus-ab-related-rates-optimization/) trains the same reflex from the derivative side: name the quantities, name their units, write the balance. Where related rates differentiates a known relation, modeling here builds the relation itself, but the discipline of assigning symbols and tracking units is identical, and sloppiness in either unit costs the same points.

Some prompts stop at the setup, asking you to write the equation and the initial condition without solving, and those prompts are pure translation tests. Others pair a setup with a data table and ask whether the model fits, which is a proportionality test: divide each observed rate by the corresponding amount and check whether the quotients agree. If the rate at amount 50 is 3 and at amount 100 is 6, the quotients 0.06 and 0.06 confirm proportionality with k = 0.06. If the quotients drift, the story needs a different law, perhaps the gap-to-target form. Reading a table this way takes seconds and routinely decides multiple-choice questions built around experimental data.

One more translation deserves its own line because wording tricks so many students. "The rate at which water leaks is proportional to the amount of water remaining" describes decay of the amount, dV/dt = -kV, the negative sign belonging to the word leaks rather than to proportionality itself. Proportionality supplies magnitude; the narrative supplies direction. When in doubt, ask the primitive question: is the quantity growing or shrinking, and does the rate's size track the amount, the gap, or something else? Two answers, direction and dependence, determine the entire equation.

## A Workshop of Particular Solutions

Three further solutions, each built around a different structural wrinkle, consolidate the method better than three repetitions of the same shape. Begin with dy/dx = y cos x, a growth law whose proportionality factor breathes with x. Separation gives dy/y = cos x dx, and integrating produces the logarithm of the absolute value of y equal to sin x plus a constant. Exponentiation yields y = Ce^(sin x). The condition y(0) = 3 forces C = 3, since e raised to sin 0 is 1, so the solution is y = 3e^(sin x). This curve never decays to zero and never blows up; it oscillates gently between 3/e and 3e as the sine in the exponent swings between -1 and 1, a population whose growth rate turns negative for half of each cycle yet whose level stays positive throughout. Verifying takes one line: the derivative of 3e^(sin x) is 3e^(sin x) cos x, which is y cos x exactly.

Branch discipline stars in the second example, dy/dx = 4x/y with y(0) = -3. Separation gives y dy = 4x dx, then y squared over 2 equals 2x squared plus a constant, then y squared = 4x squared + C after doubling. The initial condition yields 9 = 0 + C, so the relation is y squared = 4x squared + 9. Extracting the root offers two candidates, and the negative starting value selects the lower one: y = the negative square root of (4x squared + 9). The prediction at x = 2 is gratifyingly clean: the radicand becomes 16 + 9, which is 25, and the solution value is -5. A solver who had grabbed the positive root out of habit would report 5 and contradict the given start, an error the initial condition exists to prevent.

The third example hides a small joke. The equation dy/dx = e raised to (x - y) does not look separable until the exponential law splits it into e^x times e^(-y). Moving the y part across gives e^y dy = e^x dx, and integrating both sides yields e^y = e^x + C. Impose y(0) = 0: the left side is 1, the right side is 1 + C, so C = 0, and the relation collapses to e^y = e^x, meaning y = x. The most unassuming line in the plane solves an equation that looked transcendental, and the check is instant: the derivative of x is 1, while e raised to (x - x) is e raised to 0, also 1. Surprises like this reward students who finish the algebra instead of abandoning a problem whose first line looks unfriendly.

Patterns across the three examples generalize into a checklist worth reciting. Separate completely before integrating. Integrate both sides in the same breath, writing the constant once. Use the condition only after the constant exists. Extract roots or logarithms carefully, honoring absolute values until the condition fixes the sign. State the branch and, when the formula can die, the interval. Five moves, in that order, and the analytic half of the unit becomes procedure rather than inspiration, freeing attention for the interpretive half where the points hide.

## Concavity Without Solving: Differentiating the Equation

A favorite free-response maneuver asks about a solution's bending without ever asking for the solution, and the tool is to differentiate the differential equation itself. Since y is a function of x, the chain rule turns d/dx of f(x, y) into an expression involving dy/dx, and the original equation then replaces that dy/dx with known quantities. The result is a formula for the second derivative written entirely in x and y, evaluable at any point, no solution required.

The slope field star provides the cleanest demonstration. From dy/dx = x + y, differentiation gives the second derivative as 1 + dy/dx, and substituting the equation back gives y double prime = 1 + x + y. The solution through any point is concave up exactly where 1 + x + y is positive, which is the half-plane above the line y = -x - 1, and concave down below it. Inflection occurs where a solution crosses that line. Notice the elegance: the zero-slope line of the field was y = -x, and the zero-concavity line sits one unit beneath it, a parallel shadow. A question asking where the solution through (0, 1) changes concavity is answered by finding where that curve crosses y = -x - 1, a point the field itself can estimate.

The logistic family bends by a rule worth memorizing through its derivation rather than as a fact. With y prime = y(1 - y/10), the product rule and chain rule give y double prime = y prime times (1 - y/5), after collecting terms. Since y prime stays positive for y between 0 and 10, the sign of the second derivative rides on (1 - y/5), positive below y = 5 and negative above it. The inflection level is 5, exactly half the capacity 10, confirming from the calculus side what the growth-rate argument claimed from the modeling side. Two independent routes to L/2 make the fact nearly impossible to forget and give you a justification in whichever dialect a prompt prefers.

Even a solved example repays the technique. The guide's curve y = the square root of (x squared + 3) satisfies dy/dx = x/y, and differentiating the quotient gives y double prime = (y - x times y prime) over y squared. Substituting y prime = x/y turns the numerator into y - x squared over y, which is (y squared - x squared) over y, so y double prime = (y squared - x squared) over y cubed. Along the solution, y squared - x squared is identically 3, so the second derivative is 3 over y cubed, positive on the upper branch everywhere. The branch is concave up across its whole domain, a fact the shallow bowl of the graph confirms and a fact obtainable without ever writing the solution's own derivative from scratch.

Exam prompts deploy this machinery in a standard costume: given the equation and a point on a solution, determine whether the solution is increasing and concave up or down there, or find the x value where the solution through a point has a local extremum or inflection. Each is a substitution exercise once the second derivative formula exists. The scoring rubric reserves its final point for the reasoning sentence, the one that says the concavity follows from the sign of the computed second derivative, so write that sentence every time, even when the arithmetic feels obvious.

## Verification: The Two-Minute Insurance Policy

Verification inverts the solving process, and the inversion is so much easier than the original that skipping it is pure waste. Given a candidate function, differentiate it, then substitute the candidate and its derivative into the equation and demand an identity. A second, separate check confirms the initial condition, because a function can satisfy an equation beautifully while passing through the wrong point, and the two checks guard different doors.

Run the policy on the separation section's answer, y = the square root of (2x cubed + 4), claimed to solve dy/dx = 3x squared over y with y(0) = 2. The chain rule gives the derivative as one half of (2x cubed + 4) raised to the negative one-half, times 6x squared, which simplifies to 3x squared divided by the square root of (2x cubed + 4). That denominator is y itself, so the derivative equals 3x squared over y, matching the equation exactly. The condition check is arithmetic: the square root of 4 is 2. Both doors guarded, the solution is certain, and the whole audit consumed less time than one anxious reread of the separation steps.

Multiple-choice questions turn verification into an elimination sport. Asked which option solves dy/dx = 2xy, you need not solve anything; differentiate each candidate and compare. The candidate e raised to (2x) fails instantly, since its derivative is 2e^(2x) while the equation demands 2x times the candidate. The candidate e raised to (x squared) survives, because its derivative is 2x e^(x squared), a perfect match. A still faster filter tests the initial condition first when one is given: candidates failing the point test die without any differentiation at all. Order the filters by cost, condition first, derivative second, and most such questions collapse in under a minute.

Verification also rehabilitates partial memory under pressure. A student unsure whether the growth solution carries e raised to kt or e raised to negative kt can settle the question by differentiating the guess and comparing against the model; the derivative never lies about the sign. A student unsure whether the constant multiplies or adds can test both skeletons against the equation and keep the survivor. In a unit where families differ by small decorations, the habit of testing decorations against the equation converts fragile recall into reliable derivation, and reliable derivation is what a timed exam actually measures.

## Matching Fields to Equations: A Worked Elimination

Suppose a field shows gentle segments near the bottom of the window growing steeper toward the top, identical across each horizontal row, and three candidate equations compete: dy/dx = x, dy/dx = y, and dy/dx = x + y. Probe the point (0, 1) first. The first candidate predicts slope 0 there, the other two predict 1. If the drawn segment at (0, 1) climbs at forty-five degrees, the first candidate dies. Probe (1, 0) next: the second candidate predicts 0, the third predicts 1. A climbing segment there kills the second, leaving x + y as the survivor, and the row repetition in the picture was a red herring, because along the visible window the sums happen to mimic stripes. Two probes, two funerals, one answer, and the method never required solving or sketching anything.

The same probe logic reads unfamiliar fields. A field whose segments all lean like a calm sea of shallow positives, steepening smoothly with height and identical across rows, announces an autonomous growth law such as dy/dt = 0.04y, where slope is a fixed percentage of height. A field flattening toward a middle row, with segments above that row leaning down and below it leaning up, announces an approach law like dy/dx = 2 - y/5, whose equilibrium y = 10 gathers solutions from both sides. Naming the family from the silhouette, growth, approach, bounded, or blow-up, usually answers the question before any point is probed, and probing then serves as confirmation rather than discovery.

Periodic fields form the last common silhouette. An equation like dy/dx = cos x depends on x alone, so its columns repeat every full period, and solutions are vertical shifts of the sine curve, rising through the bands where cosine is positive and sagging where it turns negative. A solution traced through such a field waves without growing, a behavior no autonomous equation in the unit can imitate, since autonomous rows cannot alternate in time. When a field's pattern varies along the horizontal direction, some x dependence is guaranteed; when it varies along the vertical, some y dependence is guaranteed; when it varies along diagonals, both variables are at work. That three-way diagnostic, applied in the first ten seconds of looking, sorts nearly every matching problem the course can pose.

## After Solving: Inverse Questions and Rate Questions

Solving the equation is rarely the last instruction. Two follow-up shapes recur, and each has a mechanical answer once recognized. The first shape asks when: at what time does the population reach a stated level, when does the liquid cool to a stated temperature, when does the tank hold a stated amount? Set the solution equal to the target and peel the exponential with a logarithm. The growth colony P(t) = 250e^(0.04t) reaches 500 when e^(0.04t) equals 2, at t = ln 2 over 0.04, the doubling time of about 17.33 returning in disguise. The cooling liquid T(t) = 70 + 130e^(-0.05t) reaches 100 degrees when the exponential factor equals 30/130, which is 3/13, giving t = ln(13/3) over 0.05, about 29.3 minutes. The salt tank reaches 100 pounds when e^(-0.03t) equals one half, at t = ln 2 over 0.03, about 23.1 minutes. Three stories, one skeleton: isolate the exponential, take the logarithm, divide by the rate constant, and sanity-check the sign, since a negative time answer announces a setup error rather than a paradox.

The second shape asks how fast at a stated amount rather than at a stated time, and it ambushes students who reach for the solution formula out of habit. The equation itself answers directly: substitute the amount into the rate law. For the rumor law or the growth law dP/dt = 0.04P, the rate when the population stands at 500 is 0.04 times 500, or 20 per unit time, regardless of when the population passes 500. For the tank law dA/dt = 6 - 0.03A, the rate when the tank holds 100 pounds is 6 - 3, or 3 pounds per minute. No solving, no logarithms, one substitution. Recognizing which shape you are facing, when versus how fast at an amount, is worth a full question's points across a whole exam, because the two shapes sit side by side in the same problem set wearing similar wording.

Justification sentences convert these computations into earned credit. The rubric's favorite sentence names a value, attaches units, and states a meaning: at t = 10 minutes the salt is entering at a net rate of about 3 pounds per minute, so the amount is increasing. Template the sentence in your head before the exam, quantity, value, units, direction, and the interpretation parts of any modeling question become transcription. The [AP Calculus AB free response strategy](/2008/08/01/ap-calculus-ab-frq-strategy/) guide develops this sentence-level approach for the whole exam, and differential equations reward it more than most units because so many of their subquestions are interpretive by design.

## Anatomy of a Free-Response Question

A composite problem shows how the unit's strands braid into one scored performance. Consider the equation dy/dx = x(y - 2), a product form with an equilibrium at y = 2 and slopes that vanish along the vertical line x = 0 as well. A typical opening part asks for the slope at a few named points and segments on a printed grid. The arithmetic is quick: at (0, 3) the slope is 0 times 1, which is 0; at (1, 3) it is 1; at (1, 1) it is -1; at (-2, 4) it is -4. Segments at those points must sit centered on the lattice points with those tilts, and the zeros along the y-axis mean the whole central column lies flat, a feature graders check at a glance.

A second part typically asks for the particular solution through (0, 3), and separation handles it: dy over (y - 2) equals x dx, integrating gives the logarithm of the absolute value of (y - 2) equal to x squared over 2 plus a constant, and exponentiating gives y - 2 = Ce^(x squared over 2). The condition forces C = 1, since at x = 0 the exponential is 1 and y - 2 is 1. The solution y = 2 + e^(x squared over 2) sits above the equilibrium forever, growing with gathering speed, and at x = 1 it takes the value 2 + e raised to 0.5, about 3.649. Note how the initial value above the equilibrium selected the positive sign for the constant; a start below 2 would have chosen the mirror family member, sliding toward negative values as the exponential swells.

A third part might ask for an Euler estimate of that same value with step 0.5, and the contrast is the lesson. Starting at (0, 3), the slope is 0, so the first step holds the height at 3 through x = 0.5, a suspicious stillness that the true curve, already bending upward, does not share. At (0.5, 3) the slope is 0.5 times 1, which is 0.5, so the second step climbs to 3 + 0.5 times 0.5, or 3.25, at x = 1. Against the exact 3.649, the estimate runs low, and the reason is structural: the walk's first step used a slope of zero while the curve's slope grew throughout the interval, so the approximation trailed from the opening move. Naming that reason, grounded in the growing slopes visible in the equation, earns the justification point that pure arithmetic cannot.

The composite teaches the exam's rhythm. Pictures first, because they are fast and they orient the algebra. Algebra second, with the constant honored and the branch chosen by the condition. Numerics third, with the table discipline that keeps steps honest. Interpretation woven through everything, because the rubric pays for meaning at every stage. Practicing problems as composites rather than as isolated skills mirrors the scoring and exposes the seams where points leak, which are almost always the transitions: a field sketched but not used, a solution found but not checked against the picture, an estimate computed but not compared with the exact value sitting one part earlier.

## Mistakes That Cost Points, and the Habits That Replace Them

The error census for this unit is short and stable, which makes it conquerable. Dropping the constant of integration leads the census, because every subsequent line inherits the omission and the particular solution becomes unreachable. The replacement habit is ceremonial: the moment an integral sign resolves, write the constant before anything else, even before simplifying. Mishandling the absolute value inside the logarithm runs second; students either keep the bars forever and split into cases unnecessarily, or discard them and lose the negative branch. The condition decides the sign, once, at the end, and that timing is the habit.

Separation performed on an unseparated equation ranks third, the term-by-term integration of a sum that was never factored. The two-second scan for stray variables, described earlier, is the vaccine. Fourth comes the branch error, reporting the positive root for a solution whose initial value is negative, preventable by glancing at the condition before extracting any root. Fifth is the Euler slip of reusing a stale slope, preventable by the four-column table. Sixth is interpreting a rate at a stated time when the question asked at a stated amount, preventable by circling the givens and asking which variable each number describes.

Calculator discipline deserves a sentence of its own, since this unit's exam sections split between no-calculator and calculator-active parts. Separation, fields, and Euler tables live mostly in the no-calculator world, where exact forms like the square root of 7 are final answers and decimal approximations are neither needed nor wanted. Growth evaluations with exponentials live in the calculator world, where the discipline is to store intermediate values rather than retyping rounded ones, because chained rounding quietly moves a final answer outside the tolerance band. The [AP Exams complete guide](/2008/11/08/ap-exams-complete-guide/) maps which skills the two sections emphasize across the whole test, and this unit follows the general rule: structure by hand, arithmetic by machine, meaning by sentence.

Preparation for the unit should therefore spiral through the four representations in rotation: equation to field, field to solution sketch, sketch to formula, formula back to story. A student who can start at any corner of that square and reach the other three owns the unit in the way the exam measures ownership. The questions below rehearse the corners one at a time.

## A Circuit of Worked Examples

Four problems, four different muscles. The first hides a line inside a rational law. Solve dy/dx = y/(x + 1) with y(0) = 2. Separation gives dy/y = dx/(x + 1), and integrating produces the logarithm of the absolute value of y equal to the logarithm of the absolute value of (x + 1), plus a constant. Exponentiating turns the pair of logarithms into a proportion: y = C(x + 1). The condition y(0) = 2 fixes C = 2, so the solution is y = 2x + 2, an ordinary straight line. Verification is almost comic in its brevity: the derivative is 2, and y divided by (x + 1) is (2x + 2)/(x + 1), which is 2. Transcendental machinery, polynomial answer; the unit delights in such collapses, and students who verify catch them with pleasure instead of suspicion.

The second problem builds a model from two observations, the standard empirical move. A culture counts 300 cells at the start and 900 after 5 hours, growing proportionally to its size. The model P(t) = 300e^(kt) must satisfy 900 = 300e^(5k), so e^(5k) = 3 and k = ln 3 over 5, about 0.2197 per hour. Prediction at hour 12 uses the same law: P(12) = 300e^(12k), and since 12k is 12 ln 3 over 5, the value is 300 times 3 raised to the 2.4 power. That power is e raised to about 2.6367, roughly 13.97, so the prediction lands near 4,190 cells. The method generalizes to any two points: the ratio of amounts, logged, divided by the elapsed time, is always k, and every later question about the model is arithmetic on that one recovered constant.

The third problem recovers a cooling constant from data, then spends it. Coffee at 180 degrees sits in a 70-degree room and registers 150 degrees after 10 minutes. The gap law says the gap follows 110e^(kt), since the initial gap is 110. The observation gives 80 = 110e^(10k), so e^(10k) = 8/11 and k = ln(8/11) over 10, about -0.0318 per minute. Thirty minutes in, the gap is 110 times (8/11) cubed, because tripling the time cubes the ten-minute factor: (8/11) cubed is 512/1331, about 0.3847, the gap is about 42.3 degrees, and the temperature is about 112.3 degrees. Notice the elegance the problem is testing: equal time chunks multiply the gap by equal factors, the heartbeat of every exponential process, visible here without a single new logarithm.

The fourth problem exercises implicit patience. Solve dy/dx = x squared over y squared with y(0) = 1. Separation gives y squared dy = x squared dx, integration gives y cubed over 3 equal to x cubed over 3 plus a constant, and clearing the thirds gives y cubed = x cubed + C. The condition sets C = 1, so y cubed = x cubed + 1 and y is the cube root of (x cubed + 1). At x = 2 the value is the cube root of 9, about 2.080. The radicand x cubed + 1 vanishes at x = -1, and the derivative misbehaves there, so the honest domain is x greater than -1, the interval containing the initial point where the formula stays a smooth function. Each problem in the circuit ends with its domain spoken aloud; make that your reflex too.

## Constructing One Field Completely

Abstract talk about fields sharpens when one field gets built from nothing, row by row. Take dy/dx = y - 2 on the grid where both coordinates run through -2, -1, 0, 1, and 2. Because the equation ignores x, five columns of work collapse into five rows. Along y = 2 every slope is 0, so that row is a shelf of flat segments. Along y = 1 every slope is -1, a row of diagonals leaning down at forty-five degrees, and y = 0 doubles the steepness to -2, segments leaning harder. Above the shelf, y = 3 would give slope 1 and y = 4 slope 2, rows leaning up with growing confidence. The shelf at y = 2 is an equilibrium, and the field shows its temperament immediately: segments above tilt away upward, segments below tilt away downward, so the shelf repels. It is an unstable equilibrium, a pencil balanced on its point.

Trace two solutions to feel the repulsion. The curve through (0, 3) starts on a slope-1 segment, climbs, and as its height grows its slope grows, bending it ever upward; separation names it y = 2 + e^x, fleeing to infinity. The curve through (0, 1) starts on a slope of -1, descends, and steepens as it falls; its formula is y = 2 - e^x, diving without bound. Between them, the shelf solution y = 2 runs flat forever, a hairline separating destinies. One picture has taught what three paragraphs of sign analysis struggle to say: equilibria come in temperaments, and the field announces the temperament before any formula confirms it.

## Stability: Which Equilibria Gather and Which Disperse

A sign chart converts the picture into a verdict for any autonomous equation. Take dy/dx = (y - 2)(y + 5) and test one height per region. At y = 3 the factors are 1 and 8, the rate positive, so solutions there rise away from 2. At y = 0 the factors are -2 and 5, the rate negative, so solutions sink away from 2 and toward -5. At y = -6 the factors are -8 and -1, the rate positive, so solutions climb toward -5. Reading the arrows, y = -5 gathers solutions from both sides and earns the name stable, while y = 2 sheds them on both sides and is unstable. A population governed by this law persists at the stable level after small disturbances and abandons the unstable one after any nudge at all.

The logistic equilibrium at capacity is stable in the same arrow sense, which is why the model predicts settling rather than oscillation. The approach law's target is stable too; gaps decay because the arrows on both sides point home. The growth law's zero equilibrium is unstable, since any positive seed grows away, and that instability is precisely the content of exponential growth: zero is a place populations leave, not a place they keep. Exam interpretation questions often ask exactly this, whether a described equilibrium attracts or repels, and the sign chart is the whole method: sample each region, note the direction of flow, and read the verdict off the arrows.

A harvested population ties the ideas into one judgment. The law dP/dt = 0.3P - 45 describes growth at thirty percent balanced against a fixed harvest of 45 per year. The equilibrium sits where 0.3P = 45, at P = 150. Above 150 the growth outearns the harvest and the population rises; below it the harvest wins and decline accelerates toward extinction, so 150 is an unstable threshold, the minimum viable stock. Solving confirms the drama: P(t) = 150 + Ce^(0.3t), and a start of 100 gives P(t) = 150 - 50e^(0.3t), which reaches zero when e^(0.3t) = 3, at t = ln 3 over 0.3, about 3.66 years. Fisheries science is this paragraph with boats; the mathematics of the threshold is identical, and an interpretation answer that names the threshold and its instability has said the scientifically important thing.

## When the Picture and the Formula Grade Each Other

The healthiest habit in the unit is cross-examination: let the slope field audit the algebra, and let the algebra audit the sketch. Suppose separation on dy/dx = x(y - 2) produced the candidate y = 2 - e^(x squared over 2) for the condition y(0) = 3. The field objects immediately, since at the origin the equation demands slope 0, while the candidate's derivative there, the negative of x times the exponential evaluated at zero, is also 0, so far so good; but at (1, 3) the equation demands slope 1 while the candidate's height at x = 1 is 2 - e^0.5, about 0.35, nowhere near the starting region the condition requires. The condition check, y(0) = 2 - 1 = 1 rather than 3, exposes the wrong constant without any field work. Two cheap audits, either sufficient, and the correct member y = 2 + e^(x squared over 2) stands.

Run the audit in the other direction as well. A sketched solution that crosses an equilibrium row, bends against the segments, or levels where the field steepens is wrong no matter how confident the pencil felt, and the fix is always local: find the first point where sketch and segments quarrel and redraw from there. In timed conditions this cross-examination costs under a minute and rescues the most losable points in the unit, because field parts and algebra parts of the same question are graded independently, and each can betray an error in the other while there is still time to repair both.

## Interpretation Drill: Three Small Prompts

Fluency shows in miniature. First prompt: a reservoir's volume obeys dV/dt = 12 - 0.4V, with V in millions of liters and t in days. Interpret the terms. The 12 is a steady inflow of 12 million liters per day; the term 0.4V is an outflow consuming forty percent of the stored volume daily. The equilibrium is V = 30, stable, since a fuller reservoir drains harder and an emptier one fills on net. Starting at V = 20, the volume rises toward 30 along V(t) = 30 - 10e^(-0.4t), and the rate at the start is 12 - 8, or 4 million liters per day, a number obtainable from the equation alone without the solution.

Second prompt: a rumor spreads through a school of 1,200 students according to dN/dt = 0.6N(1 - N/1200). Identify the structure. The capacity is 1,200, the whole school; growth runs fastest at N = 600 knowers, at a rate of 0.6 times 600 times one half, which is 180 students per day. Early on, with N small, the spread mimics exponential growth at rate 0.6, and the model's realism lives in the braking factor as the pool of ignorant students drains. One sentence per fact, each fact read straight off the equation: that is the interpretation genre at full marks.

Third prompt: the amount of a pollutant obeys dA/dt = -0.08A + 2, a cleanup proportional to the amount fighting a steady industrial discharge of 2 units per year. The equilibrium solves 0.08A = 2, giving A = 25 units, stable, and a site starting cleaner than 25 units dirties toward it while a dirtier site cleans toward it. The moral the equation carries, that proportional cleanup cannot beat a constant source past the balance point, is exactly the kind of sentence interpretation rubrics exist to reward.

## Slope Fields of the Standard Families

Recognition speeds up when each family owns a remembered silhouette. The pure growth field, dy/dx = 0.5y, paints calm at the bottom and excitement at the top: along y = 0 the segments lie flat, along y = 2 they climb with slope 1, along y = 4 with slope 2, each row a steeper copy of the last, identical across columns because the law is autonomous. Solutions bend upward everywhere and flee the flat axis row, the visual form of instability at zero. The decay twin, dy/dx = -0.5y, mirrors the picture through the horizontal: rows above the axis lean down with steepening commitment, rows below lean up, and every solution glides toward the axis and flattens against it, stability drawn as a funnel.

The approach field, dy/dx = 3 - 0.5y, adds a twist worth studying, because its slope ignores x yet changes sign at the row y = 6. Below that row the segments climb, more gently as height increases: slope 3 at the axis, slope 2 at y = 2, slope 1 at y = 4. Above the row they descend, slope -1 at y = 8 and -2 at y = 10. Solutions funnel toward y = 6 from both directions and flatten there, the portrait of a stable target, and unlike pure decay the interesting row floats above the axis, wherever the balance of the constant inflow and proportional outflow places it.

The product field dy/dx = xy breaks the stripe habit entirely. Slopes vanish along both axes, where either factor dies, and the plane splits into four quadrants of alternating sign: positive in the first and third quadrants, where the coordinates share a sign, and negative in the second and fourth. Segments steepen as points move away from the origin along any ray, so solutions hug the axes cautiously near the center and then whip away. A solution through (1, 1) climbs with growing speed; a solution through (1, -1) plunges; the field's pinwheel symmetry is the equation's signature, and a matching question offering one striped field and one pinwheel is decided by checking a single off-axis point.

The logistic field completes the gallery with its two flat rows. Segments lie flat along P = 0 and along the capacity row, tilt gently in the shallows near each, and stand steepest along the middle row at half capacity. Solutions traced from small starts thread the whole drama: reluctant rise, confident surge through the steep band, reluctant settle against the ceiling. Set the five silhouettes side by side, funnel down, funnel to a shelf, pinwheel, single fleeing floor, double-shelved S, and most sketch-and-match questions reduce to choosing which silhouette the printed segments are already tracing.

## Particular Solutions Wearing Context

A dosage problem shows decay doing clinical work. A patient absorbs 250 milligrams of a drug that the body clears at a rate proportional to the amount present, with constant 0.3 per hour. The law dA/dt = -0.3A with A(0) = 250 solves to A(t) = 250e^(-0.3t). Clinicians asking when the level falls below 10 milligrams pose an inverse question: set 10 = 250e^(-0.3t), so e^(-0.3t) = 0.04, take logarithms, and t = ln 25 over 0.3, about 10.73 hours. The half-life of the drug in this patient is ln 2 over 0.3, about 2.31 hours, and the answer is a bit under five half-lives, a consistency check worth running aloud: after five half-lives the amount would be 250 divided by 32, just under 8 milligrams, bracketing the computed time from above.

A savings problem shows the approach family's arithmetic running in the growing direction. An account earns interest at 6 percent continuously while its owner deposits 1,200 per year, so dB/dt = 0.06B + 1200. Rewrite the law as 0.06 times (B + 20,000), exposing a shifted gap variable whose equilibrium sits at B = -20,000, a mathematical landmark rather than a reachable balance. The solution B(t) = -20,000 + Ce^(0.06t) with B(0) = 5,000 fixes C = 25,000, giving B(t) = 25,000e^(0.06t) - 20,000. At year 10 the exponential factor e^0.6 is about 1.8221, the first term is about 45,553, and the balance stands near 25,553, comfortably more than deposits alone would explain, since interest compounds on the deposits as they land. The negative equilibrium never troubles the story; it is the fulcrum the shifted exponential levers against, and recognizing shift structure converts many ugly setups into the gallery's calmest form.

Carbon dating converts decay into a clock. Living wood maintains a carbon-14 level that, after death, decays with a half-life of about 5,730 years. A sample measuring 60 percent of the living level satisfies 0.6 = (1/2) raised to t over 5730. Logarithms give t = 5730 times ln(1/0.6) over ln 2, and ln(1/0.6) over ln 2 is about 0.7370, so the sample's age is about 4,223 years. The same computation with 25 percent remaining would need no logarithms at all, since a quarter is two half-lives exactly, 11,460 years; data chosen near clean fractions is a kindness problem writers extend, and spotting it saves the logarithm entirely.

## Long-Term Behavior: Asking the Equation About Forever

Many prompts end by asking what happens eventually, and the families answer in distinct dialects. Exponential growth answers with explosion, decay with a fade toward zero that never completes, and the approach family with a settle onto its target. The draining tank broke ranks by finishing in finite time, its height hitting zero at t = 16 and the model retiring; blow-up solutions like y = 1/(1 - x) also end finitely, by escaping to infinity rather than by settling. Classifying an unfamiliar model's ending, explode, fade, settle, finish, or blow up, is a one-glance skill once the family's silhouette is known, and it is the skill long-term questions actually grade.

Monotonicity and bounds come from the same glance. For the product law dy/dx = (y - 2)(y + 5), a solution starting at y(0) = 0 lives in the region where the rate is negative, so it decreases; confined between the equilibria, it must approach the stable one at -5, since it cannot cross either boundary and cannot turn around inside a region of constant sign. A solution starting at 3 increases and, having no ceiling in its region, grows without bound. Piecewise answers of exactly this shape, one fate per starting region, are the unit's signature interpretation genre, and the sign chart is the complete technology for producing them.

## Vocabulary and Notation of the Unit

Precision with the unit's nouns prevents self-inflicted confusion. Every equation here is first order, meaning the highest derivative present is the first, and ordinary, meaning the unknown function depends on one variable. An initial value problem packages an equation with a condition, and its solution must satisfy both. The general solution is the family with its constant still free; a particular solution has the constant spent. A solution that separation loses, typically a constant equilibrium hiding at a zero of the factor you divided by, must be reported separately, since the family portrait is incomplete without it. Direction field and slope field name the same picture, and integral curve is the dignified synonym for a solution curve drawn in it.

Notation varies more than substance. Leibniz form dy/dx dominates modeling problems because the units ride visibly in the fraction. Prime notation y prime appears in shorter prompts. Function notation f prime of x equals some expression in x and f of x appears when the problem wants abstraction. All three say the same thing, and translating among them instantly is a small fluency with outsized returns, since a surprising number of errors are really misreadings of which letter is the input and which is the output in an unfamiliar costume.

## Multiple Choice Tactics Specific to This Unit

The unit's multiple-choice questions yield to four filters in sequence. First, the condition filter: when an initial value is given, substitute it into each candidate answer before any calculus, since at least one option usually fails the point test outright. Second, the derivative filter: differentiate the survivors and compare with the stated equation, the verification engine doing elimination work. Third, the probe filter for fields: evaluate the candidates at two or three discriminating points, the origin, an axis point, a sign-changing point, and let the drawn segments arbitrate. Fourth, the silhouette filter for behavior questions: name the family, growth, approach, bounded, product, and discard any option whose fate disagrees with the family's known ending.

Unit analysis quietly kills a fifth category of distractor. An interpretation option that names the wrong units, a rate in grams when the context measures grams per hour, or a constant of proportionality carrying no units when the story requires per-time units, cannot be right regardless of its arithmetic. Similarly, an Euler option that reports a value on the wrong side of the exact solution, when the concavity forces a definite direction of error, dies by the over-under rule without any recomputation. Writers plant these options for students who compute without reading; the filters are reading, systematized.

Time budgeting follows from the filters. A verification question should cost about a minute, a field-matching question about ninety seconds of probing, and a two-step Euler table about two minutes of careful arithmetic. Saving those minutes funds the free-response section, where this unit's questions are among the most generous on the test: the parts build on each other, the arithmetic stays gentle, and the justification sentences follow templates you can rehearse. Carry the templates in, spend the saved time on the transitions between parts, and the unit pays its full dividend.

## Separation in Slow Motion

One more derivation, narrated at the speed of thought, exposes the decision points that fast examples hide. Solve dy/dx = e^x times the square root of y, with y(0) = 4. The first decision is recognition: the right side factors into e^x, a pure x part, times the square root of y, a pure y part, so the equation is separable. The second decision is the move itself: dividing by the square root of y places y raised to the negative one-half beside dy, while e^x stays beside dx. Dividing demands a footnote, since y = 0 would be destroyed by the move; checking it against the original shows the derivative there would be zero and the right side also zero, so y = 0 is in fact an equilibrium solution that the family about to emerge will not contain, and it must be remembered separately.

Integration is the third decision, and both sides stay elementary: the left yields 2 times the square root of y, the right yields e^x, and the constant joins on the right. Now the fourth decision, the condition, spent at exactly the moment the constant exists: at x = 0 the left side is 2 times 2, which is 4, and the right side is 1 + C, so C = 3. The relation reads 2 times the square root of y = e^x + 3, and the fifth decision, extraction, divides by 2 and squares: y = ((e^x + 3)/2) squared. Verification, the sixth decision and the cheapest insurance in mathematics, differentiates the answer: the derivative is 2 times the quantity ((e^x + 3)/2) times (e^x over 2), which simplifies to the square root of y times e^x, precisely the stated law, and the value at zero returns 4 as required.

Notice how the lost equilibrium behaves. The family y = ((e^x + 3)/2) squared, and its relatives for other constants, all live strictly above zero, swelling exponentially. The solution y = 0 runs beneath them like a floor none of them touches, visible in the slope field as a flat row along the axis with every segment above it leaning up. A complete answer names both, the family and the floor. Exam rubrics occasionally hide a point inside that completeness, and the habit of asking what division destroyed, every single time, collects it.

## Growth Laws in Disguise

Proportionality wears costumes across the sciences, and recognizing the costume is faster than re-deriving the wardrobe. Light dimming through water or glass obeys dI/dx = -kI, intensity decaying with depth, the same exponential with distance playing time's role. Air pressure thins with altitude by a similar law at constant temperature. A falling object with drag approaches its terminal velocity under dv/dt = g - cv/m in physics notation, which is the approach family: acceleration fades as velocity nears the balance point where drag cancels gravity.

Run the falling body with friendly numbers: dv/dt = 10 - 0.2v, starting from rest. The terminal velocity solves 0.2v = 10, giving 50 units. Rewriting the law as -0.2 times (v - 50) exposes the gap variable, and the solution v(t) = 50(1 - e^(-0.2t)) starts at zero and leans toward the ceiling. At t = 5 the exponential is e raised to -1, about 0.3679, so the velocity is about 50 times 0.6321, roughly 31.6 units, a bit under two-thirds of terminal, matching the intuition that the approach is brisk early and patient late. Every approach story shares that personality: the gap decays exponentially, so half the remaining distance closes in each equal time slice, forever.

The disguises matter because exam contexts rotate through them while the mathematics stands still. A cooling probe, a discharging capacitor described in words, a drug infusion approaching steady state, a rumor nearing saturation in its exponential phase, all reduce to two questions: proportional to the amount, or proportional to the gap? Answer that, choose the sign, and the model is written. The rest is the machinery you already own.

## Testing a Model Against a Table

Data tables invite a different kind of question: not solving a given law but judging whether a law fits given numbers. Suppose a culture is counted at two-hour intervals, reading 80, then 108, then 146. If growth is exponential, equal time steps should multiply the count by equal factors. The first factor is 108 over 80, which is 1.35; the second is 146 over 108, about 1.3519. Agreement to within counting noise is the fingerprint of proportionality, and the per-hour growth constant follows from either step: 1.35 = e raised to 2k, so k = ln 1.35 over 2, about 0.1501 per hour. The model P(t) = 80e^(0.15t) then predicts the next count, at hour 6, as 146 times 1.35, roughly 197, and a measured value near 197 would deepen confidence while a value like 160 would indict the model and send the analysis hunting for a ceiling, a logistic brake beginning to bite.

When a table reports rates directly, the quotient test from the modeling section applies: divide each rate by its amount and look for constancy. When a table reports only amounts, average rates over intervals approximate the derivative at midpoints, and the same quotient test runs on those estimates with slightly dirtier hands. Either way, the intellectual move is constant: a differential equation is a claim about a relationship between a quantity and its rate, so data must exhibit that relationship, measurably, before the law earns belief. That stance, treating equations as testable claims rather than oracles, is the deepest thing the unit teaches, and it is why the course places this material last: everything before it was technique, and this is judgment.

## The Five Free-Response Archetypes

Past papers recycle five skeletons, and recognizing the skeleton under a new costume is the fastest preparation there is. The first archetype braids the strands: sketch segments for a given equation on a printed grid, draw the solution through a marked point, then solve the equation exactly and reconcile the two. Its grading weight sits at the seams, because the sketch grades the picture, the solution grades the algebra, and a closing part often asks for the value both methods estimate, quietly checking whether you notice they describe one curve.

The second archetype is the modeling narrative. A story arrives, water, wildlife, temperature, money, and the parts march in order: write the equation from the prose, solve it with the stated initial condition, evaluate at a later moment, and interpret a long-term consequence. A specimen spine runs through dW/dt = 5 - 0.25W for a tank's water weight, whose equilibrium of 20 tons and solution W(t) = 20 - 15e^(-0.25t) from a start of 5 tons answer every standard subquestion: the rate at the start is 5 - 1.25, or 3.75 tons per day; the weight at day 4 is 20 - 15e^(-1), about 14.48 tons; and the long-term fate is a settle onto 20 tons. Learn to see any new narrative as this spine with renamed characters.

The third archetype is the numerical braid: an Euler table for a few steps, the exact solution for comparison, and a verdict on the direction of error justified by concavity. The fourth is the investigation: no solving at all, only properties, where the solution through a point is increasing, where it bends, where it might level, each answer squeezed from the equation and its differentiated form. A taste of its algebra: from dy/dx = (x - 2)y, differentiation gives y double prime = y(1 + (x - 2) squared), so every solution bends upward where it runs positive and downward where it runs negative, an answer obtainable in two lines with no solution formula anywhere in sight.

The fifth archetype argues from data. A table of amounts or rates appears, and the prompt asks which model the data supports and why, or asks for the constant a stated model would need. Counts of 20, 26, and 34 at consecutive times make the case for exponential growth in one breath, since the factors 1.3 and about 1.3077 nearly agree, fixing k near ln 1.3, about 0.262, while a rival linear model would demand equal differences, which 6 and 8 plainly are not. Each archetype privileges a different section of this guide, and a balanced week of practice touches all five, because the exam will.

## Euler Revisited: Step Size as a Dial

The step-halving experiment deserves its own numbers, because the improvement pattern is itself examinable. Return to dy/dx = y with y(0) = 1 and estimate the value at x = 1 three ways. With a single step of width 1, the walk adds the starting slope once and reports 2, a crude guess. With two steps of width 0.5, each step multiplies the height by 1.5, giving 1.5 and then 2.25, an error of about 0.468 against the true e of 2.71828. With four steps of width 0.25, the earlier run produced 2.44140625, an error near 0.277. Each halving of the step cut the error substantially, though never to zero, and the ratio of successive errors hovering a little above one half is the fingerprint of a first-order method grinding toward the truth at a predictable rate.

Exam questions exploit the dial in both directions. A prompt may ask which of two estimates lies closer to the truth and accept the reasoning that the finer grid wins, or may ask for the value a stated step count produces and nothing more. A subtler prompt gives an estimate and a concavity and asks whether the truth sits above or below, which is the over-under rule again. What the exam never asks is for a hand computation with tiny steps; the step counts stay humane, two to four, because the concept being graded is the iteration, not stamina. Guard the bookkeeping accordingly: the product of step width and slope is the only arithmetic in the method, and writing it in its own column, step after step, is what separates a clean table from a numerological accident.

There is a kinship worth naming between Euler's method and the accumulation ideas from earlier in the course. Each step adds slope times width, a rectangle's worth of change, exactly the geometry of a Riemann sum applied to the derivative. The difference is the feedback: a Riemann sum reads slopes from a known function, while Euler reads them from points on the very curve being built, so errors compound as the walk proceeds. Seeing the kinship explains both the method's power and its drift, and it closes a loop the course opened when accumulation first appeared.

## The Printed Grid: Craft of the Sketching Parts

Sketching parts are graded by human eyes scanning for features, and knowing the features sharpens the pencil. Readers check whether segments at named points carry the right tilt, whether the flat rows and steep bands sit where the equation puts them, and whether a drawn solution respects the field everywhere it travels. A segment that is roughly right in tilt earns its point even if its length wanders, since length carries no information; uniform short segments remain the professional habit because they make tilt comparisons effortless for the reader and for you.

Draw solution curves with the same feature awareness. The curve must pass through the specified point exactly, not near it, because that point is the part's anchor and readers look there first. It must approach equilibria without touching them, cross regions in the direction segments lean, and exit the window still obeying the local tilt at the border. When the initial point sits between lattice rows, interpolate calmly: the curve threads between segments, borrowing tilt from both neighbors, and smoothness matters more than contact with any particular dash.

Estimates harvested from a sketch inherit a tolerance band, so report them with the humility the method deserves, an approximation sign and a sensible number of digits. A reading of about 2.4 from a field is a complete answer; a reading dressed as 2.4137 pretends to a precision the picture cannot supply, and experienced readers discount the pretense. The same humility applies backward: if your exact solution and your field estimate quarrel beyond the band's width, believe the quarrel, not either party, and audit both while the clock still allows. The picture and the formula grading each other, one final time, is the unit's whole philosophy in miniature.

## Boundary Cases: Where the Guarantees Bend

One famous specimen keeps thoughtful students humble about uniqueness. The equation dy/dx = the square root of y, with the condition y(0) = 0, admits the obvious solution y = 0, since both sides vanish everywhere along it. But separation on the assumption that y is positive gives 2 times the square root of y = x + C, and the condition would force C = 0, yielding y = x squared over 4, whose derivative x over 2 genuinely equals the square root of y wherever x is nonnegative. Worse, hybrid solutions exist that rest at zero until any chosen moment and then lift off along a shifted copy of that parabola. Infinitely many curves through one point obey one law, because the rate's sensitivity to y, the derivative of the square root, explodes at y = 0, breaking the smoothness the uniqueness guarantee quietly requires.

The course never asks you to manufacture such pathologies, yet knowing they exist clarifies the rules used everywhere else. Solution curves in well-behaved fields never cross because the rate law is smooth enough to enforce one future per point. Equilibria repel or attract but are never joined mid-flight for the same reason. When an exam equation involves roots of y or fractions with y in a denominator, the smoothness fails exactly at the interesting places, along the axis or along a horizontal line, and those are the places the questions probe: vertical tangents where a denominator dies, rest solutions hiding where a root vanishes, domains that end where the formula's own machinery jams. Read the breakdown points as information rather than as defects. They mark the borders of the model's competence, and naming them precisely is advanced interpretation, the kind that separates a strong paper from a merely correct one.

With the borders mapped, the unit's interior is fully charted: a small gallery of solvable forms, a picture language for the rest, a numerical walk when both fail, and a grammar for saying what the mathematics means. That is the complete toolkit, and the questions below now range across all of it.

## Two Reading Modes: Amount and Rate

Every question in the unit is secretly asking about one of two objects, the amount or the rate, and confusing them is the quietest way to lose a point you understood. Amount questions are answered by the solution: evaluate the function, solve it for a target time, or describe its long-term level. Rate questions are answered by the equation: substitute the given amount or moment into the rate law and report the derivative's value with its units. The wording signals the mode. Phrases like how much, how many, and when will it reach ask for amounts; phrases like how fast, at what rate, and is it increasing ask for rates. A hybrid phrasing, how fast is it growing when it reaches 500, still belongs to the rate mode, because the clause when it reaches names an amount to substitute into the law, not a time to hunt down with logarithms. Spend two seconds classifying each prompt before computing anything. That tiny pause, repeated across a whole paper, quietly returns several points that knowledge alone had already earned but haste was about to spend. Watch the two modes share one stage in the salt tank, whose amount after twenty minutes is 200 times (1 - e raised to -0.6), about 90.2 pounds, while its rate at that same moment, from the law dA/dt = 6 - 0.03A, is 6 minus 0.03 times 90.2, about 3.29 pounds per minute. Same story, same instant, two different questions wearing two different answers, one a stock and one a flow. The numbers even audit each other, because differentiating the amount formula returns 6e raised to -0.6, about 3.29, matching the rate read straight from the law. When the two modes agree like this, confidence is earned; when they disagree, the classification was wrong somewhere, and the disagreement itself tells you where to dig. Keep the distinction visible on your scratch paper by labeling every computed number with its kind, an amount in its units or a rate in its per-time units, so that a hurried final line never hands a flow to a question that asked for a stock, or the reverse.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What are differential equations on AP Calculus AB?

A differential equation is an equation that relates a function to one or more of its derivatives, so the unknown being sought is an entire function rather than a number. In the AB course every such equation is first order, involving only the first derivative, and typical specimens look like dy/dx = ky, dy/dx = x/y, or dP/dt = 6 - 0.03P. Read aloud, each states a law of change: the slope of the unknown curve at any point must equal the value the right side computes there. Solving means finding every function that obeys the law everywhere on some interval, and because a law of change constrains shape more than position, solutions arrive in families indexed by a constant. Pairing the equation with an initial condition, a single known point, turns the family into one particular solution and forms an initial value problem. The unit's whole skill set, separating variables, sketching slope fields, applying conditions, interpreting models, and approximating numerically, exists to move among these objects: law, family, member, and picture.

### Q: How do you solve a separable differential equation on AP Calculus AB?

Begin by factoring the right side into a factor depending only on x multiplied by a factor depending only on y, then move every y factor beside dy and every x factor beside dx. Integrate both sides at once, attach one constant to the result, and simplify. For dy/dx = x divided by y squared, the rearrangement gives y squared dy = x dx. Integration yields y cubed over 3 on one side and x squared over 2 plus a constant on the other, and clearing fractions produces the family y cubed = (3/2)x squared + C, with the constant renamed after scaling. If the problem supplies an initial condition, substitute it now to fix the constant; if it asks for an explicit answer, extract the root or logarithm carefully, honoring the sign the condition demands. Close by checking what the division discarded: any constant value that makes the y factor zero is a separate equilibrium solution the family may not include, and a complete response names it.

### Q: What is a slope field on AP Calculus AB?

A slope field is a picture of a differential equation drawn before anything gets solved. On a lattice of points spread across the plane, you evaluate the derivative the equation assigns at each point and draw a short line segment centered there with exactly that slope. The collection of segments sketches the terrain every solution must traverse, because any genuine solution curve passing through a point must run tangent to the segment waiting for it there. Fields expose structure that formulas conceal: rows that repeat reveal autonomy, flat rows reveal equilibria, and the tilt pattern forecasts whether solutions gather, flee, bend, or blow up. On the exam, slope fields appear as things to sketch on a given grid, things to read for estimates and long-term behavior, and things to match against candidate equations. They also serve as an audit for algebra, since a solved solution that fights the segments is wrong no matter how elegant its derivation felt.

### Q: How do you sketch a slope field on AP Calculus AB?

Work point by point and let arithmetic lead. For each lattice point on the printed grid, substitute its coordinates into the equation and compute the slope, keeping a small table if the grid is large. Then center a short segment on that point and tilt it to match: horizontal for slope zero, forty-five degrees for slope 1 or -1, steeper for larger magnitudes, and vertical where the slope is undefined. Keep segment lengths uniform so the eye reads tilt rather than length. Shortcuts appear once you notice structure: if the equation ignores x, compute one column and copy it across every row; if it ignores y, compute one row and copy it down; if equal slopes trace lines, as the sums in dy/dx = x + y do, compute along diagonals. Finish by checking a few segments against the equation's sign regions, because a single sign error repeated across a row is the classic way these sketches hemorrhage points.

### Q: How do you find a particular solution to a differential equation on AP Calculus AB?

First obtain the general solution with its constant still free, then let the initial condition spend that constant. For dy/dx = 4y, separation and exponentiation give the family y = Ce^(4x). The condition y(0) = 6 substitutes cleanly because e raised to zero is 1, leaving 6 = C, so the particular solution is y = 6e^(4x). When the condition sits at a nonzero input or the algebra is heavier, expect a small equation to solve: substitute the pair, isolate the constant, and carry its exact value into the final line rather than a rounded decimal. Two disciplines protect the result. Substitute only after the constant exists, since a condition applied during separation corrupts both sides. And when extraction involves roots or logarithms, let the condition choose the branch and the domain, because the family formula alone is silent about both. Stating the finished solution with its interval is the complete form of the answer.

### Q: What is exponential growth and decay on AP Calculus AB?

Exponential change is the behavior governed by dy/dt = ky, the law that the rate of change is proportional to the amount present, and its solution y = y0 times e raised to kt, where y0 is the starting amount. Positive k produces growth, and the quantity doubles every ln 2 over k time units regardless of where it starts. Negative k produces decay, described either by the same formula or by a half-life h in the form y = y0 times (1/2) raised to t over h, the two dialects linked by k = negative ln 2 over h. A substance with a five-hour half-life, for instance, carries k = negative ln 2 over 5, about -0.1386 per hour, and falls to one eighth of its start in fifteen hours, three halvings. Populations, balances, radioactive samples, drug levels, and cooling gaps all speak this language, which is why the model anchors the unit: separable in one breath, interpretable in one sentence, and endlessly reusable across contexts.

### Q: How do you interpret a differential equation in context on AP Calculus AB?

Interpretation means translating each symbol into the story's language, with units attached. Take dP/dt = 12 - 0.3P for a managed fish population measured in thousands, with t in years. The derivative itself is the net rate of change, in thousands of fish per year. The constant 12 is a stocking program adding twelve thousand fish annually, independent of the population. The term 0.3P is a harvest or mortality removing thirty percent of the standing population per year. Setting the rate to zero locates the equilibrium at P = 40 thousand, the level where stocking exactly balances removal, and the sign of the rate on either side shows populations below it recovering and populations above it declining toward it. Graders award these points for complete sentences that name a quantity, give its value or meaning, and state its units and direction. Practicing the translation both ways, symbols to story and story to symbols, turns interpretation parts into the most reliable points in the unit.

### Q: How do you use an initial condition on AP Calculus AB?

An initial condition is a known point on the solution, given as a function value at a stated input, and its job is to select one member from the general family by fixing the constant of integration. The mechanics are substitution and light algebra. For dy/dx = 2x with y(4) = 3, antidifferentiation gives the family y = x squared + C. Substituting the point gives 3 = 16 + C, so C = -13 and the particular solution is y = x squared - 13. Conditions arrive in costume inside word problems: a tank starting with 40 gallons means A(0) = 40, a sample of 12 grams at the start means m(0) = 12, and a census of 800 in the initial year means P(0) = 800. The condition also anchors the domain, since the solution lives on the interval containing the anchor point and bounded by the nearest place the equation or the solution breaks down. Apply the condition after integrating, never before.

### Q: How do you match a slope field to its differential equation on AP Calculus AB?

Matching is elimination by probing. Compute what each candidate equation predicts at a few strategic points and compare with the drawn segments. The origin is the cheapest probe, and points on each axis are nearly as cheap, because many candidates disagree there. Sign regions carry the next vote: the set of points where a candidate predicts positive slope must coincide with the region where segments climb. Zero-slope loci are decisive fingerprints, since each candidate flattens along its own curve: horizontal segments along y = -x for dy/dx = x + y, along the horizontal line y = 2 for dy/dx = y - 2, along the vertical line x = 0 for dy/dx = 2x. Structure offers the final check, because repeated rows mean the equation ignores x, repeated columns mean it ignores y, and vertical segments betray a vanishing denominator. Two or three probes usually leave one survivor, and the survivor should also pass a global smell test for growth direction and equilibria before you commit.

### Q: How do you set up a differential equation from a word problem on AP Calculus AB?

Start by naming the quantity, its units, and the independent variable, then translate the narrative's mechanisms one at a time into terms with matching units. A population that grows at a rate proportional to its size while a fixed harvest removes 200 individuals per year becomes dP/dt = 0.05P - 200 once the proportionality constant is known or requested symbolically. A tank receiving 5 gallons per minute of fresh water while mixture drains at 4 gallons per minute becomes a volume equation dV/dt = 5 - 4 = 1, or, if the question tracks dissolved salt instead, a balance of salt in minus salt out with the outflow term built from the concentration A over V. The reliable sequence is: define variables, list mechanisms, give each mechanism a signed term, and assemble rate in minus rate out for accumulation stories. Pair the equation with the initial condition the story implies, since most prompts grade the two together, and sanity-check the result by asking whether the sign of the rate makes physical sense at the starting amount.

### Q: What is the general solution to a differential equation on AP Calculus AB?

The general solution is the complete family of functions satisfying the equation, written with an arbitrary constant that sweeps out every member as it varies. For dy/dx = 5y the general solution is y = Ce^(5x), one curve for each real number C, stacked to fill the plane without overlap. The word general is a claim of completeness, so two checks defend it. The derivation must not have silently divided away an equilibrium: if the y factor can vanish, the constant sitting there is a solution in its own right and belongs in the report, sometimes inside the family, as C = 0 supplies y = 0 above, and sometimes outside it, when the zero belongs to a shifted factor like y - 3. The family must also be stated at the right level of explicitness, with y isolated when isolation is reasonable and left implicit when it is not. Imposing an initial condition converts the general solution into a particular one, and the pair of terms, general and particular, frames nearly every analytic prompt in the unit.

### Q: How do you verify a solution to a differential equation on AP Calculus AB?

Differentiate the candidate, substitute both the candidate and its derivative into the equation, and demand that the two sides come out identical. To verify that y = 1 + e raised to (x squared over 2) solves dy/dx = x(y - 1), compute the derivative: the chain rule gives x times e raised to (x squared over 2). The equation's right side, x times (y - 1), equals x times that same exponential, so the identity holds for every x and the candidate truly solves the equation. A proposed particular solution needs a second, independent check: the candidate must also pass through the given initial point, here y(0) = 1 + 1 = 2. Verification questions invert the usual direction of work and reward care over speed, because a single mismatched term anywhere ends the candidacy. The same routine doubles as self-audit after solving, and two minutes spent differentiating your own answer is the cheapest insurance available anywhere in the course.

### Q: How do differential equations model real situations on AP Calculus AB?

They model any situation where a sensible law governs the rate rather than the amount. Population biology supplies the archetype, with growth proportional to size when resources abound and the logistic brake when they do not. Physics contributes cooling, where a temperature gap decays proportionally to itself, and radioactive decay, where the disintegration rate tracks the surviving amount. Chemistry and medicine reuse decay for reaction rates and drug clearance, which is why half-life language crosses all three sciences freely. Engineering stories center on accumulation: tanks filling and draining, pollutants balancing discharge against proportional cleanup, reservoirs gaining steady inflow while leaking in proportion to depth. Finance runs the growth law with the sign of optimism, balances compounding continuously while deposits add a constant term. Even rumor spread and learning curves take the stage, modeled by logistic and approach laws respectively. The course returns to these contexts because the mathematics is one small family of equations, and fluency means seeing the same skeleton under many costumes.

### Q: How do you separate variables correctly on AP Calculus AB?

Correct separation is a checklist executed in order. Confirm the right side factors into an x part times a y part; if it is a sum that refuses to factor, stop and choose another tool. Move every y factor, including those hiding in denominators, to the dy side and every x factor to the dx side. Note any constant value that makes the y factor zero, since division is about to discard it and it may be an equilibrium solution owed separately. Integrate both sides in one step, writing exactly one constant. Keep absolute values inside logarithms until an initial condition or a sign argument releases them, then let the exponential absorb signs into a constant that may be positive, negative, or zero. For dy/dx = x times y squared, the routine gives dy over y squared = x dx, then negative 1 over y = x squared over 2 plus a constant, and finally y = negative 1 divided by (x squared over 2 + C). A final scan, confirming no stray variable sits on the wrong side, catches the classic blunder before it metastasizes.

### Q: What differential equation mistakes are common on AP Calculus AB?

The census of costly errors is short enough to memorize. Dropping the constant of integration leads, because the particular solution becomes unreachable from the moment the constant vanishes. Substituting an initial condition before integrating, or spending it and then forgetting which branch of a root it selected, corrupts otherwise clean work. Integrating an equation whose variables were never fully separated produces fluent nonsense, especially when a sum was treated as a product. Separation itself hides an error: dividing by a y factor without reporting the equilibrium at its zero. Slope fields suffer from segments drawn with the right tilt but centered carelessly, and from sketches of solutions that cross equilibria or other curves. Euler tables fail by recycling a stale slope instead of recomputing at the new point. Interpretation answers lose points for missing units and for answering with a rate when the prompt asked for an amount, or the reverse. Every entry on the list is a habit failure rather than a knowledge failure, which is good news: habits can be drilled.

### Q: How does Euler's method approximate a solution on AP Calculus AB?

Euler's method replaces the unknown curve with a chain of short tangent segments. Start at the known point, read the slope the equation assigns there, and advance one step: the new value equals the old value plus the step width times the slope at the old point. Recompute the slope at the new point and repeat until the target input is reached. With dy/dx = 1 + y, the condition y(0) = 0, and step width 0.5, the slope at the start is 1, so the first step gives y = 0.5 at x = 0.5. The slope there is 1.5, so the second step gives y = 0.5 + 0.5 times 1.5 = 1.25 at x = 1. The exact solution, y = e^x - 1, gives e - 1, about 1.718, so the estimate runs low, as it must: the solution is concave up throughout, and tangent lines trail beneath a concave-up curve. Smaller steps shrink the error roughly in proportion to the step width.

### Q: What is the logistic growth model on AP Calculus AB?

The logistic model describes growth that brakes as it approaches a carrying capacity, written dP/dt = kP(1 - P/L) with L the capacity. When P is small the parenthesis sits near 1 and growth mimics the exponential law at rate k. As P nears L the parenthesis strangles the rate toward zero, and the population settles onto the capacity without overshooting when it starts below. The rate itself peaks at half capacity, where P = L/2 and the growth rate equals kL/4, which is also the inflection point of the solution curve. For dP/dt = 0.5P(1 - P/400), the capacity is 400, the fastest growth occurs at P = 200, and its value is 0.5 times 200 times one half, or 50 per unit time. The solution takes the form P(t) = L divided by (1 + Ae^(-kt)) with A set by the initial population, tracing the S curve from slow start through steep middle to level maturity that the model is famous for.

### Q: Why does a particular solution carry a domain restriction on AP Calculus AB?

Because a solution is a function, and a function exists only where its formula and the equation both behave. Separation of dy/dx = y squared with y(0) = 1 produces y = 1/(1 - x), which detonates at x = 1; the solution lives on the interval x less than 1, the piece containing the initial point, and no continuation past the asymptote exists. Other restrictions come from the algebra: a square root solution requires its radicand to stay positive, and a logarithm arising from separation, like ln of the absolute value of x, confines the solution to one side of the singularity, so a condition anchored at a negative x yields a solution defined only for negative x. The equation itself can impose the wall, being undefined where a denominator vanishes. Graders treat the stated interval as part of the answer because two different domains genuinely give two different solutions wearing one formula. Always report the interval that contains the initial point and stops at the nearest breakdown.

### Q: How do you read values from a slope field on AP Calculus AB?

Place a pencil on the point whose value you know, then draw the unique curve that flows through the field tangent to every segment it meets, extending it smoothly in both directions if needed. To estimate the solution's value at some target x, follow your curve to the vertical line at that x and read off the height, accepting the modest imprecision the method carries. Slopes read directly from segment tilt: a segment leaning at forty-five degrees reports derivative 1, a flat one reports 0, and a steep negative lean reports a large negative derivative. Long-term readings come from watching where traced curves accumulate: flattening against a horizontal row signals an approach to equilibrium, while steepening flight out of the window signals unbounded growth or finite-time blow-up. Because solution curves never cross, the traced path is essentially forced once the starting point is fixed, which makes field reading more reliable than it first feels. Answers within a reasonable band earn full credit; the method grades judgment, not eyesight.

### Q: What does the constant of proportionality mean in a growth equation on AP Calculus AB?

In dy/dt = ky, the constant k is the fractional growth rate: the proportion of the current amount added per unit time. Its units are per time, per hour or per year, because it must convert an amount into an amount per unit time. A value of k = 0.07 per year means the quantity grows continuously at seven percent of itself annually, and the constant alone determines the clock of the process: the doubling time is ln 2 over k, about 9.9 years here, whatever the starting amount happens to be. Negative k describes proportional decay, with the half-life at ln 2 over the absolute value of k. Distinguish k from a percentage quoted for discrete compounding: continuous growth at k = 0.07 multiplies a quantity by e raised to 0.07, about 1.0725, over one year, slightly more than a flat seven percent. In richer models the same idea multiplies gaps and braking factors, but the meaning persists: k sets the tempo at which the present amount generates change.
