Almost everyone who writes about platform capitalism begins from an experience so ordinary that it barely registers as an experience at all. A person searches, scrolls, books, orders, messages, watches, or rides, and an intermediary that owns neither the goods being sold nor, very often, the labour being coordinated takes a share of the transaction or sells the attention the transaction gathered. The person doing the scrolling has usually heard two things said about this arrangement. The first is that the intermediary is a marvel of efficiency, a piece of neutral plumbing that matches supply with demand at a scale no earlier institution could manage. The second, heard from the critical side, is that the user is the real product and the real worker, labouring without wages to generate the data and the audiences from which the intermediary grows rich. Marxist analysis enters this conversation with an embarrassment that its popular summaries rarely admit. Marxists disagree with each other, sharply and on the record, about whether the scrolling person produces anything of economic significance at all.

That disagreement is the subject of this article, and it deserves to be stated as a disagreement before any side of it is defended. On one side stands an account in which user activity is labour, the value it creates is appropriated without payment, and the platform user belongs inside the theory of exploitation as an unwaged member of the workforce. Against it stands a rival account, holding that user activity, however useful to the intermediary and however carefully harvested, fails the test that Marx's own theory sets for value production, so that the money platforms earn must have been produced somewhere else and merely collected at a digital toll point. The distance between those two accounts is not a quarrel about tone or about how rude to be concerning large firms. It is a quarrel about what the concept of value requires, and therefore about what kind of income a platform earns, and therefore about whether the correct theoretical neighbour for the platform is the factory or the landed estate. Readers who grasp why the quarrel matters will find that most writing on the subject, celebratory and denunciatory alike, quietly assumes an answer to a question it never poses.

![A reading desk with platform type cards, a rent ledger and notes on the value dispute, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The question underneath the quarrel can be put plainly. Value, in the tradition this series examines, is not a synonym for usefulness, importance, or desirability. Plenty of indispensable things carry no value in the technical sense, beginning with the air in a reader's lungs. Value names a specific social form that labour takes when it is performed for exchange under conditions that discipline it, measure it against the labour of others, and compel it to meet a socially established standard of efficiency or perish. Whether scrolling, posting, rating, and watching meet that description is an analytical question with a right and a wrong answer, or at least with stronger and weaker answers, and the answer determines the classification of everything downstream. If users produce value, platform income resembles profit drawn from production, and users are exploited in the strict sense the theory reserves for producers of surplus value. If users do not produce value, platform income must be traced to surplus value created in the production of commodities elsewhere in the economy, and the platform's take resembles the rent a landowner collects for permitting access to something scarce that others need.

Neither answer is available cheaply. The first account has to explain how activity that nobody is compelled to perform at a socially necessary pace, activity whose withdrawal would inconvenience an advertising market rather than halt a production line, satisfies criteria the theory built for the factory floor. The second account has to explain why an activity so obviously harvested, measured, packaged, and sold leaves no value behind it, and it has to face the genuine grievance that gives the first account its moral force: people generate something an intermediary sells, and they receive none of the proceeds. A serious treatment owes both difficulties their full weight. This article therefore proceeds by giving each position its strongest form, attributed to the writers who actually hold it, before grading them against the criteria the tradition itself supplies. The grading standard throughout is internal. The question is never whether a claim sounds radical or sounds respectable. The question is whether the claim survives contact with what Marx's categories, carefully read, actually demand.

A definition should be fixed before the dispute begins, since loose definitions are how the dispute gets talked past. Platform capitalism denotes firms whose business model rests on owning and operating an intermediary infrastructure that connects other parties, extracting income from access to that infrastructure and from the data generated by its use. Every element of that sentence carries weight. The firm owns an infrastructure rather than a stock of commodities. The infrastructure connects parties who are not the firm: buyers with sellers, riders with drivers, advertisers with audiences, firms with computing capacity, households with one another's spare rooms and idle hours. The income arrives through the connection itself, as a fee, a commission, a subscription for access, or a payment for the attention and information the connection yields. And the data generated by use is not exhaust but asset, recorded because the record itself can be sold, or used to sell, or used to govern the parties the infrastructure connects. A firm matching that description may also employ thousands of people, own buildings, and run warehouses, and those activities belong to other parts of the tradition's analysis. The platform, as a form, is the owned junction and the toll charged at it.

One discipline governs everything that follows, and a reader is entitled to have it declared openly. This article names no company, no product, no service, no application, and no brand, anywhere, and it offers no figures for market share, revenue, user counts, workforce size, or earnings. That abstinence is not coyness. It follows from the kind of claim being made. An argument about mechanisms, about what value production requires and what a chokepoint extracts, either holds for the form as such or it holds for nothing. Naming a firm would invite the reader to grade the argument by their feelings about that firm, and quoting a revenue figure would invite the reader to mistake magnitude for explanation. Mechanisms outlast the firms that instantiate them. The intermediary forms examined here will consolidate, fragment, rename themselves, and be regulated in ways nobody can foresee, and an analysis tied to their present names and sizes would expire with the next annual report. An analysis tied to the structure of intermediation itself, to who owns the junction and on what basis income is drawn from it, remains standing when the names have changed beyond recognition. That is the wager of the structural method, and this article makes it deliberately.

## Platform capitalism and the question it actually poses

Strip the subject of its novelty vocabulary and a familiar shape emerges. Capital has always sought positions from which income can be drawn without organising production directly. The merchant who controlled the route between producer and market, the owner of the only bridge across a river on a trade road, the proprietor of the marketplace where exchange was permitted to happen, each occupied a junction and charged for passage. What the digital intermediary adds is not the junction but the junction's reach, its memory, and its capacity to govern the parties passing through it in fine detail. The analytical question the form poses is therefore double. First, what exactly does the owner of the junction own, and how did that asset come to be scarce enough to charge for? Second, from what fund is the charge ultimately paid? The first question concerns the asset: a network of users, a body of recorded behaviour, a standard others must adopt to reach the market, a reputation system that travellers through the junction cannot carry away with them when they leave. The second question concerns the income's source, and it is the harder and more interesting of the two, because answering it requires deciding where in the economy value is being produced and where it is merely being divided.

Most commentary answers the second question by pointing at the user. The user clicked, watched, posted, travelled, rated, and lingered, and the intermediary's income rose in step with those activities, so the user must be the income's producer. The inference feels like common sense and it powers a large literature. Yet the same inferential shape, applied elsewhere, would prove far too much. A theatre's income rises in step with the audience's arrival, but nobody supposes the audience produces the play's value by watching it, even though a performance without watchers is worthless. A toll bridge grows rich in step with the traffic crossing it, but the traffic does not produce the bridge's income in the sense that matters for classification; the traffic is the occasion for a charge levied on value earned elsewhere, in the wages and profits of those who need to cross. Proximity between an activity and an income establishes that the activity is a condition of the income. It does not establish that the activity is the income's source in the value theoretic sense, and the entire dispute examined in this article lives inside the gap between condition and source. Keeping that gap visible is the single most useful habit a reader can take from the pages ahead.

### Is data the new oil?

No, and the comparison misleads in both directions. A barrel of the older resource is consumed by use and must be extracted afresh, while a behavioural record can be copied, combined, and resold without depleting. The metaphor also directs attention to a raw material, when the analytical action lies in the owned infrastructure doing the gathering.

The metaphor deserves a slower burial than a short answer can give it, because its persistence reveals something about how the subject is usually discussed. Comparisons to extractive resources flatter the intermediary twice over. They suggest that the valuable thing simply lies in the ground of human behaviour waiting to be pumped, which naturalises the gathering apparatus and makes its ownership look like a reward for drilling rather than a legal and technical construction. And they suggest that whoever refines the crude material adds the value, which quietly assigns production to the intermediary's engineers and absolves the analyst from asking whether the underlying activity was production at all. A Marxist treatment should be suspicious of any metaphor that settles the classification question before it has been asked. Behavioural records are not a substance. They are a byproduct of participation in an owned environment, and their economic character depends entirely on the relations surrounding their capture: who must participate, on whose terms, who may inspect the record, and who is paid when the record changes hands. Ask those questions and the oil evaporates, leaving the junction, the toll, and the traffic exactly where the analysis needs them.

There is a further reason to begin with the dispute rather than with the firms. The platform debate is one of the few places where the labour theory of value is asked to do live work on an unfamiliar object, and the tradition's handling of it is a fair test of the theory's condition. A theory that can classify any income whatsoever as profit from exploited labour has not explained the platform; it has merely annexed it, at the cost of the distinctions that made the theory worth holding. A theory that refuses the annexation, that insists on asking whether the criteria for value production are met and accepts the answer when they are not, demonstrates the discipline that separates analysis from vocabulary. The stakes are therefore larger than the subject. How a reader learns to grade the platform dispute teaches them how to grade every claim in which a fashionable object is declared a new frontier of exploitation or a new mode of production. The criteria either bind or they do not. If they bind here, where the temptation to relax them is strongest, they bind anywhere.

The route through the article follows the logic of the dispute itself. A taxonomy of platform types comes first, because the value question receives a different answer depending on what a given platform intermediates, and arguing about platforms in general is the original sin of the literature. Then the tests for value production are set out from the tradition's own texts, stated carefully enough to be applied rather than merely quoted. Against those criteria the free labour account is presented at full strength, in the words and works of its serious proponents, followed by the rent reading that this article defends and by the evidence each side can genuinely claim. The labour question is treated separately, because the people who drive, deliver, clean, and care through platform coordination stand in a different relation to value than the people who merely use the same infrastructures, and conflating the two groups is the second original sin of the literature. Concentration and network effects follow, then the influential surveillance framing and the Marxist objection to it, then the question of what evidence would settle the dispute, stated as openly as the tradition's method requires. Throughout, the analysis links outward to the neighbouring treatments in this series: to the article on the labour theory of value for the criterion itself, and to the debate over rent versus profit for the classification of income that the platform case puts under pressure.

## The five types, and why the taxonomy comes first

Arguments about platforms fail in a predictable way: they select the type of platform that best fits the arguer's thesis, analyse it thoroughly, and then publish the conclusion as a finding about platforms in general. The writer who wishes to prove that users labour chooses an advertising funded service where user attention is plainly the commodity being sold. A writer bent on proving platforms are rentiers chooses a marketplace taking commissions from independent sellers. A writer determined to show that nothing has changed chooses a labour coordinating intermediary and points at the antiquity of piece rates. Each argument can be locally sound and globally worthless, because the object named in its title is not one object. A taxonomy is therefore not a preliminary courtesy in this subject. It is the difference between an analysis and a selection effect, and the most useful taxonomy available remains the one Nick Srnicek set out in Platform Capitalism, published in 2016, which distinguishes platforms by what they intermediate rather than by the industries they disturb or the fortunes they accumulate.

Srnicek's first type gathers the advertising platforms, intermediaries whose service to users is supplied without charge because the actual transaction happens elsewhere: the platform assembles an audience, records its behaviour in fine grain, and sells advertisers access to that audience sorted by predicted susceptibility. What is intermediated here is attention. The user receives communication, entertainment, or information; the advertiser receives a probability, refined by data, that a message will land on a receptive mind; the platform keeps the difference between the cost of assembling the audience and the price the probability commands. Srnicek's second type gathers the cloud platforms, owners of computational infrastructure rented to other firms by the hour or by the operation. What is intermediated here is capacity: storage, processing power, and the software environments in which other businesses run, supplied as a utility to firms that would rather rent computing than own it. The third type gathers the industrial platforms, which embed sensing, coordination, and data collection inside the production processes of manufacturing firms, intermediating between machines, components, and managers. The fourth type gathers the product platforms, which convert goods into services by retaining ownership of an asset, a vehicle fleet's worth of music catalogues or machinery or media, and selling access to it by subscription rather than selling the asset itself. The fifth type gathers the lean platforms, intermediaries that own almost nothing beyond the coordinating software itself while arranging for other people's labour and equipment to meet other people's demand: rides, deliveries, cleaning, care, short stays in spare rooms. Lean names the asset structure, not the moral character, though the two are often confused in commentary.

The taxonomy earns its keep the moment the value question is asked of each type separately, because the question changes shape as the intermediated object changes. Ask whether users produce value of an advertising platform and the question is at least intelligible, since the audience's attention is unmistakably what gets sold. Ask it of a cloud platform and the question dissolves, because the customer is a firm renting capacity, and the income looks like payment for the use of produced means of production, closer to the hire of machinery than to any audience puzzle. Ask it of a lean platform and the question splits in two, since the paying customer, the coordinated worker, and the platform stand in three distinct relations, and the worker's labour raises the classical questions of wages and surplus value in a form the user question never touches. Ask it of a product platform and the subscription resembles rent on an owned asset so closely that the interesting question becomes why anyone thought otherwise. Ask it of an industrial platform and attention shifts to the production process itself, where sensing and coordination may genuinely reorganise labour inside client factories. A single verdict about platforms and value, pronounced across all five types, is therefore not a bold generalisation. It is a category error with a confident tone, and the first service a Marxist analysis performs is to refuse it.

### What are the main types of platform, in one view?

Srnicek's taxonomy separates platforms by the object they intermediate: attention sold to advertisers, computing capacity rented to firms, coordination embedded within industrial production, owned assets converted into subscription access, and the matching of workers and equipment to demand by firms owning little beyond the coordinating software itself.

Beneath the five types sits a common skeleton, and naming it prevents the taxonomy from dissolving the subject into unrelated fragments. Every type owns an infrastructure that other parties need in order to reach each other or to reach a market. Every type records the traffic passing through that infrastructure and treats the record as an asset. Every type benefits from scale in a specific way: the junction becomes more useful to each participant as more participants gather at it, a dynamic examined in the discussion of network effects later in this article. And every type draws income from a position rather than from a product in the classical sense, which is precisely why the classification of that income, as profit or as rent, presses so insistently. The skeleton explains why the five types belong in one article despite their differences. The differences explain why the article's central question must be answered five times, once per type, with the answers assembled only at the end. The table that follows performs that assembly in compressed form. It is the article's findable artifact, the value question by platform type, and the prose sections after it exist to justify each cell.

| Platform type | What is intermediated | Where the income comes from | Profit or rent? | What would settle it |
| Advertising | Audience attention and behavioural records | Payments from advertisers for sorted access to audiences | Best analysed as rent on a chokepoint, drawn from surplus value produced in the advertised goods' production | Tracing whether advertiser payments behave as a cost deducted from surplus value elsewhere |
| Cloud | Computing capacity and software environments | Rental payments from firms for infrastructure use | Closest to profit on produced assets, with rent elements where standards lock tenants in | Whether returns track the value of the infrastructure or the scarcity of the position |
| Industrial | Sensing and coordination inside client production | Fees from manufacturing firms for embedded systems | Mixed: fees for a service that may raise client productivity, charged from client surplus | Whether the system reorganises client labour productively or merely monitors it |
| Product | Access to owned assets by subscription | Recurring payments from subscribers | Rent on owned assets, formally similar to hire and leasing income | Whether the asset's value or the gatekeeping position explains the payment level |
| Lean | Other people's labour and equipment matched to demand | Commissions withheld from payments between customers and workers | A toll on the junction, while the coordinated labour itself raises the classical wage question | Whether the commission behaves as a deduction from the worker's product or a fee on market access |

Read the table's fourth column slowly, because it contains the article's thesis in embryo. Only one type, the cloud platform, yields an answer in which profit on production plays the leading role, and even there the profit belongs to the production and maintenance of an enormous physical infrastructure of buildings, machines, cables, and employed technical labour, not to intermediation as such. Everywhere else the income leans toward rent: toward payment extracted because the owner controls access to something others need, a assembled audience, an owned asset, a standard, a junction between workers and customers. That lean is not an assumption smuggled into the table. It is the conclusion the following sections argue for, type by type and criterion by criterion, and the table states it in advance so that a reader can watch the argument being built and can identify exactly which cell they wish to contest. Contest is welcome and expected. What the table forbids is the lazy alternative, in which platform income is declared exploitation's newest form without anyone specifying which type, which fund, and which criterion the declaration rests upon.

A word is needed on what the taxonomy leaves out, because taxonomies mislead by their tidiness as well as by their gaps. Real firms combine types promiscuously: an advertising platform may rent cloud capacity, a lean platform may sell the behavioural records its coordination generates, a product platform may intermediate third party sellers alongside its own catalogue. The types are analytical, not corporate, and a given firm's income must be decomposed before it can be classified, stream by stream. This decomposition requirement is itself an analytical result worth underlining. Commentary that points at a single firm's total revenue and demands to know whether users produced it has already failed, because the total bundles infrastructure hire, subscription rent, advertising tolls, and commissions into one number that answers no question anyone should be asking. The Marxist habit of taking a revenue stream apart and asking of each part where it originates and what sustains it is older than the digital economy by a century and a half. It is the habit this subject most needs and least often receives.

The taxonomy also disciplines the political imagination in a way worth noticing before the argument proceeds. If the types differ, then the grievances differ, and the remedies differ, and a politics addressed to platforms in general will misfire in five directions at once. The user whose attention is sold, the firm locked into rented infrastructure, the factory whose production data flows to a supplier, the subscriber who will never own the goods they use, and the driver whose terms are set by an allocation rule they cannot inspect are not one subject with one complaint. They stand in different relations to different chokepoints, and some of those relations involve the production of value while others involve only its capture. The sections that follow keep those relations separate with deliberate stubbornness. Analysis that respects the distinctions may conclude by reuniting the types under the rent heading, as this article largely does. But the reunion will have been earned by passing through the differences rather than by ignoring them, and earned reunions are the only kind the tradition, at its best, has ever offered.

## What value production requires, stated as criteria

Every grading exercise needs its standard published in advance, and the standard here comes from the tradition's own account of what labour must be, and must undergo, before it counts as producing value. The account is developed across the opening chapters of the first volume of Capital and refined in the manuscripts on productive and unproductive labour, and its elements are more demanding than the platform literature usually acknowledges. Four criteria matter for the dispute, and each will be stated plainly, defended briefly, and then held in reserve until the competing accounts are brought before them. A reader who accepts the four criteria but rejects this article's verdict will at least know where the disagreement lives. A reader who rejects the criteria themselves is having a different argument, about the labour theory of value as such, and that argument belongs to the treatment at [labour theory of value explained](/2010/07/01/labour-theory-of-value-explained/), which owns it.

The first criterion is that value producing labour is performed for exchange. Labour expended on objects consumed by their maker, or given away within relations of kinship and custom, may be useful, skilled, exhausting, and indispensable, and still stand outside the value form entirely, because its product never confronts other products in a market where labours are compared and equalised. The second criterion follows from the first and does the heaviest work in this article: value producing labour is subject to competitive compulsion. Because products meet in exchange, producers are disciplined by a socially necessary labour time they do not set. A producer who takes twice the standard time to make a saleable object does not create twice the value; the market recognises only the standard, and the laggard is punished by diminished returns, by the loss of the market, and in the limiting case by ruin. This compulsion is not an incidental feature of capitalist production that a gentler capitalism might relax. It is the mechanism by which private labours are forcibly socialised, measured against one another, and made commensurable, and without it the category of value has no work to perform. Marx's phrase for the force involved, in a related context, speaks of the dull compulsion of economic relations, and the dullness is the point: the discipline operates without anyone needing to intend it, through prices, deadlines, and the standing threat of replacement.

The third requirement holds that value producing labour is organised, directly or through the market's mediation, as abstract labour: concrete activities of incomparable kinds rendered comparable as expenditures of human labour in general. The tailor and the teacher and the miner do not produce value because their activities resemble one another. They produce value because a social process abstracts from everything that distinguishes their activities and counts only duration measured against the social standard. The fourth criterion concerns the wage relation's characteristic position in the theory, and it must be stated carefully to avoid a common misunderstanding. Value production in the capitalist form centres on labour power purchased and set to work by capital, which then appropriates a product whose value exceeds the wage. Yet the criteria of compulsion and exchange are broader than the factory: an independent producer selling into a competitive market is likewise disciplined by socially necessary labour time, and the tradition has always treated such petty commodity production as value producing without any wage changing hands. What matters for the platform dispute is the discipline, not the employment contract. Keep that ordering fixed, because both sides of the dispute are tempted to invert it: defenders of the free labour account point to the absence of a wage as though unwaged status itself proved exploitation of a new kind, while the account's hasty critics treat the missing wage as though it settled the matter. Neither shortcut survives the criteria. The wage is evidence about a labour relation's form. The compulsion is the substance the form usually delivers, and it is the substance that must be looked for, in whatever dress, when a novel activity claims the status of value production.

### Why does the compulsion criterion matter more than effort?

Because value, in this theory, is a social measurement before it is anything else. Effort nobody measures against a rival's effort generates usefulness without the commensuration the concept exists to name. Exhaustion is a fact about a body; value is a fact about a system of exchange, and confusing the registers dissolves the theory's edge.

With the criteria on the table, some clarifications can prevent predictable misreadings. The criteria are not a moral test. Activity failing them is not thereby declared pleasant, voluntary in every meaningful sense, or free from domination, and the tradition's critics within this dispute are right to insist that a person can be manipulated, exhausted, surveilled, and used without being a producer of value. Domination is a wider category than exploitation in the technical sense, and a theory that reserves its sharpest term for one mechanism inside that wider field is not minimising the rest of the field. It is keeping its instruments distinguishable so that different mechanisms can be fought with different means. Nor are the criteria a test of whether an activity is necessary to a firm's income. Necessity is cheap: the rain is necessary to the harvest and produces no value, the audience is necessary to the performance, the road is necessary to the haulage firm. The criteria ask a narrower question, whether the activity is labour socialised through exchange under competitive discipline, and narrowness is the criteria's virtue rather than their defect. A concept of value that expands to cover everything a firm finds profitable explains the profitability of nothing, since the explanation would be the same in every case and therefore informative in none.

One more element of the standard must be recovered before the disputants arrive, because the rent reading depends on it: the tradition's distinction between the production of surplus value and its distribution. Surplus value is produced in one set of locations, where the tests are satisfied and labour is set to work beyond the time needed to reproduce its own cost. It is then distributed through a competitive and institutional process among claimants who produced none of it: commercial capital takes a share for circulating commodities, financial capital takes interest for advancing money, landowners take rent for access to scarce natural and locational advantages, and the state takes taxes for purposes ranging from infrastructure to war. The existence of a wealthy recipient therefore proves nothing, by itself, about production at the point of receipt. Fortunes gathered at distribution points can exceed fortunes gathered at production points, and the theory not only permits this but predicts it wherever a distribution point is scarce and well defended. The platform question, posed in the tradition's own grammar, is whether the intermediary's junction is a production point or a distribution point. Everything in the dispute reduces to that placement, and the placement is decided by the criteria, not by the size of the fortune gathered there or by the visibility of the activity that the junction harvests.

It is worth pausing on the landowner, because the analogy will carry weight later and should be introduced honestly, with its limits attached. Ground rent, in the classical analysis, is paid not because land produces value but because land is monopolised, scarce, and needed. The payment's level is set by the surplus generated in the production that the land permits, not by any labour the landowner performs. The landowner's contribution to the arrangement is purely positional: a legal title, enforced by the state, standing between producers and a condition of production they cannot do without. Critics of the rent reading of platforms will object, correctly, that a digital junction differs from a wheat field in being built rather than found, maintained rather than merely fenced, and improved by the traffic it hosts. Those differences are real and will be confronted in their place. What the analogy claims at this stage is narrower: that a payment for access to a monopolised condition of other people's economic activity is a known category in this tradition, with known mechanics, and that any account of platform income must explain why the category does not apply before it reaches for a new one. The burden of proof sits with novelty. Categories, like hypotheses, should not be multiplied beyond necessity, and the rent category is sitting there, fully tooled, waiting to be ruled out by evidence rather than by fashion.

## The free labour account, presented at full strength

Fairness in a dispute of this kind is a technical requirement, not a courtesy, because a verdict against a weakened opponent proves nothing about the strong one. The free labour account will therefore be built here as its best proponents build it, from its genuine insights, before a single objection is raised. Its lineage begins before the platform era, with Dallas Smythe's intervention of 1977, which argued that the mass media's real commodity was not the programme or the newspaper but the audience itself, assembled by content given away and sold to advertisers as a quantified capacity to attend and to buy. Smythe's move was to relocate the economic action of media from the visible product to the invisible one, and every later version of the free labour account is a descendant of that relocation. What broadcasting did with audiences measured in coarse demographic blocks, the platform does with individuals measured in behavioural detail, and the descendant argument claims the inheritance openly.

The account received its canonical name from Tiziana Terranova, whose essay on free labour, published in 2000, described the activities of internet users, building sites, writing, moderating, posting, participating, as simultaneously voluntarily given and unwaged, enjoyed and exploited. Terranova's formulation was subtle in ways her popularisers often lose. She did not claim that every click was factory labour under another name. She claimed that the digital economy had learned to extract value from activity that capital did not have to organise, command, or pay for, because the activity was produced by desires for expression, connection, and recognition that capital could channel rather than create. The phrase free labour carries a deliberate double sense: labour given freely, in both the volunteer's meaning and the price tag's meaning, and the doubleness is the argument. Capital found a way to make the boundary between production and social life porous, so that the stuff of ordinary sociability could be harvested as an input without ever passing through a labour market.

Christian Fuchs developed the account into a systematic theory across a body of work including Digital Labour and Karl Marx, published in 2014, and his version is the strongest because it is the most explicit about its theoretical commitments. On Fuchs's reading, the users of advertising funded platforms perform labour that creates the data commodity sold to advertisers; the wage paid for this labour is zero; therefore the rate of exploitation, surplus value divided by variable capital, tends toward the infinite, since the denominator vanishes while the numerator grows. The conclusion is meant to scandalise, and the scandal is the point: the platform user appears, on this accounting, as the most exploited worker in the history of the mode of production, contributing the entire product and receiving none of it, while classical factory labour at least bargains over the division of the working day. Fuchs extends the analysis along the whole chain of the digital economy, to the miners of the minerals inside devices, the assembly workers who build them, the software engineers who write the coordinating code, and the users whose activity crowns the edifice, treating the chain as one integrated labour process with radically unequal terms at each link. Whatever verdict the criteria eventually deliver, the chain picture permanently improved the subject, because it refuses to let the clean interface hide the mines and the factories standing behind it.

The account's attractions deserve enumeration in prose rather than dismissal in a clause. It explains, as the rent reading initially seems not to, why the activity matters to the firm: the posts, photographs, ratings, routes, and viewing histories are not decorative byproducts but the very material the firm refines and sells, and an account that calls the users mere conditions of income can sound like a conjuring trick performed on an obvious fact. It names a real asymmetry of compensation: value undeniably flows from the many to the few along channels the many built with their attention, and the language of exploitation gives that flow a moral grammar that the language of rent, with its echoes of landlords and inherited acres, may seem too archaic to supply. It captures something true about design: platforms are engineered to elicit activity, to prolong sessions, to provoke posting and reaction, and an environment engineered to elicit an input resembles a workplace organised to elicit labour more than it resembles a bridge waiting for traffic. And it honours the subjective texture of participation, the felt sense of performing for an audience, of maintaining a presence, of working at one's profile, which users themselves describe in labour's vocabulary without any theorist prompting them. A verdict that cannot absorb these attractions will persuade nobody who was not already persuaded, and the rent reading will be required, in its own section, to absorb as many of them as honesty permits.

### Are users the product, and does that make them workers?

The first half survives scrutiny better than the second. Advertisers do purchase access to audiences and their behavioural records, so the user population, packaged and sorted, functions as the thing sold. But being sold is not the position of the labour that produced a thing: a field's fertility is sold in every harvest without the field becoming a worker.

Now the objections, which are decisive in this article's judgement, and which arise from applying the criteria of the previous section rather than from any squeamishness about the account's radicalism. Begin with compulsion. The factory worker and the petty commodity producer alike labour under a discipline that sets the pace and standard of their work from outside: produce at the socially necessary rate or be displaced by someone, or some machine, that does. No corresponding discipline governs the user. A person may post daily or never, linger for hours or glance for seconds, produce material of dazzling quality or of none, and the variation carries no penalty remotely resembling the market's punishment of the slow producer. Platforms cajole, nudge, reward, and addict, and the engineering of attention is a serious subject in its own right. But cajoling is not the compulsion the value form requires, because the cajoled activity is not measured against rival performances of the same activity and equalised into abstract labour by exchange. There is no socially necessary labour time for a photograph caption, no market in which one user's evening of scrolling competes with another's and drives the recognised value of an evening down to the efficient standard. Where there is no such equalisation, there is, on the theory's own terms, nothing for the category of value to attach to. The free labour account does not dispute this so much as decline to need it, treating the absence of the criterion as evidence that the criterion is obsolete. That is a permissible theoretical move, but it should be recognised for what it is: not an application of Marx's value theory to a new object, but a revision of the theory in which the concept of value is loosened until it covers any activity a firm can monetise. The loosened concept then explains everything and distinguishes nothing, and the price of the revision is the theory itself.

The wage argument, pressed to its mathematical conclusion, reveals the same loosening from another angle. A rate of exploitation that tends to infinity because the wage is zero sounds like a discovery about platforms, but the arithmetic is available for any unwaged activity that a firm benefits from, including the customer's journey to the shop, the reader's attention to a billboard glimpsed from a train, and the word of mouth by which satisfied buyers recruit new ones. If all of these are infinitely exploited labour, the term exploitation has been stretched across so many unlike mechanisms that it can no longer tell a union organiser, a legislator, or a reader which mechanism is operating in a given case or what would change it. Fuchs's chain picture partly escapes this objection by embedding the user within a sequence of genuinely waged and coerced labours, the mine and the assembly line, where the criteria hold without strain. But the escape concedes the point at issue: the chain's links do not share one mechanism, and the user's link is held to the chain by analogy rather than by the value form. An honest cartography of the chain would label its links differently, waged exploitation here, coerced extraction there, rent bearing capture at the junction, and the map would lose none of the chain's horror by gaining the distinctions. It would gain the ability to say where the surplus is produced, which is the question the whole dispute exists to answer.

A further difficulty concerns what, exactly, the advertiser buys, and it takes the argument back to Smythe's original formulation with profit. The audience commodity, in broadcasting, was always a peculiar commodity: what changed hands was a prediction, statistically assembled, that certain people would be attending in certain numbers with certain dispositions. Advertisers did not purchase attention itself, which cannot be warehoused or delivered, but a warranted probability of exposure. The peculiarity matters because commodities in the value theoretic sense are products of labour whose value is determined before exchange by the labour socially necessary to produce them. A probability assembled from records of past behaviour fits that template awkwardly at best. The records are produced as byproducts of activities undertaken for other purposes entirely, social, expressive, erotic, bored, and the firm's labour, the waged work of engineers and analysts, is expended on the assembling, sorting, and warranting rather than on the behaviour recorded. On the criteria, that waged work may well be value relevant, though its classification raises the separate question of whether circulation activities produce value or merely realise it, a question the tradition debates in the parallel case treated at [logistics and value theory](/2021/01/01/logistics-and-value-theory/). The user's behaviour, by contrast, enters the process as raw material enters a mill: necessarily, valuably, and without the mill's output being the raw material's product in the value sense. Wheat does not produce flour's value by growing. It is produced upon, and the distinction between producing and being produced upon is exactly the distinction the free labour account must dissolve to stand.

None of this requires denying that something is taken from users without payment, and the strongest version of the objection to the free labour account never denied it. Data is taken. Attention is taken. The social graph, the map of who knows whom and who trusts whom, built by millions of people pursuing their own relationships, is taken and enclosed and made the foundation of a fortune. The question is what the taking is called, and the tradition is rich in names for takings that are not the extraction of surplus value from value producing labour: enclosure, appropriation, dispossession, rent. Marx's own writings on the original accumulation of capital describe takings of common resources by force and law that made later exploitation possible without themselves being exploitation in the technical sense. The user whose social life is enclosed inside an owned junction may stand closer to the commoner fenced off the common than to the spinner fenced into the mill, and that placement is not a demotion. The commoner's grievance was real, foundational, and world changing. It was also a grievance about access and ownership rather than about the working day, and confusing the two grievances produces movements that demand wages for scrolling when the coherent demand, on the commoner's model, is the junction's social ownership or its dissolution as private property. Classification is not pedantry here. Classification is strategy wearing its working clothes.

## The rent reading: income drawn at a chokepoint

The rent reading begins by refusing the question's usual framing. Instead of asking what users produce, it asks what the platform owns, why that ownership yields an income, and from which fund the income is ultimately paid. The answer to the first part has already been assembled: the platform owns a junction, an infrastructure through which parties must pass to reach one another, together with the records the passing generates and the standards the passing entrenches. The answer to the second part is that the junction is scarce in the way that matters for rent. Its scarcity is not natural, as a harbour's scarcity is natural, but it is no less effective for being manufactured. A junction that gathers most of a market's participants becomes, by that gathering, the only junction worth passing through, since a seller goes where the buyers are and a buyer goes where the sellers are, and each side's rational choice fortifies the other's captivity. Ownership of such a position yields income for the same reason ownership of the only bridge yields income: not because the owner adds a product to the world at the moment of crossing, but because the owner can charge for a passage that others cannot avoid. The third part of the answer identifies the fund. The charge is paid out of incomes generated elsewhere in the economy: out of the surplus value of firms that advertise or rent capacity or pay commissions, out of the wages of workers who surrender a portion of each payment to the junction that allocated their work, out of the revenues of subscribers. Platform income, on this reading, is a share of the surplus that commodity production across the economy generates, gathered at points where access to markets, audiences, infrastructure, and work has been enclosed inside private property.

Brett Christophers gave this reading its widest contemporary statement in Rentier Capitalism, published in 2020, arguing that the commanding heights of several economies are increasingly occupied not by firms that organise production but by owners of scarce assets, platforms prominent among them, whose income is rent in the strict sense of payment for access to what they monopolise. The platform literature's own founder supplies supporting evidence from an unexpected direction: Srnicek's analysis emphasises that platforms position themselves as intermediaries precisely in order to occupy the ground on which other economic activity happens, becoming the terrain rather than a competitor upon it, and terrain ownership is the rentier's classical posture. The reading also draws strength from a quarter the free labour account cannot easily reach, the observed behaviour of platform income itself. Rent, unlike profit on production, is limited not by the recipient's costs of production but by the payer's ability to pay and by the position's defensibility, and it tends to expand by tightening the position: raising commissions once dependence is established, degrading the free tier once the audience is captive, charging both sides of a market once both sides are locked in. Commentary on platforms returns obsessively to exactly these manoeuvres, describing them in the vocabulary of betrayal, of a service that was generous becoming grasping. The rent reading translates the moralised description into a mechanism. A junction owner whose income is rent has no stable interest in the quality of passage, only in the indispensability of the bridge and the toll the traffic can bear, and the observed arc from generosity to extraction is what rent maximisation looks like from the traffic's point of view.

### How can an income stream's character be tested?

Ask which force limits it. Profit on production is earned under competitive pressure forcing producers toward a shared standard, and rivals producing more cheaply can compete it away. Income drawn behind an unreplicable position, a gathered network or enclosed standard, persists because competition cannot reach it, and dependence, not efficiency, sets its ceiling.

The reading must now absorb the free labour account's strongest points, as promised, rather than leaving them as unexploded shells on the field. It absorbs the point about design first. Platforms are indeed engineered to elicit activity, and a bridge, the lazy analogy, is not. But the engineered elicitation is exactly what a rational junction owner does to deepen the position from which rent is drawn: more activity means a richer record, a more complete social graph, a standard more thoroughly entrenched, an audience more precisely sortable, and each improvement raises the toll the position can command from advertisers and firms. Elicitation is investment in the scarcity of the asset, comparable to a landowner draining a marsh to raise the rent of the surrounding acres, and the comparison clarifies rather than embarrasses the reading. The activity elicited is real, and its products are really appropriated. What the criteria deny is only that the appropriated product is value created by the activity in the technical sense, and the rent reading can afford the denial because it never needed the activity to be valueless in the ordinary sense. The record of a million people's friendships is enormously useful and commercially potent. So is a harbour. Usefulness was never the criterion.

It absorbs the compensation grievance second, by relocating rather than refusing it. Users receive no payment for what is taken from them, and the rent reading says the taking is real but its name is enclosure: a collectively produced resource, the web of social connection and the archive of collective behaviour, has been fenced into private ownership, and the fence, not a missing wage, is the injury. The distinction directs remedy. Wages for data, occasionally proposed as the fair settlement, would on this analysis be a category error with a price tag: a trivial payment legitimising the enclosure, converting a common into a market in which each person's social life is purchased for a pittance by the fence's owner. The coherent remedies for enclosure are the classical ones, common ownership, public provision, interoperability that breaks the fence's continuity, or the junction's regulation as a utility, and the rent reading's superiority shows itself in generating remedies that match the mechanism it diagnoses. A theory is not only a description. It is a compass for action, and a compass that points at wages when the injury is enclosure will march its bearers to the wrong destination with great moral confidence.

Third, the reading absorbs the chain. Fuchs's integrated picture of mineral extraction, assembly, engineering, and use remains standing under the rent reading, with its links relabelled. The miner and the assembly worker produce value and surplus value under compulsion as classical as any in the tradition's archive. The engineer occupies a contested position the tradition has debated since its own century, skilled wage labour whose product is a system rather than a commodity for sale. The user stands at the chain's end not as its most exploited link but as the population whose enclosure supplies the junction's scarcity, the crowd whose gathering makes the bridge the only bridge. Relabelling loses nothing of the chain's indictment and gains the ability to say which link bleeds value and which link collects it. A chain in which every link is called by one name can be denounced. A chain whose links are distinguished can be analysed, and analysis, unlike denunciation, can specify where the surplus originates and therefore where its redirection must begin.

The reading's fit with the tradition's own architecture should be made explicit, because it is the deepest argument in its favour. The rent reading is an application of the pattern this series has traced before, in the financialisation articles and in the [rent versus profit debate](/2021/02/15/rent-versus-profit-debate/): an income form that presents itself as the yield of a new kind of production turns out, on inspection of the criteria, to be a claim on surplus value produced elsewhere, advanced through a position of scarcity or privilege. Finance did not abolish that pattern when its instruments grew exotic, and platforms do not abolish it when their junctions grow digital. The tradition's most consistent contribution to the study of new economic forms is precisely this refusal to be dazzled: the insistence on asking where the value was produced before admiring where it was collected. A reader trained by that insistence will greet each successive innovation, each claim that data or attention or engagement has become the new substance of wealth, with the same unglamorous question, and will find, more often than the innovators promise, that the new substance is an old claim in new dress. The platform is the junction, the junction is enclosed, the toll is rent, and the traffic pays it out of value produced in the unglamorous places where labour still meets the criteria: the field, the factory, the warehouse, the kitchen, the ward, the mine.

One refinement protects the reading from its own overreach, and stating it keeps the reading honest. Not every pound or dollar a platform firm receives is rent, because platform firms are conglomerates of activities, and decomposition, demanded earlier against the free labour account, is demanded here with equal force. The firm that rents computing capacity owns produced infrastructure maintained by waged labour, and its income includes a profit component on that production alongside any rent its standards extract. The firm that employs drivers directly, where it does, stands in a classical wage relation to them. The firm that sells its own goods through its own junction collects merchant's profit on circulation as well as rent on the junction. The rent reading is a claim about the income attributable to intermediation as such, to the owned position between parties, and it is strongest exactly where intermediation is purest: the advertising toll on an assembled audience, the commission on other people's labour and assets, the subscription for access to a catalogue the subscriber will never own. Precision about scope is what distinguishes a reading from a slogan, and the slogan version, platforms are rentiers, full stop, should be refused as firmly as the slogan it replaces. The claim is that the distinctively platform element in platform income, the element that explains the form's characteristic fortunes and its characteristic manoeuvres, is rent. The rest is older businesses wearing the junction as a coat.

## Advertising, audiences, and where the money is actually made

The advertising platform is the free labour account's home ground, and the rent reading should be tested there at its most exposed point rather than in friendlier territory. Follow the money with the slowness the subject deserves. An advertiser pays the platform for the delivery of messages to a sorted audience. The advertiser's payment is not made from sentiment; it is a cost of selling commodities, advanced in the expectation that the commodities will sell at prices recovering the cost together with a profit. In the tradition's classification, expenditure on selling belongs to the costs of circulation, and circulation costs are paid out of surplus value produced in production. The merchant who rents a shop, hires a sales force, and buys advertising does not thereby produce new value; the merchant's operations realise value already embodied in commodities and are compensated by a deduction from the total surplus. Advertising expenditure is a slice of that deduction, competed for by every medium that can assemble attention. When the platform receives the advertiser's payment, it receives a transfer within the distribution of surplus value, routed through the advertiser's circulation budget. The user's attention is the occasion and the condition of the transfer, the thing the advertiser hopes the transfer buys. But the fund transferred was filled in the factories, farms, and logistics networks where the advertised commodities were produced by labour meeting every criterion the theory sets. Trace the payment to its source and the trail ends at production, not at the screen.

This tracing exercise is the rent reading's characteristic method, and its power becomes visible when the alternative is attempted. Trace the same payment on the free labour account and the trail ends at the user, whose activity is declared to be the payment's source. But then a second question arises that the account answers awkwardly: why does the payment's size track the advertiser's expected sales of commodities rather than any property of the user's activity itself? An hour of attention from a person likely to buy an expensive commodity commands a high price; an hour of identical attention from a person with nothing to spend commands almost nothing, though the activity, as activity, is indistinguishable. On the value account, identical labour performed under identical conditions should not yield wildly different values depending on the wealth of the audience the labourer happens to belong to. On the rent account, the variation is exactly what the theory predicts: the toll on a bridge depends on the value of the cargo crossing it, not on the bridge's exertions, and the price of access to an audience depends on the surplus the advertiser expects to realise through that audience. The price formation of attention, examined closely, behaves like a rent gradient and not like a wage bill, and price formation is evidence, harder to argue with than definitions because it is the market's own testimony about what is being bought.

### Why is rich attention worth more than poor attention?

Because the advertiser purchases not attention as a product of labour but passage to a market, and the toll reflects the value expected on the far side. Audiences sorted by spending power command different prices as identical pitches do in rich and poor market towns: location means proximity to money in motion.

The audience commodity's peculiar character, noted earlier, completes the section's argument. What the platform sells is warranted access to a predicted audience, and the warrant is manufactured by the platform's own waged labour: the engineers who build the sorting machinery, the analysts who refine the predictions, the sales staff who package the probabilities. That labour is real labour, performed under employment relations, and its classification belongs to the theory of circulation: labour that helps realise value, that moves commodities toward their buyers and money toward their sellers, without itself adding value to the commodities moved. The tradition has never been embarrassed by such labour's existence or by its necessity; an economy of any complexity needs its circulation performed, and the people performing it are workers in every sociological sense. The embarrassment belongs only to accounts that need the user to be the producer, because the closer one looks at the advertising transaction, the more the producing and the labouring are done by identifiable employees and identifiable commodity producers elsewhere, while the user does the one thing the theory has a different name for: the user is there, gathered, enclosed, and charged for at the gate. Being gathered is not nothing. Whole histories turn on gatherings and enclosures. But the name for the income a gate yields is rent, and no quantity of gathering turns the crowd outside the gate into the gatekeeper's workforce.

There is a historical rhyme here that the tradition's students will recognise and that strengthens confidence in the classification. The commercial media of the broadcasting era posed the same puzzle in a simpler form, and Smythe's audience commodity was the first serious attempt to solve it from within a Marxist frame. The puzzle was that broadcasters gave their apparent product away and prospered, which inverted the factory's visible logic and invited the conclusion that the audience must be the product and therefore the producer. The platform era inherited the puzzle with the audience's measurement refined from the sample to the individual. Yet refinement of measurement does not alter the structure of the transaction, and a solution that was questionable for broadcasting does not become sound when the audience is counted person by person instead of household by household. What changed is the gate's precision: the toll can now be varied by the predicted value of each crossing, discriminating among users as a landlord discriminates among plots. Precision in rent extraction is still rent extraction, and the history matters because it shows the free labour account to be the broadcasting puzzle's answer generalised, carrying the original answer's weakness into an era whose technical novelty disguises the continuity. The Marxist treatment of advertising and ideology as a system belongs to [advertising consumption ideology](/2012/06/01/advertising-consumption-ideology/), and the present article deliberately leaves that treatment there, taking from the advertising platform only what the value dispute needs: the direction in which its money flows, and the fund from which its money comes.

## The labour question: work coordinated through a junction

Everything said so far concerns users, and the lean platform's other population must now be given its separate hearing, because the driver, the courier, the cleaner, the carer, and the task worker coordinated through a junction are not users in the relevant sense at all. They are workers selling their capacity to work, and the questions their situation raises are the classical ones, sharpened rather than replaced by the digital setting. Is the surplus they produce appropriated by the platform, making the platform their employer in substance whatever the contract says? Or does the platform merely rent them access to customers, taking rent on the junction while the worker remains an independent petty producer selling services into a market? The answer determines whether the commission withheld from each payment is best understood as the platform's appropriation of surplus value or as a toll deducted from the worker's earnings, and the two understandings point toward different analyses, different solidarities, and different demands. The question is genuinely hard, and this article treats it as the place where the rent reading must work for its living rather than coast on the user case's momentum.

Begin with what is old, because the literature's inflation of novelty is the first obstacle. Piece rates, payment by the task rather than by the hour, are among the oldest wage forms the tradition analysed, and Marx's own discussion of piece wages treats them as a form perfectly adapted to intensifying labour and disguising the wage relation's real terms. Subcontracting, in which a nominal intermediary stands between the worker and the firm that ultimately profits, is older than the factory system it accompanied. The putting out system, in which merchants distributed materials to workers labouring in their own homes with their own tools and collected the product at a price that squeezed the workers' margin, organised whole industries before steam power gathered production under one roof. Measured against that archive, the lean platform's contractual architecture, the worker as independent contractor, supplying their own vehicle or tools, paid per task, bearing the costs of waiting and of wear, is not an innovation in the form of exploitation. It is a restoration, executed with better instruments, of arrangements capital abandoned when the factory's economies of scale made direct employment more profitable. Stating this plainly performs a double service. It denies the platform the glamour of having invented a new world, and it arms the worker's side with a long archive of precedent, since arrangements that were fought and regulated in their earlier incarnations can be fought with the earlier victories in hand. The precarity that the contractual form manufactures is treated in its own right at [precarity and the working class](/2021/02/01/precarity-and-the-working-class/), which owns that subject; here the contractual form matters only for the classification question it feeds.

### What does the contractor label conceal?

It conceals how badly the binary fits the substance. The worker owns tools and bears costs like a petty producer, yet cannot set prices, inspect the allocation rule, or carry a reputation away, which are the powers making independence real. Rights attach to the label, but the commission rests on manufactured dependence either way.

What is new, then, must be located with precision, and the brief for this article fixes the location exactly: the novelty lies in the asymmetry of information and in the granularity of control, not in the contract. The putting out merchant knew what he collected and what he paid, and the cottage worker knew the price per piece and could reckon, however bitterly, the terms of the bargain. The platform worker confronts a counterparty that observes everything and discloses selectively. The coordinating system records the worker's position, speed, acceptance pattern, customer ratings, idle intervals, and route choices continuously, building a picture of the labour process more complete than any foreman's notebook. Against that total visibility the worker sees a screen of offers whose generating rule is hidden: why this task, at this price, at this moment, offered to this worker rather than another, is information the system holds and the worker cannot audit. Prices vary by mechanisms the worker can only guess at, demand predictions shape offers in ways designed to move workers through space and time like pieces whose board they cannot see, and the discipline of deactivation, exclusion from the junction altogether, hangs over conduct whose exact offences are nowhere published. Control without a visible controller, discipline without a stated rule, management without a manager who can be argued with: these are the form's real innovations, and they are innovations in the technology of domination within the labour relation rather than in the labour relation's economic skeleton.

The classification question can now be answered with the distinctions in hand. The coordinated worker's labour does produce value where the criteria are met, and in the central cases they are met: the courier's and driver's activity is performed for exchange, disciplined by mechanisms functionally equivalent to competitive compulsion, since the allocation system punishes slowness, refusal, and poor ratings with reduced access to work, which is the market's discipline administered by software rather than by a foreman's eye. The worker who lingers is not merely unpopular; the worker's future income is throttled by an apparatus that has converted socially necessary labour time from an emergent market outcome into a computed and enforced standard. Here, and almost only here in the platform economy, the free labour style of argument finds genuine footing, because a compulsion equivalent is demonstrably present. But notice what follows for the platform's income. The platform's commission is not thereby shown to be profit from organising production, because the platform does not organise the production in the manner of a factory owner purchasing labour power and directing a labour process it owns. It interposes itself between worker and customer, owns the junction at which they must meet, sets the toll for the meeting, and disciplines the worker's conduct to protect the junction's value to customers. The commission acts as a deduction enforced by a gatekeeper, scaled to what the traffic will bear, rising when dependence deepens, and that is rent's signature written across a wage relation's territory. The worker can therefore be exploited in the production of the service and charged rent for access to the customer in the same transaction, two mechanisms stacked in one payment, and an analysis that insists on choosing a single label for the stack will misunderstand both layers. The tradition is capable of holding two mechanisms in view at once. This subject requires exactly that capacity, and rewards it.

## Algorithmic management and the precise location of novelty

The previous section located the platform's novelty in informational asymmetry and granular control, and the claim deserves a section of its own, because getting the novelty right is what separates an analysis from both the booster's fantasy of total innovation and the debunker's shrug that nothing has changed since the putting out system. Something has changed. The change is specific, and its specificity can be demonstrated by comparing the instruments of control across the tradition's archive of labour management. The overseer's control was personal, intermittent, and expensive: one pair of eyes, limited hours, attention that wandered, and a wage bill for supervision that capital always resented. Scientific management refined the overseer into a system, decomposing tasks into timed elements and lodging the knowledge of the labour process in management's planning office rather than in the worker's craft, a transfer of knowledge that the tradition's analysts, following the labour process debate, recognised as a shift in the balance of power inside production. The assembly line embodied control in machinery, setting pace by the chain's speed and making the discipline physical. Each stage cheapened supervision and deepened the employer's knowledge advantage. Algorithmic management is the next term in that sequence, and its difference from its predecessors is one of degree so large that it behaves, in several respects, like a difference of kind.

Consider the dimensions of the change separately. Observation becomes continuous and total within the sphere the junction governs: not sampled, not confined to working hours in a building, but woven into the instrument through which work is offered, accepted, performed, and judged. Evaluation becomes automatic and cumulative: every task generates ratings, timings, and comparisons that persist, aggregate, and govern future access, so that the worker accumulates a record whose contents they cannot fully inspect and whose weightings they cannot know. Allocation becomes opaque by design: the rule assigning work is a trade secret and a moving target, adjusted as the system's models learn, so that the worker's strategic knowledge, the craft of knowing how the boss thinks, which generations of workers cultivated as a defence, is devalued by a counterparty that has no stable thinking to know. Direction becomes personalised: two workers in the same city on the same evening receive different offers, different incentives, different nudges, each computed for the individual profile, so that the common experience from which collective understanding and collective organisation grow is deliberately fragmented into private feeds. Discipline becomes infrastructural: the ultimate sanction is not dismissal after a hearing but deactivation by a process whose reasons may never be stated to a human standard, exclusion from the junction that functions, in a concentrated market, as exclusion from the trade itself. Katherine Kellogg, Melissa Valentine, and Angie Christin, surveying this terrain in their 2020 synthesis on algorithms at work, catalogue the mechanisms under headings of directing, evaluating, and disciplining, and their catalogue confirms the pattern: the employer's side of the labour relation has acquired instruments of a resolution earlier management could not have afforded at any price.

### What is genuinely unprecedented in algorithmic management?

The instruments, not the function. Extracting effort from labour whose interests diverge from the buyer's, under unequal knowledge, is perennial. Continuous observation, cumulative scoring, opaque allocation, personalised direction, and discipline by silent exclusion are new in combination and cheapness, shifting knowledge's balance against the worker further than any predecessor system managed.

Alex Rosenblat's ethnographic work on ride hail drivers, published across the decade's middle years and gathered in a 2018 study of the industry's information practices, supplies the ground level evidence for what asymmetry feels like from the driver's seat: passengers and drivers shown different pictures of the same transaction, surge displays that summoned drivers toward demand that evaporated as they arrived, earnings representations that omitted costs the driver alone bore. The value of such ethnography for the present argument is that it documents asymmetry as a designed property rather than an accident of implementation. The information gap is not a bug awaiting a patch. It is load bearing: a worker who could see the whole board could refuse strategically, coordinate refusals, arbitrage the incentives, and bargain, and each of those capacities is worth money to the junction precisely because the worker lacks it. Alex Wood and colleagues, studying remote gig work in 2019, added the necessary complication that workers also report real autonomy, choosing hours and declining tasks in ways factory hands cannot, and the complication must be kept: the form's discipline operates through the allocation of opportunity rather than through direct command, which makes it simultaneously looser at the level of the individual task and tighter at the level of the worker's overall dependence. Freedom to choose among offers one cannot inspect, from a counterparty one cannot question, inside a market one cannot leave without leaving the trade: the tradition has a word for freedom of that shape, and the word is formal, the freedom of the seller of labour power who is free in the double sense, free to sell and free of any other way to live.

What does the novelty do to the article's central classification? Less than commentary assumes, and the restraint is instructive. Algorithmic management intensifies the extraction at the junction and deepens the worker's dependence, and dependence is the soil in which rent grows, since a toll's height is set by the cost of refusing to pay it. The more completely the junction monopolises access to a trade's customers, and the more completely its records and ratings imprison the worker's accumulated reputation inside its walls, the higher the commission the junction can sustain. Novel control therefore feeds the rent reading rather than competing with it: the algorithm is the fence's modern material, electrified, self repairing, and rebuilt nightly from the traffic's own data. At the same time the algorithm's disciplinary equivalence to market compulsion is what qualifies the coordinated worker's labour as value producing in the first place, so the same mechanism settles two questions in two directions, and an analysis alert to mechanisms can let it. The temptation to make novelty do classificatory work, to argue that because the control is new the economics must be new, should be resisted as firmly here as in the user case. New instruments, old relations, sharper extraction: that is the tradition's characteristic finding whenever it examines an innovation without dazzlement, and the platform's management systems confirm the finding rather than refute it.

## Concentration, network effects, and the centralisation of capital

Why do platforms tend toward monopoly? The question is among the seeded enquiries this article must answer, and the mechanism behind it is the cleanest piece of economics in the whole subject. A platform's usefulness to each participant rises with the number and variety of other participants. A marketplace with more sellers offers buyers more choice; more buyers offer sellers more custom; a communication network with more members reaches more of the people a new member wishes to reach; a labour junction with more workers offers customers shorter waits, and with more customers offers workers steadier earnings. Effects of this shape are called network effects, and their consequence is a dynamic with a winner's gravity. The junction that pulls ahead becomes more useful by virtue of its lead, attracting the next participant more strongly than its rivals can, which extends the lead, which strengthens the attraction. Rivals are not merely outcompeted in the ordinary sense of offering a worse product at the price. They are starved of the very input, participation, that constitutes the product, and a market with strong network effects therefore tends toward one dominant junction surrounded by niches too specialised or too local for the dynamic to swallow. The tendency is a mechanism, not a law without exceptions, and its strength varies with how easily participants can use several junctions at once and how sharply usefulness rises with scale. But the direction of the pressure is constant, and it operates without any conspiracy, incompetence, or policy failure on anyone's part.

### What gives the leading junction its compounding advantage?

The feedback inside network effects. Each additional participant raises the junction's usefulness to every other participant, so the leader's advantage compounds while rivals starve for the participation constituting the product itself. Concentration is a mechanism of the form, reproducing by digital means the centralisation of capital the tradition described for industry.

The tradition has a drawer ready for this mechanism, and opening it connects the platform case to the series' hub treatment at [marxism and modern capitalism](/2020/11/01/marxism-and-modern-capitalism/). Marx distinguished the concentration of capital, its growth by accumulation in the same hands, from its centralisation, the redistribution of already existing capitals into fewer hands through competition and merger, and he treated centralisation as the faster and more violent of the two processes. Network effects are centralisation's digital accelerator. They do not merely reward the largest junction; they actively devalue the assets of smaller ones, since a rival's network is worth little precisely because it is small, and they raise the cost of entry to a height that ordinary competition cannot finance, because the entrant must somehow purchase a network's worth of participation before its service is worth using. The established junction's moat is therefore not a stock of machinery that a rival could replicate with sufficient investment. It is the gathered population itself, an asset that cannot be bought off the shelf because it consists of other people's habits, relationships, records, and mutual expectations. Capital centralises here not by seizing factories but by gathering crowds, and the crowd, once gathered, is the barrier.

Concentration completes the rent reading's architecture, and the connection should be drawn tightly. Rent requires scarcity that competition cannot erode, and network effects manufacture exactly that scarcity as a byproduct of ordinary operation. The sequence runs: intermediation gathers participants; gathering compounds usefulness; compounded usefulness forecloses rivals; foreclosure converts the junction into a monopoly of access; monopoly of access supports a toll limited only by the traffic's dependence. Each step follows from the form rather than from any firm's villainy, which is why moralised accounts of platform greed consistently miss the target the analysis identifies. Replace the firm's managers with saints and the mechanism continues: the saintly junction still becomes more useful as it grows, still starves its rivals of participation, still finds its toll limited by dependence rather than by rival efficiency, and still collects rent, perhaps distributing it more politely. The tradition's structural temper shows its worth in such cases. It does not ask whether the toll collector is wicked. It asks what the toll is, why the bridge cannot be duplicated, and who ultimately pays, and it regards the answers as the politics, since remedies that address character leave the bridge standing.

Two qualifications keep the concentration argument honest. The first is that network effects can be weakened by design and by conduct: participants who maintain presence on several junctions at once blunt the winner's gravity, standards that allow records and reputations to travel between junctions dissolve the gathered asset, and some markets segment by locality or taste so thoroughly that several junctions coexist by never truly competing for the same crowd. The tendency toward monopoly is therefore a pressure with counterpressures, and the empirical landscape of any given market is the resultant. The second qualification is that concentration at the junction coexists with fragmentation everywhere else: the dominant marketplace presides over thousands of small sellers, the dominant labour junction over an atomised crowd of individual contractors, and this combination, monopoly at the centre and competition at the edges, is not a paradox but the form's signature. The junction monopolises the one position that matters, access, and then enjoys the benefits of fierce competition among everyone who needs that access, since competing sellers bid down their own margins and competing workers bid down their own terms, enlarging the surplus from which the toll is drawn. Monopoly and competition, which textbook presentation treats as opposites, operate here as partners, each assigned to the level where it best serves the junction's income. A Marxist analysis that grasps the partnership understands more about the platform economy than any account that asks, in the textbook manner, whether the sector is competitive or monopolised and expects one answer.

There is a further consequence for the value dispute, easily missed, and it cuts in the rent reading's favour. If users produced value, then the gathering of users would be the gathering of a workforce, and the junction's dominance would rest on its success in recruiting and retaining producers. The language of the industry itself testifies otherwise: participants are acquired, retained, and monetised, spoken of as an audience, an installed base, a network, in short as an asset whose presence raises the value of access sold to others. Firms do not describe their users as their producers even in the privacy of their own accounting categories, because the users appear in those accounts as the inventory of attention and data that advertisers and firms pay to reach. The self understanding of capital is not authoritative, and the tradition has often corrected it. But when an account of the platform requires users to be workers, and capital's own bookkeeping, the industry's vocabulary, and the price formation of attention all treat them as the terrain on which income is earned from third parties, the burden on the labour account grows heavier. Terrain is owned, fenced, and rented. The crowd at the junction is the terrain's population, and the toll is charged to those who need to cross the crowd to reach a market.

## The surveillance framing, and what Marxists object to in it

No account of the platform economy has reached a wider public than the one Shoshana Zuboff assembled in The Age of Surveillance Capitalism, published in 2019, and a Marxist analysis owes it the respect of an accurate summary before the respect of a serious objection. Zuboff's argument, compressed to its skeleton, runs as follows. A new economic logic emerged when firms discovered that the behavioural data generated by users of digital services could be claimed as a free raw material, appropriated without payment or meaningful consent, refined by machine intelligence into predictions of future behaviour, and sold in markets whose customers sought not merely to predict behaviour but increasingly to shape it. She names the raw material behavioural surplus: the portion of recorded behaviour exceeding what service improvement requires, diverted instead into the prediction products from which the new logic draws its revenue. The logic is, on her account, a rogue mutation of capitalism rather than its normal operation, an unprecedented form that breaks with the reciprocal disciplines of market democracy, in which firms prospered by serving needs they had to discover and respect. Its trajectory points toward a world of guaranteed outcomes, in which the modification of behaviour at scale hollows out autonomy, corrodes the conditions of democratic self government, and installs an instrumentarian power that governs populations through the architecture of their digital environment rather than through ideology or terror. The remedy implied by the diagnosis is restorative: law and democratic counterpower should drive the rogue form back, reclaim behavioural data as subject to rights, and restore the healthier capitalism whose norms the mutation violated.

The account's virtues are genuine and the Marxist objection is not advanced by denying them. Zuboff documented, at a scale nobody else attempted, the sheer extent of behavioural appropriation and the deliberate secrecy surrounding it. She gave the public a vocabulary, behavioural surplus above all, that names something real about the difference between data gathered to run a service and data gathered to sell predictions. She insisted, against the industry's own soothing language of personalisation and connection, that the extraction is a political fact with political consequences, and she traced its logic forward into the modification of behaviour with a moral seriousness the subject deserves. A reader who has absorbed her archive knows more about the platform economy's data practices than a reader of any rival single work. The objection begins only where her explanation begins: at the claim that what she documents is a deviation from capitalism's normal course, a mutation explicable by the choices and blindnesses of a particular industry at a particular moment, rather than an expression of tendencies the mode of production carries within itself wherever a new commons becomes available for enclosure and a new scarcity becomes available for rent.

### Where does the surveillance account's history go wrong?

It treats behavioural appropriation as a rogue departure from a capitalism that once respected reciprocity and served discovered needs. The objection holds it is the same logic of enclosure applied to new material, continuous with appropriations never consented to and never reciprocal. If the deviation diagnosis fails, restoration fails with it.

Unfold the objection into its components. The first is the myth of the normal capitalism. The capitalism of the tradition's archive never operated by the reciprocities the surveillance account mourns: its founding accumulations proceeded by enclosure, colonial seizure, and the legal manufacture of a propertyless class, and its mature industries shaped needs as energetically as they served them, building advertising, planned obsolescence, and the managed manufacture of desire into the ordinary machinery of selling. An account that treats behavioural manipulation as a fall from that history's grace has misread the history, and the misreading is consequential rather than decorative, because it locates the platform's offence in its novelty and thereby acquits the structure that produced it. The second component concerns the theory of value the surveillance account quietly assumes. Zuboff's narrative treats behavioural surplus as a new source of wealth, extracted and converted into revenue by the firms that claim it, and the Marxist reader will recognise the free labour account's founding gesture in a grander register: revenue at the point of extraction mistaken for production at the point of extraction. The rent reading's tracing discipline applies unchanged. Prediction products are sold to firms that pay for them from revenues and surplus generated in their own operations, and the behavioural material, however novel its refinement, functions as the enclosed asset on which a toll is charged rather than as a substance that generates value by being watched into existence. The third component is the remedy. Restoration presupposes a prior condition worth restoring, and if the prior condition is mythical, the remedy dissolves into nostalgia armed with statutes. The Marxist alternative does not promise that transformation is easier than restoration. It insists that the diagnosis determines the destination, and that a diagnosis of deviation will march toward a capitalism that never existed, arriving, if it arrives anywhere, at the same junctions with better manners.

A fourth component, quieter than the others, concerns what the surveillance framing does to the subject's politics by the choice of its central victim. In Zuboff's telling the injured party is the autonomous individual, the self whose inner experience is colonised and whose future is predicted and shaped without its knowledge. The injury is real, and the tradition need not minimise it. But framing the platform's offence as an offence against autonomy, against the sanctity of an inner life that markets once left alone, directs the response toward privacy, consent, and individual rights, the liberal repertoire. The Marxist framing directs attention to ownership: to the fact that the infrastructure of social connection, the archive of collective behaviour, and the means of prediction are private property, and that the population's subjection follows from that property relation rather than from any technology's inherent creepiness. Privacy remedies leave the property standing and negotiate the terms of the extraction. Ownership remedies contest the extraction's foundation. The two politics can cooperate tactically and often should, but they are not the same politics, and an analysis that cannot tell them apart will mistake every privacy settlement for a victory over the junction itself. The junction survives such settlements comfortably, since a toll collector can afford excellent manners.

Balance requires recording where the Marxist objection reaches its limit, because a critique that concedes nothing has usually stopped thinking. Zuboff's account grasps something the rent reading, left alone, can understate: the product sold is increasingly not access to an audience as it is but a capacity to intervene in behaviour as it forms, and intervention of that kind is a power over persons that the vocabulary of rent, with its bridges and tolls, does not naturally express. The tradition has resources for that dimension, in its theories of ideology and of the manufacture of consent, and the advertising system's ideological work is treated at the neighbouring article already linked in this discussion. But the Marxist reader should admit that the surveillance account's central anxiety, the industrialisation of behaviour modification, names a frontier where the older categories need supplementing rather than merely applying. Admitting the frontier costs the rent reading nothing, since the classification of income and the diagnosis of power are separable questions. Platform income can be rent while platform power is something the tradition must stretch to describe, and an analysis secure in its classification can afford generosity about its gaps. What the objection refuses is only the package deal: the claim that because the power is new, the economics are new, the history is a fall, and the remedy is a return. Each element of that package fails separately, and the Marxist objection, properly made, is four objections wearing one coat.

## Data, knowledge, and the claim of the general intellect

A sophisticated defender of the free labour account, pressed on the compulsion criterion, often retreats to higher ground: the claim that digital production has changed the character of labour itself, so that the old criteria fail because the object has outgrown them. The retreat usually invokes the general intellect, the tradition's own name, from the notebooks published as the Grundrisse, for the stage at which social knowledge, science, and collective intelligence become the direct forces of production, embodied in machinery and systems rather than in the individual worker's skill. If production now runs on the accumulated knowledge and sociability of the whole population, the argument goes, then the population's activity is production in the deepest sense, whatever the factory era criteria say, and the platform, which organises and captures that collective intelligence, stands at the centre of the new productive order rather than at a toll point on its edge. The claim deserves a full hearing, because it is the free labour account's most philosophical form and because the text it invokes is genuinely suggestive. The full treatment of the concept belongs to [general intellect explained](/2021/01/15/general-intellect-explained/), which owns it; this article takes from the notebooks only what the platform dispute requires.

What the famous fragment on machines actually argues, read with care, is nearly the opposite of the use commonly made of it. Marx's speculation runs that as production comes to depend on the general state of science and on knowledge objectified in the system of machinery, the direct labour of individuals shrinks toward a supervisory and regulatory role, and the theft of alien labour time, the foundation of the existing order, becomes an increasingly absurd measure of wealth. The fragment is a thought about the tendential abolition of value's foundation from within: the more production rests on collective knowledge, the less the wealth created can be coherently measured by labour time at all. Writers in the post workerist tradition took the fragment as a charter for treating all social activity as productive labour, since knowledge and sociability now do the producing. But the fragment's logic points the other way for classification purposes. If wealth's creation increasingly escapes the measure of labour time, then the income capital draws from knowledge based systems is increasingly not profit on value production measured in the old way, but appropriation of a collectively produced abundance through positions of ownership, which is the rent reading's home territory. The platform that encloses the general intellect, that fences the population's accumulated knowledge, language, relationships, and recorded ingenuity inside a proprietary junction and charges for passage, is not refuting the fragment. It is illustrating the fragment's darkest implication: that capital, as direct labour's role shrinks, survives by converting itself into the owner of the conditions under which social knowledge may be used, a landlord of the intellect rather than an organiser of production.

### Does the general intellect make all social activity productive?

No; the fragment's argument cuts against that reading. As collective knowledge becomes production's foundation, wealth creation escapes measurement by labour time, so income drawn from enclosing that knowledge resembles appropriation through ownership rather than profit measured against a working day. The population's intelligence is the enclosed common here, not a workforce.

The distinction between a common and a workforce, introduced here, is worth holding onto because it organises several of the article's threads at once. A workforce is engaged, directed, and disciplined by capital in a production process capital owns. A common is a resource produced by a community's life together, which capital encloses and then charges the community, or third parties, for access to. Language is the purest common: nobody's employer organised its creation, everybody's activity sustains it, and its usefulness to any firm is beyond calculation. The social graph resembles language in structure, produced by relationships pursued for their own sake, maintained by the population's ordinary sociability, and valuable to the junction exactly because it never had to produce it. When a platform encloses the graph, records it in proprietary form, forbids its export, and sells access to the map of a society's connections, the transaction's anatomy is enclosure followed by rent, and the population's role is the commoner's role, dispossessed of a collectively made resource, not the operative's role, exploited within a production process. The grievance's dignity is undiminished by the placement. The English enclosures were not a minor episode because the commoners were not factory hands; they were the founding dispossession on which the factory system itself was later built. If the platform's enclosure of social knowledge is the digital era's comparable founding act, then the analysis that names it enclosure has located its historical weight more accurately than the analysis that names it employment without wages.

Data itself, examined as an object, confirms the placement. A behavioural record is not produced the way a commodity is produced, by labour applied to materials under a production plan. It precipitates out of activity undertaken for other purposes, as a residue, and becomes an asset only through an apparatus of capture, storage, and interpretation that the platform owns and staffs. The waged labour in the data economy is the labour of that apparatus: building sensors into every interaction, cleaning and joining records, training models, selling predictions. Around that labour the criteria can be debated in the tradition's usual terms, and much of it will classify as circulation or supervisory labour rather than as value production in the strict sense, a debate this article leaves to the neighbouring treatments. The residue itself, the population's recorded life, stands to the apparatus as the river stands to the mill: indispensable, appropriated, and not the miller's employee. Accounts that call the residue's generation labour must explain why the generation requires no engagement, no direction, no standard, and no possibility of dismissal, and why its quantity is governed by the population's size and sociability rather than by any labour process's organisation. The explanation, when attempted, always reintroduces the criteria through the back door, by pointing to the design that elicits and shapes the activity. Design elicits; it does not employ. The gardener who channels a river has not hired the rain.

## The cloud and industrial types: where the rent reading bends

An analysis that only wins on friendly territory deserves suspicion, and the advertising platform, for all its prominence in the dispute, is friendly territory for the rent reading. The cloud and industrial types are not. A provider of rented computing capacity owns one of the largest physical infrastructures ever assembled: buildings filled with machines, power and cooling systems, fibre connections, and a waged workforce of engineers and technicians who build, maintain, and continuously rebuild the whole apparatus. Its customers pay for the use of that apparatus, and the payment resembles the hire of machinery, a category the tradition has always treated as bound up with production rather than with pure access. If the rent reading cannot handle the cloud without strain, its claim to grade the platform economy collapses into a claim about advertising with imperial ambitions. The strain should be admitted and then measured, because measuring it produces the article's most instructive refinement: platform income is a composite, and the rent proportion varies by type in ways the taxonomy predicts.

Decompose the cloud payment as the tradition decomposes any composite income. One component pays for the produced infrastructure's use: the machines wear out, the buildings age, the power is consumed, and a charge covering depreciation together with an average return on the capital invested in producing and maintaining the apparatus is profit's familiar territory, or more precisely the return on productive capital advanced in a service industry. Another component pays for the waged labour of operation, the engineers whose work keeps the apparatus running, classified by the same debates that govern technical and supervisory labour elsewhere. A third component, though, answers to neither machinery nor labour. It answers to position: to the fact that a customer whose operations are built inside one provider's environment, whose data rests in its formats, whose staff are trained on its interfaces, and whose systems speak its dialects, faces costs of leaving that have nothing to do with the quality or price of the computing itself. Where that component dominates, where the payment exceeds what the apparatus and its operation would command in a market of easy exit, the excess is rent on an enclosed standard, collected because departure is expensive rather than because the service is superior. The cloud is therefore the type where the two income forms interpenetrate most completely, and the analytical task is not to crown one but to estimate the proportions and to watch which proportion grows as the market matures. The rent reading predicts the rent proportion grows with lock in, and that prediction is testable against the behaviour of prices and margins as switching costs accumulate, which is the sort of prediction a reading should be proud to risk.

### When does renting infrastructure stop being rent?

At the point where payment tracks the value of what was produced to supply the service: the machinery's depreciation, the power consumed, the maintenance labour, an ordinary return on capital advanced. Beyond it, where payment is sustained by the customer's cost of leaving rather than the supplier's cost of providing, the excess is rent.

The industrial type bends the reading in a different direction, toward the interior of production itself. A platform embedded in a client's factories, sensing machine states, coordinating components, predicting failures, and feeding managers a continuous picture of the process, sells something that can genuinely raise the productivity of the client's labour, and a payment drawn from the surplus that higher productivity generates has a respectable pedigree in the tradition: it resembles the innovator's temporary extra profit, or the fee of a supplier whose machinery lets client capital extract more relative surplus value. Yet the same embedding also gives the platform something no machinery supplier historically obtained: a continuous view inside the client's production, an accumulating archive of the client's processes, tolerances, failures, and rhythms, which is knowledge the client generated and the platform keeps. As installations multiply, the archive becomes a position: the platform learns an industry's production the way the labour junction learns a city's traffic, and the client grows dependent on interpretations of its own operations that only the platform can supply. The income's centre of gravity can therefore migrate over time from a fee for a productive service toward rent on an enclosed knowledge of the client's own factory, and the migration is the industrial version of the pattern the whole article traces: a service enters through production's door and settles, as dependence deepens, into a toll position. Watching for that migration, in any given case, is the empirical programme the reading prescribes, and it is a better programme than asking the static question of what the income essentially is, since the answer changes as the enclosure matures.

The product type, by contrast, barely bends the reading at all, and its simplicity is clarifying. Converting a good into a subscription service means retaining ownership and selling access in slices of time: the catalogue, the vehicle, the software's features, the machine's operating hours. The subscriber's payment is rent in the plainest commercial sense, the hire of an asset the subscriber will never own, and the type's interest for the analysis lies in what the conversion does to the population's relation to goods. Ownership diffuses; access concentrates. A society in which the means of listening, reading, travelling, and computing are rented by the month from a handful of owners is a society in which the rent relation has been generalised from land and housing, its classical territories, into the texture of everyday consumption. The tradition's category, forged for the landlord, turns out to describe the subscriber's monthly statement with uncomfortable precision, and that descriptive success across so unlikely a territory is evidence for the category's vitality. Where the free labour account sees the subscriber as a worker manqué, the rent reading sees the subscriber as a tenant, and tenancy, with its insecurities, its prohibitions, its dependence on the owner's continued permission, matches the lived texture of subscription life, the vanished catalogue, the altered terms, the account suspended without appeal, far better than employment does. People do not feel like workers when their rented world is rearranged overnight. They feel like tenants whose landlord has repainted the locks, and an analysis should take the feeling as data about the relation's form.

## Intermediation in the history of capital: an old position with new instruments

Placing the platform in a lineage is not antiquarian decoration. It is how the tradition tests whether a form is genuinely new, and the platform's lineage, once traced, shows the junction to be one of capital's oldest positions, successively re-equipped. Merchant capital occupied it first in the modern sequence, standing between producers and markets, advancing money, controlling the knowledge of distant prices, and drawing an income that the young Marxist tradition initially struggled to classify, since the merchant plainly grew rich without organising the production from which the riches ultimately came. The resolution of that early puzzle is the template for the platform's: commercial profit was explained as a share of the surplus value produced by the productive capitals whose commodities the merchant circulated, a deduction justified within the system by the circulation work performed and limited by competition among merchants. The marketplace owner occupied a neighbouring position, charging stallholders for access to the gathered crowd, and nobody in the tradition's archive mistook the stallholders' payments for the market owner's production. The market owner provided the gathering; the gathering was the asset; the charge was for the gathering. Substitute a gathered network for a gathered crowd and the platform's advertising and marketplace incomes slot into a classification older than the factory system, which does not make the classification automatic, since old drawers can be wrongly opened, but does shift the argumentative burden onto anyone claiming the digital junction escapes it.

The lineage continues through the great infrastructures. Railway companies occupied junctions between regions and charged for passage over networks whose usefulness likewise rose with their reach, and their economics mixed, exactly as the cloud type mixes, a produced apparatus of enormous cost with a positional income limited mainly by the traffic's dependence. Telegraph and telephone networks repeated the pattern, and the tradition's analysts of monopoly capital treated such networks as the characteristic form of the era's centralisation, infrastructures whose ownership conferred a taxing power over every activity obliged to pass through them. Each successive infrastructure was greeted, in its day, with the claim that it had transformed the nature of economic activity itself, and each claim contained the same partial truth the platform's boosters now repeat: the infrastructure did transform the conditions under which other activities occurred, their speed, their reach, their costs, their possibilities. What the transformation never did was relocate the production of value into the infrastructure's owner by the mere fact of passage. The goods carried by rail were produced in fields and workshops; the messages carried by wire were composed in offices and homes; the value, where value was produced, was produced at the ends of the network and divided, in part, to the network's owner as the price of connection. A reader who sees the platform in this sequence sees the contemporary dispute with the tradition's eyes: the user at one end of the network, the advertiser or customer at the other, the owner in the middle collecting for the connection, and the only genuinely new question being whether the middle's collection is still a deduction from value produced at the ends or whether the ends have somehow become the middle's factory floor. The criteria answer that question, and the lineage shows why the criteria, rather than the novelty of the instruments, are the right tribunal.

One element of the lineage deserves separate emphasis because it prefigures the platform's most distinctive asset. The great infrastructures learned, each in turn, that the traffic itself generates knowledge that compounds the owner's advantage: the railway's knowledge of freight flows, the telegraph's knowledge of markets, the telephone network's knowledge of who calls whom. That knowledge let owners price more finely, plan extensions more shrewdly, and sell intelligence about the traffic to the traffic's participants. The platform radicalises this inheritance by making the knowledge continuous, individual, and total within its sphere, and by feeding it back into the junction's operation automatically, so that the fence learns from every crossing how to charge for the next. But a difference in the knowledge's resolution is not a difference in the income's category, any more than a landlord's intimate knowledge of every tenant's circumstances converts rent into wages paid in reverse. The lineage's lesson is uniform: infrastructures gather, gathering confers position, position yields a claim on value produced elsewhere, and the claim's sophistication grows with the technology while its classification stands still. Analysts who find that conclusion deflating should ask what work the inflation was doing. Novelty is exciting and fundable and publishable. Classification is none of those things, and it is what the tradition, on its good days, is for.

### Is the platform a merchant, a landlord, or a factory owner?

The landlord, with the merchant a secondary resemblance and the factory owner the misleading one. Like the merchant, it performs circulation work paid from surplus that circulation helps realise. Decisively, like the landlord, its characteristic income rests on a monopolised condition of others' activity and is limited by dependence, not rival efficiency.

## Four figures at the junction: user, worker, tenant, commoner

Confusion in this subject often reduces to a cast list error. The platform economy populates its stage with figures that commentary merges into one wronged protagonist, and the analysis improves the moment the figures are separated and each is assigned the relation, the injury, and the remedy that actually belong to them. Four figures recur through this article, and a section that lines them up side by side can serve as the argument's map. The user participates in an advertising or communication junction, supplies the attention and the behavioural residue from which the junction's asset is built, and receives the service without monetary payment. The coordinated worker sells labour through a lean junction, produces services under algorithmic discipline, and pays a commission for access to the customers the junction gathers. The tenant, the subscriber of the product type and the business customer of the cloud, rents access to assets or capacity owned by another and lives under terms the owner can revise. The commoner is the figure the other three collectively compose: the member of a population whose shared life, relationships, knowledge, and movements have been enclosed as the junction's private asset, and whose dispossession underlies the other figures' more visible grievances. Each figure's injury has a distinct anatomy. The user's is appropriation without payment inside an environment engineered to maximise the appropriation. The worker's is the classical extraction of surplus within production, compounded by rent charged on access to the market for that production. The tenant's is dependence without ownership, the insecurity of a life furnished by permission. The commoner's is the founding loss, the conversion of a collectively made world into a privately owned one.

Remedies follow anatomies, and the map's practical value is that it prevents the misapplication of cures. For the worker, the classical repertoire applies with full force: organisation, collective bargaining, employment status with its attached rights, limits on the working day and on the intensity the allocation system may impose, because the worker's relation is a production relation and production relations are contested at the point of production. For the tenant, the repertoire is the law of tenancy generalised: security of tenure, notice and justification for exclusion, portability of the records and reputations accumulated during the tenancy, prohibitions on unilateral revision of terms, because dependence without ownership is governed, where it is governed at all, by constraining the owner's discretion rather than by pretending the tenant is something else. For the user, the honest remedies are the awkward ones the earlier sections derived: the terms of extraction can be regulated, the residue's collection limited, the engineering of compulsion restrained, but the user's claim is not a wage claim and dressing it as one produces the data wage proposal, whose triviality would ratify the arrangement it protests. For the commoner, the remedy is the oldest and largest: the enclosed asset's return to common or public ownership, or its effective dissolution through interoperability and portability so complete that no fence can hold the population's social life as private property. A politics that knows which figure it is addressing can sequence these remedies and combine them without incoherence. A politics that merges the figures will demand wages for users, privacy for workers, and tenancy rights for commoners, and will win, at best, the remedy least threatening to the junction in each case, which is to say it will win the data wage, the consent banner, and the clearer terms of service, and the junction will collect its toll behind all three.

### Who is harmed most at the junction?

No single answer exists, because the harms differ in kind and ranking incommensurable injuries is rhetoric, not analysis. The worker's extraction is most classical and measurable; the commoner's dispossession most foundational, since other relations stand on the enclosure; the tenant's dependence perhaps most universal. Serious analysis assigns each harm its mechanism.

The cast list also clarifies the tradition's own internal geography, because different Marxist writers have built their accounts around different figures and then generalised from them. The user's theory, generalised to the whole stage, is the free labour account, and its characteristic error is the generalisation rather than the portrait, which is vivid and in its own terms largely accurate. The labour process literature is the worker's theory, and its error, when it errs, is to treat the user as a distraction from the real exploitation occurring in warehouses and on roads, which understates the enclosure's foundational role. The rentier capitalism literature is the tenant's and commoner's theory, strong on ownership and position, weaker on the production relations humming inside the junction's own operations and its clients' factories. The surveillance account is the user's theory in a liberal key, with autonomy in place of exploitation as the injury's name. This article's synthesis, if the word is permitted, is simply the refusal to generalise from one figure: the stage holds all four, the mechanisms differ by figure, and the classification of any given income stream follows the figure whose relation generated it. That refusal is less thrilling than a unified theory of platform exploitation. It has the compensating virtue of being true to the mechanisms, and the tradition's history suggests that its unified theories of new phenomena have aged worst exactly where they were most thrilling, while its patient decompositions, of merchant capital, of ground rent, of the credit system, are still in working order long after the phenomena that prompted them changed beyond recognition.

## Attention, time, and the seduction of the labour analogy

Beneath the free labour account's technical claims sits an intuition about time that gives the account its emotional durability, and the intuition should be faced directly rather than outmanoeuvred. People spend a substantial share of their waking lives inside platform environments, and the environments are designed, with great sophistication and at great expense, to extend that residence. Time spent feels like time given, and time given to a firm's enrichment feels like work, whatever the theory says, because the working day is the form in which most people have learned to experience the surrender of their hours to another's purposes. The intuition is not stupid. It registers the real engineering of capture, the real opportunity cost of hours absorbed by feeds and streams, and the real transfer of the residue those hours leave behind. Where the intuition misleads is in its inference from surrendered time to performed labour, an inference the tradition's criteria exist precisely to test. Time is labour's measure only inside the value relation, where hours are homogenised, compared, and disciplined. Outside that relation, surrendered time is a far older and more various phenomenon: time surrendered to ritual, to spectacle, to courtship, to idleness, to grief, to the radio, to the window. A theory that reads every surrendered hour inside a commercial environment as labour will find the population working sixteen hour days of which employment statistics know nothing, and will have traded the concept's cutting edge for a metaphor about modern life's busyness.

The spectacle comparison is instructive and should not be flinched from. Audiences surrendered hours to commercial entertainment long before behavioural records existed, and advertisers paid for access to those hours on the same logic of predicted exposure that governs the platform's market. The tradition's analysts of that era debated the audience commodity, as this article has recorded, and the debate's unsettled state is itself evidence: if the labour reading of attention were as straightforward as its platform era proponents suggest, the broadcasting version would have carried the field, since the mechanism alleged is the same. What the platform adds to the spectacle is not the sale of attention but the individuation of the audience and the residue's permanence: the broadcast audience evaporated when the programme ended, while the platform's record of attention persists, accumulates, and can be re-sold, combined, and mined indefinitely. Permanence changes the asset's character, making it more like a accumulated stock than a passing flow, and stocks of appropriated material belong, in the tradition's grammar, to the analysis of property. The user does not clock in. The user's passing presence is surveyed, and the survey map is kept, and the map is the property. Surveying a population and keeping its map is an act of statecraft older than capitalism, and capital's adoption of the practice extends the enclosure of the social rather than the factory's working day.

### Is time spent on a platform a working day?

No, though the question's persistence is diagnostic. A working day is defined by the sale of labour power and production under discipline for an employer. Residence inside a commercial environment, however engineered and harvested, lacks the sale, the socially necessary standard, and the product whose value the hours would measure. The two captures are resisted differently.

There is, however, a border territory where the analogy stops being an analogy, and mapping it sharpens both sides of the dispute. Some user activity is commissioned, directed, and disciplined in ways that approach the criteria: the creator whose output a junction commissions and promotes under content standards enforced by demonetisation, the contributor whose unpaid moderation labour keeps a community commercially viable under rules the junction sets and sanctions it enforces, the player whose generated content a game environment sells. In such cases the junction specifies the product, sets quality standards whose violation carries economic punishment, and organises the activity for exchange, and the activity's classification genuinely trembles on the criteria's edge. The honest treatment concedes the border and patrols it: where an intermediary commissions output, enforces standards through livelihood affecting sanctions, and sells the result, the relation is a putting out system in digital dress and the contributors are outworkers, exploited in a recognisable sense that the tradition's archive of domestic industry fully covers. The concession costs the rent reading nothing, because the border cases are defined by exactly the features, commission, standards, sanctions, sale of the product, whose absence in ordinary use grounded the reading. A criterion that identifies its own borderline, and explains why the borderline lies where it does, is a criterion in good health. A theory that must deny the border to protect its verdict, on either side, is confessing that its verdict was a posture rather than a finding.

## Competition among capitals, and what the junction does to it

The sections so far have examined the junction's relations with users, workers, and tenants. A further relation, quieter and in the long run perhaps more consequential, runs between the junction and the other capitals that must pass through it, and the value dispute's resolution illuminates this relation in ways worth drawing out. A dominant junction stands to the firms in its market as a private government stands to its subjects: it writes the rules of visibility, deciding which sellers the crowd sees and in what order; it levies the toll, revising commissions and fees by its own calculation; it adjudicates disputes under procedures it designs; and it competes, where it chooses, with its own subjects, selling its own goods and services inside the market it governs, with full knowledge of the subjects' affairs gathered from their passage. The tradition has a rich vocabulary for relations of this shape between capitals, because the subordination of smaller capitals to larger ones, of industrial capital to financial capital, of producers to the owners of circulation's chokepoints, is one of its perennial themes. What the platform adds is the fusion of governor and competitor in a single firm armed with total information about the governed, a fusion that the older chokepoint owners, the market proprietors and railway companies, approached only intermittently.

For the classification question, the significant point is the direction in which surplus flows along this relation. The firms passing through the junction are, in large part, productive capitals or their commercial arms, employing labour that meets the criteria and generating surplus value in production. The junction's fees, commissions, advertising charges, and infrastructure rents are deductions from that surplus, negotiated under duress of dependence. The platform economy's aggregate income therefore represents, in significant measure, a redistribution of the surplus value produced across the whole economy toward the owners of its junctions, a centralisation of claims accompanying the centralisation of access. This is the sense in which the rent reading scales from the individual transaction to the system's shape: the platform sector's celebrated profitability is, on this analysis, less a measure of new value created than an index of how thoroughly the economy's traffic has been routed through privately owned gates. A sector can grow rich by producing more or by positioning itself across more of what others produce, and the two growths have opposite implications for how the wealth should be judged and what its continuation requires. The first requires the sector's own productivity. The second requires only the maintenance of the gates and the deepening of everyone else's dependence, and an economy tilting toward the second growth is exhibiting, in the tradition's diagnostic language, a rentier deformation: the increasing dominance of income from position over income from production.

### Do platforms create wealth or redistribute it?

Both occur inside platform firms, and the analysis fails when it refuses the decomposition. Produced infrastructures, logistics, engineering, and coordinated services involve real production by real labour. The distinctively platform income, the toll on passage through enclosed junctions, redistributes wealth produced elsewhere, its growth measuring dependence's growth rather than production's. Judge the proportions, not slogans.

The rentier deformation thesis should be stated with its proper modesty attached. It is a claim about tendencies and proportions within the income of a sector and an economy, not a claim that nothing is produced or that the deformation is total or irreversible. Its value lies in the questions it teaches an analyst to ask of any platform firm's accounts: how much of this income would survive the junction's opening to rivals on equal terms, how much rests on standards and networks that interoperability would dissolve, how much is payment for apparatus and labour that any competent supplier could provide, and how much is the pure yield of the gate. Those questions are askable, investigable, and answerable in degrees, which distinguishes the thesis from the rhetorical denunciation of rentier capitalism as a mood. They also reconnect the platform analysis to the series' wider concerns, since the same decomposition applied to finance produced the tradition's most durable modern work, and the student who learns to decompose a platform's income has learned a transferable instrument. The examination of that instrument's use, and of the verdicts it supports, is where the article turns next: to the standards of evidence by which the whole dispute could, in principle, be settled, and to the defeaters that each side, this article's included, is obliged to publish alongside its verdict.

## The wider literature: positions between the poles

The dispute has been staged so far as a duel, and the staging, while faithful to the argument's sharpest form, flattens a literature that contains intermediate positions worth mapping, because a reader who can locate the intermediates understands the poles better. Jakob Rigi and Robert Prey, writing on the value question in the digital economy, attempted a rescue of the labour account by a different route from Fuchs's: they argued that the data produced by users functions analogously to a product whose exchange value is realised in advertising markets, and that the labour embodied in the platforms' own operations and in the production of the devices and infrastructures should be counted together with user activity in one accounting. Their intervention is valuable for its candour about the difficulty: the rescue requires treating the audience's activity as productive by analogy with commodity production while conceding that the mechanism of socially necessary labour time does not operate on it in the standard way, which is to say the rescue preserves the conclusion by amending the theory, and the amendment's cost is the very thing the duel is about. Adam Arvidsson and Nicolai Colleoni, in a 2012 contribution that the rent reading should claim as partial kin, argued that what is accumulated in social media environments is better understood through the logic of financial valuation and reputation than through the production of value by labour: the platform's worth rests on the anticipated capacity of its gathered relations to generate future revenue, an asset priced by expectation rather than by embodied labour time. That argument, developed independently of the rent tradition, converges with it from the direction of finance: income and valuation at the junction are claims on future surplus, capitalised expectations of tolls yet to be collected, rather than crystallisations of labour already performed by the crowd.

Trebor Scholz's organising work around digital labour, through the conferences and writings that named the field, contributed less a theory than an insistence: that the invisibility of the human effort inside digital systems is a political achievement of the systems' design, and that naming the effort is the first step toward organising around it. The insistence survives every verdict in the classification dispute, because the coordinated worker's effort, the moderator's, the content producer's, and the data labeller's are all rendered invisible by interfaces built to present their outputs as the platform's own magic, and the political work of making labour visible does not depend on the user's activity sharing the mechanism. A reader should therefore be wary of a false implication that travels with the rent reading when it is carelessly held: the implication that because users are not value producers, nothing at the junction merits the language of labour politics. The junction is dense with labour, waged and unwaged, classified variously by the criteria, and the rent reading's whole point is that precision about which labour does what is the precondition of a politics that addresses the right mechanisms. Denunciation of the careless implication belongs inside the reading's own camp, addressed to its own sloganeers, and this article issues it in that spirit.

The intermediate map also contains the position that the dispute itself is misconceived because value theory was built for a world of material commodities and should be retired rather than applied. That position at least has the courage of its obsolescence, and it usefully forces the disputants to say why the old categories deserve their keep. The answer this article has been building is that the categories earn their keep by the discriminations they perform: between production and capture, between the worker and the commoner, between a toll and a wage bill, discriminations without which the platform economy dissolves into an undifferentiated spectacle of extraction in which everything is exploitation and nothing in particular can be done about any of it. A retired theory cannot be wrong, but it cannot be used either, and the platform debate shows the tradition's instruments, applied with discipline, still cutting. The writers who would retire the instruments owe an alternative set capable of the same discriminations, and the surveillance account, the most serious alternative on offer, was found in an earlier section to discriminate less finely at exactly the joints that matter: it cannot separate the toll from the product, or the enclosure from the working day, and its history is a romance. The duel's verdict is therefore also a verdict on the retirement proposal. The old categories stay, not from piety, but because nothing offered in their place yet does their work.

## What would settle the question, and the defeaters each side must publish

A dispute that cannot state its own settlement conditions is a liturgy, and the tradition's better angels have always insisted that verdicts travel with their defeaters attached. The platform value question is unusually well supplied with potential settlements, because its competing accounts make different predictions about observable magnitudes and mechanisms, and a section that collects the tests performs a service beyond adjudication: it shows a student what a theoretical dispute looks like when it is conducted as an empirical one. The first test concerns the price formation of attention, already introduced as evidence. If user activity produced value, the price of access to audiences should bear some systematic relation to the activity's quantity and quality, as prices in labour's vicinity track labour's costs and scarcities. If the rent reading holds, the price should track the advertiser's expected returns from reaching the audience, varying with the audience's wealth and purchase intentions while remaining indifferent to how the audience's hours were spent. The observable pattern, in which identical attention commands wildly different prices according to the audience's spending power, is the rent reading's exhibit, and a demonstration that prices instead tracked the labour characteristics of attention would be the free labour account's strongest possible reply. The second test concerns the behaviour of platform income under the withdrawal of activity. Value production, when its producers withdraw, stops: a struck factory produces nothing. Rent, when the traffic thins, declines in proportion to the position's erosion but is defended by the position's remaining scarcity, and the junction's frantic engineering to retain participation, its subsidies, its acquisitions of potential rivals, its terror of the crowd's migration, reads as the defence of an asset whose value is the crowd's presence rather than as an employer's relation to a workforce. Employers facing a strike do not typically respond by paying the strikers' rivals to stay away; landlords facing abandonment of a district do exactly that in spirit, and junction behaviour under competitive threat resembles the landlord's repertoire far more than the employer's.

The third test is the decomposition test, applied to the income of mature junctions. The rent reading predicts that as a junction's position matures and dependence deepens, its margins expand without corresponding expansion in its production apparatus or its waged workforce's product, because the toll rises with dependence while the costs of passage stay flat. The free labour account predicts, if anything, the reverse pressure, since a growing user base on its logic is a growing workforce whose product the firm must still organise its systems to harvest. The observable signature of platform maturity, margins celebrated precisely for their detachment from headcount, is again the rent reading's exhibit. The fourth test concerns the fate of the record. If the behavioural archive were the product of its generators' labour in the value sense, its value should perish or transfer with them in ways analogous to a workforce's departure. In practice the archive's value resides entirely in the junction's ownership: users departing cannot take the social graph, the reputation scores, or the trained predictions with them, because those assets are constituted by the junction's property rights over the collective record. Wealth that vanishes for its alleged producers the moment they leave the owner's premises, while remaining intact for the owner, has the legal anatomy of a landlord's improvement, not of a worker's product, and the anatomy is evidence.

### Which observations would discriminate between the two accounts?

Evidence about mechanisms rather than magnitudes: whether audience access prices track spending power or the character of activity; whether junction income expands with dependence rather than production; whether the archive's value resides in the owner's property rights rather than travelling with its generators. Each test discriminates because the accounts predict different patterns.

This article's verdict must now publish its defeater in the form the series requires, without softening. The rent reading would be defeated by a demonstration that user activity is subject to a compulsion functionally equivalent to competitive discipline, such that performances are measured against a social standard and failures are punished by exclusion from the means of life in the manner of the labour market's discipline; or by evidence that platform income cannot be traced to surplus value produced in commodity production, that the funds paying the tolls originate somewhere other than the economy's productive surplus, which would break the distribution model's spine. Either demonstration, if made, would restore the free labour account or force a third construction, and the reading's defenders are bound by the statement to accept the restoration. The defeater is not a rhetorical ornament. It specifies the observations that would change the verdict, and it exposes the reading to the discipline it recommends to others. Symmetrically, the free labour account's defeater should be stated by its opponents fairly, since a verdict is only as strong as the opposition it can afford to arm: the free labour account would fall if no mechanism could be shown to equalise user activity into abstract labour, together with a complete tracing of platform income to surplus produced elsewhere, and this article has argued that both halves of that demonstration are, in substance, already available. The asymmetry between the two defeaters' distances from fulfilment is the verdict's real content. Both sides can state what would change their minds. Only one side's conditions are close to being met by the evidence in hand, and grading the dispute means saying so.

A closing caution about settlement itself is owed to the student reader. Theoretical disputes in political economy are rarely settled the way a race is settled, by a single observable finish. They are settled the way a research programme is vindicated, by the accumulation of discriminations its categories successfully perform across many cases, and by the sterility or fertility of the questions it teaches its holders to ask. The rent reading's claim on a reader's allegiance rests finally on that fertility: it generates the decomposition questions, the price formation tests, the cast list of figures, the remedy matching discipline, and the lineage placement, each of which organises further inquiry, while the free labour account, for all its moral energy, generates chiefly the demand that the user's status be recognised, a demand whose satisfaction would change no analysis and direct no remedy. Fertility is not truth, and the defeater stands. But when criteria, price evidence, income behaviour, and legal anatomy all point one way, and the research programme built on that pointing keeps paying for its keep, a verdict is entitled to be stated without trembling. Platform income, in its distinctively platform element, is rent. The crowd at the junction is not the junction's workforce. The toll is paid from value produced in the places where labour remains subject to the full discipline of the criteria, and the political task the classification prescribes is the contesting of the enclosure, not the payroll.

## Misreadings to correct, and the strongest objection answered once more

The article's findings can be consolidated by correcting the misreadings that cluster around the subject, since a finding is only secure when the errors it excludes are named. The first misreading holds that all platforms share one business model, so that a verdict about any is a verdict about all. The taxonomy refutes it: the five types intermediate different objects, draw income from different funds, and answer the value question differently, with the cloud type's composite income standing as the standing rebuke to every generalisation. The second misreading holds that the free labour account is the Marxist position on platforms, the tradition's official line from which the rent reading dissents. There is no official line; there is a dispute, conducted in the tradition's journals and books, between accounts that apply its criteria differently, and the free labour account's prominence in popular summary reflects its quotability rather than its standing among those who work the criteria hardest. The third misreading holds that the gig contract is a new form of exploitation invented by digital capital. The archive refutes it: piece rates, subcontracting, and putting out are centuries deep, and the novelty is confined to the instruments of information and control, a confinement that strengthens rather than weakens the worker's case by supplying it with precedent. The fourth misreading holds that surveillance capitalism is a Marxist analysis because it is a critical one. Criticism is a genre, not a tradition, and the surveillance account's liberal history, its romance of the normal market, and its restorative remedy place it outside the tradition it is mistaken for, however useful its archive. The fifth misreading holds that network effects are a policy failure, an accident of lax enforcement that vigilance could have prevented. They are a mechanism of the intermediation form itself, operating wherever usefulness rises with participation, and policy addresses their consequences without abolishing their logic.

### Is platform work a new mode of production?

No, and the claim's inflation is itself instructive. A mode of production is a whole configuration of forces and relations reproducing a society. The platform is a form of intermediation inside capitalism's existing configuration, rearranging circulation and deepening enclosure without displacing the wage relation or disciplined commodity production as the system's value producing core.

The strongest objection to this article's verdict was stated at full strength in the free labour section and must be answered once more in its most concentrated form, because a verdict that survives only the weak version of its opposition has survived nothing. The objection: users perform activities that generate the data and content on which platform revenue depends; they are not paid; the platform appropriates the entire product of the activity; this satisfies any ordinary sense of exploitation; and a value theory that cannot register the fact has failed at the task of describing the world it claims to explain. The answer distinguishes, as it must, between exploitation in the ordinary sense and value production in the technical sense, and then concedes what the distinction is sometimes used to dodge. The ordinary sense registers a real relation: uncompensated appropriation, engineered and systematic, of the products of people's activity, and the grievance it names does not evaporate when the technical classification is corrected. People whose social lives are harvested to build a private fortune have been wronged in a way that any adequate politics must answer, and the rent reading answers it with the enclosure analysis and its remedies, which are more radical than the wage demand the objection's logic implies, since they contest the junction's ownership rather than negotiating the harvest's price. What the answer refuses is only the inference from the grievance to the classification, from the fact of appropriation to the conclusion of value production, because that inference, generalised, dissolves the distinctions on which every remedy in the tradition's repertoire depends. A theory that calls the commoner a worker will send the commoner to bargain for wages over the common. A theory that calls the commoner a commoner sends them to abolish the fence. The objection's moral force is honoured by the second sending, not betrayed by it, and the value theory that insists on the distinction has not failed to register the wrong. It has registered the wrong's address.

One more concession completes the honesty of the verdict, and it concerns what the rent reading does not explain. It does not explain, by itself, why the appropriated activity is uncompensated, why the crowd accepts the junction's terms, or why the enclosure was politically permitted, and those questions belong to the theories of ideology, of the state's role in constituting property, and of the population's fragmented relation to its own collective products. The rent reading is a classification of income and a location of the surplus's origin, not a total theory of the platform, and its defenders should resist the imperialism that afflicts successful classifications, the temptation to answer every question with the category that answered one question well. The platform is also a machine for governing conduct, a theatre of social performance, a reorganisation of urban space and of time, and a chapter in the history of the state's retreat from providing common infrastructures, and each of those platforms requires its own analysis, several of which this series houses elsewhere. What the classification contributes to all of them is the discipline of the fund: whatever else the junction does, its income is a claim on value produced elsewhere, and any account of its power that forgets the claim's direction will mistake the gate for the road, the toll for the cargo, and the crowd for the crew.

## Ownership of the junction: property, the state, and the fence's legal anatomy

Rent is never merely an economic relation, because the scarcity it feeds on must be constituted and defended, and a section on the junction's legal anatomy completes the analysis by showing what the fence is made of. Land's scarcity is partly natural, but even land rent presupposes a legal order that titles, registers, and enforces exclusive ownership against the dispossessed. The junction's scarcity is manufactured entire, and its manufacture is legal and technical in equal parts. The technical parts have been examined: the gathered network, the enclosed standard, the proprietary record. The legal parts deserve the same attention. The junction's terms of use are a private law, authored by the owner, revised unilaterally, and enforced by exclusion, governing the conduct of populations larger than many states' citizenries with none of a state's formal obligations to justify itself. The behavioural record's ownership rests on a lattice of contract, intellectual property, and trade secrecy that assigns the collective product of the population's activity to the junction as completely as any enclosure award assigned a common to a lord. The reputation a worker or seller accumulates inside the junction is, by the same lattice, a non transferable licence rather than a property, so that the asset a person builds by years of rated conduct remains the junction's to extinguish at departure or deactivation. Each element is a choice made by law, not a fact of nature, and the tradition's insistence on this point is among its oldest: property is a relation between people, enforced by institutions, wearing the disguise of a relation between people and things. The disguise is unusually thin at the junction, where the thing owned, a network of relationships, is visibly made of people, and the thinness is an analytical opportunity the tradition should seize: nowhere in the economy is it easier to show a student that ownership is a social relation than at a junction whose entire asset is a society in miniature.

The state stands behind the fence in the usual double posture, as its guarantor and as its occasional antagonist, and the analysis should describe the posture without inventing a regulatory narrative the structural discipline of this article forbids it to date or place. As guarantor, public authority supplies the contract enforcement, the property registrations, the liability shields, and the infrastructural commons, from publicly funded research to publicly built networks, on which the junction was erected, a genealogy that gives the commoner's claim a second foundation: the junction encloses not only the population's social life but an inheritance of public investment, privatised at the moment of its commercial ripening. As antagonist, public authority periodically contests the fence's height, the toll's level, and the terms' fairness, in proceedings whose outcomes redistribute the rent among the junction, its business subjects, and the public purse without necessarily questioning the fence itself. The tradition's characteristic question about such contests is whether they regulate the enclosure or dissolve it, and its characteristic finding, across two centuries of factory acts and antitrust campaigns, is that regulation humanises a relation while entrenching it, buying the population relief at the price of the relation's legitimacy. Whether that finding governs the junction's future is a political question this analysis deliberately leaves open. What the analysis supplies is the distinction by which the future's settlements can be graded when they arrive: a settlement that lowers the toll leaves the rent relation standing, a settlement that opens the fence, mandating the portability of the graph, the record, and the reputation, attacks the scarcity on which the rent rests, and the two settlements are as different in kind as a rent reduction is from the abolition of the estate.

### Who owns the social graph?

In law, the junction; in production, the population whose relationships the graph records. That divergence between legal title and material authorship is the enclosure's signature. The graph was not built by the junction's employees as part of their duties; it accumulated as the byproduct of a society conversing with itself, hosted under the junction's terms.

The property analysis also returns a dividend to the value dispute, because it explains the free labour account's intuitive pull better than the account explains itself. People sense, correctly, that something they made has become someone else's wealth, and the language of labour is the nearest vocabulary ordinary speech keeps for made things and their makers. The property analysis supplies the more exact vocabulary: what the population made was a common, what happened to it was enclosure, what the junction draws from it is rent, and what the population lost was not a wage but a world it had built and briefly inhabited as its own. Exactness here is not pedantry, because the vocabularies prescribe different recoveries. Stolen wages are recovered by payment. Enclosed commons are recovered by restoration of access and ownership, a larger and more interesting politics, and one with the whole history of common right behind it. The platform debate, followed patiently to its legal foundations, thus ends where the tradition began, with the question of who owns the conditions of social life and on what title. That the question returns wearing the newest technology the economy has produced is the strongest evidence this article can offer for its method: the forms innovate, the relations persist, and the criteria, patiently applied, tell the analyst which is which.

## The verdict assembled: grading the dispute on the criteria

The article's centre can now be stated in one place, at the length a verdict deserves and with its reasoning compressed into the sequence a student should be able to reproduce. The dispute asked whether platform users produce value. The criteria, drawn from the tradition's own account, require labour performed for exchange, under competitive compulsion, equalised into abstract labour against a socially necessary standard. User activity meets none of these in its ordinary form: it is not performed for exchange by its performer, it is disciplined by no standard whose violation carries the market's punishments, and its products are never equalised with the rival performances of other users. To sustain its verdict, the free labour account must relax the compulsion criterion, and relaxing it dissolves the concept's discriminating power across every other case. The rent reading requires no relaxation: it classifies platform income by the position from which it is drawn, traces the paying funds back to surplus generated by commodity production across the economy, predicts the observed behaviour of attention prices, of margins under maturity, and of the archive's legal anatomy, and locates the user's real injury in enclosure, for which it prescribes remedies matched to the mechanism. On the criteria, on the price evidence, and on fertility, the rent reading is the stronger account, and this article adopts it as its verdict, with the defeater published in the settlement section standing unamended.

### Do platform users produce value?

most likely not, on Marx's criteria. Value production requires labour performed under competitive compulsion and organised for exchange, which user activity is not. Platform income is better analysed as rent extracted from surplus value produced elsewhere, chiefly in the goods whose sale advertising facilitates.

The verdict's scope must be restated as carefully as the verdict itself, because verdicts decay into slogans when their scope is forgotten. The verdict classifies the income attributable to intermediation, that element in platform firms' revenues which the junction alone explains. It does not classify the whole of those firms' operations, which include produced infrastructures, waged engineering, logistics, and in some configurations directly employed service labour, each governed by its own analysis. Nor does it pronounce on the element distinctive to platforms within every firm's revenue as though the proportions were uniform. It does not deny that coordinated workers produce value under algorithmic discipline functionally equivalent to the market's compulsion; it affirms that production and separates the commission charged on it as a second, rent bearing mechanism stacked upon the first. It does not deny the reality of uncompensated appropriation from users; it renames that appropriation enclosure and assigns it the commoner's remedies. And it does not claim that the categories are eternal: it publishes the observations, a compulsion equivalent governing user activity, an untraceable origin for the toll paying funds, that would defeat it. A verdict with its scope and defeater attached is a scientific instrument. A verdict without them is a badge, and the tradition has suffered more from its badges than from its opponents.

For the reader who has followed the argument from the opening pages, the verdict also completes a methodological arc worth noticing. The article began with an experience, the toll silently taken at an owned junction, and with two ready interpretations of it, the booster's efficiency story and the critic's unpaid worker story. It refused both, not from contrarianism but because both assumed the classification they needed: the booster assumed the toll was a price for a service rendered, the critic assumed it was a wage bill evaded. The tradition's method, applied patiently, replaced assumption with inquiry: what is owned, what discipline governs the activity harvested, from which fund is the payment drawn, what do the prices testify, what does the legal anatomy show. Each question is unglamorous, and their cumulative answer is the analysis. That is the transferable lesson, worth more than the verdict itself, because the reader will meet the next innovation, whatever it is, accompanied by the same two ready interpretations in new costume, and the questions will still work. Ask them in order, publish the criteria before the verdict, attach the defeater after it, and the innovation will classify itself.

## The junction and the reproduction of everyday life

A dimension of the platform economy that the value dispute touches only obliquely deserves direct treatment before the article closes its analytical sections, because it explains the depth of the population's dependence, on which the height of the toll ultimately rests. Platforms have inserted themselves into the reproduction of everyday life: into how people eat, move, communicate, court, care, celebrate, and remember. The tradition's concept of reproduction, developed to analyse how labour power is daily and generationally remade, usually in unwaged domestic labour whose invisibility the theory laboured to expose, gives this insertion its proper frame. The junction does not merely intermediate consumption choices among others. It becomes the environment in which the choices are presented, ranked, priced, and remembered, so that the reproduction of life itself increasingly routes through privately owned passages. Each routing deepens the enclosure in two directions at once: the population's dependence grows, since the skills, contacts, and habits of unaided reproduction atrophy as the junction's convenience becomes the default, and the junction's archive grows, since every act of reproduction routed through it leaves its record in the owner's vault. Dependence and archive are the toll's two foundations, and reproduction is where both are manufactured, meal by meal and message by message.

The parallel with the tradition's analysis of domestic labour is worth drawing precisely, because it shows the criteria working in their most familiar territory. The feminist Marxist tradition established that unwaged domestic labour, though indispensable to capital and though performed under real social compulsion, does not produce value in the technical sense; it produces and reproduces labour power, the commodity whose value is set by the labour embodied in its means of subsistence. That establishment was not a demotion of housework but its accurate placement, and the placement directed politics toward the socialisation of reproduction rather than toward wages for housework in its classical form, a strategic fork debated for decades. The platform user's activity sits in a structurally similar position: indispensable to the junction, socially encouraged to the edge of compulsion, productive of everything except value in the technical sense, and mis-served by a politics that demands its wages instead of contesting its enclosure. The parallel is not decorative. It shows that the tradition has faced this exact configuration before, an indispensable unwaged activity harvested by capital, and that its mature response was placement before remedy then as now. Readers who know the domestic labour debate will recognise the platform dispute as its digital recurrence, and the recognition is a shortcut to the verdict: the tradition has already had this argument, in harder circumstances, and the criteria won it.

### Does indispensability make an activity value producing?

No, and the tradition's hardest cases prove the negative. Domestic labour is indispensable to reproducing the workforce, the audience to the performance, the crowd to the junction, yet indispensability never supplies the missing criteria of exchange, compulsion, and equalisation. It establishes that capital needs an activity; classification asks what the activity is.

Reproduction also names the junction's most quietly radical effect, which a purely economic analysis can miss: the reorganisation of the population's time and attention at the level of the day. The working day was the classical unit of extraction's politics, bounded, contested, and eventually regulated. The junction's day has no boundaries of that kind, because its harvest is not labour time but life time, gathered in the interstices of every other activity, in queues and carriages and sleepless hours, in the small vacancies of attention that earlier media could not reach. Zuboff's account grasped this reach and named its product behavioural surplus; the rent reading adds only the classification, that the reach extends the enclosure's fence around the whole of waking life rather than extending the working day around it. The distinction prescribes the resistance's shape. The working day was shortened by limiting the employer's claim on hours. Life time's enclosure is resisted by limiting the fence's claim on the record and the passage: by rights of departure that carry the graph and the reputation with the departing, by public provision of the passages a society cannot leave to toll collectors, by the deliberate cultivation of unmediated spaces whose value lies precisely in leaving no residue for anyone's vault. Whether such a politics assembles itself is beyond this article's competence. That the classification points to it, and away from the payroll, is the article's contention, and the contention stands or falls with everything argued so far.

## Valuation, finance, and the capitalisation of tolls

A platform firm's market valuation presents the value dispute in its most theatrical form, because the sums involved appear to measure something enormous that the firms' tangible operations cannot obviously account for, and each side of the dispute reads the theatre according to its script. On the free labour reading, the valuation measures the capitalised product of the users' unpaid labour, the crowd's exploitation rendered as a stock price. On the rent reading, the valuation measures something the tradition understands very well: the capitalisation of expected future rent, the price of a toll position calculated from the traffic's projected dependence, exactly as the price of land capitalises the rent the land is expected to yield and rises or falls with expectations about the position's durability rather than with anything the landowner produces. The second reading has the archive on its side. Marx's analysis of fictitious capital treated the capitalisation of any regular revenue, government debt, railway shares, ground rent, as the formation of paper claims whose prices move with expected income and the rate of interest, independently of any production the claim's holder performs. A junction's valuation is a fictitious capital of the purest strain: a claim on tolls not yet collected, from traffic not yet gathered, under a fence not yet tested by time, priced by the market's confidence that the dependence will deepen. The valuation's notorious volatility, its sensitivity to growth in the gathered crowd rather than to earnings in hand, is the signature of that strain, since a claim on future rent is worth exactly the durability of the position and nothing else.

This reading also dissolves a puzzle that the labour account handles awkwardly: the long periods in which dominant junctions operate without profit, subsidising the traffic, buying participation, and absorbing losses that would bankrupt an ordinary firm, while their valuations climb. An employer who cannot extract surplus from a workforce does not grow richer by enlarging it at a loss. A landlord assembling an estate, by contrast, rationally spends to gather what will later be charged for: the enclosure phase of any rent asset is expensive, because the fence must be built, the crowd must be attracted inside it, and rivals must be starved before the toll can be raised to the level dependence permits. The observed sequence of platform maturation, subsidised gathering, then the tightening of terms, rising commissions, degraded free access, and the conversion of the gathered crowd into a revenue engine, is the enclosure to rent sequence executed as a business plan, and commentary's habit of narrating the tightening as a betrayal of the firm's founding spirit merely records the sequence's second phase discovering that the first phase was never the business. The business was the fence. The generosity was the fence's construction budget, and the rent reading, alone among the accounts on offer, predicts the sequence in advance rather than moralising it in retrospect.

### Why do unprofitable platforms grow more valuable?

Because the market prices the fence, not the year's takings. A junction still gathering its crowd is assembling the asset from which future tolls will be drawn, and capitalised expected rent rises with the crowd's size and dependence even while operations lose money. The losses are the enclosure's construction costs; the valuation, its anticipated yield.

Finance's role completes the picture and connects the platform analysis to the financialisation articles elsewhere in this series. The enclosure phase's losses must be funded, and they are funded by capital markets willing to advance present money against future rent, which means the platform economy's shape is partly decided by finance's appetite for capitalised tolls. Where that appetite is strong, fences multiply and gathering races intensify; where it cools, the toll must be raised sooner and the crowd's patience is tested earlier. The tradition's analysis of rent has always noted rent's dependence on the credit system that capitalises it, and the platform case is the dependence in modern dress. None of this requires a single user to have produced a single unit of value. It requires only a fence, a crowd, a credible expectation of tolls, and a market in claims on those tolls, four elements the tradition catalogued in the age of the railway share and the government bond. The instruments gleam. The elements are old, and recognising them is not a refusal of the new but the precondition of seeing what, in the new, is actually new: the instruments, the resolution of the record, the granularity of control, and nothing in the classification of the income at all.

## The measurement problem: socially necessary labour time at the junction

Every value theoretic claim carries a measurement obligation, and pressing the free labour account on its obligation produces the dispute's most technical and perhaps most decisive exchange. If user activity produces value, its value is determined, as all value is in the theory, by the labour time socially necessary for its production, and the account owes an answer to the question of what that time is for a unit of user activity and how the market establishes it. For commodities, the establishment mechanism is visible: producers compete, techniques diffuse, laggards are ruined, and the recognised value of a unit gravitates toward the labour required under normal conditions with average skill and intensity. For user activity, no corresponding mechanism can be described. Users do not compete to supply attention at lower labour cost; platforms do not select among users by the efficiency of their scrolling; no user's bankruptcy disciplines the population's average performance; and the platform's payments, where the account needs them most, are not made to users at all, so there is not even a wage whose level might anchor the measurement. The account's defenders sometimes answer that the measurement problem is the theory's problem, that value in digital conditions must be measured differently, by engagement, by data volume, by the prices the refined products command. But those measures are prices of the junction's outputs, and deriving the value of the alleged labour from the price of the product it supposedly creates reverses the theory's explanatory direction: value was supposed to explain price, and a version in which price explains value has quietly become a different theory wearing the first theory's vocabulary. The rent reading carries no such obligation and feels no such strain. Rent requires no labour time measurement at the junction, because nothing is produced there to be measured; the toll's level is set by dependence and by the payer's surplus, magnitudes with their own determinants, and the reading's coherence on measurement is a quiet but real point in its favour.

The measurement lens also clarifies the one population at the junction where value production genuinely occurs, returning to the coordinated worker with a sharper instrument. The allocation system does establish something functionally identical to socially necessary labour time for the services it coordinates: it computes expected durations, compares performances across the workforce, throttles the slow, rewards the swift, and continuously revises the standard as the distribution of performances shifts. That is the market's equalising discipline rebuilt as software, and its presence is exactly why the worker's labour classifies as value producing while the user's does not. The contrast is the criteria's vindication in miniature. Where the measurement mechanism exists, the classification follows; where it cannot even be described, the classification fails; and a theory whose central category tracks the presence of a real mechanism, rather than the analyst's sympathies, is behaving as a theory should. Critics who find the criteria pedantic should be asked to contemplate the alternative's yield: a value concept with no measurement mechanism anywhere in the digital economy, applied by intuition to activities a writer finds significant, is not a more humane theory. It is a vocabulary in search of a discipline, and vocabularies without disciplines produce the literature this article has been correcting, in which the same activity is priceless exploitation on one page and worthless distraction on the next, according to the needs of the paragraph.

### Can engagement be measured as labour time?

No, because measurement in the value sense is not counting but equalisation. A platform can count hours, clicks, and keystrokes precisely and still possess no measure of value, since nothing in the counting compares performances, punishes the laggard, or establishes a social standard. Precision of surveillance is not commensuration.

## Sellers at the junction: small producers and the toll on market access

One population remains unexamined in its own right, and its testimony may be the most direct evidence the dispute possesses, because its members describe their own relation to the junction in terms that require no theoretical translation. The independent sellers, makers, hosts, and small firms who reach their customers through a marketplace junction stand in a relation the tradition knows intimately: they are petty commodity producers and small capitals, owning their tools and stock, performing or employing labour that meets every criterion, and selling into a market they do not control. The junction charges them for the market's access in commissions, in fees for visibility without which their goods sink below the crowd's notice, and in compliance with rules whose revision can halve a livelihood overnight. Ask such a seller where the junction's charge comes from and the answer is immediate and exact: it comes out of the margin, out of the surplus the seller's production generated, surrendered because the customers are on the other side of the gate. No seller supposes that the junction produced the goods, the value, or the margin it deducts from. The deduction's anatomy is visible to its payer in a way the user's appropriation is not, because the seller watches the percentage leave each transaction, and the visibility makes the marketplace the rent reading's clearest exhibit: here the toll is itemised, the fund it draws on is the producer's own surplus, and the position that levies it is nakedly the ownership of the gathered crowd.

The seller's situation also refines the concentration analysis, because the junction's power over sellers grows through a mechanism subtler than exclusion. Visibility inside a crowded marketplace is itself scarce, since the crowd's attention is finite while the sellers multiply, and the junction sells relief from the scarcity it administers: prominence, ranking, the favourable position in the list the customer scrolls. The seller thus pays twice over, once for access to the market and again for access to the market's attention, and the second payment is rent on a scarcity manufactured by the first payment's success, since only a junction that gathered everyone could make obscurity the default condition of each. Rents stacked on rents, each layer's scarcity produced by the layer beneath, is the mature junction's characteristic revenue architecture, and it generalises: the worker pays in commission and then in the purchase of priority, the advertiser pays for access and then for the data that makes access efficient, the tenant firm pays for capacity and then for the standards compliance that keeps its operations portable in name only. An analysis that classified the first toll and stopped would underestimate the form. The junction is not a bridge with a booth. It is a territory whose owner sells, street by street, relief from the territory's own congestion.

### What does the seller's commission prove?

More than any other single payment, because its payer can identify its fund. The commission is deducted from a margin the seller's production created, in a proportion the seller does not set, for access to a crowd otherwise unreachable. It is the toll with the wrapping off: rent on market access, itemised on every statement.

## Inside the firm: engineers, moderators, and the junction's own workforce

The platform firm is also an employer, and its internal workforce poses classification questions that a complete analysis should not skip, because the answers locate the boundary between the junction's rent and the firm's genuine production with unusual clarity. The engineers who build and maintain the infrastructure perform waged labour under employment discipline, and their product, the functioning junction, is a produced means of intermediation whose construction resembles any large technical system's. Whether their labour produces value depends on the classification of the firm's output, and the tradition's treatment of analogous cases, the builders of machinery for machinery makers, the constructors of the railways, suggests the productive character of labour that creates the durable apparatus from which services are sold. The apparatus builders stand upstream of the toll; their labour is embodied in the fence, and the fence's yield is rent on their product combined with the crowd's gathering, a combination no more paradoxical than a mill's yield combining the millwright's embodied labour with the miller's toll on the district's grain. Content moderators occupy a darker corner of the internal workforce: waged, disciplined, measured to the second, and exposed by design to the worst material the crowd produces, their labour is circulation's sanitation department, indispensable to the junction's commercial viability and classified, on the criteria, with the labour that maintains the conditions of exchange rather than with the labour that produces the commodities exchanged. Data labellers, often employed through contractors at the economy's periphery, train the prediction systems by classifying the archive's contents, and their labour is the archive's hidden factory floor: genuinely disciplined, genuinely productive of the refined prediction commodity, and genuinely exploited in forms the free labour account's focus on users has oddly neglected. The neglect is diagnostic. A literature scanning the horizon for unwaged exploitation walked past waged exploitation performed at piece rates under algorithmic discipline, because the waged version did not flatter the theory that the digital economy had transcended the wage relation. It had not. It had hidden the wage relation behind the interface and behind the contractor's veil, and the tradition's older categories find it without difficulty in either hiding place.

The internal survey returns the analysis to its master distinction with the survey's full weight behind it. Inside the firm: production, circulation, sanitation, and discipline, labour of every classical kind, classifiable by the criteria with no innovation required. At the junction's boundary: the crowd, the archive, the gathered network, appropriated without a wage because they were never labour in the first place, and yielding rent because the boundary is owned. The platform economy's mystery dissolves when the two zones are kept apart, and its mystery persists exactly as long as they are merged, since a merged account must either find wages inside the crowd or find production inside the toll, and both findings are mirages. Keeping zones apart is not a pedantic preference. It is the whole of the method this article has practised, from the taxonomy's first separation of types to the cast list's separation of figures, and the reader who has absorbed the habit owns the article's real conclusion, of which the rent verdict is only the most quotable instance.

## Analogies tested: the company town and the truck system

Analogies earn their place in an analysis by surviving hostile inspection, and two historical analogies for the platform junction deserve inspection because each illuminates a different face of the form and each fails at an instructive point. The company town, in which one firm owned the housing, the shops, the utilities, and the employment of a whole settlement, resembles the junction in the totality of its enclosure: the resident earned in the company's coin of employment and spent under the company's prices, and every circuit of life returned a portion to the owner. The resemblance deepens at the level of dependence's manufacture, since the town's owner, like the junction, profited not from any single transaction's margin but from the impossibility of transacting elsewhere. The analogy fails at the point of production: the company town's residents were the firm's workers, employed in its mines or mills, and the town's rents were a clawback of wages within an employment relation, a secondary extraction stacked on a primary one. The junction's crowd is not employed, and its extraction is primary rather than a clawback, which makes the junction in one respect the purer rentier: the town's owner at least had to organise production and pay wages before recovering them, while the junction's owner organises nothing but the gate. The truck system, under which workers were paid in tokens redeemable only at the employer's shop, resembles the junction in its capture of a circuit: the worker's earnings could be spent only where the capturer profited, as the platform worker's accumulated reputation can be spent, in effect, only inside the junction that issued it, and the seller's customer relationships exist only within the marketplace that hosts them. Non transferable reputation is the truck token's exact digital descendant, value earned in a form that binds its earner to the issuer, and the analogy's failure is again instructive: truck was an abuse within a wage relation, abolished by statute in its classical heartlands, while reputation capture operates within relations the law classifies as independent contracting and commerce, beyond the old statutes' reach. The analogies together locate the platform historically with some precision: company town totality without the town's production, truck's circuit capture without truck's wage, an enclosure whose owner has dispensed with the employer's burdens while retaining and refining the employer's instruments of dependence. Dispensing with the burdens while refining the instruments is, in one sentence, the platform form's economic biography, and the rent reading is that sentence translated into a classification.

## Open passages: what the analysis implies for alternatives

An analysis that classifies is sometimes accused of quietism, as though naming an income rent were a substitute for contesting it, and the accusation should be answered by drawing out what the classification actually prescribes, since prescriptions follow from it with unusual directness. If the junction's income is rent on an enclosed passage, then the strategic question is the passage's ownership and openness, and the repertoire of alternatives arranges itself along a spectrum defined by how much of the fence each alternative removes. At the spectrum's modest end stand the portability settlements: the departing user's right to carry the social graph in usable form, the worker's right to carry a reputation record between junctions, the seller's right to carry a customer history, each of which attacks the scarcity at its root, because a crowd that can leave together, carrying its connections, dissolves the gathered asset that made the toll possible. Portability is the enclosure's solvent in a way that toll regulation is not, since a regulated toll on an intact fence leaves the dependence, and therefore the toll's future height, in the owner's hands. Further along the spectrum stand the interoperability settlements, which oblige junctions to interconnect as the older networks were obliged to interconnect, so that a participant on one passage reaches participants on others and the network effect is socialised across the system rather than privatised within one fence. The historical resonance is exact: the telephone system's value to the public was multiplied when rival networks were compelled to interconnect, converting competing fences into one common passage, and the compulsion was a political act imposed on owners who preferred their fences. At the spectrum's far end stands public or cooperative ownership of the passages themselves: municipal or common infrastructures for the functions a society decides are too foundational to toll, from the coordination of local transport and care to the hosting of the social graph, run as utilities or commons rather than as estates. The tradition's contribution to this spectrum is not any single point on it but the criterion by which points are graded: does the proposal reduce the toll, or does it reduce the dependence that makes the toll payable. Only the second kind touches the rent relation itself, and a politics educated by the classification will not mistake the first kind for the second when the settlements are offered, as they will be, in the language of the second.

The alternatives discussion also disciplines the analysis in return, by exposing a romanticism the rent reading must avoid. Commons and public passages are not automatically benign: a publicly owned junction can surveil, exclude, and stagnate, and a cooperative one can reproduce the toll in miniature for its own insiders. The classification does not canonise the alternatives; it specifies what they must achieve to count as alternatives at all, namely the dissolution of the dependence on which rent feeds, by whatever ownership form proves capable of dissolving it. Nor does the analysis promise that dissolution is the crowd's actual desire: the gathered crowd often loves its junction, experiences the fence as a home, and resists portability as an engineer's abstraction, and a politics of the passage must reckon with the affection as seriously as with the toll. The tradition has met that configuration before, in every case where a population's real satisfactions were bound up with the relation that exploited or enclosed it, and its mature answer was never contempt for the satisfaction. It was the patient construction of passages in which the satisfaction survives the fence's removal. Analysis ends there, at the threshold of a politics it can inform but not perform, and an article that has kept its classification honest has done the part of the work that writing can do.

## Rent theory applied: differential, monopoly, and absolute elements at the junction

The rent reading gains precision when the tradition's internal distinctions among rents are applied to the junction, because platform income is not one rent but a braid of the classical kinds, and unbraiding it shows how much of the older theory survives translation. Differential rent arose, in the agricultural analysis, from differences in the fertility and location of plots worked simultaneously: capital applied to the better plot yielded a surplus over the return on the worst plot under cultivation, and that surplus accrued to the better plot's owner. The junction reproduces the structure in the market for attention and access. Audiences differ in spending power as plots differ in fertility, and access to the richer audiences commands a higher toll for reasons located entirely in the audience's composition rather than in the junction's exertions, which is differential rent wearing the price list of an advertising market. Positions inside the junction differ as locations differ: the prominent place in a ranking, the first screen, the default setting, the pre installed passage, each yields its occupant an advantage over rivals consigned to the junction's interior deserts, and the junction charges for the fertile positions or awards them to its own goods, collecting differential rent on a geography it manufactures. Even the second form of differential rent, which arose from successive investments of capital in the same plot, has its analogue: the junction's investments in its own infrastructure, its prediction machinery, its accumulated archive, raise the position's yield, and the increment accrues to the owner as surely as the drained field's increment accrued to the landlord, though the tenant's labour and the crowd's activity supplied much of the improvement's substance.

Monopoly rent, the classical category for income resting on a price set by the purchasers' desire and ability to pay where supply cannot be increased, fits the junction's core toll more exactly than any other. The gathered network cannot be supplied by a rival at any price within the time that matters, because its supply is the crowd's accumulated habit and mutual presence, and the toll is therefore limited by the traffic's dependence and resources rather than by any cost of production or any rival's undercutting. Absolute rent, the most contested of the classical categories, arose from the barrier that private property in land itself interposes between capital and a sphere of production, exacting a payment for entry even to the worst plot. Its analogue is the basic commission, the entry toll every seller, worker, and advertiser pays for passage regardless of position or audience quality, the payment that exists because the passage is owned at all. A classification exercise that can assign the junction's revenue streams to differential, monopoly, and absolute elements, and can argue the assignments, has demonstrated something worth more than the verdict it supports: it has shown the old theory's internal machinery still turning under a load its makers never imagined, discriminating within the new object's income as it once discriminated within the landlord's. Theories are vindicated by exactly such unglamorous endurance. The platform did not refute the rent theory by being digital. It gave the rent theory its most extensive new province since the urban ground rent of the industrial city, and the province's administration requires nothing the theory does not already stock, except analysts willing to do the assigning.

## Crisis, saturation, and the junction's vulnerabilities

A rent position looks eternal from inside the traffic's dependence, and the tradition, which watched landed property survive every prediction of its irrelevance, will not be hurried into declaring the junction permanent. But rent positions have their own crisis forms, distinct from the crises of productive capital, and mapping them completes the analysis by showing where the form is brittle. The first vulnerability is the crowd's migration. The gathered asset consists of habits and relationships, and habits, however locked in by the archive's non portability, can break when a rival passage offers the crowd a reason to move together, since network effects that compounded the leader's advantage can compound a challenger's once a tipping threshold of migration is crossed. The history of communication networks is littered with dominant passages abandoned within a few seasons, their fences intact and their crowds gone, and the litter is a warning against treating any junction's rent as a freehold rather than a leasehold on the crowd's attention. The second vulnerability is the payer's revolt: the sellers, advertisers, and business tenants whose surplus funds the toll are themselves capitals with interests, capable of combination, of building common passages, of shifting their activity's centre of gravity to channels the junction does not govern, and the toll's height is in the long run set by their tolerance as much as by the crowd's dependence. The third vulnerability is saturation of the harvest itself: the behavioural archive's refinement faces diminishing returns, the prediction products approach the ceiling of what past behaviour can foretell, and the advertiser's willingness to pay for marginal improvements in targeting declines as every rival buys the same improvements, so that the rent on attention is squeezed by the very universality of its sale. None of these vulnerabilities abolishes the form. Together they describe a rentier structure whose income must be continually defended by deepening the enclosure, acquiring the passages the crowd might migrate to, and extending the fence into new territories of life, which is to say the crisis tendencies explain the expansionary restlessness that observers mistake for the sector's creative energy. The junction expands because standing still is how its rent dies, and an analysis that grasps the compulsion to expand has grasped the form's motor more exactly than any celebration of its innovativeness.

## The city and the street: the junction's spatial life

The platform is often described as placeless, a cloud above geography, and the description is one more novelty claim the analysis should refuse, because the junction's operations are intensely spatial and its rent has a geography as legible as any landlord's. The lean junction reorganises the city's movement, summoning vehicles to the districts its predictions favour, thinning service where the traffic's spending power is low, and converting the kerb, the doorway, and the pavement into contested stages of a logistics the municipality did not plan. The marketplace junction reorganises retail geography, emptying the high street whose rents it undercut while raising, through its warehouses and delivery networks, a new geography of sheds and routes at the city's edge. The short stay junction reorganises housing, converting dwellings into accommodation stock and bidding residential rents toward the level a visitor's nightly payment can sustain, which is ground rent's oldest mechanism executed through a booking passage. In each case the platform's toll is entangled with the city's older rents, sometimes competing with them, sometimes collecting them, sometimes arbitraging between them, and the entanglement confirms the classification from an unexpected direction: the junction behaves, spatially, as a new storey built on the rent structure of the urban economy, drawing income from location, scarcity, and access in combinations the theory of ground rent already parses. A form that were truly a new mode of production might be expected to abolish the old geography. The platform instead annotates it, street by street, and the annotations are written in rent.

## An analyst's walkthrough: decomposing one junction's income in imagination

The decomposition discipline this article recommends can be taught in a single imagined exercise, and the exercise is worth performing in prose because it converts the verdict from an opinion into a procedure any reader can run on any platform firm. Imagine a mature junction whose accounts show a large and growing income, and interrogate the income in the order the tradition prescribes. First, set aside every stream that pays for a produced thing: the hire of computing capacity insofar as it tracks the apparatus's costs and ordinary return, the sale of goods the firm itself produces or stocks, the fees for services whose performance consumes the firm's own waged labour in proportion to the fee. Those streams belong to production and circulation, and their classification follows the older analyses without remainder. Second, isolate the streams that pay for passage: the commission on other parties' transactions, the charge for access to the assembled audience, the subscription that rents the firm's catalogue, the fee for prominence inside the junction's own geography. For each, ask the dependence question: what would happen to this stream if the crowd, the sellers, or the tenants could depart tomorrow, carrying their records, reputations, and relationships to a rival passage of equal technical quality. The portion of the stream that would vanish with the departure is rent, since it was paid for the fence rather than for the service. The portion that would survive, because the service itself is superior or cheaper, is the service's genuine price. Third, ask the maturity question: as the junction has aged, which portion has grown faster, the passage streams or the produced streams, and has the passage portion's growth been accompanied by investment in service or by investment in the fence, in acquisitions of potential passages, in defaults and pre installations, in the deepening of the archive. The answers, assembled, yield the firm's rent proportion and its trajectory, and with them a judgement that no slogan about the firm, favourable or hostile, can supply. Run the exercise on the advertising junction and the rent proportion dominates. Run it on the cloud junction and the proportions contend. Run it on the lean junction and the commission stands exposed as rent stacked upon the worker's exploited production. The procedure's portability across the types is the taxonomy's final vindication: five objects, one procedure, five different answers, and an analysis that can say why each answer differs. That is what grading a subject, rather than denouncing or celebrating it, looks like when it is finished.

## Machinery and large scale industry: the chapter the platform rewrites least

The tradition's most famous treatment of a technological transformation is the chapter on machinery and large scale industry in the first volume of Capital, and setting the platform beside it yields a comparison that disciplines both the boosters and the debunkers of digital novelty. Marx's machinery analysis refused two errors at once: the error of blaming the machine for effects that belonged to the relations governing its use, and the error of treating the machine as a neutral tool whose effects relations merely coloured. The machine, in his handling, really did transform the labour process, dissolving craft skill, drawing new populations into production, lengthening and intensifying the working day where law permitted, and cheapening commodities by revolutionising the productivity of labour. The transformation was material and irreversible, and its social meaning was set by the property relations into which the machine was inserted. The platform invites the same double refusal. Its instruments really do transform the processes they touch: coordination at a granularity no despatch office achieved, observation no overseer matched, prediction no merchant's intuition rivalled. Denying the transformation, in the debunker's manner, is as unserious as the booster's inference from it. But the transformation's social meaning, the question of whose income it swells and whose dependence it deepens, is set by the property relation at the junction, and on that question the platform's chapter rewrites remarkably little of the older text. The machine cheapened commodities and concentrated capital; the junction cheapens coordination and concentrates access. The machine's owner appropriated the productivity of social knowledge embodied in the system; the junction's owner appropriates the social knowledge embodied in the archive. The parallel is not an analogy imposed for elegance. It is the same analytical operation performed on two objects, and its yield is the same verdict shape: real transformation of process, continuity of relation, and a politics addressed to the relation rather than to the instrument. Readers who master the operation on the platform will find they have also mastered it for the machine, which is why this subject, fashionable as it is, belongs in a series devoted to a tradition a century and a half old. The newest object in the economy turns out to be one of the best classrooms for the oldest method in the archive.

## The other side of the advertising market: capitals buying passage

The advertising transaction has been traced from the platform's side and from the user's; its third side, the advertiser's, completes the circuit and supplies the value dispute with a final body of evidence. Advertisers are capitals engaged in the realisation of value embodied in commodities, and their purchase of passage is a circulation expenditure competing with every other circulation expenditure: the sales force, the shopfront, the packaging, the discount. From the advertiser's ledger, the platform is a landlord of attention in the most literal bookkeeping sense, a supplier of access whose charges are scrutinised exactly as rents are scrutinised, resented as a fixed cost of reaching the market, renegotiated when alternative passages appear, and passed, where competition permits, into the prices of the commodities whose sale the access serves. The advertiser does not experience itself as an employer of the audience, does not organise the audience's activity, does not discipline it, and would find the suggestion that it exploits the audience's labour unintelligible as a description of its own accounts, in which the audience figures as a market to be reached at a price. The self understanding of capital is not, as this article has cautioned, an authority. But the convergence of every participant's bookkeeping on the same anatomy, the platform's own accounts treating the crowd as an asset, the advertiser's treating access as a cost of circulation, the seller's statement itemising the toll as a deduction from margin, is the kind of evidence a classification dispute should prize, because it shows the mechanism wearing the same shape from every vantage inside it. The free labour account requires all three bookkeepings to be systematically deceived about the transaction they record, and while the tradition has never shrunk from finding ideology in accounts, a deception so uniform, so functional, and so invisible to every party except the theorist should give the theorist pause. The rent reading requires no deception at all: the accounts say a toll is charged at an owned passage and paid from the surplus of the commodities carried through it, and the theory replies that the accounts, for once, are describing the mechanism exactly as it is.

## Passages through public life: the junction beyond commerce

The intermediation form does not stop at commerce's border, and its migration into the organisation of public and civic life extends the analysis in a direction the tradition is well equipped to follow. Where access to public services, to education, to health provision, to civic information, and to the administration of entitlements is routed through privately owned passages, the junction's logic enters territories where the traffic cannot, even in principle, take its dependence elsewhere, since the service is a right or a necessity rather than a purchase. The toll in such territories may be charged to the public authority rather than to the citizen, or extracted as the archive of a population's dealings with its own institutions, but the structure is the structure this article has anatomised: an owned passage, a gathered and dependent traffic, an income or an asset drawn from position. The tradition's name for the underlying process is the commodification of the commons extended into the state itself, and its analysis of earlier waves, the enclosure of public utilities, the contracting out of public functions, supplies the platform wave with its interpretive frame. What is new is again the instrument rather than the relation: the passage does not merely administer the service but records the population's use of it in a proprietary archive, so that the enclosure captures not only the function's revenue but the knowledge of the function's users, an asset that outlasts any contract and that no earlier contractor could accumulate. A citizenry whose dealings with its own collective provision are archived as a firm's private property has undergone an enclosure of a peculiarly intimate kind, and the commoner figure from this article's cast list here reaches its fullest stature: the population dispossessed, through the passages of its own public life, of the record of that life. The classification of the income involved follows the by now familiar route. Fees for administering a service are circulation's wages at one remove; the durable yield, the archive and the dependence, is rent's territory, and the remedy the classification prescribes is the remedy of the commons: passages for public functions held as public property, with their archives public too.

## Why the dispute matters beyond the platform

It is fair to ask, at this length, why a classification quarrel inside one tradition, about one sector, deserves the attention this article has demanded, and the answer reaches beyond the sector to the condition of the tradition itself. Every generation of Marxist analysis faces an object that arrives trailing claims of civilisational novelty, and the tradition's credibility, with friend and foe alike, depends on how it handles the arrival. Handled by annexation, by stretching the central categories until the new object fits inside them, the tradition confirms the suspicion that its categories are elastic enough to mean anything and therefore explain nothing. Handled by refusal, by denying that anything has changed and filing the object under the nearest old heading without examining its instruments, the tradition confirms the opposite suspicion, that its categories are a museum's labels pasted over a world that has moved on. The platform dispute is valuable because it forced the third handling into the open: criteria stated in advance, the new object's genuine novelties conceded and located with precision, the classification following the criteria rather than the sympathies, the verdict published with its defeater. That handling is the tradition's method at its best, and it is rarer than either annexation or refusal because it is harder than both, requiring the analyst to resist the applause that annexation earns from the converted and the applause that refusal earns from the orthodox. A reader who learns to recognise the third handling, and to demand it, has acquired the skill this whole series exists to transmit, of which the platform verdict is one exercise among many. The next novel object is already being announced somewhere, with its annexationists and its refusers already in print. The criteria are already written. The dispute, in that sense, is not about the platform at all. It is about whether a tradition of analysis can still analyse, and the platform, indifferent to the honour, has provided the test.

## The global chain beneath the interface

Fuchs's chain, invoked earlier for its relabelling, deserves a fuller passage of its own, because the platform economy's geography is the strongest available corrective to the impression, which the interface cultivates, that the digital world is weightless. Behind every junction stands a material sequence of extraordinary brutality and ordinariness combined: minerals torn from the earth by labourers whose compulsion is not algorithmic but geological and economic in the oldest senses, metals refined under conditions the tradition's archive of industrial horror describes without need of updating, devices assembled by workers whose working days are measured, disciplined, and extended by methods continuous with the factory system's classical forms, cables laid across ocean floors, warehouses staffed by pickers whose movements are timed by systems that are the lean junction's discipline turned inward on a waged workforce, and at the sequence's end the discarded devices returning as toxic salvage to be picked apart by the world's poorest hands. Value is produced along that chain in quantities the criteria assess without strain, and surplus value is extracted at its links by employers whose relation to their workers would be recognised by any nineteenth century inspector of factories. The platform junction sits atop the chain as its toll collecting crown, and the rent reading's tracing exercise, applied globally, yields the dispute's most sobering picture: the tolls gathered in the economy's cleanest rooms are paid, through the circuit of advertisers, firms, and subscribers, from a surplus substantially produced in its dirtiest ones, and the crowd whose activity supposedly creates the platform's wealth is, in the global division of labour, among the least exploited populations in the entire sequence, outranked in misery by every miner, assembler, picker, and salvager whose names the interface never displays. That ordering does not minimise the crowd's enclosure. It situates it, and a politics that cannot situate its grievances in the global order of extraction will mistake the chain's crown for its foundation and organise at the wrong end. The tradition's internationalism was always, at bottom, this situating discipline: the insistence that a local relation be read within the world division of labour that funds it. The platform, which presents itself as the most placeless of forms, turns out to require the discipline most urgently, since its placelessness is precisely the disguise its funding wears.

## Consent at the gate: the fiction that guards the fence

Every enclosure in the tradition's archive was accompanied by a legal story that made it appear as something other than a taking, and the junction's story deserves the same sceptical reading the tradition gave to the stories of earlier enclosures. The junction's story is consent. The user agreed to the terms; the worker accepted the contract; the seller signed the marketplace's conditions; the archive was compiled, the toll was levied, and the reputation was imprisoned, all under agreements that the parties, in the law's fiction, freely made. The tradition's critique of contractual freedom in the labour market applies to the gate with undiminished force and in an aggravated form. The classical worker at least confronted a contract whose central terms, wages and hours, were stated in numbers and could be compared across employers. The junction's terms are a private legislation of indefinite length, revised at the owner's pleasure, presented on a take it or leave it basis to parties whose alternative is exclusion from the gathered crowd, which is to say from the market, the audience, the social world, or the trade itself. Consent given under a dependence the consent itself deepens is the form of freedom the tradition has always recognised as compatible with the substance of compulsion, and the recognition does not require the consenting party to be deceived or foolish. The crowd is neither. It reads the situation accurately: the passage is where the people are, the toll is the price of the people, and the price, resented or not, is paid. What the analysis adds is only the refusal to let the contract's form settle the relation's classification. A toll paid under agreement is still a toll. An enclosure accepted for want of an outside is still an enclosure, and the long history of enclosures accepted, signed, and even petitioned for by populations with no better option is the archive in which the junction's consent forms will eventually be filed.

## Free time and its capture: the oldest stake in the dispute

Beneath the classification quarrel lies the oldest stake in the tradition's politics, and a closing analytical section should name it, because it explains the heat the dispute generates out of proportion to its technical surface. The stake is free time. The tradition's vision of a society beyond capital was never, at its deepest, a vision of fuller employment or fairer wages; it was a vision of the expansion of the time in which human beings develop themselves outside the discipline of necessary labour, the realm of freedom that begins where labour determined by necessity ends. The platform economy's significance, in that long perspective, is that capital has learned to draw revenue from the realm of freedom itself, not by lengthening the working day in the old manner but by enclosing the spaces in which free time is spent and harvesting the residue of its enjoyment. The feed that fills the vacancy, the stream that fills the evening, the game that fills the journey: each converts the hours beyond work into a traffic from which a toll of attention and data is drawn, and the conversion is experienced, accurately, as a kind of colonisation of life by the economy's logic. That experience finds its theoretical registration in the free labour account, whose error is one of address rather than of perception: it locates the colonisation in a labour relation, where the tradition's own categories direct it to the enclosure of a commons, the commons in this case being the population's uncommodified time and sociability. The correction matters for the politics of free time above all. If the evening's capture is employment, the remedy is the evening's wage, a coin so small and a legitimation so large that the capture would survive it triumphantly. If the evening's capture is enclosure, the remedy is the defence and extension of unenclosed time and unenclosed sociability: spaces, media, and institutions whose use leaves no residue in any owner's vault, and whose value to a civilisation the tradition can state in its oldest language, as the realm of freedom's necessary ground. The dispute's technical verdict thus opens onto the tradition's largest horizon, and a reader who has followed the criteria through forty pages of tolls and archives has earned the sight of it: what is being fenced, at the junction, is not only a market. It is the population's life outside the market, and the fence's rent is the measure of how far the market's logic has followed life home.

## The household ledger: subscriptions, tolls, and the rentier texture of ordinary budgets

Follow the classification into the household's monthly arithmetic and it acquires a texture that aggregate analysis misses. The ordinary budget in a platform saturated economy increasingly resembles a rent roll: recurring payments for access to music and moving images once owned outright, for storage of the family's photographs, for the software with which work is done, for passage to markets, audiences, and employments, each payment small, each cancellation costly in lost archives, lost contacts, lost convenience, and the sum of them a standing charge on income that behaves exactly as rent behaves in a household's experience, prior to every other expenditure, inflexible downward, and owed to owners whose names the payer may barely know. The tradition analysed the working class budget when its great recurring charges were rent for housing and the price of bread, and the analysis of exploitation was incomplete until it followed the wage home and watched it be claimed. A contemporary analysis owes the same follow through. The wage is now claimed, before it is spent on anything the tradition would recognise as subsistence, by a sheaf of access charges for the infrastructures of ordinary life, and the proportion of income those charges command is a measure of the enclosure's depth that no firm's accounts reveal. Where the charges purchase passage to employment itself, the circuit closes with a neatness the nineteenth century would have recognised from the truck system and the tied cottage: the worker pays the junction for the right to be exploited, and pays again for the tools, the vehicle, the storage, and the connection through which the exploitation is organised. Naming the sheaf a rent roll is not a metaphor indulged for colour. It is the classification doing its final work, at the scale of the kitchen table, where classifications are ultimately verified by whether they organise the experience of the people living under the relations they describe.

## Cooperative and municipal passages: the alternatives examined without romance

The alternatives spectrum sketched earlier deserves one harder look, because the tradition's students are entitled to ask whether the passages that claim to dissolve the fence actually do, and the examination strengthens the classification by testing its criterion against the candidates. Platform cooperativism proposes that the workers and users of a junction own it themselves, converting the toll into a collectively governed surplus and the private legislation into a constitution. The proposal's virtue is that it aims at the property relation rather than at the toll's level, which the classification identifies as the correct target. Its difficulty is the network effect's indifference to virtue: a cooperative passage must still gather the crowd against an incumbent whose gathering is complete, and the crowd's migration, on which everything depends, is governed by usefulness rather than by sympathy, so cooperative passages survive most readily in niches where the incumbent's advantages are thinnest, in local services, in professional communities, in markets where trust is the scarce input and ownership by the trusted is itself an attraction. Municipal passages face the mirror difficulty: a public owner can compel gathering, by making the passage the official route to a service, but compulsion converts the fence into an arm of administration and imports the state's own temptations, surveillance with a public mandate and exclusion without a competitor's check. The classification grades both candidates by the single test derived earlier: does the arrangement dissolve the dependence that sustains the rent, by making departure possible, records portable, and governance answerable, or does it merely change the toll collector's identity. Some cooperative and municipal passages pass the test in part, and partial passage is not failure; a fence lowered is a fence lowered. But the test prevents the ownership label from doing duty for the mechanism, and it explains the melancholy pattern in which alternative passages reproduce the incumbent's manners within a few seasons of gathering their own crowds. The crowd, gathered, is an asset whoever owns it, and only arrangements that deliberately disable the asset's enclosure, by portability, interoperability, and the archive's common ownership, resist the reproduction. The tradition's realism about its own alternatives is not pessimism. It is the classification's last lesson: relations are dissolved by dissolving their mechanisms, not by renaming their beneficiaries.

## The argument in one movement, for the reader who must reproduce it

Compression is a test of understanding, and the article's whole case can be carried in a single movement of thought that a reader should be able to reproduce, in their own words, after one reading. Platforms own passages between parties and charge for the crossing. The charge's classification depends on what the crossing's participants are doing. Where a participant labours under a discipline that measures performance against a social standard and punishes failure with exclusion from livelihood, as the coordinated worker does under algorithmic allocation, value is produced and the classical analysis of exploitation applies, with the passage's commission stacked upon it as a second extraction. Where a participant merely attends, posts, watches, and wanders, as the user does, no such discipline operates, nothing equalises the activity into abstract labour, and the platform's income from that participant's presence must be traced to the funds of those who pay to reach the assembled crowd, which are funds of surplus value created in commodity production across the economy. Income drawn from a monopolised passage, capped by the traffic's dependence and capitalised by finance as a claim on future tolls, is rent by every test the tradition stocks: by its source, by its limits, by its price behaviour, by its legal anatomy, and by the remedies it prescribes. The user's genuine injury is the enclosure of a collectively produced world, the social graph and the behavioural archive, inside private property, and its remedies are the commons remedies of portability, interoperability, and common ownership, not the wage remedies of a workforce that does not exist. That is the case. Its defeater stands where this article placed it: show the discipline governing the user's activity in the market's own form, or show the tolls paid from a fund that production never filled, and the case falls. Until then, the junction is a fence, the crowd is its commoners, and the toll is rent.

## Work on the self: profiles, reputations, and the border territory revisited

The border territory identified earlier, where user activity approaches the criteria, has a populous province that deserves its own inspection, because it is where the largest number of people now experience something that genuinely feels like unwaged work for a junction. The province is the work of self presentation: the maintenance of a profile, the cultivation of a following, the production of content intended to build a reputation that might, someday, be converted into income. Millions perform this work with a discipline that is entirely real: posting to schedules, studying the allocation system's preferences, shaping their personalities into legible brands, enduring the anxiety of metrics whose movements govern their visibility. If any user activity were to satisfy the compulsion criterion, this would be it, since the aspiring creator's activity is measured, compared, and punished by invisibility in a manner that mimics the market's discipline with cruel fidelity. The inspection must therefore be exact. What the creator's discipline lacks, until the moment of monetisation, is the exchange relation itself: the activity is performed not for a buyer but for a gatekeeper, in hope of admission to the position where buyers appear, and its economics resemble less the wage relation than the economics of the audition, the apprenticeship, the speculative venture of the petty producer building goodwill in a shop not yet profitable. Goodwill, reputation, and audience are assets the creator builds on rented ground, and the tradition's category for the arrangement is tenancy again: the creator is a tenant farmer of attention, improving a plot whose title belongs to the junction, liable to eviction by algorithm, and entitled to none of the plot's appreciation when the junction sells access to the crowd the creator helped gather. Where monetisation arrives, the analysis shifts by degrees toward the putting out relation described earlier, since the junction now commissions, in effect, the content that holds the crowd, sets the standards by demonetisation, and takes its share of every payment the content earns. The province thus spans the cast list from commoner through tenant to outworker, and its span is the taxonomy's lesson one final time: the question is never what the activity feels like, though the feeling is evidence, but which mechanism governs it, and the mechanisms here are the gate, the tenancy, and only at the monetised margin the commission. A literature that calls the whole province labour has mistaken the province's most poignant feature, that its inhabitants work like labourers while standing in relations of tenancy and enclosure that labour's remedies cannot reach.

## A note on sources, and on what this article refused to use

Method is usually left implicit, and a short explicit passage can serve the student reader who wishes to judge the article's own discipline by the standards it applied to others. The article's positive sources are the tradition's primary texts on value, rent, machinery, and merchant capital; the named contributions to the platform literature, Srnicek's taxonomy of 2016, Terranova's formulation of 2000, Smythe's audience commodity of 1977, Fuchs's systematic development of 2014, the intermediate positions of Arvidsson and Colleoni and of Rigi and Prey, Rosenblat's ethnography of information asymmetry, the synthesis of Kellogg, Valentine, and Christin on algorithmic control, the gig economy studies of Wood and colleagues, Christophers's rentier synthesis of 2020, and Zuboff's surveillance account of 2019 together with its critics. Quotations were rationed to a handful of phrases under fifteen words each, one per source, because the article's claims rest on arguments that paraphrase carries more honestly than quotation decorates. The refusals are as much a part of the method as the sources. No firm, product, service, application, or brand is named, so that no verdict can borrow authority from a reader's feelings about a company. No figure for market share, revenue, users, workforce, or earnings appears, so that no magnitude can impersonate a mechanism. No regulatory episode is narrated, so that the analysis cannot be dated by its examples and discarded with them. The refusals implement the structural discipline the run of articles to which this one belongs imposed on itself: to write about the present in a manner that a reader a decade hence could not date from internal evidence, because the mechanisms described would still be the mechanisms at work. Whether the discipline succeeded is for that reader to judge. That it was attempted, on the record, is the article's final claim on the trust of the reader it has.

## The junction and the small hours: care, kinship, and the social graph's substance

The social graph has been treated so far as an asset and an archive, and a section on its substance is owed, because what the graph records is not traffic in the abstract but the population's care for one another, and the character of that substance sharpens the enclosure's meaning. The connections a communication junction gathers are the sediment of kinship maintained across distance, of friendships tended, of communities of affliction and interest and worship and play, of the thousand small attentions by which people hold one another in existence. Much of that tending is labour in the honourable ordinary sense, the unpaid reproductive work of social life, disproportionately performed by those the tradition's feminist analysts taught us to see, and it is performed for love, duty, loneliness, and joy, in proportions no analyst can separate. The junction did not commission this work, does not direct it, and could not purchase its equivalent at any wage, since its value to the junction derives precisely from its authenticity, from the fact that the connections are real bonds rather than manufactured engagement. What the junction does is host the tending inside walls it owns, record it in forms it controls, forbid its removal, and sell access to the map of it. The sequence deserves its plain name in the tradition's vocabulary of appropriation: a collectively and lovingly produced commons, enclosed by the party that provided its meeting ground, as a village green's owner might claim the harvest of every conversation held upon it. Stating the substance plainly also corrects a sentimental error in the opposite direction. The crowd is not a community that the junction betrayed, because the crowd was never gathered by the junction's promise of commons; it was gathered by the people's need for one another, routed through the only passage that reached everyone. The betrayal narrative flatters the junction with a founding intention it never had. The enclosure narrative needs no intention at all: only a meeting ground, an owner, a record, and a toll, assembled in that order while the people were busy with one another.

## Taxation and the public share: the state's quiet partnership in the toll

One further relation completes the anatomy, the relation joining the junction's rent to the public purse, and it can be described structurally, without narrating any jurisdiction's choices, by following the tradition's treatment of rent's fiscal history. Ground rent was always partly socialised in practice: the land tax, the rates, the public capture of increments in land value created by public works, each represented the community reclaiming a share of an income that the community's own growth had created. The political argument for the reclamation wrote itself once the classification was granted, since an income arising from position rather than production, and swollen by the surrounding society's development rather than by the owner's exertion, presents the weakest possible moral title against taxation. The junction's rent reproduces the argument with the elements intensified. The crowd that makes the passage valuable is the public itself; the infrastructure beneath the passage was substantially built by public investment and public research; the legal lattice that constitutes the fence is publicly enforced; and the archive's value is the public's own life, recorded. A fiscal order that taxes the junction's rent lightly while taxing wages at their full weight has made a classification decision by default, treating the toll as though it were the fragile yield of production whose discouragement would impoverish everyone, when the classification shows it to be the most taxable income in the economy, the one whose taxation discourages nothing but the fence's height. The tradition's fiscal thinkers drew exactly that conclusion for land, and their conclusion's migration to the junction requires no new premise, only the verdict this article has argued. Whether any public authority acts on the migration is a political question outside the analysis. That the analysis identifies the junction's rent as the natural first object of a rational fiscal order, ahead of every wage and every productive margin, is a consequence worth stating in the open, since consequences stated in the open are how classifications prove they were arguments rather than decorations.

## The long view: what a reader a decade hence should still find standing

The structural discipline imposed on this article can be tested only by imagining its future reader, and the exercise is a fitting close to the analytical sections because it restates, as a wager, everything the method has claimed. That reader will inhabit a platform economy whose firms have renamed themselves, whose dominant passages may have migrated or fallen, whose instruments will have refined the record's resolution and the allocation's granularity beyond the present descriptions, and whose public arguments will have cycled through several new vocabularies for the same relations. What should still be standing for that reader is the anatomy: an owned passage, a gathered crowd, a toll limited by dependence, an archive held as property, a workforce at the junction's coordinated edge labouring under a discipline that genuinely produces value, and a fund of surplus, created in production across the economy, from which every toll is finally paid. If the anatomy still describes the reader's world, the article's wager on mechanism over instance has been honoured by events. If some passage has emerged whose users labour under the market's own compulsion, whose performances are equalised and whose failures are punished by destitution, that reader holds in this article the defeater that instructs them to reverse its verdict, and the reversal will be the method's success rather than its failure, since a method that publishes the conditions of its own correction has done more for its future readers than a verdict that merely happened to survive. The tradition this series serves has lasted by exactly that discipline across a century and a half of objects designed to refute it. The platform was the newest design. The criteria are still standing next to it, unrelaxed, and the toll is still rent.

## Habits of the reader: how to interrogate the next platform

The article's method can be left behind as a habit, stated as a sequence of interrogations to be performed on any platform, announced or rumoured, before its publicity has set. Ask first what the passage connects and what it owns, refusing every description that begins with the service's benefits to its users, since benefits are the fence's flowers and the questions concern the fence. Ask second who labours, under what discipline, measured against what standard, punished how, and let the answers sort the population into producers, circulators, and the gathered, without permitting the interface's presentation to merge them. Ask third who pays, from which fund, and follow each payment backward through the payer's accounts until it rests in a surplus whose production can be located, because a payment that cannot be traced to production is either rent's confession or the analysis's unfinished business. Ask fourth what the departure of each class of participant would cost them, in records, reputations, relationships, and reach, since the departure cost is the toll's ceiling and the enclosure's depth gauge in a single number's clothing. Ask fifth what the owner does when the gathering falters, since the defence repertoire, subsidies, acquisitions, defaults, the tightening of terms, reveals the asset's nature more honestly than the prospectus. Ask sixth, and last, what observation would overturn the classification the first five answers produced, and write the answer down before publishing the classification, so that the verdict enters the world with its correction already addressed to whoever finds the evidence. A reader who performs the six interrogations on the next celebrated passage will find that the celebration survives approximately none of them intact, and that what remains standing, when the interrogations finish, is an analysis: unglamorous, decomposable, and true in the only sense this tradition has ever claimed for its findings, that its categories have been applied without fear or favour to an object that would have preferred adjectives.

## The crowd's knowledge of itself: a final asymmetry

Every asymmetry examined in this article has run in one direction, from the junction's knowledge of the crowd toward the crowd's ignorance of the junction, and a closing observation should register the one asymmetry that runs the other way, because it contains the analysis's only grounds for optimism and the tradition's oldest political wager. The junction knows the crowd's behaviour in aggregate and in detail, but it does not know, and cannot purchase, the crowd's knowledge of its own condition: the seller's exact resentment of the commission, the worker's private map of the allocation system's habits, the user's dawning sense that the passage home is owned. Such knowledge circulates in the crowd's own channels, in the forums and conversations where participants compare scores, fees, and suspicions, pooling their fragments of the board into a picture no individual was shown. The tradition has a name for the process by which a population in a shared relation comes to know that relation collectively, and the name's career, from the factory floor to the tenant's association, is the history of every successful contestation of a rent or an exploitation this archive records. The junction's countermeasure is the personalisation examined earlier, the fragmentation of the crowd's experience into private feeds in which no two members see the same board and comparison loses its common object. The contest between the crowd's pooling of knowledge and the junction's fragmentation of it is therefore the political form the classification dispute takes in practice, and its outcome will decide whether the toll's height is set by the owner's calculation alone or by an organised knowledge of the dependence on the other side. Analysis cannot decide that contest. It can only hand the crowd the anatomy of its own position, criteria, cast list, and fund, and note, as the tradition has noted at every fence it ever described, that a crowd which understands the fence has already begun, in the only way that finally matters, to be something other than a crowd.

## The junction's seasons: gathering, tightening, and the archaeology of terms

A platform's life, read through its terms and charges rather than through its announcements, falls into seasons that the rent reading predicts and the other accounts merely describe. The first season is the gathering, in which passage is free or subsidised, standards are generous, early participants are courted with the owner's money, and the language of the enterprise is the language of community and openness. The gathering is not hypocrisy laid over the business; it is the business's first phase, the purchase of the crowd whose presence will constitute the asset, and its generosity is budgeted as exactly that purchase. The second season is the entrenchment, in which the passage becomes the default route for its traffic, rivals wither for want of participation, records and reputations accumulate inside the walls, and the costs of departure quietly compound. Little changes in the terms during entrenchment, because nothing needs to: dependence is doing the work that charges will later harvest. The third season is the tightening, in which commissions rise, free access narrows, prominence is sold, the terms are revised in the owner's favour at intervals short enough to prevent organised response and long enough to be absorbed as weather, and the enterprise's language shifts from community to sustainability, as though the toll were a reluctant necessity imposed on the owner by arithmetic. The fourth season, where it arrives, is the estate's maturity, in which the passage is simply the environment, its charges as unremarkable as the price of land beneath a city, and its ownership a fact of the landscape that politics must excavate to make visible again. Reading a junction's season from its charges is a skill the classification makes possible, and it has a practical corollary for every figure in the cast: the time to contest a fence is the gathering, when the owner still needs the crowd more than the crowd needs the passage, and every season after that raises the price of contest by exactly the dependence the seasons manufacture. Movements that understand the calendar organise early. The tradition, which watched the factory system's seasons and the enclosures' seasons before this, recognises the calendar's shape, and recognition is the beginning of timing.

## What the junction teaches about reading an economy

Step back from the verdict and the subject one last time, and the platform's largest service to a reader of this series comes into view: it is a training ground on which every instrument in the tradition's kit must be used at once, in public, on an object that resists them all. The taxonomy teaches decomposition. The criteria teach the difference between a category and a compliment. The tracing of funds teaches that wealth at a point of collection is not wealth at a point of origin, and that the distance between the two is where distribution, power, and rent live. The cast list teaches that a wronged population is usually several populations, whose injuries differ and whose remedies must be fitted one by one. The defeater teaches that a verdict is a commitment to the evidence that would reverse it. The lineage teaches that novelty is a property of instruments before it is a property of relations, and that the analyst's first duty to a new object is the unfashionable question of what it resembles. None of these lessons is about the digital economy in particular, which is why the digital economy, studied properly, teaches them better than most subjects: its glamour makes the lessons necessary, since no reader is tempted to apply the kit carelessly to a subject they find boring, and the platform is nothing if not interesting. The tradition survives, when it survives, not by the loyalty of its adherents but by the continued sharpness of its instruments in hands willing to use them on whatever the age supplies. This article has used them on the age's favourite object and found the toll to be rent, the crowd to be commoners, the worker to be exploited in the old way by new instruments, and the criteria to be standing. A reader who disputes any of those findings now possesses, in the article itself, the standards by which to dispute them, which is the only inheritance a work of analysis can honestly leave.

## The junction abroad: passages between economies

The analysis has proceeded as though a junction's crowd, workers, and payers shared one economy, and the simplification should be relaxed before the personas are reached, because the platform form is international in its skeleton and the relaxation tests the classification against the world market's frictions. A passage may gather its crowd in one country, coordinate its workers in a second, sell its audiences to advertisers in a third, and book its income in a fourth chosen for the gentleness of its fiscal climate. Each crossing of a border by one element of the arrangement multiplies the analyst's tracing work without altering its logic. The worker's labour is disciplined where the worker stands, and its product's value is governed by the standards of the market the service enters. The crowd's enclosure is constituted where the crowd lives, under whatever property settlement its own legal order supplies, though the passage's terms are written wherever the owner prefers. The toll, when it is finally booked, may surface in a jurisdiction that hosted neither the production that funded it nor the dependence that sustained it, and the fiscal anatomy of that surfacing is a study in itself. What the international skeleton adds to the dispute is a further test of the rival accounts, and both should be made to sit it. If users produced the platform's value, the geography of value production would follow the geography of use, and the world's surplus would be substantially created wherever crowds gather with devices in their hands, a conclusion that inverts everything the tradition knows about the location of production in the world economy. On the rent reading the geography coheres: production remains where disciplined labour and produced means of production combine, disproportionately in the world's manufacturing and extractive belts, while the tolls gather where the passages are owned and booked, disproportionately in the economies that host the owners. The map of platform income and the map of platform use diverge, on this reading, for the same reason the map of ground rent and the map of agriculture diverged in the classical world: the owner of a condition need not live where the condition is used, and the fund that pays the owner is filled wherever the conditioned activity produces its surplus. A form that lets a passage in one hemisphere tax production in another is not a refutation of the old categories. It is their longest journey, and they arrive with their discriminations intact.

## Doubts at the margin: three honest difficulties for the verdict

A verdict that has survived its opponents deserves, as a final discipline, to face the difficulties its own defenders find hardest, stated without the softening that advocacy applies. The first difficulty concerns the engineers of prediction. This article classified much of the junction's internal labour under circulation and supervision, yet the prediction systems that labour builds are sold as products in their own right to firms that use them in production, and a product embodying waged labour, sold into other capitals' production processes, presses toward the productive classification with a force the article's brisk treatment may have understated. The difficulty is real but bounded: it concerns the classification of one internal workforce's labour, not the classification of the toll, and conceding ground there moves the rent proportion within composite firms without touching the advertising and lean cases on which the verdict chiefly rests. The second difficulty concerns the crowd's activity at its most organised edge, the commissioned and disciplined production examined in the border territory, where the article conceded the classification trembles. If the monetised margin grows until the border territory becomes the centre, if junctions increasingly commission the activity they harvest and discipline it by livelihood, then the putting out analysis will claim territory the enclosure analysis now holds, and the verdict's centre of gravity will shift with the facts. The concession is the defeater wearing empirical dress, and it is recorded here so that the shift, if it comes, finds the article's readers prepared rather than betrayed. The third difficulty is the oldest in the tradition and concerns the category of rent itself, whose boundaries with profit have been contested since the classical economists, most sharply where the scarce condition is itself produced, as a junction is produced, rather than found, as land is found. Produced scarcity, the objection runs, is capital, and its return is profit on the capital embodied in the fence, however monopolistic the fence's operation. The article's answer has been the decomposition discipline: the return on the fence's produced substance is profit's share, and the return on the fence's position, on the gathered crowd no investment can summon twice, is rent's, and the two shares can be argued stream by stream. But the objection identifies the verdict's true frontier, the estimation of those shares in real firms, and honesty requires naming it as the place where the analysis passes from classification, which it has completed, to measurement, which it has only programmed. A reader who finishes the article knowing exactly where its certainty ends has been served better than a reader promised certainty everywhere, and the tradition, which has buried more total systems than any rival, should be the last to promise it.

## The archive's afterlife: memory, evidence, and the owned past

One asset of the junction has been mentioned throughout without receiving its own reckoning, and it may prove, over the longest horizon, to be the enclosure's most consequential element: the archive as the owned past. Every passage through the junction deposits a record, and the deposits accumulate into something no earlier owner of a chokepoint possessed, a continuous, searchable, privately held history of a population's communications, movements, purchases, labours, and associations. Landlords knew their tenants' rents were paid; they did not hold the transcript of the tenants' lives. The junction holds the transcript, and the holding has dimensions the income classification does not exhaust. As evidence, the archive is a power over the past's availability: what the owner retains can be produced, what the owner deletes is gone, and the historical record of an era increasingly conducted inside passages will be written under that asymmetry, as the historian's persona in this article warned. As leverage, the archive disciplines the present with the past's permanence: the worker's every scored performance, the seller's every dispute, the user's every youthful folly persists in the owner's vault, available to algorithms whose judgements the subject cannot appeal to any statute of limitations the passage has not chosen to grant. As capital, the archive appreciates with age in a manner unlike any wasting asset, because the past's completeness is what trains the predictions the owner sells, and each year of deposits deepens the moat around the passage more surely than any fence of standards. The tradition's categories reach this asset without strain, since an accumulated stock of appropriated material, yielding income through ownership and deepening the dependence of everyone recorded in it, is the enclosure's mature form, the common fully converted into an estate. But the conversion's completeness deserves a sentence of its own, because it marks the point where the analysis of platforms stops being only an analysis of income. A society whose past is private property has outsourced its memory to its toll collectors, and the recovery of that memory, its portability, its public custody, its subjects' rights over their own deposits, belongs on the commons programme beside the graph and the reputation, as the third item in the inventory of what the fence holds. The inventory is the article's practical residue: the graph, the reputation, the archive. Whoever would open the passage must carry all three out of the vault, or the crowd will leave its history behind as the price of its freedom, and a freedom purchased by amnesia is the enclosure's final toll.

## Reading the toll in the wild: three scenes

Abstractions that cannot survive contact with scenes are not yet analysis, and the article's classification should be walked through three ordinary scenes before it is allowed to rest, each scene chosen because a different figure stands at its centre and a different stream of the junction's income flows through it. In the first scene, a household cook of genuine talent prepares meals in a home kitchen and sells them through a delivery passage to neighbours who have learned to trust the cook's hand. The cook's labour meets every test the criteria impose: the meals are produced for exchange, the cook is disciplined by ratings whose decline throttles the flow of orders, by delivery windows whose breach is punished automatically, by the visible prices of rival cooks whose offerings the passage displays beside the cook's own. Value is produced in that kitchen as surely as in any workshop in the tradition's archive, and surplus is produced with it, since the cook works longer than the value of the ingredients, the fuel, and the passage's charges combined. Now watch the charges. The passage withholds its percentage before the cook is paid, sells the cook prominence without which the meals sink beneath the listings of rivals, and owns the entire history of the cook's reputation, so that a cook who leaves departs naked, stripped of the reviews that were the business's goodwill, to begin again as a stranger in another passage or in the street. The percentage and the prominence fee are not payment for any labour the passage performs at the moment of the meal. They are the toll on the crowd the passage gathered, the crowd of hungry neighbours whose gathering the cook did not accomplish and cannot reproduce, and the reputation's imprisonment is the fence's maintenance, the deliberate raising of the cook's departure cost so that the toll can rise in future seasons. Exploitation in the kitchen, rent at the gate, the two mechanisms stacked in one small business's accounts: the scene is the article in miniature, and a reader who can decompose the cook's statement can decompose any platform income in the economy.

In the second scene, a scholar in a small town maintains, over years, a page of local history inside a communication passage, gathering photographs, correcting dates, answering the questions of former residents scattered across the world, and building, entry by entry, the only archive of the town's past that the scattered community possesses. No money moves. The scholar is paid in gratitude, in the pleasure of the work, in the standing the page confers, and would be bewildered by the suggestion that the evenings spent scanning photographs were a working day. Yet the passage's position in the scene is anything but innocent. The archive exists inside the owner's walls, under the owner's terms, retrievable only through the owner's search, interleaved with the owner's advertisements, and harvested, in aggregate with a million such pages, into the behavioural record whose refinement the owner sells. The scholar cannot remove the archive without destroying its usefulness, since its value is the community's presence around it, and the community is the passage's crowd. When the owner revises the terms, degrades the page's reach, or in the limiting case extinguishes the passage altogether, the town's past is extinguished with it, a loss no wage demand could have priced and no employment tribunal could remedy. The scholar is the commoner in purest form, a maker of a common enclosed by its meeting ground, and the scene demonstrates why the enclosure analysis, and not the labour analysis, fits the largest share of what the population actually does inside the passages: it builds the world it lives in, together, for its own reasons, and wakes to find the world owned.

In the third scene, a regional manufacturer of machine parts, employing machinists whose skill the tradition would classify without hesitation, discovers that its customers have migrated to a marketplace passage and that orders outside the passage have thinned to a remnant. The firm joins, pays the commission, buys the prominence, submits to the passage's standards for photography, despatch, and dispute, and watches its margin divide three ways: to the machinists' wages and the surplus the firm retains, to the materials and machines, and to the passage, whose share arrives with the regularity of a landlord's quarter day and grows, season by season, as the firm's dependence deepens and its own channels to customers atrophy from disuse. The firm's owners do not experience the passage as an employer of anyone, nor as a supplier whose efficiency they chose. They experience it as the ground on which their market now stands, ground they did not buy and cannot leave, and their bookkeeper, reaching for the account heading under which the commission belongs, files it where firms have always filed the price of ground. Three scenes, three figures, one anatomy: labour producing value where the criteria hold, a toll drawn from the product where the passage is owned, and a crowd, a reputation, and an archive held inside the fence as the toll's security. The classification was never a verdict about technology. It is a description of where, in each scene, the money is born and where it is merely, and lucratively, stopped.

## A coda on the word platform itself

The word that names the subject deserves a parting glance, because words in this tradition are never innocent, and platform is a small masterpiece of ideological carpentry. A platform is flat, neutral, raised slightly for the convenience of those who stand on it, and empty of any interest of its own: a station's edge, a speaker's stage, a surface provided for the activities of others. Every element of the image works to conceal the anatomy this article has described. The flatness conceals the toll, since surfaces do not charge for standing. The neutrality conceals the private legislation, since stages do not revise the terms on which speeches are given, rank the speakers by a secret rule, or record the audience's faces for sale. The emptiness conceals the archive, since a platform, in the image, keeps nothing, while the passage in reality keeps everything and is valued for the keeping. Even the slight elevation flatters the owner, suggesting a service performed in raising the crowd a few inches toward visibility, when the elevation is the fence's height measured from the other side. The tradition has met such words before and learned to handle them by translation: wherever the age supplies a term that makes a relation sound like a thing, and a thing sound like a kindness, the analyst's first task is to translate the term back into the relation it names. Platform translates, in the vocabulary this article has used, as an owned passage between parties, fortified by a gathered crowd and a kept archive, drawing rent from the traffic it hosts. The translation is less elegant than the original. It has the compensating virtue of telling the truth about the toll, and elegance, in the naming of economic relations, has usually been the toll collector's contribution to the language.

The same translation test can be carried to the subject's satellite vocabulary, and a reader who performs it habitually will find the whole lexicon rearranging itself. Sharing, in the satellite vocabulary, names the rental of assets through a passage that takes its percentage of every act of generosity it intermediates. Community names the crowd whose gathering constitutes the owner's asset, addressed in the grammar of belonging precisely because belonging is what the asset is made of. Connection names the passage itself at the moment of its most successful concealment, when the toll, the record, and the rulebook have all been edited out of the picture and only the meeting remains. Creator names the tenant of attention at the moment the tenancy is dressed as a vocation. None of these translations denies the reality the words gesture toward: people do share, do belong, do meet, do make. The translations deny only the innocence of the setting, the suggestion that sharing, belonging, meeting, and making are happening on neutral ground rather than inside an owned passage whose owner is paid by the act, records the act, and governs the act's visibility. Language of that kind is not a conspiracy; it is something more effective, a habit of speech in which an entire population learns to describe its enclosure as its convenience. Breaking the habit, word by word, is slow work, and it is the work with which analysis properly ends: not with a verdict filed away, but with a reader who can no longer hear the word platform without seeing the fence, the crowd, and the hand at the gate.

There is a discipline hidden in that small exercise of translation, and it generalises beyond this subject to every object the series examines. When a fashionable term arrives carrying its own interpretation inside its metaphor, the interpretation must be unpacked and priced before the analysis begins, since an analyst who accepts the metaphor has accepted the verdict it smuggles. The platform's metaphor smuggled neutrality. Others, in other articles of this series, have smuggled novelty, inevitability, or benevolence, and the unpacking has in each case been the analysis's true first step, before the criteria, before the evidence, before the verdict that the unpacked term finally permits a reader to judge on its merits rather than on its music.

## For students and exam candidates

The platform question is a gift to the prepared candidate, because it tests the one skill the examiners of political economy most want to certify: the application of a criterion to an object the textbook never mentioned. Answers fail on this topic in a predictable shape, by reciting a position, usually the free labour position because it is the most quotable, without ever stating the criterion the position must satisfy. The repair is mechanical and worth drilling. Open by defining the object through its mechanism, an owned intermediary infrastructure drawing income from access and from the record of use. State the criterion before any verdict: value production requires labour for exchange under competitive compulsion, equalised against a socially necessary standard. Apply the criterion separately to each figure the question might concern, the user, the coordinated worker, the seller, the subscriber, and let the verdicts differ as the mechanisms differ, since an answer that grades by type and by figure demonstrates the method in the act of reaching its conclusion. Close with the verdict and its defeater in adjacent sentences: platform income in its distinctive element is a rent charged on an enclosed passage, and the verdict would fall if user activity were shown to labour under a compulsion equivalent to the market's discipline. That closing pair, verdict with defeater, is the signature of the strong script across this series, and on this topic it also happens to be the honest summary of where the literature's argument actually stands. The wider paper's treatment of the period's capitalism is gathered at [modern capitalism exam answers](/2021/03/15/modern-capitalism-exam-answers/), where the platform answer can be set among its neighbours.

Build the revision kit around the discriminations rather than around the narrative. One card for the criteria, stated as questions to ask of any activity: is it performed for exchange, is it disciplined by a social standard, is failure punished by exclusion from livelihood, is its product equalised against rivals. One card for the taxonomy, the five types with the object each intermediates. One card for the cast list, the four figures with the injury and remedy proper to each. One card for the table's logic, the fund each income stream draws on. The companion resources support exactly this kit: the Marxist study notes tool at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) turns the criteria and the taxonomy into drills that can be rehearsed in short sessions, and the UPSC previous year question explorer at [ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/upsc-pyq-explorer.html) lets candidates in the civil service stream test the criterion method against questions examiners have actually set on cognate topics, which is the only reliable calibration of whether a rehearsed method survives contact with a real paper. Practise the defeater sentence aloud until it costs no time. In the hall, the candidate who can name the evidence that would move their verdict is the candidate the marker believes understood the question.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The platform dispute is the most teachable controversy in the contemporary literature of this tradition, because its two sides divide cleanly on a criterion that students can be brought to state, apply, and defend within a single seminar sequence. Stage the teaching as the article staged the analysis. Begin with the taxonomy and forbid generalisation until each type has been interrogated separately; the discipline of the five separate verdicts is the seminar's first achievement and the one students resist hardest, since the preference for a single verdict about a fashionable object is the very habit being trained away. Then set the criteria from the primary texts before admitting the disputants, so that Terranova and Fuchs are read as applicants before a standard rather than as authorities competing in charisma, and Christophers and the rent tradition are subjected to the same standard's demand for traced funds and observable predictions. The seminar's pivotal exercise is the defeater exchange: each side must state, in writing, the observation that would convert it, and the class grades the statements for specificity, since a defeater that names no possible observation is a confession that the position is a loyalty. Close by returning to the cast list and asking which figure each student had silently assumed throughout, because the discovery that one has been arguing about the user while one's neighbour argued about the worker explains more sterile disagreement, in this literature and beyond it, than any difference of doctrine. The topic's decay resistance makes it a durable seminar asset: no firm's fate can date the exercise, because no firm was ever named.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Three injunctions from this analysis travel into professional practice. For the researcher: treat every platform income claim as a decomposition problem before treating it as anything else, and distrust any dataset that reports a platform firm's revenue without separating the passage streams from the produced streams, since the aggregate conceals the very quantity the analysis needs. The research frontier the article identifies is the estimation of rent proportions by type and their movement over time, together with the measurement of dependence itself, the cost to each class of participant of the junction's disappearance, which is the toll's true ceiling and the enclosure's true depth. For the historian: the platform archive poses a preservation problem of a new kind, since the record of the population's passage through the junctions is private property, perishable at the owner's convenience, and the social history of the period will be written from whatever fragments escape the vaults, a selection effect future historians should be warned about by their predecessors while warning is still possible. For the journalist: refuse the two ready stories. The innovation story and the exploitation story are both supplied, pre written, by interested parties, and the questions that break them are the ones this article has modelled: what is owned, who is disciplined and by what standard, from which fund is this payment drawn, what would the payer do if the passage opened. A newsroom that asks those four questions of every platform announcement will produce coverage that ages as this analysis is designed to age, by mechanism rather than by headline, and will find, as the tradition found before it, that the unglamorous questions are the ones the powerful least enjoy answering.

## Errors in the other direction: the rent reading's careless friends

A verdict that wins adherents acquires careless friends, and the rent reading's friends have developed errors of their own that a responsible statement of the reading should disown before its opponents discover them. The first careless error is the dismissal of the user's experience: the slide from users do not produce value to users lose nothing, as though the classification were a consolation prize chart in which only value producers merit concern. The article has insisted throughout that enclosure is a founding injury with its own remedies, and any version of the reading that shrugs at the harvest of the population's social life has kept the verdict and lost the analysis. The second careless error is the laziness of the universal toll: the habit of calling every digital income rent without performing the decomposition, so that the cloud's produced infrastructure, the engineers' labour, and the logistics of physical delivery are swept into the same category as the advertising toll. That habit reproduces, inside the winning camp, the exact sin the reading was built to correct, the refusal to distinguish mechanisms, and it will discredit the reading when the first careful opponent decomposes a composite firm and finds production the sloganeers denied. The third careless error is triumphalism about the tradition: the claim that because the criteria classified the platform, the tradition needs nothing new, no account of behaviour modification's power, no theory of the archive as an instrument of government, no engagement with the phenomenology of life inside the feed. The classification settles the income question. It does not settle the power question, the experience question, or the government question, and a tradition that treats one settled question as a settlement of all questions has begun the conversion from a research programme into a catechism. The reading's careful friends will therefore be found doing the opposite of its careless ones: conceding the frontier where the categories need supplementing, performing the decomposition even when the slogan would be easier, and defending the user's grievance with the enclosure analysis at full strength rather than waving it away. A verdict is only as durable as the discipline of the camp that holds it.

## Why rent travels: the portability of a category

The platform case closes with a reflection on the category that did the work, because the category's performance here explains something about the tradition's intellectual economy that students rarely see stated. Rent is the most portable concept in the Marxist repertoire. Forged for agricultural land, it travelled to the mine, to the urban plot, to the spectrum and the fishery and the taxi licence, and now to the digital junction, and at each border crossing it performed the same discrimination: it separated the income that ownership of a scarce condition extracts from the income that production earns, and it identified the fund from which the extraction is paid. Portability of that order is not a verbal trick. It is evidence that the category captures a real and recurring structure in economies organised by private property in the conditions of activity: wherever a condition that others need can be owned, enclosed, and made scarce, an income of rent's shape appears, limited by dependence, defended by law, capitalised by finance, and disguised by the idiom of service. The platform is the structure's most extensive modern habitat because the conditions of social and economic activity themselves, communication, reputation, market access, the record of the past, have become ownable in a way the agricultural world could not have imagined, and each newly ownable condition is a new province for the old category. The tradition's critics sometimes present this portability as a defect, a sign that the concept stretches to fit whatever it meets. The platform case suggests the opposite reading: the concept does not stretch, because its criteria, scarcity, enclosure, dependence, payment from a fund produced elsewhere, either hold or fail in each application, and they held here under hostile inspection, against a fashionable rival, on an object designed by its novelty to escape them. A category that survives its hardest border crossing with its criteria intact has not been stretched. It has been vindicated, and the vindication, more than any single verdict about any single sector, is the platform debate's durable contribution to the tradition that hosted it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is platform capitalism?

It names an arrangement in which a firm owns and runs a passage connecting parties who need one another, buyers with sellers, audiences with advertisers, workers with customers, and draws its earnings from the crossing itself and from the records the crossing leaves behind. The firm typically owns little of what passes: not the goods, often not the tools, sometimes not even the labour. What it owns is the meeting place, together with the standards for entering it and the archive of everything done inside it. The term matters because it directs attention away from the visible transactions and toward the owned junction between them, which is where the arrangement's power and its income both originate.

### Q: Do platform users produce value?

Judged by the standards the tradition sets for itself, almost certainly not. Producing value demands work carried out for exchange while a competitive discipline holds the performer to a shared standard and punishes the laggard through lost livelihood. Ordinary use answers to no such discipline: nobody's survival depends on matching a socially set pace of posting or watching, and performances are never weighed against rivals so as to shrink into abstract labour. What users supply is presence, attention, and a residue of recorded behaviour, all genuinely harvested and genuinely uncompensated. The harvest is real; its correct name is enclosure of a collectively built resource, and the earnings it supports are drawn from surplus created by labour elsewhere in the economy.

### Q: Is platform income rent or profit?

Chiefly rent, in the element that makes the income distinctively a platform's. A commission for reaching a gathered crowd, a charge for access to a sorted audience, a subscription for goods the payer will never own: each is levied from a position, scaled by the payer's dependence, and paid out of earnings generated in production somewhere else. Composite firms complicate the picture honestly. Where a company builds and staffs a vast computing infrastructure, part of its return belongs to production and must be classified there. The analytical task is always decomposition, stream by stream, asking of each payment whether it would survive the crowd's free departure with records and reputations intact. Whatever would vanish with the fence is rent.

### Q: What is actually new about gig work?

Not the contract. Payment by the task, subcontracting chains, and arrangements in which a merchant distributed work to people labouring with their own tools in their own homes are all centuries deep in the archive of this tradition. The novelty sits in the instruments of oversight and allocation. A coordinating system watches the work continuously, scores every performance into a permanent record, varies prices and offers by rules the worker cannot inspect, and disciplines through quiet exclusion from future tasks rather than through any visible superior. Control of that granularity, exercised by a counterparty that discloses almost nothing about its own reasoning, is the genuine innovation, and it deserves precise statement rather than either celebration of a new world or dismissal of the whole arrangement as old.

### Q: Why do platforms tend towards monopoly?

Because usefulness at a junction grows with the crowd already gathered there. Each newcomer makes the passage more valuable to everyone waiting on the other side, so the leading junction attracts the next arrival more strongly than any rival can, and its advantage compounds while competitors starve for the participation that constitutes the product itself. Rivals cannot simply build a better bridge, since the asset is not the bridge but the crowd upon it, assembled from habits, relationships, and records that cannot be purchased ready made. The result is concentration by mechanism rather than by conspiracy: monopoly at the centre, governing access, while fierce competition continues among the sellers and workers who need that access and bid against one another beneath it.

### Q: What do Marxists object to in the surveillance capitalism account?

Above all to its history, and through the history to its remedy. The influential account of behavioural extraction published in 2019 presents the harvesting of human experience as a mutinous break with a market order that once proceeded by reciprocity and respect for discovered needs. The objection replies that appropriation without consent is not a departure in this tradition's archive but a continuity, running from the enclosures onward, and that needs were manufactured as energetically as they were served long before behavioural prediction existed. A second objection concerns classification: revenue gathered where data is refined does not prove value is produced there. A third follows for practice. If the offence is deviation, restoration beckons; if it is the ordinary logic of enclosure applied to new material, only a change in ownership answers it.

### Q: What are the main types of platform?

A taxonomy from a study published in 2016 distinguishes them by the object each one intermediates. Advertising platforms assemble audiences and sell sorted access to them. Cloud platforms rent computing capacity and software environments to other firms. Industrial platforms embed sensing and coordination within clients' production processes. Product platforms keep ownership of goods and sell access to them by subscription. Lean platforms own little beyond coordinating software while matching other people's labour and equipment to other people's demand. The separation is not pedantry, because the central questions of the analysis, where income originates and whether any participant produces value, receive different answers for different types, and every confident generalisation about platforms in general collapses on contact with the differences.

### Q: What would settle the platform value question?

Evidence about mechanisms, since magnitudes cannot discriminate between the rival accounts. Watch how the price of reaching an audience is formed: if it moves with the audience's spending power while remaining indifferent to how its hours were spent, the toll is behaving as rent behaves; if it tracked qualities of the activity itself, the labour account would gain its strongest exhibit. Watch how a mature junction's earnings grow: expansion driven by deepening dependence, without matching growth in any production apparatus, fits the rent prediction. Watch where the behavioural archive's worth resides: if departing users cannot carry its value away while the owner retains it entire, the asset's anatomy is a landlord's improvement rather than a worker's product. Each test separates the accounts because each account predicts a different pattern.

### Q: Is algorithmic management a new form of control?

In its instruments yes, in its function no, and the exact answer holds both halves together. The function is perennial: extracting effort from people whose interests conflict with the buyer's, under an inequality of knowledge about the process. The instruments surpass every predecessor in combination: observation without intermission, evaluation accumulated into permanent scores, allocation by rules kept secret and revised continuously, direction personalised to each individual profile, and discipline delivered as silent exclusion from the passage itself. Earlier management bought supervision by the hour and received it in fragments. The algorithmic form receives it whole, cheaply, and as a byproduct of the same system that offers the work, which is why the balance of knowledge between the parties has shifted further than any earlier refinement managed.

### Q: Are platform workers employees or contractors?

The contract says contractor; the substance fits neither box cleanly. Such workers own tools and absorb costs like independent producers, yet they cannot set their prices, inspect the rule that allocates their tasks, negotiate with the counterparty that scores them, or carry their accumulated reputation to another passage, which are the powers that would make independence a reality rather than a label. Classification disputes matter practically because rights, protections, and bargaining standing attach to the label a legal order assigns. Analytically, the sharper point survives either assignment: the worker's labour can be exploited within production while a rent is simultaneously charged on access to the customer, two mechanisms stacked inside a single payment, and any account that insists on naming only one of them will misunderstand the payment it set out to explain.

### Q: How does the rent reading grade the platform value dispute?

It grades by criteria published in advance, which is what separates a grading from a preference. The free labour account is credited with the dispute's best phenomenology: it saw that activity is harvested, that design elicits the harvest, and that the harvest is uncompensated. It is marked down where it must relax the discipline requirement, because a value concept without competitive equalisation cannot distinguish the platform user from the theatre audience or the billboard's passerby, and a concept that distinguishes nothing explains nothing. The rent reading is credited with fitting the price evidence, the maturity behaviour of junction earnings, and the archive's legal anatomy, and it is held to its published defeater: a demonstrated compulsion equivalent governing ordinary use, or tolls traced to a fund production never filled, would reverse the grade.

### Q: What should a student separate between producing value and capturing it?

Two questions that commentary habitually fuses. The production question asks where new value comes into existence, and it is answered by examining labour: whether the activity is exchanged, disciplined against a social standard, and equalised with rival performances. The capture question asks how value already in existence is divided among claimants, and its answer comes from examining positions: who owns a condition that producers need, how scarce that condition is, and how completely the need can be escaped. A student who keeps the questions apart can watch a fortune accumulate at a junction without inventing a factory beneath it, and can watch a factory's surplus drain toward landlords, lenders, merchants, and junction owners without doubting where the surplus was born. Most errors in this literature are fusions of the two questions wearing the costume of discoveries.

### Q: Why does user activity fail the test for value production?

Because the test's decisive element is missing, and the other elements cannot substitute for it. The decisive element is compulsion of the market's specific kind: performance measured against a standard the performer did not set, with failure punished by the loss of the activity's economic basis, so that private efforts are forcibly converted into comparable quantities. A person may attend a passage daily or never, linger or glance, excel or idle, and no mechanism translates those variations into the discipline that makes labour abstract. Design nudges and engineered habit are real pressures, and this analysis has treated them seriously as instruments of capture. But pressure to participate is not the equalisation of performances, and without equalisation the category of value has nothing to fasten upon, however valuable the participation plainly is to its harvester.

### Q: How does the value question by platform type order the platform kinds?

It arranges them along a gradient defined by how much of each type's income survives decomposition as production's return. At one end stand the advertising and lean types, where the passage is nearly the whole business and the income resolves almost entirely into tolls on access, audiences in the first case and other people's labour in the second. The product type follows closely, its subscriptions being rent on owned assets in a nearly classical shape. The industrial type sits midway, its fees partly purchasing a service that can raise a client's productivity and partly hardening, as dependence deepens, into a charge on the client's own enclosed knowledge. The cloud type stands furthest along the gradient, its produced infrastructure and operating labour claiming a genuine share, with rent gathering wherever its standards make departure expensive.

### Q: What separates rent on access from profit on production?

The force that limits the income. Profit on production is limited by rivals: another producer can always, in principle, make the thing more cheaply, and the discipline of that possibility is what ties profit to the productivity of labour and keeps it contestable. Rent on access is limited by the payer's dependence and ability to pay, quantities the owner works to deepen rather than to fear, and no rival's efficiency can undercut it while the passage itself cannot be duplicated. Apply the test to any income stream by asking a single question: if a competitor appeared tomorrow offering the same service at half the price, would this income fall. Where the answer is yes, production is speaking. Where the crowd, the record, and the standard hold the payer regardless, the income is rent, and its owner is a landlord of the passage.
