Marxism after Marx never travelled as a single doctrine. Within a generation of its founder's death it had divided into currents that disagreed about strategy, about philosophy, and about what kind of knowledge a revolutionary theory was supposed to be. One of those currents acquired a label that has stuck so firmly that it is easy to forget the label was invented afterwards, by an outsider, and that the people it collects never used it of themselves. Western Marxism names the body of Marxist theory produced in continental Europe from the nineteen twenties onward, and what distinguishes it is not first of all a doctrine. It is a location and a set of preoccupations: its theorists worked increasingly inside universities rather than inside mass parties, and they wrote about philosophy, culture, art, and consciousness where their predecessors had written about wages, crises, and the state. Everything else about the tradition, its difficulty, its pessimism, its strange durability, follows from that placement and from the political catastrophe that produced it.

This guide examines the label as carefully as the work it labels, because the label itself is an argument. Three findings organise what follows. The term was coined in the nineteen fifties by a French phenomenologist describing a Hungarian critic, not by any member of the supposed school. The most influential analysis of the tradition, published two decades later, argues that its defining traits were stamped on it by political defeat, by the failure of revolution in Western Europe after the First World War and the long isolation that followed, rather than chosen freely by thinkers who preferred seminars to strikes. And the geography inside the name does quiet work of its own: a tradition called Western silently leaves outside itself the Marxists of Peru, India, and the Caribbean who were writing in the very same decades, which is a fact about the category rather than about the history of Marxist thought. A reader who holds those three findings can use the term intelligently, as a map with its projections marked, instead of mistaking it for the territory.

## What the name Western Marxism picks out

Begin with the plain definition, because it is serviceable even if everything after it complicates the picture. Western Marxism is the tradition of Marxist theory developed in continental Europe from the nineteen twenties, characterised by a turn away from political economy toward philosophy, culture, and aesthetics, by a growing separation from organised working-class movements, and by the relocation of Marxist intellectual work into universities. Each clause of that sentence marks a contrast with the Marxism that came before. The founding generations had been party intellectuals: economists, historians, journalists, and organisers whose writing answered to a movement that expected to use it. The figures gathered under this later label were, with important exceptions examined below, professors, critics, and independent scholars whose writing answered to other writing. Where the older Marxism treated culture as a reflection of economic relations, interesting mainly as evidence, the newer one treated culture, consciousness, and ideology as the very terrain on which domination was secured or contested. Where the older Marxism expected capitalism to generate its own gravediggers more or less automatically, the newer one brooded on why it had not.

The tradition's centre of gravity can be fixed by a handful of names and one institution. A Hungarian critic reconstructed the concept of reification out of the analysis of the commodity. A German philosopher, writing in the same year, restored philosophy to the centre of Marxist inquiry. An Italian party leader, imprisoned by a fascist regime, filled notebooks with a theory of hegemony, of how a social order rules by organising consent. A research institute in Frankfurt gathered economists, sociologists, psychoanalysts, and philosophers into a single programme that produced, among much else, the analysis of the culture industry. A French existentialist attempted, late and at enormous length, to fuse Marxism with a philosophy of individual freedom, and a French philosopher of the next generation answered by purging Marxism of humanism altogether and rebuilding it as a science of structures. These are not a school. They quarrelled, ignored one another, and in some cases never met. What makes them a tradition is a shared predicament and a shared displacement: after the revolutionary wave that followed the Russian Revolution broke against Western Europe, Marxist theory survived there mainly as thought, detached from a movement capable of acting on it, and it bore the marks of that survival in everything it produced.

### What holds the members of the tradition together?

No manifesto, membership, or shared name binds them, since none of them used the label of themselves. What they share is a predicament: after the revolutionary defeats in Western Europe, Marxist theory survived detached from a mass movement, and its members responded to that isolation with a common displacement toward philosophy, culture, and the problem of consciousness.

The displacement is the tradition's signature, and it can be misread in two directions. Read uncharitably, it looks like surrender: the theory of a workers' movement retreating into studies and lecture halls, exchanging the factory gate for the footnote. Read as its members experienced it, the displacement was an investigation forced on them by events. If the working class of the most advanced capitalist countries had not risen, if it had instead voted for nationalism in 1914, accommodated itself to fascism in Italy and Germany, and settled into consumer prosperity after 1945, then the interesting questions were no longer only about ownership and crisis. They were about why people consent to orders that exploit them, how needs and desires are manufactured, what forms of consciousness the commodity form itself produces, and whether reason, the Enlightenment inheritance Marxism claimed, had turned into an instrument of domination. The turn to philosophy and culture was, on this reading, the tradition following its own problem where the problem led. Both readings recur throughout this guide, and the tension between them is precisely what the defeat thesis tries to settle.

A second signature is ancestry. Classical Marxism had drawn on German philosophy, English political economy, and French socialism, the famous three sources, but it treated that inheritance as superseded. The Western tradition kept returning to the ancestors as living resources: to Hegel above all, whose dialectic was reactivated against the mechanical materialism of party orthodoxy; to Kant, whose account of the constituting subject shadowed every discussion of consciousness; to Spinoza, recovered by later generations as a thinker of immanence; and to two figures outside the classical inheritance entirely, Freud, whose account of repression offered a mechanism for the internalisation of authority, and eventually Heidegger, whose analysis of everydayness and technology seeped into existential and critical variants alike. A Marxism that reads its predecessors' predecessors is a Marxism doing philosophy, and the depth of that philosophical work is a large part of why the tradition's texts are demanding, a question taken up at length below.

### What separates this current from the Marxism that preceded it?

Its predecessors wrote as party intellectuals for a movement expecting power, and treated economics and politics as the theory's core. This current wrote largely from universities and studies, after the movement's defeats, and treated consciousness, culture, and ideology as the decisive terrain, because consent, not only coercion, was what needed explaining.

Finally, the label's boundaries matter as much as its centre, and they are drawn by the analysis that codified them rather than by the record itself. The tradition as usually mapped runs from the early nineteen twenties to roughly the nineteen seventies, and from Germany, Hungary, Italy, and France, with Britain largely absent and the Iberian and Scandinavian worlds absent altogether. It includes Catholic-raised Italians, Jewish Germans in American exile, and a Romanian working in Paris; it excludes, by construction, Marxists in the same decades writing in Spanish from Lima or in English from Trinidad. Whether those boundaries illuminate or distort is the standing question of this guide, and it is why the tradition map below lists relationships to organised movements as a column in its own right: the variation in that column, from a party leader in a fascist cell to professors who never held a card, is the single most instructive fact the map contains.

## A label coined from outside

The expression Western Marxism entered print in a book that was not about a school at all. Maurice Merleau-Ponty, the French phenomenologist, published Adventures of the Dialectic in 1955, a study of the fate of dialectical thinking that moved from Weber through the revolutionary generation to the dilemmas of communism after Stalin. In its central chapters he examined the young Lukacs, the Lukacs of the essays on reification written just after the First World War, and he needed a name for the kind of Marxism those essays represented: a Marxism centred on consciousness, on the proletariat as the identical subject and object of history, on the recovery of the dialectic from what he saw as the dead mechanism of official doctrine. He called it Western Marxism, and the contrast built into the phrase was with the East, with the codified dialectical materialism of the Soviet Union, which he treated as having abandoned the dialectic for a philosophy of nature administered by a party. The label began, then, as one term of a polemical pair inside somebody else's argument about phenomenology and politics. It named a tendency its author half admired and half feared, not a movement that existed on the ground.

Notice how much of the later usage is already present in embryo. Merleau-Ponty applied the phrase to a narrow object, essentially the early Lukacs and, by extension, Korsch, yet the features he singled out, philosophical depth, distance from party orthodoxy, preoccupation with consciousness and alienation, are exactly the features the later, wider usage would generalise across half a century and a dozen countries. Notice also what the coinage was not. It was not a self-description: Lukacs, who lived until 1971 and wrote voluminously about his own intellectual path, did not organise his self-understanding around being a Western Marxist, and neither did Gramsci's Italian editors, the Frankfurt Institute's directors, or the Parisian structuralists. It was not a party category: no congress adopted it, no journal bore it as a banner. And it was not, in its first appearance, a term of abuse or of praise exactly; it was diagnostic, a way of marking that something had happened to Marxism in the West that made it a different animal from the orthodoxy of the East, something its coiner regarded with genuine ambivalence, since he thought the Western variant preserved the dialectic at the price of detaching it from history's guarantees.

### How did a phenomenologist come to name the tradition?

Merleau-Ponty needed a term for the philosophically rich, consciousness-centred Marxism he found in the young Lukacs, set against the codified doctrine of the Soviet Union. Writing a book on the dialectic's adventures, he coined the phrase for that contrast, and later writers widened his label for one Hungarian critic into the name of a half-century tradition.

The phrase then lay comparatively quiet for two decades, available but not yet load-bearing, until the nineteen seventies gave it a second and decisive life. In 1976 the British historian and editor Perry Anderson published Considerations on Western Marxism, a short, dense survey that took Merleau-Ponty's expression, enormously expanded its membership, and supplied it with a systematic anatomy and a causal theory. It is Anderson's version of the category, far more than Merleau-Ponty's, that syllabuses, encyclopedias, and reading lists transmit. Between them, the two books illustrate the double life of such labels. A coinage proposes; a codification disposes. The phenomenologist noticed a style of thought. The historian built a period, a population, and an explanation around the notice, and in doing so quietly redrew the map: figures Merleau-Ponty never discussed, Gramsci in his prison cell, Adorno in his American exile, Althusser in his Paris seminar, were gathered into a single tradition whose unity none of them had asserted. Understanding Western Marxism therefore requires holding two objects in view at once: the writing itself, which is real and magnificent and strange, and the retrospective frame that presents that writing as one thing, which is an interpretation with its own purposes, examined in the objection section below.

There is a further quiet point about the coinage that readers should register. The pair Western and Eastern, as Merleau-Ponty used it, described a division inside European Marxism, between the unorthodox philosophy of the continent's west and the state doctrine of its east. It said nothing about the rest of the planet, because the rest of the planet was not in his field of view. When the label was later generalised, that provincial horizon was generalised with it, and a division of Europe hardened into an implicit division of the world, in which Marxism worth theorising about happened between Frankfurt and Paris while Marxism elsewhere was filed under politics, nationalism, or development. The exclusion criticised later in this guide is therefore not an accident of one author's oversight. It was inscribed in the label at birth, in the innocent-looking geography of a contrast coined for another purpose.

### What contrast did the coinage draw with the Soviet doctrine?

The phrase marked off a Marxism of consciousness, dialectic, and philosophical inquiry from the official dialectical materialism administered by the Soviet party, which the coiner saw as a mechanical philosophy of nature. Western named a style of thought and a relation to orthodoxy, not a passport: its exemplary figure was a Hungarian writing in German and Viennese exile.

## The founding coincidence and the condemnation

If the tradition has a birthday, it falls in a single publishing season. In 1923 Georg Lukacs, a Hungarian commissar turned exile, brought out History and Class Consciousness, a collection of essays arguing that the heart of Marx's method was the category of totality, that the commodity form had stamped consciousness itself with the structure he named reification, and that the proletariat could become the identical subject-object of history by recognising its own position in the whole. In the same year Karl Korsch, a German jurist and briefly a minister in a coalition government in Thuringia, published Marxism and Philosophy, which argued that Marxism had passed through phases of degeneration into vulgar materialism, that philosophy could not be abolished by decree because ideology was a real part of the social whole, and that the theory had to be applied to itself, historically, as a product of the movement that carried it. Two books, two countries, one thesis from opposite sides: Marxism was a philosophy of praxis centred on consciousness and totality, and the reigning party versions of it, social democratic fatalism in one camp and the hardening Soviet codification in the other, had forgotten its soul.

The response fixed the pair as the founders of something. At the Fifth Congress of the Communist International in 1924, the Comintern's leadership denounced the new philosophical turn by name. Grigory Zinoviev, presiding over the organisation that claimed to direct world revolution, singled out Lukacs and Korsch, along with Karl Kautsky from the opposite flank, as revisionist professors whose theories threatened the doctrine's integrity, and the congress's orthodoxy campaign treated the two books as symptoms of a deviation to be corrected. Lukacs, dependent on the movement for his political existence, recanted in form and withdrew the book from circulation, spending later decades alternately disowning and quietly re-owning its arguments. Korsch refused the discipline, was expelled from the German party within a few years, and drifted into the council communist margins and eventually American exile. The condemnation matters analytically out of proportion to its literary quality, because it is the first moment at which the official movement itself recognised the new writing as a distinct current, distinct enough to need suppressing. A tradition that its members never named was, in a sense, christened by its prosecutors.

### Why were the two founding books condemned together?

The congress leadership treated both books as one deviation: a restoration of philosophy, consciousness, and totality against the mechanical doctrine the International was codifying. Lukacs and Korsch had arrived independently at kindred positions, and their joint denunciation, not any act of their own, first marked the new writing as a recognisable current inside Marxism.

Beneath the coincidence sits the catastrophe that made the books possible and necessary. Both authors had lived through the sequence that defines the tradition's prehistory: the collapse of the Second International in 1914, when the great socialist parties voted for their own governments' war credits; the Russian Revolution of 1917, which seemed to prove that history could be seized; and the failed revolutions that followed in the West, the German risings of 1919 and 1923, the Hungarian Soviet Republic of 1919 in which Lukacs served as a commissar of education, the Italian factory occupations of 1920. A Marxism formed by that sequence faced a theoretical emergency that the older doctrine could not process. The crisis of capitalism had arrived on schedule; the revolutionary subject had not. Either the theory of consciousness was wrong, or consciousness itself had to become the problem, something produced, deformed, and potentially remade, rather than a reflex that could be awaited. History and Class Consciousness and Marxism and Philosophy are the two great attempts to rebuild Marxism around that emergency, and nearly everything the tradition later produced, Gramsci's hegemony, the Frankfurt School's studies of authority and culture, the existentialists' and structuralists' rival reconstructions, can be read as further attempts on the same problem under worsening conditions.

The condemnation also established the tradition's recurring institutional pattern: an uneasy, often broken relation to the communist parties that claimed Marx's inheritance. Gramsci led such a party and wrote his masterwork in a fascist prison, cut off from it. The Frankfurt Institute kept a careful, funded independence from party discipline, and its director treated party affiliation as a private matter for members rather than a condition of the work. Sartre fellow-travelled, quarrelled, and broke in cycles. Althusser spent his career inside the French party, fighting its orthodoxy from within its own philosophical apparatus. Lukacs survived inside the movement by periodic self-criticism that his readers have never stopped arguing about. The pattern is not accidental decoration on the theory. A current whose founding texts were condemned by the movement's international centre, and whose members thereafter worked at every distance from party membership except comfortable obedience, was bound to develop its characteristic themes, its suspicion of administered doctrine, its investment in culture and consciousness as relatively autonomous terrains, and its pessimism about organisations as carriers of emancipation. The map in the next section lays the variation out in rows, because seeing it tabulated teaches more than any summary of the themes.

## The tradition map

A reading list tells a newcomer what to open first. The tradition map does different work: it lays the figures side by side so that the pattern of the whole, the drift of countries, the sequence of concepts, the recycling of ancestors, and above all the loosening tie to organised movements, can be seen at a glance and argued with. Read it down the final column. At the top stands a party commissar and a party leader in prison. In the middle, an endowed institute keeping its distance from every party. At the bottom, a professor inside a party he spent his life correcting, and an existentialist who never joined one. The map is the defeat thesis in tabular form before the thesis is even stated: row by row, the movement recedes and the university advances.

Three lessons fall out of the rows. First, the tradition is polycentric: no capital governs it, and its German centre of gravity before 1933 scatters under fascism into Paris, New York, Geneva, Moscow, and the prison cell on Turi, so that exile and translation are among its material conditions, not biographical colour. Second, the concepts arrive as answers to a single recurring question in different vocabularies: how does an order reproduce itself inside the heads of those it exploits? Reification, hegemony, the culture industry, ideology as material practice, and overdetermination are five generations of one investigation. Third, the ancestry column shows a tradition thinking with the whole of European philosophy, not with Marx alone, which is why its texts demand philosophical preparation their predecessors did not and why the boundary between Marxist theory and general critical philosophy blurs across the period.

### What does the map show that a reading list hides?

A list sequences books; the map exposes structure. Side by side, the rows reveal the movement column emptying as the decades pass, the same question about consent recurring under five concept names, and the ancestry widening from Hegel to Spinoza and Freud, patterns no order of reading, however well chosen, makes visible by itself.

Notice, too, what the map cannot show without distortion, since an honest instrument advertises its own limits. Gramsci's row compresses into six cells a body of writing, the Prison Notebooks, whose concepts were forged in a party leadership and a prison, and whose relationship to the academic pattern of the later rows is the weakest in the table; several analysts treat him as the great exception inside the category, a point the grading discussion below takes up. The Frankfurt Institute's row conceals internal plurality: the Institute housed an economist of breakdown theory in its early years, a psychoanalyst of authority, and aesthetes who disagreed with its director about nearly everything except the enemy. And the map's tidy period column stops where the codification stopped, in the nineteen seventies, not where influence stopped, since the tradition's concepts went on working in feminism, cultural studies, and postcolonial thought long after the rows end. A map is a claim about what matters. This one claims that predicament and displacement matter most, and the sections that follow test that claim against the evidence.

## The anatomy of a tradition

Anderson's Considerations on Western Marxism did for the label what a Linnaean table does for a genus: it listed the traits by which specimens could be recognised, across all their local variety. The traits deserve stating one by one, because together they compose the standard portrait that later scholarship confirms, qualifies, or contests. First, a structural divorce between theory and political practice: where Marx and the classical generation had united the roles of theorist and organiser in single careers, the Western tradition separated them, producing writers whose work had no strategic addressee and strategists, in the parties, whose work had no theory. Second, a displacement of subject matter: the centre of Marxist inquiry migrated from the economy and the state toward philosophy, method, aesthetics, and culture, so that the tradition's characteristic products are treatises on the dialectic, on art, on consciousness, rather than analyses of accumulation or programmes of action. Third, a change of institutional home: the work moved into universities, research institutes, and publishing houses, acquiring the forms of academic production, the monograph, the seminar, the footnote, along with academic standards of rigour and academic distances from urgency.

Fourth, a change of language: the writing grew esoteric, dense with technical vocabulary and allusion, addressed to initiates. Anderson treated this difficulty not as personal vice but as a structural symptom, the mark of a theory speaking to itself in the absence of the movement that would have forced it into plain speech. Fifth, a return of the ancestors: cut off from a living practice that might have settled disputes by experiment, the tradition disputed by exegesis and affiliation, attaching itself to pre-Marxist masters, Hegel for Lukacs and the early Frankfurt generation, Kant for the Italians of the anti-Hegelian school, Spinoza for the structuralists, and to the new sciences of the mind, Freud above all, whose mechanisms promised an account of how domination gets inside its subjects. Sixth, a prevailing pessimism: from the Frankfurt School's wartime verdict on enlightenment itself to the melancholy that saturates the whole corpus, the tradition expected less and less from history, and its masterpieces are shadowed by defeat in a way the classical texts, written in expectation of victory, never are. To these one might add a seventh trait Anderson pressed hard: an inversion of the classical order of inquiry, in which superstructures, culture, ideology, law, art, received the attention once reserved for the economic base, until the tradition's Marxism could seem a theory of everything except production.

### How did the codifier turn a label into a period?

By supplying what the coinage lacked: a bounded membership, a set of structural traits shared across the membership, and a causal story explaining the traits. With a population, a syndrome, and an aetiology, the phrase stopped being a passing contrast in a phenomenologist's book and became a period concept other scholars had to use, qualify, or refute.

The power of this anatomy should not be underestimated, because it converted a shelf of difficult books into an intelligible historical object. A student who grasps the six traits can predict, before opening them, why Lukacs reads Hegel into the commodity form, why the Frankfurt Institute studied authority and prejudice rather than trade cycles, why Sartre's Marxist masterwork is a method for biography, and why Althusser's intervention took the form of a theory of reading. The anatomy also travels: it explains the tradition's peculiar afterlife in the humanities, where its texts were received as contributions to literary theory, aesthetics, and the philosophy of culture by readers who often had no interest in Marxism as a politics. A body of theory produced in separation from a movement proved, by that very separation, exportable to any seminar room on earth. The price of the anatomy is the one the objection section weighs: a population chosen to fit a syndrome will always exhibit the syndrome, and a tradition defined by its distance from practice will make every specimen look like a symptom. Hold that suspicion in reserve. State first the causal theory that gives the anatomy its bite, because the defeat thesis is the most influential single claim ever made about this body of work, and the claim after which this guide is organised.

## The defeat thesis and its limit

The namable claim of this guide can now be stated in its strong form. The defeat thesis and its limit: the characteristics catalogued above are best explained not by the tastes of individual thinkers, not by some elective affinity between Marxism and metaphysics, but by the political isolation in which the theory was produced, the half-century in which Western Europe's revolutionary movement was defeated, divided, or destroyed, leaving its intellectuals to think without a practice that could test, discipline, or employ their thought. And the limit, which matters as much as the thesis: the concepts forged in that isolation have since been picked up and used by people in utterly different situations, movements, and centuries of struggle, so the explanation of where the concepts came from does not determine what they are worth. Origins are not verdicts. The sections on either side of this one supply the evidence for the first half and the complications that enforce the second.

### Was Western Marxism a product of defeat?

Yes: that is the standard analysis. The failure of revolution in the West after the First World War separated Marxist theory from any workers' movement, pushing it into universities and toward philosophy and aesthetics. Its difficulty, pessimism, and distance from economics follow from that isolation.

Consider the chronology the thesis asks us to weigh. The classical generation wrote amid rising movements: whatever their setbacks, Marx, Engels, Luxemburg, Lenin, and Trotsky addressed organisations that grew, struck, published, and in two cases took or nearly took power. The Western generation wrote amid the opposite sequence. The German revolutions of the immediate postwar years were crushed; the Hungarian Soviet Republic, in whose government Lukacs had served, fell within months; the Italian workers' movement was destroyed by fascism, which imprisoned Gramsci for the remainder of his life; the German working class, the strongest in Europe, was annihilated as an organised force in 1933 without the general strike its leaders had theorised for decades; the Spanish Republic was strangled between 1936 and 1939 while the Western democracies watched; and the organisation that claimed to embody the alternative, the Soviet Union, consumed its own revolutionaries in the purges and bound the surviving parties to its foreign policy. A theorist formed by that sequence did not need a melancholic temperament to conclude that the classical expectations had failed. The expectations had failed, in public, repeatedly, in the most advanced countries where they were supposed to succeed. Theory that survived the wreckage survived as reflection on the wreckage: on consent manufactured by culture, on authority internalised in character, on reason itself turning into administration. The Frankfurt Institute's great wartime book, which traced domination into the structure of enlightenment rationality, is unintelligible except as a product of the years in which European civilisation, at the height of its technical powers, organised industrial murder.

### What evidence carries the defeat thesis?

Four converging lines: the dates, since the traits appear after the postwar defeats and deepen with fascism; the biographies, since exile, prison, and academic refuge mark nearly every career; the texts, whose themes are consent, authority, and blocked consciousness; and the contrast with the classical generation, whose rising movements produced a very different kind of writing.

The thesis also explains the tradition's odd geography of survival. When fascism closed the continent, the work migrated with its makers: the Institute to Geneva and then New York, Korsch to the American Midwest and East, Benjamin to Paris, where he died at the Spanish border in 1940, Bloch first to America and then to the Eastern Germany whose orthodoxy he never quite obeyed. Exile is not a metaphor in this tradition; it is the material form of its isolation, and it left traces in the writing, in its address to no national public, in its accumulation of private vocabularies, in its preference for the essay and the fragment, forms that can be carried in a suitcase. After 1945 the isolation changed character without ending. Western Europe's workers' movements revived as parties and unions, but as managers of welfare capitalism rather than as revolutionary forces, and the communist parties that retained a revolutionary vocabulary retained also their subordination to Moscow, whose tanks in Budapest in 1956 and Prague in 1968 completed for a generation the discrediting of the organisational alternative. The Western theorist of the postwar decades thus faced movements that existed but could not be used, which is arguably a deeper isolation than having none: Sartre's cycles of alliance and rupture with the French party, and Althusser's guerrilla war inside it, are the shapes thought takes when practice is present but unusable.

Now the limit, stated with equal care. A theory of origins explains why the questions were asked; it does not grade the answers. Reification was formulated by a defeated commissar in Viennese exile, but the concept has since done work in sociology, in media studies, in the analysis of digital platforms by writers who share nothing with Lukacs's politics, because the mechanism it names, the stamping of social relations into apparently natural things and of consciousness into contemplative passivity, is either real or not, independently of its author's circumstances. Gramsci's hegemony, elaborated in a cell as the theory of a defeat, became the working vocabulary of movements on several continents, of cultural studies in Britain, of strategists in Latin America and South Africa who found in it a guide for exactly the long war of position its author never lived to conduct. The culture industry chapter, written in Pacific exile at the darkest moment of the century, still opens arguments about entertainment and consent in seminar rooms whose occupants have never heard of its authors' predicament. This portability is not a minor qualification of the defeat thesis. It is the reason the tradition remains a live inheritance rather than a pathology report, and it sets the standard for judging the category itself: a frame that explains the birth of these concepts while obscuring their reach would fail at the moment it succeeded.

### Does an account of origins settle the worth of the concepts?

No, and the distinction disciplines both camps. Defenders of the tradition sometimes treat its suffering as a credential, as if exile and prison certified insight. Its critics sometimes treat the same facts as a refutation, as if a concept born of isolation were contaminated by it. Both commit the same error in opposite directions.

## Currents that argued with each other

Nothing falsifies the lazy picture of this tradition faster than its internal quarrels. The figures on the map did not form a choir; several of them devoted their sharpest pages to one another, and the lines of fracture are the fastest route into the substance. The deepest fracture concerns the status of the subject, of human consciousness and agency, inside the theory. At one pole stands the young Lukacs, for whom the proletariat's class consciousness is the pivot of history, the point where the identical subject and object of the social process can know and transform itself. At the other stands Althusser, half a century later, for whom history is a process without a subject, consciousness is an ideological effect, and the humanism of the young Marx, the very texts the Lukacsian current had used to rebuild Marxism, is a pre-scientific residue to be discarded. Between those poles the whole tradition is strung like a wire: Gramsci's philosophy of praxis, which treats consciousness as real but as organised, made and remade by institutions; Sartre's existential Marxism, which tried to hold individual freedom and structural constraint in a single method; the Frankfurt School's psychoanalytic turn, which relocated the subject's formation into the family and the culture that programmes it. The series treats the central stretch of this quarrel in its own comparison of [humanist vs structuralist marxism](/2016/02/15/humanist-vs-structuralist-marxism/), because the quarrel outgrew the tradition and reorganised Marxist debate everywhere in the nineteen sixties and seventies.

The poles reward a closer look, and the series gives each its own study: the [lukacs reification guide](/2015/11/15/lukacs-reification-guide/) reconstructs the Hungarian's founding concepts, and the profile of [louis althusser intellectual biography](/2015/12/15/louis-althusser-intellectual-biography/) traces the Frenchman's counter-system. What matters here is the shape of their opposition, because it refutes the idea that the tradition's displacement toward philosophy produced a single philosophy. Lukacs's Marxism is Hegelian to the marrow: reality is a process, categories must be grasped in their becoming, and knowledge culminates in the self-knowledge of the class that embodies the process. Althusser's is anti-Hegelian on principle: reality is a structure of unevenly developed instances, knowledge is produced by theoretical practice working on raw materials, and the Hegelian expressive totality, in which every part mirrors one essence, is the chief theoretical enemy. A tradition that contains both, as phases of its own history, is not a doctrine but a field of forces, and its unity lies in the questions its members could not escape rather than in the answers they imposed on one another. Even its gentler figures quarrelled in print: Benjamin's theses on history were written partly against the Frankfurt Institute's official line, to Horkheimer's discomfort; Adorno corrected Benjamin's great essay on mechanical reproduction in a correspondence that remains the most instructive quarrel in the whole corpus; Sartre and the structuralists conducted a public duel over whether man or structure explains history; and the Italian anti-Hegelians Della Volpe and Colletti built their school on the charge that the Hegelian Marxism of Lukacs and Gramsci was a philosophical deviation from Marx's actual logic. Colletti ended by leaving Marxism altogether, an exit the map records without embarrassment, since a tradition is also defined by the doors its members finally walk out of.

### Where do the tradition's two poles stand?

At one end, the young Lukacs: history has a subject, the proletariat, whose consciousness is the lever of transformation. At the other, Althusser: history is a process without a subject, and consciousness is an effect of structures. Every major figure of the tradition can be located by their distance from those two answers.

A second fracture runs along the theory of ideology, and tracing it shows the tradition working as a relay rather than a repetition. The classical inheritance spoke of false consciousness, a veil of ideas draped over real relations by the ruling class. The Western generations found that picture too thin to explain the durability of the orders they lived under, and each thickened it. Lukacs grounded distorted consciousness in the commodity form itself, so that reification was not a lie told by anyone but a structure of perception generated by everyday practice. Gramsci relocated the mechanism into civil society, into schools, churches, newspapers, and associations, where consent is manufactured as common sense rather than imposed as doctrine. The Frankfurt School, in works like the [frankfurt school complete guide](/2015/06/01/frankfurt-school-complete-guide/) surveys, added the industrial production of culture and the psychoanalytic formation of the authoritarian character, so that domination reproduced itself through entertainment and upbringing. Althusser completed the relay by arguing that ideology is not a set of ideas at all but a material practice, embedded in rituals and institutions he called ideological state apparatuses, in which individuals are constituted as subjects. A reader who follows that single thread from 1923 to 1970 possesses the tradition's spine: four generations answering one question, why the exploited consent, with successively more material and more pessimistic mechanisms.

The quarrels also had a geography and a sociology worth noting. The Hegelian current dominated the German and Hungarian beginnings and, through exile, the Frankfurt diaspora; the anti-Hegelian reaction arose in Italy and France, in countries with mass communist parties whose official philosophies were themselves Hegelianising or humanist, so that the philosophical quarrel doubled as a quarrel about the party's intellectual regime. Sartre's intervention, the Critique of Dialectical Reason in 1960, attempted to mediate the entire field from outside party discipline, and its chilly reception by both camps, the humanists found it too structural in its machinery, the structuralists too humanist in its premises, confirmed that the field's poles defined the space in which even a masterpiece had to be judged. When historians call the tradition a set of currents rather than a school, this is what they mean: a population held together by shared problems, shared ancestors, and shared enemies, producing systems that negate one another as often as they extend one another. The category survives that internal negation if, and only if, the predicament really was shared, which is the question the next sections keep open.

## The Eastern mirror

The tradition is clearest when set against the doctrine it defined itself against, the official Marxism of the Soviet Union. Between the wars, and with increasing rigidity after them, the Soviet state codified its Marxism into a system called dialectical materialism: a philosophy of nature and history laid down in textbooks, administered by party institutes, and summarised for cadres in canonical expositions of which the most widely circulated was the chapter on dialectical and historical materialism in the official history of the party published in 1938 under Stalin's name. Its traits were the photographic negative of the Western syndrome. Where the Western writing was esoteric, the codification was exoteric to the point of catechism, designed for memorisation. Where the Western writing wandered into aesthetics and psychoanalysis, the codification confined itself to laws: the laws of the dialectic, the succession of modes of production, the formulas of historical materialism recited as a natural science of society. Where the Western theorist worked in a university answerable to peer judgment, the Soviet theorist worked in an institute answerable to the party, and the price of error, after the nineteen thirties, could be not refutation but arrest. The codification's philosophical centrepiece, the reflection theory of knowledge, held that consciousness copies an independently existing material world, a position the Western current, from Lukacs onward, regarded as the burial of the dialectic under a naive realism that Marx's own manuscripts contradicted.

The mirror mattered because each side used the other as its definition of error. For Moscow and its parties, the Western writers were revisionists, idealists, professors who had dissolved the science of revolution into Hegelian mist; the 1924 condemnation of Lukacs and Korsch was the founding act of this policing, and its vocabulary, liquidation of philosophy, petty-bourgeois deviation, was still being applied to their successors decades later. For the Western writers, the codification was the intellectual face of the political catastrophe: proof that a revolution could win power and lose the theory, replacing inquiry with administration. Several of the tradition's masterpieces are unintelligible without this adversary in the room. Korsch's insistence that Marxism be applied to itself was aimed at a doctrine hardening into state metaphysics. The Frankfurt School's critique of positivism and instrumental reason treated Soviet orthodoxy and Western empiricism as cousins, two forms of thought that had made peace with the given. Althusser's whole apparatus, his distinction between science and ideology, his reading of Marx against Marx's humanist interpreters, was built to fight on two fronts at once, against the party's dogmatism and against the party's humanist softening of it, which he considered the same failure in a friendlier costume.

### What divided the Western current from the official doctrine institutionally?

The official doctrine was produced inside party institutes under discipline, as a catechism for cadres, and error could end a career or a life. The Western writing was produced in universities, endowed institutes, and exile, answerable to readers. The institutional difference explains the temperamental one: catechisms must be certain; the Western texts could afford doubt.

Yet the mirror distorts if it is polished too brightly, and honesty requires the qualification. The two Marxisms were not sealed worlds. The Western tradition's founding generation had been formed inside the movement the codifiers inherited: Lukacs remained a communist until his death, surviving expulsion, exile, and the siege of Budapest in 1956, when he served briefly in Imre Nagy's government and was deported for it. Gramsci's notebooks were preserved, edited, and published after the war by the Italian party itself, under Palmiro Togliatti's direction, an act of the official movement without which the tradition's greatest text might have been lost. Althusser conducted his entire campaign from a chair at the École Normale as a card-carrying member of the French party, using its own philosophical commissions as his battlefield. And the traffic in texts ran both ways: the Soviet-sponsored scholarly editions of Marx and Engels, the great Moscow editorial projects, published the early manuscripts and drafts on which the Western return to the sources fed. The relation is therefore better described as a quarrel inside one house than as a war between two, a quarrel in which the Western rooms happened to produce the philosophy and the Eastern rooms the power, and in which each side's account of the other was a weapon before it was a description. The label Western Marxism, coined from outside by Merleau-Ponty and codified by Anderson, belongs to the Western rooms' self-understanding; the Eastern rooms had their own labels for the same people, and none of them were compliments.

One further contrast completes the picture, and it is the contrast of fate. The official doctrine, for all its institutional power, produced almost nothing that survived its state: when the Soviet Union fell, its philosophy fell with it, unread even by its former instructors. The Western tradition, produced in every condition of weakness, exile, prison, marginality, produced concepts that the entire culture now uses without attribution. Whatever verdict the argument about the category reaches, that asymmetry of survival is a datum any theory of the tradition must digest, and it is the strongest prima facie evidence for the limit clause of the defeat thesis: the worth of work is not settled by the power or powerlessness of the circumstances that produced it.

## Reading Marx again: the return to the sources

A material accident helped make the tradition's philosophy possible, and recovering it restores a sense of how contingent intellectual history is. The Marx whom the classical generation knew was the Marx of Capital, the Manifesto, and the polemics, a political economist and revolutionary whose philosophical formation was inferred from a handful of early texts. In the early nineteen thirties, editors working on the great scholarly edition of the complete works in Moscow published two bodies of manuscript that changed the available Marx beyond recognition. The Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844 presented a young Marx theorising alienation in a Hegelian and humanist vocabulary, man estranged from his product, his activity, his fellows, and his own species-being. The Grundrisse, the vast draft notebooks of 1857 and 1858, published on the eve of the Second World War and little read until its aftermath, showed the mature economist thinking dialectically about method, money, and machinery in a register far richer than the catechisms suggested. The Western tradition is, in one of its aspects, the long digestion of those two publications. Lukacs had astonishingly anticipated the manuscripts' themes in 1923, before they were available, reconstructing alienation's structure from Capital alone, which his admirers cite as proof of genius and his critics as proof that the Hegelian Marx was partly his invention. After the manuscripts appeared, no subsequent current could avoid taking a position on them: the humanists built their Marx on the young writer of 1844, the structuralists defined their Marx by an epistemological break that quarantined him, and the Frankfurt generation absorbed the alienation theme into its account of a totally administered world.

### Why did two posthumous publications change what Marxism could say?

The manuscripts of 1844 and the Grundrisse doubled the usable Marx. They supplied a philosophical anthropology, a theory of alienation, and a dialectical method in Marx's own hand, where the party catechisms offered laws and slogans. Every later current had to build on them, break with them, or explain them away; ignoring them ceased to be an option.

The episode carries a methodological moral the tradition itself was slow to learn. Its characteristic activity became exegesis: the commentary, the reading, the recovery of the true Marx beneath the accretions of the parties. Exegesis has real virtues; it rescued a thinker from his administrators and restored his texts to philosophy. But it also fed the syndrome Anderson anatomised, since a theory that argues about its founder's manuscripts is a theory talking to itself, and the energy the Western generations spent on the correct reading of Marx was energy not spent on the analysis of their own capitalism, which was meanwhile mutating through monopoly, mass consumption, and the managed economy in ways the founding texts only partly anticipated. The exceptions prove the point by their scarcity and their fame: Gramsci's notes on Fordism and Americanism, the Frankfurt Institute's empirical studies, Sartre's struggle with the concrete history of France, stand out precisely because they turned the recovered method onto contemporary material. The series returns to this balance repeatedly, because it is the honest form of the tradition's central tension: the return to the sources was both its renewal and its temptation, the means by which it recovered a living Marx and the means by which it sometimes avoided the living world.

There is also an irony in the provenance that no account should suppress. The manuscripts were published by the scholarly apparatus of the very state whose catechism the Western current despised, edited in Moscow by institute scholars working under gathering political terror, some of whom perished in it. The Western tradition's founding library was thus a gift from its adversary, and the gift was genuine: the editions were philologically serious, and the texts did the damage to the catechism from inside, since no party exegete could unprint the young Marx once the young Marx was in the libraries. Intellectual history rarely offers so clean an emblem of the limit of the defeat thesis's companion idea, that circumstances determine output. Circumstances also scatter seeds they cannot control.

## The economists the label forgets

The strongest factual objection to the standard portrait is a list of names the portrait omits. If Western Marxism abandoned economics, someone forgot to inform the economists working inside its own flagship institution. Henryk Grossmann, a Polish economist, joined the Frankfurt Institute in its early Frankfurt years and published in 1929 his masterwork on the law of accumulation and breakdown, a rigorous, mathematical reconstruction of Marx's crisis theory arguing that capitalist accumulation generates its own breakdown tendency through the falling rate of profit. Whatever its merits, it is economics of the most austere kind, produced under the Institute's roof while the cultural turn was supposedly emptying the discipline out. Grossmann quarrelled with the Institute's direction, left its centre, and died in East Germany in 1950, half forgotten; his presence in the story is an embarrassment to any account that treats the Institute as a philosophy seminar from birth. Nor was he alone in the wider field the label covers. Paul Mattick, a German council communist who emigrated to the United States, spent decades developing a crisis theory of remorseless economic orthodoxy, attacking Keynesian management from a Marxist standpoint while the philosophers of his generation attacked the culture industry. Ernest Mandel, the Belgian Trotskyist, produced in Late Capitalism a thousand-page attempt to periodise the postwar economy, waves of accumulation, the arms economy, the service sector, that whatever its contestable theses is unmistakably a work of political economy addressed to a living movement. Maurice Dobb at Cambridge sustained a scholarly Marxist economics in Britain across the whole period, on value theory, on the transition from feudalism, on Soviet planning, in a country the map barely admits to the tradition.

### Which economists worked inside the tradition's own institutions?

Grossmann is the standing refutation: an economist of breakdown theory on the Frankfurt Institute's own staff, publishing mathematical crisis theory while the cultural turn was supposedly emptying Marxist economics. Around him stand Mattick's council communist crisis theory, Mandel's periodisation of the postwar economy, and Dobb's Cambridge scholarship, none of them philosophers manqué.

Why, then, does the standard portrait persist in saying the tradition abandoned economics? Partly because the portrait's author chose his population by the trait he was studying, a circularity the objection section formalises: define the tradition as the philosophical current, and the economists fall outside by definition, their absence then cited as evidence for the definition. Partly because the exceptions differ from the rule in a way that partly exonerates it. Grossmann worked in the Institute's first, Frankfurt phase, before exile; Mandel wrote as a movement economist for a Trotskyist international, not from a university chair, which actually fits the defeat thesis's deeper claim, that economics stayed alive where a practice survived to demand it; Mattick wrote for tiny council communist journals, in the isolation the thesis describes, and his economics had no more institutional home than the philosophers' aesthetics. The honest summary is therefore double. As a claim about every Marxist in the West, the abandonment of economics is false, and the label's boundaries are what make it look true. As a claim about the prestige centre of the tradition, about where its creative energy and its posterity concentrated, it is substantially right: the works through which the period is remembered, the books that reorganised other disciplines, are the philosophy, the aesthetics, the theories of culture and ideology, and the economists labour in the footnotes of the same decades. A category that can be corrected this precisely is not useless. It is a map with a known distortion, which is the best kind of map there is, provided the distortion is printed on it.

The correction matters beyond pedantry, because it changes what a student should do. A reader who absorbs the standard portrait and then meets Grossmann's breakdown theory, or the value-form debate, or the Italian operaists' factory analyses of the nineteen sixties, will either conclude that the portrait lied or that these works are not really part of the story. Both conclusions waste an education. The portrait describes a centre of gravity, not a census, and the tradition map in this guide is deliberately drawn to print the distortion on its face: it includes the movement column that empties, the ancestry column that widens, and it leaves the economists to this section as a standing annotation, there to remind every reader that the frame is a frame. Frames that announce themselves are instruments. Frames that pass as photographs are traps.

## What the geography leaves out

Turn the label around and look at its edges, and a silence the size of three continents appears. In 1928, five years after the founding books, a Peruvian journalist and organiser named José Carlos Mariátegui published his Seven Interpretive Essays on Peruvian Reality, a work that founded an Indo-American Marxism: an analysis of land, indigenous communal traditions, and colonial inheritance that treated the Andean ayllu not as a feudal residue but as a living cell of a possible socialism, and that famously refused to copy European models, insisting that socialism in the Americas must be a heroic creation and not a tracing. Mariátegui wrote as a movement intellectual, founded a party and a journal, theorised culture and myth alongside land reform, and died in 1930 at thirty-five. By every structural trait on Anderson's list except address, he belongs in the story: the philosophical depth, the cultural turn, the fusion of Marx with other ancestries, Sorel and the indigenous tradition in his case. He is absent from the standard map because the map is of the West, and the West, in this usage, stops at the Atlantic's eastern shore and the Mediterranean's northern one. The series sets his tradition in its own frame in its guide to [marxism latin america guide](/2018/10/01/marxism-latin-america-guide/), where he is not an exception but a founder.

He is not alone in the silence. In India, M. N. Roy, a founder of communist parties on two continents who had debated Lenin himself at the Second Congress of the Communist International over the colonial question, spent the nineteen twenties and thirties building a Marxist analysis of Indian society that quarrelled with both the Comintern's stagism and the nationalist movement's romanticism, and ended by reconstructing his thought toward a radical humanism whose ancestry, the young Marx of the manuscripts, was the same one the Europeans were recovering. In the Caribbean, C. L. R. James, a Trinidadian historian and organiser, produced in The Black Jacobins of 1938 a Marxist history of the Haitian Revolution that remains a classic of the literature, joined the Trotskyist movement, and co-authored analyses of state capitalism and of the Soviet question that argued with the orthodoxies from a position no European category anticipated. In China, a generation of theorists debated the nature of Chinese society, its feudal or capitalist character, its Asiatic peculiarities, in a controversy that involved the living strategy of the largest communist movement on earth. In Japan, the Koza school and its rivals conducted a controversy over Japanese capitalism of a sophistication equal to anything in Europe. A map of Marxist theory between the wars that confines itself to the label's territory is thus a map of a province drawn as if it were the world, and the distortion is not innocent, because the province was, demographically and politically, the smaller part of the story: the revolutions that actually succeeded in the middle of the century succeeded outside it.

### What does the label do to Mariátegui?

It files a founder as a footnote. A Peruvian who fused Marx with Sorel and with indigenous communal traditions, founded a party and a journal, and theorised culture as deeply as any European contemporary, vanishes from a category whose traits he matches on every count except continent, which exposes the category's geography as its silent argument.

Why does the exclusion persist, if it is so visible once stated? Three reasons, of increasing discomfort. The first is philological and forgivable: traditions of scholarship follow languages and archives, and the European current's texts were translated, edited, and taught in the universities that write the surveys, while Mariátegui's essays waited decades for adequate English dress and Roy's and James's works were filed under area studies or movement history. The second is structural: the category was built in Europe, by Europeans, to explain a European predicament, and its builder, to his credit, knew the boundary was his, since his own later work ranged across the whole planet; the fault lies less in the construction than in the textbook afterlife that forgot the builder's modesty. The third is the uncomfortable one, and the polemical revival discussed below presses exactly here: a Marxism that theorised the whole world while reading only one continent of it reproduced, inside a doctrine of universal emancipation, the Eurocentrism the doctrine existed to oppose. The Western tradition's own best concepts turn on it at this point with embarrassing ease. Hegemony describes how a partial outlook passes itself off as the universal one. Reification describes how a human product confronts its makers as a natural fact. The category Western Marxism, a human product if ever there was one, has repeatedly confronted its users as a natural fact, the shape of Marxist theory as such, and the work of this section is simply to hand the product back to its makers.

The qualification owed in fairness is that exclusion by a label is not the same as contempt by the labelled. The European figures were not ignorant of the colonial world: the Frankfurt Institute's members wrote as refugees of a Europe that had exported its violence; Gramsci's notes on the Southern question, on the relation of northern industry to the southern peasantry of his own country, are a founding text of internal-colonial analysis; Sartre wrote prefaces that detonated in the anti-colonial movement, including the famous introduction to Fanon's The Wretched of the Earth; and the tradition's concepts were taken up with gratitude, not resentment, by many of the thinkers the label excludes, who found in hegemony and reification tools for their own situations. The silence is a property of the category, not a charge against every career inside it, and keeping that distinction straight is what separates the criticism of a map from the dismissal of the territory.

## Grading the awkward cases

A thesis earns its keep on the cases it was not designed for, and the defeat thesis has three famous awkward cases that every serious account must grade rather than hide. The first is Gramsci. The syndrome predicts that a theorist inside a living party, writing as its imprisoned leader for an imagined readership of militants, should resemble the classical generation; and in his strategic concreteness, his attention to parties, armies, newspapers, and the state, Gramsci does. Yet his masterwork is written in a cell, in a coded and fragmentary form, saturated with philosophy, Croce and Machiavelli at its elbow, and its central concept answers exactly the question the defeat posed: why the frontal assault failed in the West and what long war of position must replace it. Gramsci thus fits the thesis at the level of the question and escapes it at the level of the career, which is why he is best described as the tradition's limiting case: the proof that the syndrome describes a predicament, not a personality type, since the same predicament processed by a party leader yields strategy where in a professor it yields aesthetics. The second awkward case is the Della Volpe school in Italy, whose anti-Hegelian, Kantian Marxism inverts the ancestry trait while confirming all the others, reminding us that the syndrome's traits are a cluster, not a checklist, and that a specimen may negate one trait as its way of belonging. The third is Sartre, who at the height of the syndrome wrote the period's most ambitious attempt to reunite theory with a concrete history, and whose failure to finish the second volume of his Critique, abandoned amid the quarrels of the sixties, can be recruited by either side: evidence for the thesis, that the reunion was impossible in those conditions, or evidence against its determinism, that the attempt was made at all.

Periodisation is the other instrument for grading, and the student of the tradition should hold the periods apart as carefully as the persons. A heroic founding phase runs from the postwar revolutionary crisis to the early nineteen thirties, when the questions were still open and the parties still consequential. A phase of catastrophe and exile follows, from the fascist victories to the end of the Second World War, in which the tradition's darkest masterpieces were composed in flight, in hiding, in prison, or in the false safety of American suburbs, and in which its centre of gravity shifted irreversibly toward the theory of domination itself, toward the question of how civilisation had produced barbarism rather than how the proletariat might produce socialism. A postwar phase of academic consolidation runs to the late nineteen sixties, in which the surviving figures became professors and classics, the manuscripts were edited, the translations multiplied, and the syndrome's traits, academic address, esoteric language, philosophical ancestry, were no longer symptoms of isolation so much as conditions of employment. A final phase of diffusion and quarrel, the humanist and structuralist duel and its aftermath, carried the tradition's concepts into feminism, literary studies, and the new social movements even as its named masters died and its parties decomposed. The phases matter because the defeat thesis grades differently in each: nearly conclusive for the catastrophe years, suggestive for the founding, and increasingly metaphorical for the consolidation, when isolation had become a career structure rather than a wound. A category with phases is a historical instrument. A category without them is a stereotype with footnotes.

### What should a newcomer separate before judging any single figure?

Three separations keep judgment honest. Separate the period, since a text of the catastrophe years answers different conditions from a text of the academic consolidation. Separate the career from the concept, since a thinker may fit the syndrome in biography and escape it in influence. And separate the label's verdict from the work's, since the frame was built to argue, not to grade.

The British absence belongs in this section as a minor awkward case with a major lesson. The standard map's silence about Britain is striking, since Britain possessed the oldest labour movement in Europe and a distinguished Marxist historiography, the historians around the Communist Party Historians' Group, figures like Christopher Hill, Eric Hobsbawm, and E. P. Thompson, who produced in the postwar decades a body of Marxist historical writing of the first rank. Why are they not Western Marxists in the canonical sense? Because they wrote history, not philosophy; because their idiom was empirical and narrative rather than systematic; and because Thompson, in The Poverty of Theory, launched against Althusser the most famous polemic ever written from inside Marxism against the structuralist wing of the tradition, a book-length defence of humanist history against Parisian theory. The British case thus clarifies the category's true differentia. The label was never really about longitude. It names a deformation of Marxism toward philosophy under conditions of political defeat, and where British Marxism, defeated in its own ways after 1926 and 1956, deformed toward history instead, it simply fell outside the syndrome the label was built to describe. A student who understands the British absence understands the category better than one who can recite its membership.

## Why the prose is difficult

Every newcomer asks it, usually with some resentment: why is this writing so hard to read? The tradition's canonical texts make demands that the classical Marxist classics, for all their own density, do not. Capital is long and technical, but it argues like an economist, in the open. The Western texts argue like philosophers under surveillance, in vocabularies borrowed from Hegel, Husserl, Heidegger, and Freud, through essays, fragments, aphorisms, and notebooks that withhold their conclusions as a matter of method. Five explanations operate together, and assigning each its proper weight is the fastest way to stop mistaking the difficulty for a fraud or a virtue in itself.

The first is censorship, literal and internalised. Gramsci wrote in a prison cell with his books monitored, and developed an Aesopian idiom, speaking of the philosophy of praxis and the Modern Prince where plainer words would have drawn the censor's pen; the code outlived the need and became part of the text's permanent texture. Lukacs wrote under party discipline, in the knowledge that a sentence could be evidence at his next self-criticism, and learned a style in which commitments are advanced and withdrawn within the same paragraph. The Frankfurt writers under Nazism, and then under the anti-communist pressures of their American exile, had their own reasons for indirection. The second explanation is institutional: once Marxist writing relocated to universities, it acquired the university's dialect, in which difficulty is a professional credential and a career asset, and in which a theorist demonstrates membership by mastering the ancestors in their original terminology. The third is philosophical and principled: much of the tradition held that its object, a society that systematically misrepresents itself, could not be described in the plain language the society supplied, since ordinary words carry the misrepresentation inside them. Adorno made a method of this suspicion, arguing that the concept must be strained against itself to say what administered language forbids, and his aphoristic and paratactic styles are the difficulty of that method made visible. The fourth is the absence of the audience that disciplines prose: a writer addressing a movement must be understood by organisers on deadlines, while a writer addressing posterity and a seminar can afford to be understood eventually. Anderson treated this fourth cause as the master one, and the correlation supports him, since the tradition's clearest writers, Gramsci in his letters, Sartre in his journalism, Korsch in his pamphlets, are clearest exactly where a concrete addressee imposed itself.

### Is the difficulty a defect or a method?

Both, in proportions that vary by author and by page. Where it encodes censorship, professional habit, or the loss of a movement audience, it is a symptom, and the defeat thesis names its cause. Where it enacts a principled suspicion of ordinary language as itself ideological, it is a method, and the reader must either grant the premise or argue with it.

The fifth explanation is the one nobody inside the tradition liked to state: difficulty as theodicy. A theory that expected to be verified by a revolution that never came faced a standing temptation to relocate verification into the text, to let depth of formulation substitute for the missing tribunal of practice. The more history refused the theory, the more the theory could present its own resistance to paraphrase as proof that it had seen what plainer minds missed. Readers should keep this suspicion in their pockets, not as a verdict on any given book but as a question to ask of every forbidding page: is this hard because the object is hard, or because the hardness is doing the work of evidence? The tradition's masterpieces survive the question impressively often. Its minor works, and every tradition accumulates them, are another matter. It is also only fair to record the counterweight, that the tradition contains prose of the first order, Benjamin's sentences, Gramsci's letters, Bloch's baroque abundance, and that the difficulty complaint is often a complaint about the reader's own missing preparation in the philosophical inheritance, a preparation the reading guide in this series is designed to supply in the right order. A text can be forbidding because it is empty and because it is full, and learning to tell the difference, slowly, in company, is a large part of what studying this tradition is for.

There is a related complaint, that the tradition is all critique and no construction, that it produced magnificent instruments for unmasking domination and almost no account of the society that should replace it. The complaint is broadly accurate and its explanation is again structural: utopia is the genre of ascending movements, and a defeated intelligentsia does not draft constitutions. Bloch stands as the great exception, the philosopher of hope who made the not yet his central category, and his exceptionality inside the canon, revered, quoted, and quietly treated as an eccentric, measures the rule. Even the tradition's affirmative moments arrive as negations doubled: freedom as the refusal of the given, art as the promise of happiness held in the form of its denial, the good life as whatever the administered world is not. Whether this negativity is a failure of nerve or the tradition's deepest honesty, its refusal to paint futures it could not build, is a judgment the reader must eventually make alone, and it is a better judgment for knowing that the negativity has a history and an address, in the cell, the exile's flat, and the seminar room, rather than a birthplace in temperament.

## The objection that the category was built to win an argument

The strongest objection to everything in this guide must now be stated at the strength its proponents would demand, because a guide that cannot survive its own best criticism is a pamphlet. The objection runs as follows. The category Western Marxism is a retrospective construction, assembled in the nineteen seventies by an analyst with a political project of his own, and its construction is circular in a way that invalidates its findings. Anderson was not a neutral cartographer. He was the editor of a leading journal of the British New Left, engaged in a long campaign to re-found British Marxism on what he regarded as sounder, more classical, more strategically serious foundations, and his survey of the continental tradition was an instrument of that campaign. He selected a population of thinkers who exhibited a syndrome, distance from practice, philosophical displacement, pessimism, and then announced the syndrome as a discovery about the population, when it had been the criterion of selection all along. The economists who would have broken the pattern, Grossmann in the movement's own institute, Mandel writing for a living international, the Italians of the factory councils tradition, were excluded or marginalised by the construction itself. The British historians who would have broken it differently were excluded by a geography that was never defended because it could not be. The non-European Marxists whose existence refuted the frame's implied world were excluded by a name inherited from a phenomenologist's local contrast and never interrogated. And the effect of the construction, intended or not, was dismissive: to define a body of concepts as the ideology of a defeat is to invite the reader to treat reification, hegemony, and the rest as symptoms to be explained rather than arguments to be answered, to pathologise a library instead of reading it. On this objection, the category does not illuminate the tradition. It convicts it, in a trial where the judge chose the evidence.

The objection gains force from the sequel. Anderson's own intellectual trajectory after the survey, his turn toward a more classical and strategic Marxism and his editorial championship of the very revolutionary traditions, Russian above all, that the Western current had been measured against and found wanting, reads in retrospect like the survey's suppressed premise made explicit: the book was the anatomy of a deviation, written to clear the ground for a return. Critics of the construction also note what its success concealed. Martin Jay's alternative history of the same population, organised around the pursuit of totality rather than the syndrome of defeat, demonstrated that the same shelf of books could support a different and less prosecutorial unity, one in which the tradition's unity lay in an ambition, the recovery of a mediated whole, rather than in a failure. When two incompatible unities can be imposed on one population with equal documentary plausibility, the suspicion hardens that the unity is in the imposers.

### Whose argument does the category serve?

In its codified form, the category served its codifier's campaign to re-found his own national Marxism on classical and strategic lines, by exhibiting the continental tradition as a cautionary anatomy of theory divorced from practice. That service is documented in the sequel of his work. A category's service, however, is a fact about its history, not a disproof of its observations.

The answer to the objection must begin with concession, because much of it is true. The boundaries of the category do the argument's work; the exclusion of the economists is definitional before it is empirical; the exclusion of the non-European world is a silence built into the name; and the construction's author had a politics, as the concession in his own pages, which present the survey as a contribution to a debate within Marxism rather than as a report from outside it, never really hid. A reader who has absorbed the previous sections has already seen each of these points documented in this guide's own voice, in the map's limits, in the economists' section, and in the geography section, because the objection is strong enough that an honest guide must build it into the foundations rather than answer it from the battlements.

What survives the concession is the distinction between a thesis about origins and a verdict on worth, the distinction this guide has called the limit of the defeat thesis. The objection shows that the category was constructed to argue; it does not show that the construction's central observations were false. The divorce of theory from practice across the continental population is a documented pattern of careers, institutions, and bibliographies, visible in any reference work that does not share the constructor's politics. The defeats that preceded and accompanied the pattern are not interpretations but dates. The displacement toward philosophy and culture is a fact of the publication record. A construction can be interested and accurate at once, as a prosecutor's brief can state the evidence correctly while omitting the evidence for the defence, and the remedy for the omission is the one this guide has applied: restore the omitted evidence to the record, the economists, the British, the Peruvians, the awkward cases, and then see what the thesis still explains. It still explains a great deal, and what it no longer explains, the portability of the concepts, the fertility of the tradition in hands its constructor never imagined, marks exactly the boundary where explanation by circumstances must stop and judgment of arguments begin. The objection also cuts its own wielder, finally, in one place: to dismiss the concepts as the ideology of a defeat would be to commit the genetic fallacy in mirror image, and the strongest version of the objection, the version worth fearing, never asks for that dismissal. It asks for the frame to be used knowingly, as an argument among arguments, which is all this guide has ever claimed for it.

### What would make the category worth keeping?

Precisely the use this guide has made of it: held as a hypothesis with its construction confessed, its boundaries treated as claims, its omissions restored to view, and its central thesis forbidden to grade the concepts it explains. Kept on those terms, the category organises a century of difficult writing into an intelligible and contestable story. Kept on any other terms, it hardens into the kind of reified label its own best concepts teach us to dissolve.

## The Institute's exception: research against the syndrome

The tradition's best-known institution is also its most instructive misfit, and any honest anatomy must record the respects in which the Frankfurt Institute for Social Research breaks the syndrome it is supposed to exemplify. The Institute was founded in Frankfurt in the early nineteen twenties on a private endowment, attached to the university but financially independent of it, and directed from 1930 by Max Horkheimer, whose inaugural programme called for an interdisciplinary materialism in which philosophy would organise, but not replace, empirical research in economics, sociology, and psychology. The programme was executed, at least in the Institute's first exile decade, on a scale no other body in this guide could match: a large study of authority and the family, combining questionnaires, interviews, and psychoanalytic interpretation across several European countries; Erich Fromm's survey of German workers' and employees' attitudes, conducted before 1933 and published only decades later, which measured in advance the authoritarian dispositions that fascism would mobilise; and, in the American years, the great collaborative studies of prejudice that culminated in The Authoritarian Personality of 1950, an attempt, with American social science's own instruments, to specify the character structure receptive to fascist appeal. A tradition defined by its flight from empirical reality into philosophy should not contain the century's most ambitious empirical programme inside Marxist social research. Yet the programme is there, in the record, and the full account of it belongs to the [frankfurt school complete guide](/2015/06/01/frankfurt-school-complete-guide/) in this series.

The misfit teaches the same lesson as Grossmann's economics, at the level of method rather than subject matter. The syndrome describes a centre of gravity that shifted over time, in this case within one institution's life: the Institute of the nineteen thirties, still flush with endowment and staffed across the disciplines, practised the union of philosophy and research that its programme promised; the Institute of the wartime and postwar years, its funds depleted, its members scattered into American universities and its director and Adorno withdrawn into the great theoretical summae, fits the syndrome ever more closely, until the dialectic of enlightenment replaced the questionnaire as the characteristic instrument. The trajectory inside the institution thus recapitulates the trajectory of the tradition, from engagement through catastrophe to contemplative depth, and suggests the correct refinement of the defeat thesis: it is not a label for persons but a gradient along which persons and institutions slid as their circumstances worsened. Grading a thinker against the thesis, the task the awkward cases section began, should therefore always specify a date. The Horkheimer of the research programme and the Horkheimer of the wartime fragments are the same man at different points on the gradient, and the thesis explains the slope between them better than any psychology of pessimism.

The positivist dispute of the nineteen sixties displays the tradition defending its method against the other claimant to scientific rationality, and it is worth a paragraph because it shows the syndrome's philosophy trait operating as a considered position rather than a retreat. At a conference of the German Sociological Association in Tübingen in 1961, Karl Popper and Adorno confronted one another over the logic of the social sciences, Popper pressing the claims of falsification and piecemeal reform, Adorno insisting that a society which is itself a contradictory totality cannot be known by methods that take its categories as given. The ensuing controversy, joined by their students and published as a famous volume, was mannerly in form and total in substance: two inheritances of the Enlightenment, the critical rationalism that trusts method and the dialectical critique that suspects method of complicity, disputing the right to define reason. Whatever a reader's verdict, the dispute refutes the picture of the Western tradition as a Marxism that had stopped arguing with the present. It argued with the present constantly. What it had lost was not opponents but a movement, and its arguments consequently took the form of interventions in philosophy and social science, addressed to colleagues, in journals, with the peculiar intensity of people for whom the seminar had become the last available public sphere.

A word belongs here on the tradition's relationship to psychoanalysis, since the Freudian ancestry on the map can look like decoration and was anything but. The mechanisms the tradition needed, to explain consent that outlasted interest, required an account of how authority is installed inside the person before argument begins, and psychoanalysis was the only available theory of that installation. Fromm's concept of the authoritarian character, the fusion of submission upward and cruelty downward in a single structure of feeling, was built to answer the question the defeats posed in its sharpest form: why had the German working class, organised, educated, and numerous, accommodated itself to its own destruction? Marcuse's later fusion of Marx and Freud, distinguishing the repression any civilisation requires from the surplus repression a particular order imposes, attempted to give the theory of liberation a bodily foundation, a stratum of need that no administered consciousness could wholly reach. That these constructions are contestable, that orthodox Freudians and orthodox Marxists each found them heretical, is beside the point for the anatomy: they show the tradition's philosophical turn functioning as an instrument of explanation, not as an ornament of exile.

## Three homelands, three inflections

The label flattens its territory into a single adjective, and recovering the national inflections is the quickest corrective after the map. The German inflection came first and set the tradition's philosophical register. German Marxism worked in the shadow of the strongest philosophical culture in Europe and of the strongest labour movement, and its characteristic drama was the fall from that double eminence: a movement that had built a state within the state, parties, unions, newspapers, theatres, and then surrendered without a battle in 1933. German Western Marxism is therefore the tradition's most metaphysical and most despairing variant, the variant that interrogated reason itself, because it was German reason, German organisation, and German enlightenment that had stood by. The exile scattered this variant across the planet, and its postwar return to Frankfurt, where Horkheimer and Adorno refounded the Institute and trained a generation of German sociologists and philosophers, gave the Federal Republic an intellectual conscience formed in California and New York, an irony the Germans themselves have never tired of examining.

The Italian inflection is the tradition's most political, and the difference is a party. Italy possessed, after 1945, the largest communist party in the Western world, a party that under Togliatti pursued a national road and treated Gramsci's legacy as its founding charter, publishing the Prison Notebooks in thematic editions that made their author a national classic. Italian Marxism consequently developed inside a symbiosis of party and culture that has no equivalent elsewhere in the map: its debates, between the Hegelian readers of Gramsci and the anti-Hegelian school of Della Volpe, between the party's historicism and the workerist heresies that broke from its left in the nineteen sixties to analyse the factory directly, were conducted in party journals, party publishing houses, and the great newspapers, before a public that included ministers and metalworkers. Where German Western Marxism asked how enlightenment became barbarism, Italian Western Marxism asked how a subaltern class becomes capable of governing, and the difference in the questions is the difference between a tradition formed by a movement's annihilation and one formed by a movement's tantalising, permanent proximity to power. Italy is thus the second great limiting case for the defeat thesis: a country where the divorce of theory from practice was real but partial, where the practice existed, electorally massive and culturally hegemonic in its zones, and where the theory's characteristic product was not pessimism but a science of political strategy whose categories, hegemony, the national popular, the war of position, have entered the general vocabulary of politics.

The French inflection is the tradition's most scholastic in the honourable sense, formed by the peculiar French machinery that concentrates intellectual production in a few Parisian institutions and by the massive, immobile presence of a communist party with its own intellectual apparatus. French Western Marxism is a duel conducted in a single city: on one side the party's official philosophy and its humanist interpreters, for whom the young Marx of the manuscripts licensed a Marxism of man; on the other the successive insurgencies of existentialism and structuralism, Sartre's attempt to refound Marxism on freedom, Althusser's attempt to refound it on science, each defining itself against the party while addressing it, each drawing the era's most gifted students into its seminar. The French variant also displays the ancestry trait at its most theatrical, since its masters presented themselves as readers before they presented themselves as thinkers: Sartre reading history through the Critique, Althusser reading Capital in a famous collective symposium, so that the theory of reading became itself a contribution to theory. To these three homelands a fourth, negative case belongs by right of contrast: the Hungarian beginning, Lukacs's Budapest and Vienna, where the tradition was born inside a revolution that actually, briefly, held power, and whose founding texts carry a confidence, an expectation that philosophy and insurrection might still be one act, that none of the later homelands recovered. The gradient of the whole tradition can be plotted as the fading of that Hungarian confidence through the German catastrophe and the Italian and French accommodations, and the plot, once seen, is difficult to unsee.

## The existential embassy

Two figures stand at the tradition's border with a foot outside it, and the border is more instructive than the interior. The first is the coiner himself. Merleau-Ponty coined the phrase as a diagnostician of a Marxism he was in the process of leaving: his book of 1955 is an audit of revolutionary philosophy by a man concluding that history offers no guarantees and that the dialectic, as the parties wielded it, had become an alibi for violence. The label thus carries its author's farewell inside it, a fact worth remembering whenever it is wielded as a compliment. The second is Sartre, whose relation to Marxism passed through three distinct weathers: the wartime and postwar attempt to build an existentialism beside Marxism, treating it, in a famous formulation, as the philosophy of the time that could not be surpassed but had stopped moving; the long, quarrelsome fellow-travelling of the nineteen fifties, in which he defended the socialist camp against its critics while reserving his philosophy from its doctrine; and the monumental Critique of Dialectical Reason of 1960, which attempted to supply Marxism with the theory of history and the individual that it lacked, grounding the whole in the translucidity of individual praxis colliding with the practico-inert, the dead labour of past acts that turns men's projects against them. The Critique is the tradition's most ambitious single book and its most characteristic: a thousand pages of philosophical architecture built to answer the question the defeats had posed, how free beings produce a history that enslaves them, written by a man who held no party card and no chair, from the pure altitude of literary fame.

Sartre's quarrel and alliance with the structuralists closed the tradition's classical age in the form of a public duel over its founding question. When Lévi-Strauss devoted the final chapter of The Savage Mind to an attack on the Critique, and when Althusser's school proclaimed a theoretical anti-humanism for which man was an ideological category, the issue between the camps was exactly the issue between the tradition's poles that this guide traced from Lukacs: whether history has a subject and whether consciousness is a cause or an effect. The duel's ferocity, conducted in journals and lecture halls before a generation of students who would make the events of 1968, demonstrates something the syndrome language can obscure, namely that the tradition in its French phase was not a retirement but an arena, the place where the most consequential arguments about Marxism's future were actually happening, in the absence of any congress or central committee competent to settle them. A theory divorced from practice is not thereby divorced from stakes. Its stakes become the allegiance of intellectual generations, the syllabuses of universities, and the vocabularies in which future movements will think, and by those measures the French duel was among the most consequential political events of its decade.

The events of 1968 then staged the reunion the tradition had awaited for forty years, and the reunion was an embarrassment for nearly everyone involved. When the student movement exploded across France, Germany, Italy, and the United States, it carried the tradition's books in its pockets: Marcuse, whose One-Dimensional Man had anatomised the affluent integration of the working class and whose name the students chanted in three capitals, became the reluctant guru of a revolt whose spontaneity his theory had not predicted; the young read Lukacs and Gramsci as strategists again rather than as classics; and the movement's own theorisation, its councils, its anti-authoritarianism, its cultural revolution against everyday life, was saturated with the tradition's concepts of reification, hegemony, and false needs. Yet the reunion exposed the divorce from the other side. Adorno, whose Institute building was occupied by students of his own university in 1969, called the police to clear it and died months later amid recriminations that his theory had promised a practice it disowned when the practice arrived at his door. Marcuse defended the students against his old colleague's dismay. The French party, whose intellectuals feature throughout this guide, condemned the student movement as adventurist and thereby confirmed, before a generation, every suspicion the tradition had harboured about the apparatus. The episode does not refute the defeat thesis; it extends it, showing that a theory formed in isolation had lost, among other capacities, the capacity to recognise its own posterity. But it also supplies the limit of the thesis with its most vivid evidence: the concepts had survived their isolation so completely that they could animate a movement their authors could not lead. A library had become an arsenal for readers its writers never met, which is either the definition of a classic or the definition of a tradition that escaped its conditions, and by 1968 the two definitions had become indistinguishable.

## A term revived as a charge

A label's career does not end with its codification, and the phrase has acquired a later polemical life that a responsible guide must register without joining. In a body of argument that has circulated in books, journals, and the polemical press, the term is revived as a charge: Western Marxism, on this usage, names a dereliction, a Marxism that abandoned the anti-colonial struggles of the twentieth century, that treated the colonised world as a backdrop for European theory, that preferred the library to the liberation movement and the paradox to the partisan, and that thereby forfeited the tradition of Marx himself, who had written on India, Ireland, and the American Civil War with a journalist's urgency. The charge draws genuine evidence from the record this guide has examined: the geography of the canon is European, the classical texts of the tradition rarely address the colonial world as their central object, and the traffic of influence between the European theorists and the century's actual revolutions, in China, Vietnam, Cuba, and Algeria, ran thin compared with the influence of Lenin's and Mao's texts, which the colonised movements actually carried. It also draws strength from the comparison that shames: while the seminar Marxists refined the theory of ideology, Marxists in the colonised world were producing, under fire, a body of strategic and cultural thought, Fanon's analyses of colonial psychology among them, that the canon long treated as testimony rather than theory.

### How is the term used when it is used as a charge?

In the polemical revival, the phrase stops describing a predicament and starts alleging a betrayal: that the European theorists neglected the anti-colonial revolutions, the century's largest fact, in favour of philosophy and culture. The charge is contested, on evidence about texts, influences, and the category's own construction, and it is registered here as a live usage of the term, neither adopted nor dismissed.

The counter-arguments are also on the record, and they are not trivial. The category whose geography is indicted was, as this guide has shown, a construction of the nineteen seventies, and indicting the constructed population for the constructor's boundaries repeats the circularity the objection section exposed, with the polarity reversed. Within the labelled population, the colonial question is less absent than the charge supposes: Gramsci's Southern question generalised into a method for analysing internal peripheries, Sartre's anti-colonial interventions were neither few nor quiet, the Frankfurt refugees wrote as the products of a Europe whose violence had never been only internal, and the tradition's concepts proved, in the event, more useful to anti-colonial and postcolonial thinkers than the charge allows, since hegemony, ideology critique, and the analysis of cultural domination became standard instruments in the hands of the very intellectuals the European canon had overlooked. There is also a question of standards: the charge measures European theorists against the revolutions of their century and finds them wanting, a standard that, applied impartially, would convict every body of theory produced anywhere in the same decades, including much of the theory the charge champions, whose authors also wrote in studies while others fought. None of this dissolves the underlying fact, which stands: the canon as transmitted centred Europe, and the work of decentring it, of teaching Mariátegui beside Lukacs and Fanon beside Adorno, is a real correction that the series itself practises in its own architecture. The usage is therefore recorded here as what it is, a contested current employment of an old label, carrying a true bill about the canon and a contestable one about the careers, and the reader who meets the phrase in the wild should first ask which of its three lives, the phenomenologist's diagnosis, the historian's period, or the polemicist's charge, is being invoked, since the answer determines what would count as a reply.

## How the label crossed into English

One transmission line deserves its own account, because the tradition the English-speaking world knows arrived through a specific editorial machinery whose choices shaped the canon as decisively as any author's. Before the nineteen sixties, the English reader had access to fragments: a translation of selections from Lukacs here, the Frankfurt School barely at all, Gramsci in a single volume of selections from the prison notebooks published in the late nineteen fifties. The great transfer occurred in the following two decades, driven by the New Left that formed after 1956 and by the publishing programme of the New Left Review and its book arm, which Anderson himself directed for a period: systematic translations of Lukacs, Gramsci's full Prison Notebooks in a scholarly edition of 1971, the Frankfurt corpus, Althusser's interventions, the Italian debates, issued with introductions that told the Anglophone reader how the pieces fitted into a single story. The story they fitted into was, to a significant degree, the story the codification of 1976 then formalised, so that the canon and its interpretation arrived in English as one package, translation and thesis bound together. This is the material basis of the objection's best point: for the English-speaking student, the tradition never existed except inside its frame, and unbinding the two requires the deliberate archaeology this guide has attempted, recovering the economists, the national inflections, and the excluded continents from behind the frame's clean lines.

The transfer also altered the tradition's centre of gravity in a way its makers could not have foreseen. In their own languages, these were interventions in national debates, heavy with local reference: Gramsci arguing with Croce and the Risorgimento, Sartre with the French party and the Algerian war, Althusser with his party's philosophers. In English, stripped of their occasions and shelved together as theory, they became a curriculum, and the curriculum's effects were enormous and double-edged. A generation of Anglophone scholars in literature, sociology, history, and the new cultural studies received its Marxism almost exclusively in this Western form, as a theory of culture, ideology, and consciousness, and proceeded to build disciplines on it; while the other Marxisms, the political economy, the strategy, the non-European traditions, reached the same readers later, partially, or in rival translations with rival frames. When critics of the academy complain that the Marxism of the universities is a theory of texts rather than of the world, they are describing, whether they know it or not, the long shadow of an editorial decision taken in London in the nineteen sixties, and when defenders reply that this Marxism kept the tradition alive through its decades of political defeat, they are describing the same shadow from the other side. Both are right, which is the condition of most truths about this subject.

## The afterlife of the concepts

The most consequential fact about the tradition is one its own framework cannot accommodate, which is why this guide treats it as the standing evidence for the limit clause: the concepts escaped. Reification left its Hungarian birthplace and entered general sociology, media theory, and the ordinary critical vocabulary, so that journalists who have never heard of Lukacs describe the reified character of metrics, markets, and bureaucratic categories. Hegemony left the prison notebooks and became, first, the strategic charter of the Italian party's national road and the watchword of Eurocommunism's parliamentary turn, then the founding concept of British cultural studies, where the Birmingham school rebuilt the study of popular culture around the question of how consent to an unequal order is won and occasionally lost, and then a term of art in international relations, in discourse theory, and in the analysis of everything from curriculum design to climate politics. The culture industry, coined in wartime exile as a deliberate affront to a cheerful phrase, became the unavoidable reference point for every subsequent account of commercial entertainment, cited by its enemies as faithfully as by its heirs. Ideology, retooled by Althusser from a name for false ideas into a name for the material rituals that constitute subjects, reorganised literary studies, film theory, and the philosophy of mind's Marxist wing for two generations. Overdetermination travelled from Freud's dream book through Althusser's essays into the post-Marxist theory of the nineteen eighties, whose authors rebuilt democratic strategy on the claim that no social identity is ever fully sutured, a claim its originator would have regarded with suspicion and which nonetheless descends from his workshop in a documented line.

Feminism's encounter with the tradition deserves separate mention, because it shows the concepts working under maximum strain and surviving. The Marxist feminists of the nineteen seventies found in the Western canon a theory of ideology, consciousness, and everyday life vastly richer than the classical tradition's treatment of the woman question, and they took it up critically: the Frankfurt analyses of the family, Gramsci's account of common sense, and the whole tradition's insistence that domination is reproduced in the intimate routines of ordinary days supplied instruments for analysing housework, sexuality, and the gendered division of labour that orthodox political economy had left untheorised. The encounter was reciprocal in its effects, since feminist criticism exposed, in the canon itself, a silence about gender as systematic as the canon's silence about the colonies, a parallel this guide notes without needing to labour: the tradition's own tools, turned on the tradition, keep finding the same shape of omission, a universal theory built from a partial standpoint, and the repeated discovery is itself the strongest argument that the tools are better than the frame that delivered them.

Is the tradition finished? The question conceals three different ones, and separating them is the last analytical service this section performs. As a population of writers, it is closed: its youngest canonical figures belong to the generation formed by the Second World War, and no successor generation has been admitted to the map on the same terms, partly because the conditions that defined the syndrome, the European workers' movement as theory's addressee, dissolved with the century. As a predicament, it recurs wherever Marxist theory is produced in separation from a movement capable of using it, which is to say in most universities most of the time, so that young theorists in Seoul, São Paulo, or Johannesburg who write philosophy in the shadow of defeated movements are living the syndrome under new flags, whether or not the old label is applied to them. As a body of concepts, it is demonstrably not finished, since the concepts continue to organise research and argument in fields their authors never entered. A label that is closed as a census, recurrent as a condition, and open as an inheritance is not a period so much as a permanent possibility inside Marxist theory, the form the theory takes when history refuses to keep its appointments. That is a melancholy conclusion, and also, given the concepts' record of survival, a strangely hopeful one, and the tradition's own darkest text supplies the formula for holding both at once: the hope, Benjamin wrote in the essay he reworked for the Institute's journal, is given us only for the sake of the hopeless, a sentence whose afterlife in movements he never saw is the whole argument of this section in miniature.

## Two concepts and their careers

Abstract claims about portability are cheap. The honest test is to follow two concepts out of the tradition and watch what happened to them on the road. Take reification first. Its prehistory runs through Weber's account of rationalisation, the spread of calculative, rule-bound conduct through every institution of modern life, and through the analysis in Capital of commodity fetishism, where social relations between men assume the fantastic form of relations between things. Lukacs's achievement of 1923 was to fuse the two into a single structure: the commodity form, become universal, stamps the whole of consciousness, so that the worker, the official, the journalist, and the philosopher alike confront a world of self-moving objects governed by laws they contemplate but do not make, and their inner stance becomes that contemplation itself, passive, fragmented, specialised. The concept was denounced at its birth as idealism, suppressed with its book, and recovered in the nineteen sixties by readers who found in it the theory their own administered world required. Lucien Goldmann carried it into literary sociology, arguing that the structures of the modern novel mirrored the structures of a reified market society. The sociologists of knowledge absorbed its substance, sometimes with acknowledgment and sometimes without, into the general theory of how institutions become things in the minds of their inhabitants. In the digital economy of platforms and metrics, the concept found a third life, applied to the quantification of reputation, attention, and the self, by analysts most of whom encountered it as a tool without a pedigree. A concept that can survive denunciation, suppression, translation, and the complete disappearance of its author's political world, and go on organising inquiry in each new home, has passed every test of worth that intellectual history knows how to administer, and no account of its origins in defeat can substitute for that record.

Hegemony's career is longer and stranger, because the word had a life before Gramsci, in the debates of Russian social democracy, where it named the leading role a class might exercise in a revolution not its own, and because Gramsci, taking it up in prison, transformed it from a term of alliance tactics into the centre of a whole theory of power in the societies of the West. In the notebooks, hegemony names the organisation of consent: the process by which a class's rule is secured not primarily by the coercion of the state but by its intellectual and moral leadership throughout civil society, its capacity to present its interests as the general interest and its common sense as everyone's. The concept's career after its author's death is a history of the century in miniature. Edited and published by his party, it became the charter of a national strategy. Carried into Britain, it armed the cultural studies project and the analysis of Thatcherism as a hegemonic project, an authoritarian populism that had to be fought on the terrain of common sense before it could be fought at the ballot box. Reworked in the nineteen eighties by theorists of radical democracy, it was loosened from its class moorings into a general logic of political articulation, a development its author's party comrades regarded as apostasy and its new users as liberation. Deployed in international relations, it named the consensual dimension of great-power order. Each migration cost the concept precision and bought it jurisdiction, the usual exchange rate for ideas that travel, and a scholar can now spend a career policing the borders of a word a prisoner coined to explain why his side had lost. The two careers together state the limit of the defeat thesis more eloquently than any argument: whatever the laboratory conditions of their manufacture, these were instruments built for general use, and the proof is that the world, including worlds their makers were excluded from imagining, has used them.

## The quarrel over art

No arena displays the tradition's inner life better than its quarrel over art, because there the stakes of the whole enterprise, what consciousness is, how form relates to content, whether the new can be trusted, were fought out on concrete works by writers who cared about poems and paintings as much as about programmes. The set piece is the expressionism debate of the late nineteen thirties, conducted in an exile journal in Moscow by German writers scattered across three countries. Ernst Bloch defended expressionism, and with it the modernist rupture of inherited forms, as an authentic response to a fractured reality, arguing that a capitalism in decay does not present itself in the rounded, harmonious shapes of the classical novel and that art which breaks its forms may be registering the truth more faithfully than art which preserves them. Lukacs answered with the full weight of his theory of realism: modernism, in his reading, was the ideology of an intelligentsia hypnotised by the surface chaos of the imperialist epoch, mistaking fragmentation for depth and surrendering the category of totality, the capacity to show the individual fate inside the social whole, which the great realists from Balzac to Thomas Mann had preserved. Brecht, whose notes on the debate were published only later, attacked Lukacs's formalism from the other side, insisting that realism is not a canon of approved shapes but a fighting attitude toward reality that must invent its forms as reality invents new horrors. Adorno and Benjamin's correspondence over the artwork essay runs in parallel, the same quarrel at a higher voltage: whether mechanical reproduction liquidates the aura of the traditional work and thereby frees perception for politics, as Benjamin argued, or completes the work's absorption into the commodity, as Adorno feared, with genuine art's truth residing in its refusal of the very reproducibility the age demanded.

The debate matters to this guide for three reasons beyond its intrinsic brilliance. First, it shows the tradition's aesthetics trait operating not as an escape from politics but as politics continued on the terrain where the tradition believed, rightly or wrongly, that the century's consciousness was being decided. The exiles arguing about expressionism in 1938 were arguing about fascism, about what the masses could perceive, about whether the forms of feeling available to ordinary people were adequate to their situation, with the camps already open at the edge of the page. Second, it shows the syndrome's traits generating masterpieces rather than merely symptoms: the displacement toward aesthetics produced the century's most serious Marxist thinking about art, a body of work beside which the cultural pronouncements of the official doctrine, with its socialist realist catechism of positive heroes, read as the productions of a ministry. Third, it records the tradition disagreeing with itself along the exact fault line of its poles, the Hegelian Lukacs defending totality and the classical inheritance against Bloch's theology of the not yet and Benjamin's messianic materialism, so that a reader who wants the tradition whole must refuse every attempt to harmonise it. The quarrel was never settled, and its unsettlement is its bequest: every later argument about realism and modernism, about high culture and mass culture, about whether difficulty in art is a politics or an alibi, has been conducted in rooms these exiles furnished.

## The party question

Underneath the philosophy runs an organisational riddle that the tradition never solved and never escaped: if consciousness is the problem, what institution is the answer? The classical tradition had a candidate, the revolutionary party, and each Western generation had to rebuild, refuse, or replace it. Lukacs, in the founding essays, theorised the party as the bearer of class consciousness in organisational form, the mediation through which the proletariat's imputed awareness, what the class would think if it grasped its position whole, could confront the empirical awareness of actual workers and raise it; the construction is magnificent and dangerous in equal measure, since it licenses the organisation to know the class's interests better than the class, a licence whose abuse the century was about to document exhaustively. Gramsci rebuilt the party as the Modern Prince, not a committee but a collective intellectual, an organism that must itself be hegemonically formed, educating and being educated in the long war of position, and his version remains the tradition's most serious attempt to describe an organisation adequate to its own theory of consent. The Frankfurt Institute, by contrast, has no party theory at all, and the absence is eloquent: an institute endowed to be independent of the state was structurally incapable of imagining itself as an instrument of a movement, and its theory of domination grew ever more total in proportion as its organisational imagination shrank, until the administered world of its wartime writings has no outside from which a party could even be projected. Sartre theorised groups instead, the fused group of the revolutionary moment dissolving into the pledged group and the institution, a phenomenology of organisation that explained, with terrible elegance, why parties become their own opposite. Althusser, finally, spent a career inside a party he believed to be theoretically disarmed, arguing that its practice was sounder than its philosophy and that his own intervention, from a seminar room, could supply the deficit, a wager on the efficacy of theory inside an apparatus whose leadership regarded theory as a department of tactics.

Read in sequence, these are five answers to one question posed by the defeat: where does transformative knowledge live when the movement that was supposed to carry it is absent, defeated, or itself part of the problem? The sequence also measures the tradition's distance from its classical inheritance in the most concrete register. Marx's party was an instrument to be built and used within a rising movement; the Western parties are problems to be theorised within a falling one, and the energy the tradition spent on the riddle, energy its predecessors spent on programmes and insurrections, is the defeat thesis in its organisational form. The riddle remains open. Every contemporary attempt to think about organisation, horizontal networks, movement parties, the party form's digital successors, inherits these five answers whether it cites them or not, and inherits with them the tradition's founding suspicion, earned in the hardest school, that the instrument of emancipation is also the likeliest place for domination to reconstitute itself.

## Totality and its discontents

One methodological commitment unites the tradition beneath its quarrels, and naming it explains both its power and the resistance it provoked. The commitment is to totality: the claim that social phenomena are intelligible only inside the whole that produces them, that a wage, a novel, a neurosis, or a law yields its truth when its mediations to the total process are traced, and that the fragmentation of knowledge into specialised disciplines, each sovereign over its slice, is itself an ideological effect of the society being studied, its division of labour reproduced in the mind. Lukacs made the commitment programmatic, identifying the standpoint of totality as the decisive difference between Marxist science and bourgeois thought, which he accused of freezing its categories into eternal things. The Frankfurt School practised it as interdisciplinary research long after it ceased to preach it. Gramsci's absolute historicism is a version of it, history as the single fabric in which philosophy and politics are one cloth. Even Althusser, who attacked the Hegelian expressive totality in which every part mirrors a single essence, replaced it with a structured totality of uneven instances rather than abandoning the whole, so that his anti-totalism is better described as totality rebuilt against its founder's version.

The resistance the commitment provoked is as instructive as the commitment. Popper's attack on historicism targeted the claim to grasp wholes as a charter for prophecy and tyranny, and whatever its fairness to Marx, it fixed in the wider culture the suspicion that totality is a totalitarian category. The structuralists' quarrel, the existentialists' defence of the individual project, the later poststructuralist celebration of difference against every whole, all took totality as their chosen antagonist, so that a remarkable share of twentieth-century philosophy can be narrated as a referendum on this one commitment. Martin Jay's alternative history of the tradition, organised around the pursuit of totality, drew the moral this guide has already used against the codification: the same population supports more than one unity, and the choice among unities is a choice among arguments. A reader equipped with both frames, the syndrome of defeat and the quest for totality, possesses the two master keys to the tradition's interpretation, and will notice that they open different doors: the first explains why the tradition wrote as it did, the second what it thought it was doing, and a complete account needs the biography and the intention together.

## The domination of nature

A final thread deserves recovery because its posterity is still growing. In their wartime masterwork, the Frankfurt exiles Horkheimer and Adorno argued that the Enlightenment's project of dominating nature had rebounded: the reason that disenchanted the world and made it calculable turned outward into the administration of things and inward into the administration of souls, so that the domination of nature, the domination of other humans, and the repression of nature within the self formed a single entwined history. The thesis drew on Marx's early account of man's metabolism with nature and on Weber's rationalisation, and it gave the tradition an ecological register avant la lettre: the administered world is not only unjust but at war with the material basis of life, and its crisis would eventually arrive in material form. Later ecological Marxists took up the thread with the concept of the metabolic rift, the rupture in the cycles between town and country, soil and sustenance, that capitalist production drives ever wider, and the Frankfurt version stands behind much subsequent thinking about technology, growth, and the non-identity of nature with its human uses. Within this guide's anatomy, the thread is one more exhibit for the limit clause. A thesis composed in Californian exile, at the moment of the tradition's maximum political nullity, as part of a philosophy of history bordering on despair, became one of the seedbeds of a worldwide ecological criticism its authors could not have imagined and would not have trusted. The concepts escaped again. They usually do, when they are built to grasp something real.

## The pattern of survival

Running beneath the concepts is a biographical pattern so regular that it deserves recognition as one of the tradition's defining structures: the rhythm of affirmation, condemnation, and survival. Lukacs lived it three times. Condemned in 1924, he recanted and was readmitted to usefulness. Attacked again at the end of the nineteen twenties for his theses on the Hungarian party's strategy, he performed the required self-criticism and withdrew to archival work in Moscow, where, in one of history's ironies, the editors of the great edition put the manuscripts of the young Marx into the hands of the man best prepared to understand them. Attacked a third time after 1956, when he had served in a government the Soviet army suppressed, he was deported to Romania, returned, and lived to publish in 1967 a preface to the reissued History and Class Consciousness that disowned the book's central construction while the book itself, in the same binding, went on converting readers who ignored the preface. How to judge the recantations has divided his readers ever since: as coerced rituals whose content is worthless, since a confession under threat testifies only to the threat; or as sincere, if convenient, stages of a thinker who genuinely believed the movement's survival outranked his own consistency. This guide records the division without adjudicating it, as the series' treatment of Lukacs in his own article does at length, because the pattern matters more than the verdict. A tradition whose founder survived by periodically un-writing his masterpiece was formed inside a machinery of intellectual discipline that has no equivalent in the liberal university, and the marks of that machinery, the hedged formulations, the ritual citations, the sudden silences, are part of its texts' permanent archaeology.

The other careers trace the pattern's variants. Korsch chose the opposite pole: no recantation, expulsion, the margins, and a death in American obscurity with his books out of print, the price of refusal paid in full and in advance. Gramsci's silence was imposed by a cell rather than a committee, but his notebooks are also a negotiation with two censors, the fascist one who read his mail and the posthumous one he could anticipate in his own party, whose coming custodianship of his papers he could not control and whose editorial framing he might have contested. Horkheimer practised survival as administration, steering the Institute's endowment and its members through exile with a caution that his critics called timidity and his defenders called the reason anything survived at all. Althusser waged the pattern as internal war, using the party's own institutions as both shield and target until his breakdown and the catastrophe of his private life ended the experiment. Surveying the variants, a student grasps something the concept lists cannot teach: the tradition's famous difficulty, its indirections and esotericism, were not only methods and symptoms but survival technologies, the stylistic deposit of decades in which a sentence could end a career, a freedom, or a life. To read these books without knowing the price their sentences once carried is to misjudge their temperature entirely.

## The American mirror

The tradition's encounter with the United States is the strangest chapter of its exile and the one with the longest consequences for the receiving country. The Institute, transplanted to Columbia University in 1934, found itself in the homeland of the mass culture it would anatomise and of the empirical social science it both courted and suspected. The encounter was genuinely two-sided. American sociology offered instruments, survey methods, content analysis, the audience study, that the Europeans had never possessed, and the Institute's collaboration with Paul Lazarsfeld's radio research project at Princeton put Adorno in front of American commercial broadcasting's raw material, an experience that confirmed his theory and baffled his hosts in roughly equal measure. America offered the exiles something subtler than instruments: the spectacle of a capitalism without a feudal past and without a revolutionary workers' movement, in which integration proceeded by consumption rather than by terror, and which therefore isolated, in pure form, the mechanisms of consent the tradition existed to explain. The culture industry chapter and One-Dimensional Man, the tradition's two most famous products, are both unthinkable without the American laboratory: they are European theories built from American evidence, addressed to a European catastrophe, and their double address is the secret of their worldwide career.

The exchange rate ran both ways, though the settlement took decades. The exiles' presence seeded American universities with a style of social theory that the native traditions, pragmatist, empirical, liberal, had not produced, and when the American New Left and the new social sciences went looking for a Marxism adequate to affluence, bureaucracy, and the media, they found the Institute's library waiting, in English, in their own country. Marcuse, who remained in the United States, taught at Brandeis and San Diego, and became a public figure of the world movement of 1968, is the personal form of this settlement: a Frankfurt philosopher functioning, in his seventies, as an American campus oracle. The deeper irony is structural. The tradition had defined itself by its separation from practice and its confinement to theory. In America it acquired, posthumously and in translation, the mass readership and the movement audience that Europe had denied it, on syllabuses and in paperbacks, in the homeland of the culture industry, whose students read the critique of their own society as a classic of their own curriculum. No verdict on the tradition is complete that omits this final displacement, in which the products of Europe's defeats became America's self-knowledge.

## The question of the East

Every tradition is known by the question it cannot answer, and for this one the question was the nature of the Soviet Union. The label itself, as Merleau-Ponty coined it, implied a judgement by contrast: the Western variant existed because the Eastern had become something else. But what that something else was, the tradition never agreed, and the disagreement maps its politics more honestly than any manifesto. The Trotskyist analysis, which several figures at the tradition's edge adopted at various times, held the Soviet Union to be a degenerated workers' state, its property forms still post-capitalist, its bureaucracy a parasitic caste to be removed by political revolution. The state capitalist analysis, developed by dissident currents including the group around C. L. R. James in America, held that the bureaucracy functioned as a collective capitalist class and the system as capitalism in its most concentrated form. The theory of bureaucratic collectivism proposed a new mode of domination, neither capitalist nor socialist, whose logic was the logic of the administered world the Frankfurt School described at home. Lukacs and Gramsci, from inside their very different relations to the movement, treated the Soviet experiment as the revolution's burdened heir, to be criticised as one criticises a family catastrophe. Sartre's position migrated across all the available options across three decades, a one-man survey of the question's difficulty.

The theoretical point for this anatomy is the asymmetry the question exposes. The tradition produced, as this guide has documented, an unmatched literature on the societies it lived in: on consent, culture, authority, and the commodity form in the capitalist West. Confronted with the East, its instruments dulled. A theory of ideology built to explain how domination secures consent had little to say about a domination that ruled primarily by coercion and scarcity; a theory of reification built on the universal commodity form stumbled over an economy where the plan, not the market, allocated goods and fates; and the category of totality, turned eastward, tended to produce either apology or a mirror-image demonology in which the Soviet system figured as the West's own administered world without the consumer goods. Anderson's later trajectory is again instructive, since after the survey he devoted his editorial energies to championing the Russian revolutionary tradition and its historians, as if the analysis of the East required returning to sources the Western current had neglected. The honest conclusion is that the tradition is a theory of the West in a sense deeper than its name: of the West's distinctive mode of rule, by consent organised through culture and consumption, and its famous universality stops at the border where that mode of rule stops. Naming the limit is not a refutation. A theory of consent is not diminished by the existence of regimes that ruled without it, any more than a theory of tides is diminished by deserts. But the limit explains the label's survival better than its defenders usually admit: the name stuck because the thing it names was real, a regional theory of a regional predicament, misread ever since, by friends and enemies alike, as a theory of the world.

## The essay, the fragment, the aphorism

The tradition's preferred literary forms deserve a section of their own, because form here is not packaging but method. The treatise and the party textbook, the forms of the classical and the official Marxisms respectively, presuppose a system completed and a reader to be instructed. The Western generations, doubting the system and lacking the instructed reader, developed a repertoire of open forms. The essay, in Adorno's famous defence, is the form of a thought that proceeds by exploration rather than deduction, that takes its object seriously enough to refuse the violence of defining it in advance, and whose heresy against method is itself the method of a reason suspicious of systems. The fragment served Benjamin, whose masterwork remained a vast unfinished constellation of quotations and commentaries on the Paris arcades, a book designed, scandalously, to be built almost entirely of other men's sentences arranged until they sparked. The aphorism served the wartime Adorno of Minima Moralia, reflections from damaged life, in which the whole catastrophe is approached through the splinter: a greeting, a gift, a hotel lobby, each miniature made to bear the weight that a system no longer could. Gramsci's notebook entry is a fourth form, dictated by the cell, in which a theory of politics accumulates in numbered paragraphs that never received their final arrangement, so that the reader must perform the synthesis the censor prevented. Even the tradition's systematic works arrive disguised: Sartre's Critique as a labyrinth that breaks off, Althusser's as a symposium and a set of readings, Bloch's masterpiece as a baroque encyclopedia of hope's images from fairy tales to cathedrals.

A reader who understands the forms understands the final reason for the tradition's difficulty and its durability together. These are texts built to resist consumption, to withhold the extractable doctrine that a movement or a ministry could carry away, and their resistance is the stylistic form of the tradition's central experience: that doctrines, once extractable, become catechisms. The cost, paid in readers never won and movements never armed, was real, and the tradition's critics from the parties never tired of presenting the bill. The benefit is still compounding. A catechism ages with its church. A fragment, an essay, an aphorism, and a notebook enter the common treasury of forms, available to any later reader in any later emergency, which is why a prisoner writing in code in 1933 and an exile arranging quotations in 1939 can address a seminar, a movement, or a solitary reader in a century they never saw, in sentences that have not dated. The tradition lost the organisations. It kept the sentences. Posterity, which has no central committee, judges by the sentences.

## Who edited the prisoners: the politics of transmission

The tradition reached its readers through editors, and the editing was never innocent. The type case is the Prison Notebooks. Gramsci died in 1937 leaving thirty-three notebooks of densely written, censor-evading prose, which his sister-in-law Tatiana Schucht preserved and conveyed to Moscow, and which the Italian party, after the war, published in six thematic volumes arranged by topic under the direction of its leadership. The arrangement was an act of interpretation of the highest consequence: it presented Gramsci as the theorist of the party's own national strategy, smoothed the notebooks' hesitations into a system, and omitted or softened what did not fit, including passages touching the Soviet Union and the party's own history. For a generation, the world read that Gramsci, a classic manufactured by his heirs for their use. Only with the critical edition of 1975, edited by Valentino Gerratana, which restored the notebooks' chronological order and their full text, could scholars measure the distance between the prisoner and his portrait, and the measurement restructured Gramsci studies permanently. The lesson generalises across the tradition. Lukacs's texts arrived through his own successive self-editings and recantations. Benjamin's arrived through the editorial decisions of friends and adversaries who disputed his legacy in competing editions. The Frankfurt corpus arrived through the Institute's own control of its archive, exercised at times against its members' dissidents. A tradition produced in separation from a movement depended, for its very textual existence, on custodians with interests, and the scholarly recovery of what the custodians altered is one of the quiet epics of twentieth-century editing. For the purposes of this guide, the practical moral is the one the objection section drew in theory: every frame around these texts, including the frame called Western Marxism, has an editor, and an editor has an argument.

## Theology's long shadow

One ancestry on the map deserves expansion because it surprises newcomers: the theological. A materialist tradition might be expected to treat religion as a solved problem, an opium to be noted and dismissed. The Western generations did nothing of the kind. Gramsci treated the Catholic Church as the supreme historical example of a hegemonic apparatus, an institution that had solved, for centuries, the problem his party could not, the unity of intellectuals and the simple, of doctrine and common sense, and his notebooks return to the Church as a strategist returns to a rival general's campaigns. Benjamin opened his most famous text with the image of a chess-playing automaton, historical materialism, that wins only because a hidden dwarf, theology, guides its hand, and his concept of a weak messianic power, the claim of the dead generations on the living, imports the structure of redemption into the centre of a materialist philosophy of history. Bloch built his philosophy of hope on an archaeology of religious longing, arguing that the Bible and the heresies preserved a utopian surplus that atheism must inherit rather than discard, in the formula that only an atheist can be a good Christian and only a Christian a good atheist. Even the Frankfurt School's austerity has a theological negative form, a prohibition on graven images applied to utopia itself, which may not be pictured lest the picture become an idol of the administered world's longing for reassurance.

The shadow has a sociology as well as a philosophy. Many of the tradition's figures were formed in cultures where the churches were the standing model of cultural power, and several, Gramsci with the Jesuits' organisation before his eyes, Lukacs in the messianic atmosphere of the Hungarian revolution, Benjamin among the Jewish thinkers of Weimar, wrote as men who had measured their political hopes against religious ones and found the comparison unavoidable. For the anatomy, the theological thread confirms the ancestry trait from an unexpected side and complicates the secular self-image of the whole enterprise: a Marxism that thinks with Hegel, Spinoza, and Freud thinks also, in its greatest figures, with Paul, Augustine, and the heretics, and its account of consent, hope, and redemption is legible as a displaced theology by readers who come to it from either direction. The series' treatments of the individual figures weigh the thread case by case. Here it is enough to mark it as one more respect in which the tradition overflows the frame built for it, since a syndrome of academic secularisation should not gleam with so much stolen fire.

## The label's second career in the world academy

The phrase now leads a second life that neither its coiner nor its codifier foresaw: it is the name of a recognised field of study in universities far outside the territory it describes. Across North America and Europe it organises syllabuses, conference panels, companion volumes, and doctoral examinations, the standard furniture of academic normalisation. More remarkably, some of the most intensive scholarly work on the tradition is produced in China, where the study of Western Marxism constitutes an established research field with its own institutes, journals, and translation programmes, in which the European figures are examined, with great philological care, as a resource and a warning for a Marxism that holds state power: a resource, because the Westerners theorised the cultural and ideological dimensions that a governing Marxism must manage; a warning, because their trajectory demonstrates, to their Chinese readers, what becomes of the theory when it loses the movement and the state. The spectacle completes the ironies this guide has accumulated. A label coined by a Frenchman for a Hungarian, codified by an Englishman as the anatomy of a European failure, now functions in Beijing seminars as a mirror in which a ruling tradition inspects the road not taken, while in the universities of the West itself the labelled texts are taught, increasingly, as classics of general critical thought, shelved between the courses on modernism and the courses on film.

This second career bears on the question of the category's usefulness with which the guide must close its analytical work. A category that organises research programmes on three continents, that generates translations, editions, and controversies two generations after its construction, has passed the pragmatic test whatever its circularities: it picks out a real family resemblance in the material, real enough that scholars who reject the codifier's thesis still need his noun to say what they are rejecting. Categories in intellectual history are not true or false in the manner of measurements. They are more or less fertile, more or less blinding, and the verdict of two generations of use is that this one has been fertile in exact proportion as its users have remembered that it is a construction, and blinding in exact proportion as they have forgotten. The guide's whole labour, the restored economists, the graded exceptions, the excluded continents, the confessed argument of the constructor, has been an exercise in remembering.

## The seminar as final homeland

There remains the ambivalence of the institution that finally housed the tradition, and no honest anatomy should close without facing it. The university gave the Western theorists what the movement, the prison, and the exile's garret could not: salaries, libraries, students, time, and the protection of norms that treat even heresy as a contribution. The postwar generations of the tradition were, in the plain biographical sense, professors, and their work bears the university's virtues in its rigour, its scholarship, its patience with difficulty, alongside the vices the syndrome predicted, its self-referentiality, its careerism, its tendency to mistake the next article for the next act. The tradition's own members saw the trap with their usual clarity. Adorno, who owed the university his survival and his chair, anatomised the academic industry of his day with a contempt he usually reserved for the culture industry, and the student movement of 1968 aimed its anger precisely at the professoriate that taught emancipation as a subject while administering examinations as a fate. The contradiction is not resolvable, and the tradition's mature position, where it achieved one, was to inhabit it knowingly: to use the university's shelter to preserve and elaborate a body of thought whose premises indict the shelter, and to accept the irony as the form that fidelity takes in an unredeemed world. Whether the arrangement has preserved the thought or embalmed it is the standing question of the field, and the honest answer is that it has done both, in different departments, in different decades, sometimes in the same career. The texts remain on the syllabuses, which is embalming of a kind. The concepts remain in the movements, which is not. Between the syllabus and the movement, the tradition continues its long afterlife in the only two homes it ever really had.

## A balance sheet for the tradition

The guide has circled its subject long enough that a direct reckoning is owed, conducted in the open and in the guide's own voice. On the credit side stand, first, the instruments: reification, hegemony, the culture industry, ideology as material practice, overdetermination, the authoritarian character, the critique of instrumental reason, a set of tools for analysing consent and domination that no rival body of twentieth-century thought matched, and whose continued use across disciplines and continents is the brute fact of the matter. Second, the standard of honesty: at its best, in a prison notebook, a wartime fragment, an exile's letters, this tradition looked at the defeats of its own side without the anaesthetic of doctrine, and asked why, with a rigour that the victorious orthodoxies, East and West, rarely applied to themselves. Third, the preservation: through the decades when Marxism as a politics was reduced, in the West, to sects and apparatuses, the tradition kept the founder's texts in philosophical circulation, recovered their depths, and handed them to posterity in editions and interpretations without which the later revivals of Marxist thought, in economics as much as in culture, would have started from far less.

On the debit side stand the charges this guide has documented rather than invented. A displacement from the analysis of production that left its heirs eloquent on everything except the economy that was meanwhile transforming their societies, and that its own economists, working at its margins, reproached by their example. A politics of magnificent diagnosis and almost no prescription, in which the critique of every existing organisation curdled, at the limit, into a theory of why organisation itself betrays, a lesson that armed its readers against illusions and disarmed them for construction in a single motion. A Eurocentrism of attention, partially excused by circumstance and partially constitutive of its canon, that its best concepts now indict. And a style, at its worst, in which difficulty became a substitute for the verification that history had refused to provide, so that the reader's exhaustion could be misread as the theory's depth. The balance does not sum to a verdict of acquittal or conviction, and a guide that pretended otherwise would fail its subject's own standard. What it sums to is a profile: the most intellectually distinguished, the most politically weightless, and the most strangely durable body of Marxist writing of the twentieth century, whose weightlessness and durability are not in spite of one another but, as the defeat thesis saw, in the strictest causal sequence.

It is fitting to end the analytical work with the tradition's own best metaphor for itself, which its most political thinker forged for another purpose. Gramsci distinguished the war of manoeuvre, the frontal assault on state power, appropriate where civil society was gelatinous, from the war of position, the long siege conducted in the trenches of civil society, appropriate where the order's defences were deep and consensual. The Western tradition, whatever its members intended, became the war of position of Marxist theory itself: driven from the field of manoeuvre by the defeats, it dug into the trenches of culture, education, and philosophy, and conducted there a fifty-year siege of the common sense of its age. Sieges are not won by the besieger's timetable, and this one's besiegers mostly died in the trenches, unrelieved. But the trenches are still manned, the siege works are still being extended by hands the first engineers never saw, and the common sense of the age, on the evidence of the concepts in general circulation, has been breached in more places than the garrison admits. A tradition that set out to explain why the revolution had failed ended by demonstrating how theory survives its own failure: not by power, not by numbers, but by building instruments so well fitted to the real structures of consent and domination that the world goes on using them long after it has forgotten the siege. That survival, on those terms, is the defeat thesis and its limit in a single image, and it is where the analysis rests.

## For students and exam candidates

You meet this label in survey courses and examination papers, and the marks lie in handling the label as an argument rather than reciting it as a list. Learn the sequence as a skeleton: the coinage by a French phenomenologist in the nineteen fifties, the two founding books and their joint condemnation in the following decade's aftermath, the codification of 1976 with its six traits and its defeat thesis, and the three standing corrections, the economists, the British historians, and the excluded continents. An answer that can run that skeleton in either direction, and grade Gramsci as the limiting case, will outperform a recital of twelve names. When you are ready for the internal quarrel that reorganised the whole field, the comparison of [humanist vs structuralist marxism](/2016/02/15/humanist-vs-structuralist-marxism/) gives you the duel in its own terms. Keep the tradition map from this guide as a working instrument rather than a poster: rebuild it from memory with a tool such as the [VaultBook study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), converting each row into a card with the concept on one side and the predicament on the other, because examiners reward the candidate who can move from a concept to the defeat that produced it and back again without notes.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This material teaches best as a problem rather than as a period. Open with the label itself: ask the seminar who coined it, when, and for what contrast, and let the discovery that the members never used it unsettle the room before any doctrine is introduced. The tradition map in this guide works well as a first handout, provided students are required to attack it, to nominate the missing rows and defend the nominations, since the exercise teaches the construction of categories better than any lecture on method. Pair the codifier's anatomy with the objection at full strength, and set the economists and Mariátegui as the documentary test of both. For extract selection, sequence, and reading routes through the primary texts at different levels of preparation, the companion [western marxism reading guide](/2016/03/15/western-marxism-reading-guide/) in this series is built for the purpose. Close the unit where the evidence closes: with the concepts at work in the students' own other subjects, in media analysis, in the sociology of education, in the theory of the state, so that the defeat thesis and its limit are not a formula they recite but a judgment they have practised making.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Three research frontiers in this field remain genuinely open. The first is archival: the editorial histories of the canonical texts, the prison notebooks' passage from cell to critical edition, the Institute's control of its own papers, the successive self-editings of Lukacs, still contain unmeasured distances between the writers and their received portraits, and each distance is a publishable correction. The second is comparative: the category's construction invites controlled comparison with the Marxist traditions it excluded, and the systematic study of how the Peruvian, Indian, Caribbean, and Japanese discussions of the same decades answered the same questions of consciousness, culture, and strategy without the European syndrome is a literature still being built. The third is analytical: the defeat thesis applied to one school at a time, with the gradient specified by date, yields sharper results than the tradition-wide version, and the worked example for the Frankfurt case is the comparison of [critical theory vs classical marxism](/2015/09/01/critical-theory-vs-classical-marxism/) in this series. Journalists covering the phrase in its polemical employments should establish which of its three lives is in play before quoting anyone about it, since most public confusion on the subject is a conversation between the lives conducted as if they were one.

## The working class, reconsidered

The tradition's most delicate subject was its supposed protagonist. The classical theory had assigned the proletariat the role of universal class, the agent whose emancipation would emancipate humanity, and the Western generations inherited the assignment at the moment of its empirical collapse. Their responses form a descending scale of hope that is also a history of the century's disappointments. Lukacs, in the founding text, saved the assignment by philosophy: the class's empirical consciousness might lag, compromise, or err, but its imputed consciousness, the awareness appropriate to its objective position, remained the standpoint from which the totality could be known, and the party existed to mediate between the two. Gramsci saved it by strategy: the class could become hegemonic, could learn to lead a nation rather than merely strike against it, but only through the long construction of alliances, institutions, and intellectuals of its own, the war of position replacing the insurrectionary appointment. The Frankfurt generation subjected the assignment to empirical and psychoanalytic audit and reported the results without consolation: the surveys found authoritarian dispositions lodged deep in the class's own character structure, the culture industry integrated its leisure as the factory integrated its labour, and the wartime masterwork concluded that the class had been absorbed into the apparatus it was meant to overthrow.

Marcuse, in the nineteen sixties, drew the conclusion the others had circled: if the classical agent had been integrated, the negation of the order would have to be sought at its margins, among the substratum of the outcasts, the racial minorities, the colonised, the students, whose refusal was not yet administered, a thesis he advanced with visible reluctance, since it inverted the theory's foundation while claiming to save its intention. The student movement of 1968 took the thesis as a charter, and its subsequent history, the evaporation of the revolt into the institutions it stormed, supplied the next generation with its own melancholy data. Althusser's structuralism can be read as the refusal of this entire pathos: by declaring history a process without a subject, it relieved the theory of the embarrassment of the missing agent, at the price of an account in which classes struggle without anyone, strictly speaking, doing the struggling. A reader who follows the proletariat through these five stations possesses the tradition's emotional history in its exact sequence, from confidence through strategy, audit, substitution, and abolition, and understands why its masterpieces are shadowed: they are the work of men who had staked the meaning of history on a class that declined, in their lifetimes and in their countries, to play the part assigned to it, and who had to decide, each of them alone, what a theory owes its agent when the agent does not appear.

## Two winters: the thaw and the second shock

The tradition's periodisation has a hinge that the founding dates obscure, and restoring it explains the second, postwar life of the whole current. In February 1956 the First Secretary of the Soviet party delivered a closed-session report enumerating the crimes of the Stalin era, and in the autumn of the same year Soviet armour crushed the Hungarian revolution, in whose government the elderly Lukacs briefly served. The double event, confession and invasion together, broke the moral authority of the official movement for a generation of Western intellectuals more completely than any argument could have done, and it re-founded the Western tradition's audience overnight. Out of the exodus from the Western communist parties came the first New Left, journals, clubs, and campaigns whose members went looking for a Marxism untainted by the apparatus, and found, waiting for them in translations old and new, precisely the current this guide describes: a Marxism of consciousness, culture, and philosophy that had always defined itself against the codification. Henri Lefebvre's expulsion from the French party in 1958, the Italian party's pursuit of a national road under the sign of Gramsci, the British historians' break of 1956 and their reinvention in the socialist humanism of the following years, belong to this hinge: the moment when the Western current ceased to be a deviation inside someone else's movement and became the inheritance of a new one.

The hinge also explains a shift in the tradition's centre of gravity that the map's periods register. Before 1956, the current's characteristic situation was isolation within or against the parties; after it, the current acquired readers, students, publishers, and eventually chairs, and its problems became the problems of success, canonisation, rival schools, the routinisation of heresy into a syllabus. The humanist and structuralist duel of the nineteen sixties, the tradition's last great internal war, was fought with the confidence of men who knew the culture was listening: Sartre and Althusser were not exiles whispering in garrets but Parisian institutions conducting a public quarrel over the allegiance of a student generation that both sides correctly believed might inherit the world. That the inheritance went elsewhere, that 1968's children dispersed into the professions their teachers had taught them to criticise, is the postscript the tradition shares with its century. But the hinge of 1956 stands as the moment its predicament inverted, from a theory without readers to a theory with too many institutions and no movement, and any grading of its later figures that ignores the inversion will misread their confidence as a refutation of the defeat thesis when it is merely its second act.

## The tradition among the disciplines

A tradition is finally known by what it did to its neighbours, and the Western current's effects on the surrounding disciplines are extensive enough to constitute a second, shadow membership list. Literary studies received from Goldmann a method, genetic structuralism, that read the great texts as the coherent expression of a social group's world vision, and from Lukacs a theory of the novel and of realism that still organises syllabuses; the entire subsequent enterprise of a Marxist criticism, down to the theories of the literary mode of production and the political unconscious elaborated by later critics in Britain and America, works in rooms the tradition built. Sociology received the Frankfurt programme whole: the studies of authority, prejudice, and mass communication founded research traditions that outgrew their Marxist premises and entered the discipline's common stock, while the concept of reification passed into the theory of institutions through channels their custodians did not always acknowledge. History received, by a more circuitous route, the British Marxist historiography that the label excludes, a body of work in constant argumentative commerce with the continental current it refused to join, and cultural studies, founded in Birmingham by scholars formed in that commerce, took hegemony as its charter concept and the analysis of popular culture as its field, exporting both to every continent. Philosophy received the tradition as a standing challenge: the Frankfurt critique of positivism, the existentialists' and structuralists' rival reconstructions of the subject, and the hermeneutic and critical theories descended from them define positions that contemporary philosophy still occupies or opposes, rarely ignores.

The mediating figure of this disciplinary diffusion, for the English-speaking world, is Raymond Williams, and his case is the fitting close for this section because it repeats the category's founding irony in miniature. Williams built his cultural materialism in conscious dialogue with the continental tradition, correcting its idealism, as he judged it, from the standpoint of a British Marxist formation rooted in adult education and the labour movement's own institutions; he is, by the syndrome's every measure, a figure the tradition needed and its map omits, a Western Marxist produced by the one Western country whose defeat had taken the historiographical rather than the philosophical form. A student who has understood why Williams is both inside and outside the category has understood the category, and a discipline that absorbed the tradition through such mediators has received it in the only way living thought is ever received, critically, partially, and with the names of the transmitters half erased. The erasure is the final lesson of the balance sheet this guide has been keeping. The tradition's concepts conquered the disciplines by becoming anonymous, and the label that names their source survives as the receipt for a transaction the culture would otherwise have no record of at all.

## Science, technique, and the critique of reason

The tradition's quarrel with the official doctrine had a scientific edge that repays attention, because it determined what each side meant by knowledge itself. The codified Marxism of the East rested on a philosophy of nature: the dialectic was said to govern matter as it governed history, and the prestige of the natural sciences was conscripted to certify the party's worldview, with consequences that ranged from the merely scholastic to the catastrophic, when biological doctrine was settled by political authority and genetics was purged as bourgeois. Against this, the Western current's founding gesture, in Korsch and Lukacs alike, was to deny that Marxism was a general ontology of nature and to relocate its claims in history and society, where the knower is inside the known and knowledge is a form of practice. The gesture had a price that the tradition paid knowingly: it surrendered the borrowed certainty of natural law, and with it the catechism's promise that history's outcome was guaranteed by the structure of the universe. What remained was a theory that had to argue for its future rather than deduce it, and the pessimism of the later tradition is, in part, the sound of that argument being lost year after year.

From this foundation the tradition built its most influential critique, the critique of instrumental reason. Horkheimer argued that reason itself had been narrowed, in the epoch of monopoly and administration, to a faculty of calculation: the matching of means to ends whose worth reason no longer presumes to judge. Science, on this account, is not the enemy but the supreme expression of the narrowing, a magnificent instrument that has forgotten what it is for, and technology is its social form, neutral in the engineer's imagination and anything but neutral in its effects, since a society organised by technical rationality reproduces domination as efficiency and experiences obedience as convenience. Marcuse gave the thesis its most quotable form, arguing that technological rationality had become political rationality, that the apparatus of production and consumption delivered the goods and the ideology in the same package. The argument had formidable critics, who charged it with romanticising a pre-technical past and with a vagueness about which techniques, exactly, emancipate and which enslave; the tradition's heirs in the philosophy of technology have spent decades supplying the distinctions the founders omitted. But the critique's career confirms this guide's running verdict. Formulated in exile by men whom the century's technical triumphs, the bomber, the camp's logistics, the broadcast voice in every home, had taught to fear the instrument, it became the indispensable starting point for every subsequent attempt, ecological, feminist, or liberal, to think about technology as a political form rather than a neutral toolbox, and it survives in debates whose participants have never opened the books in which it was born.

## The everyday as the last terrain

The tradition's most quietly revolutionary discovery was a place: the everyday. Classical Marxism had located the decisive struggles at the commanding heights, the factory, the state, the party congress. Henri Lefebvre, beginning in the nineteen thirties and culminating in his Critique of Everyday Life across the postwar decades, argued that the order reproduced itself most securely at the lowest altitude, in the routines of eating, dwelling, travelling, desiring, and amusing oneself, where domination arrives not as an order but as a habit, and where, consequently, the possibility of a different life lies buried in the same trivial soil. The argument connected the tradition's oldest theme to its newest evidence. Reification had always been, at bottom, a claim about the texture of ordinary perception; the culture industry was everyday life industrialised; hegemony was the name for the order's occupation of common sense, which is the everyday thinking itself. Lefebvre's contribution was to make the terrain explicit and to insist, against the tradition's own gloom, on its residue of unadministered possibility: the festival, the moment, the lived instant in which the routines crack and people glimpse that the given day is not the only day.

The afterlife of this discovery was as unruly as the tradition's posterity generally. The Situationist International, working in Paris in the nineteen fifties and sixties in explicit descent from Lukacs, Lefebvre, and the surrealists, radicalised the critique of everyday life into a critique of the spectacle, the society in which lived experience has been replaced by its representation, and carried the analysis into the streets in May of the year that made them famous. Feminist theory found in the everyday the missing map of its own territory, the household, the body, and the timetable, where the tradition's instruments turned out to fit a domination its makers had barely seen. Urban studies took from Lefebvre the production of space and the right to the city, phrases that now organise research and activism on every continent. A concept forged by a philosopher whom the French party expelled, in books that weave Marx with Nietzsche and the village festival, thus became the operating system of half the critical disciplines, a career that Anderson's anatomy, pitched at the level of the treatise and the system, was not designed to predict. The everyday is the tradition's proof, offered against itself, that its displacement into philosophy and culture was not only a retreat from the sites of power. It was also a discovery of sites of power that the classical theory, for all its economics, had walked past every morning without seeing, on its way to the factory gate and the winter palace.

## The control cases: defeat's other children

The defeat thesis has a scientific weakness that its defenders should state before its critics do: defeat was the common condition of every Marxist current in the West, yet only one current responded with the philosophical syndrome. The control cases are the currents that shared the catastrophe and produced something else. Trotskyism, hunted by fascists and Stalinists alike, its founder murdered in exile in 1940, responded to defeat with an intensification of the classical virtues: programme, strategy, economic analysis, and the construction of rival internationals whose internal life was a permanent congress. Its characteristic products are the transitional programme, the analysis of the Soviet bureaucracy, and the polemic, not the treatise on method; its intellectuals remained, in form, party intellectuals, even when the party was a few dozen exiles around a mimeograph. Council communism, the current of the Dutch and German left that Korsch joined after his expulsion, responded with an anti-authoritarian radicalism that dissolved the party into the councils and produced, in Mattick, an economist of exemplary austerity. The anarchist and syndicalist remnants drew their own conclusions. Same defeats, same decades, same cities, in some cases the same men at different addresses: different intellectual physiognomies.

The comparison refines the thesis rather than refuting it, and the refinement is worth stating precisely, since it converts a suggestive correlation into an explanation with moving parts. Defeat alone produced bitterness, sectarianism, and strategy manuals in plenty. The philosophical syndrome required defeat in combination with two further conditions: separation from any organisation that could impose a practical agenda, whether by expulsion, by the organisation's destruction, or by its subordination to a foreign state whose interests forbade independent thought; and absorption into institutions, the university, the endowed institute, the publishing market, that rewarded philosophical production and sheltered it from the demand for use. Where the first condition held without the second, as in the Trotskyist exile, theory stayed classical and frantic. Where the second held without the first, as in the Italian party's symbiosis with culture, theory stayed strategic. The syndrome flowered where both held together, in the Frankfurt exile, in postwar Paris, in the seminar rooms of the consolidation, which is why the tradition's map and the map of European higher education in the same decades are, in the end, the same map drawn twice. A thesis that can specify its own conditions this way has earned the right to survive its control cases, and a guide that supplies the controls has done more for the thesis than a dozen defences.

## The founding text's architecture

One book stands behind the whole tradition the way a seed crystal stands behind a solution, and a guide to the tradition owes its readers a clear account of its internal architecture, leaving the full close reading to the volume this series devotes to it. History and Class Consciousness is a collection of essays written between 1919 and 1922, in the heat of the revolutionary years, and its unity is the unity of a single wager worked through successive problems. The wager is that Marx's method, properly understood, is the recovery of totality against the fragmentation of bourgeois thought: that the facts of social life, which the sciences deliver as isolated data and the economy delivers as frozen things, are in truth moments of a historical process that can be grasped whole only from the standpoint of the class whose practice might transform it. The opening essay asks what orthodox Marxism even means, and answers that orthodoxy refers exclusively to method, so that every article of the doctrine could be abandoned under the pressure of research without the Marxism being lost, a sentence whose audacity, in a movement hardening into catechism, still startles. The long central essay on reification builds the theory of consciousness on which everything else depends: the commodity form, become universal, produces a contemplative subject confronting a law-governed second nature, and this structure invades law, bureaucracy, journalism, and the soul itself, so that the fragmentation of knowledge and the passivity of the modern citizen are one phenomenon seen from two sides.

The essays on class consciousness and on organisation then draw the wager's political conclusion: the proletariat, positioned at the point where the commodity sells itself, can become the identical subject and object of history by recognising its own reification as its own product, and the party is the organisational form in which that recognition confronts the class's empirical confusion. Read cold, a century later, the architecture's strengths and fault lines are equally visible. Its strength is the unity of the construction, in which a theory of knowledge, a theory of society, and a theory of organisation are one theory, so that the book can be entered at any door and the whole building reached. Its fault lines are the ones its author's later self-criticisms and its critics, friendly and hostile, have catalogued: an identity philosophy that risks dissolving the stubbornness of nature and of other people into the subject's self-mediation; a confidence in the party as bearer of imputed consciousness that the decade was about to abuse beyond forgiving; and an account of reification so comprehensive that its own author, in the preface of 1967, charged it with trying to out-Hegel Hegel. What no reading can honestly deny is the architecture's generativity. Gramsci's hegemony is unthinkable without its theory of consciousness, and was developed in parallel from a shared Hegelian and Leninist inheritance; the Frankfurt School's entire programme is its research agenda translated into institutes and questionnaires; Althusser's anti-humanism defines itself against its subject philosophy point by point; and the concept at its centre escaped, as this guide has shown, into the general theory of modern societies. The tradition has larger books, darker books, and more finished books. It has no other book of which the whole subsequent century of the current is the commentary.

## The ancestors, generation by generation

The ancestry column of the tradition map records names; this section records the transactions behind them, because the tradition's returns to its predecessors were not decorative learning but the very mechanism of its renewals, each generation purchasing a new Marxism with an older philosophy. The first and greatest transaction was with Hegel, and it occurred twice, in two keys. In the German key, Lukacs reconstructed the dialectic from the inside of the revolutionary years, and then, in Moscow exile, spent the nineteen thirties writing his vast study of the young Hegel, a work of scholarship produced in the shadow of the purges that demonstrated, by archival accumulation, how Hegel's dialectic had been born from the problems of political economy, the division of labour, and the English economists, so that the supposed idealist turned out to have been reading Adam Smith with a notebook. In the French key, a Hegel renaissance was staged in Paris by teachers rather than revolutionaries: Jean Wahl's study of the unhappy consciousness, Jean Hyppolite's translation of the Phenomenology of Spirit, and above all the lectures on that text delivered through the nineteen thirties by Alexandre Kojève, a Russian emigré whose reading of Hegel through the master and slave dialectic, as an anthropology of desire, recognition, and the end of history, formed a generation that included Lacan, Bataille, and Merleau-Ponty himself. The coiner of Western Marxism was thus formed in the same Parisian Hegel seminar that formed the tradition's French phase, an entanglement of judge and judged that the record offers without comment and that this guide simply lays on the table: the label was coined from inside the world it named.

The second transaction was with Kant, and its theatre was Italy. Della Volpe and his school, rebelling against the Hegelian historicism that dominated the Italian party's culture, recovered Kant as the philosopher of the determinate, of the logic of the specific difference, and proposed an anti-Hegelian Marx whose method was a materialist logic modelled on Galileo's rather than on the dialectic's self-moving concept. The Kantian recovery looks like a scholastic preference until its function is seen: it was the philosophical form of a political refusal, the refusal of a historicism that had taught the party to see its own compromises as the cunning of reason, and its exponents' later trajectories, Colletti's passage from Kantian Marxism to an outright break with the tradition, measure the instability of the whole position, a Marxism held together by an ancestor it shared with its liberal enemies. The third transaction, with Spinoza, belongs to the structuralist generation and its aftermath: Althusser's circle found in Spinoza the model of a knowledge that proceeds without a subject, by the adequate ideas of a structure's effects, and in his parallelism of mind and body the charter for a materialism of ideology that owed nothing to reflection; the Spinoza current then flowed beyond Marxism altogether, into the philosophy of immanence that dominated Parisian thought in the following decades, carrying the tradition's DNA into hosts that disowned the family name.

The fourth transaction, with Freud, has been touched already and needs only its balance sheet. Psychoanalysis gave the tradition its missing mechanism of internalisation, the means by which the external order becomes the internal censor, and the price was a permanent instability in the theory of the subject, since the Freudian subject, divided, desiring, and opaque to itself, sits uneasily in every Marxist construction built to receive it, from the Frankfurt character studies to Marcuse's wager on a non-repressive civilisation. Standing back from the four transactions, the pattern the codifier noticed, the recurrence of the ancestors, reveals its function with unusual clarity. A theory cut off from a practice that replenishes it must replenish itself from its library, and the quality of a generation of Western Marxism can be gauged, with surprising reliability, by the depth of the ancestor it chose to argue with: Hegel for the founders, in the years when the argument was about history itself; Kant and Spinoza for the mid-century schools, when it was about method; Freud throughout, whenever it was about the soul. The ancestors were not escapes from Marx. They were the instruments by which successive defeats were thought, and the library, in this tradition, was never the opposite of the world. It was where the world, having closed its other doors, was admitted.

## After the obituaries

The tradition has been buried on schedule by each succeeding generation, and the burials form a pattern worth recording, because what survives a burial is the surest index of what was alive. The first obituary was written by its prosecutors in the nineteen twenties, who expected the condemned books to vanish with their authors' recantations; instead the books went underground and resurfaced forty years later as classics. The second was written by the movements of 1968 in the act of reviving it, since the students who carried Marcuse in their pockets also announced that the old theory was dead and only its weapons would be kept, a distinction the weapons have never respected. The third and most confident obituary followed the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, when the end of the Eastern twin was widely taken to close the Western one's account as well, and the tradition's books were remaindered by history along with the catechisms. What in fact followed was a sorting. The tradition's party-political husks, its orthodoxies and its scholastic quarrels about the correct reading, fell away with the world that had sustained them. Its instruments remained in service, and when, a generation later, a crisis of the financial system sent readers back to Marx in numbers the publishers could measure, the Marx they reached for had been kept in print, in translation, in scholarly editions and in living interpretive traditions, substantially by the custodianship this guide has described. The defeats had produced the custodians; the custodians preserved the estate; the estate was claimed by heirs its testators never met.

There is a temptation, in closing a guide of this kind, to convert that survival into a triumph, and the temptation should be refused in the tradition's own spirit. Its members did not write in order to stock the libraries of a future critical academy, and measuring them by their posterity repeats, in a kindly form, the genetic reasoning this guide has resisted throughout, as if a concept's survival proved its truth rather than merely its fitness for uses its makers did not foresee. The accurate note is narrower and stranger. A body of theory produced under conditions that should, by every materialist expectation, have extinguished it, in cells, in exile, in the seminar rooms of a hostile prosperity, developed instruments that the following century found indispensable for understanding the order that had defeated its authors. Whether that outcome vindicates the theory or merely the instruments, whether the siege is still in progress or long since became a garrison town of the academy, are questions this guide has equipped its readers to argue with evidence rather than with the label alone. The label, meanwhile, continues its double career, as the name of a closed population of masters and as the name of an open predicament, and the readers who use it well will always say which of the two they mean. That discipline, simple to state and rare in practice, is the whole of the law for this subject; the rest, as a greater tradition than this one put it, is commentary.

## Five recoveries: how each era re-edited the tradition

The tradition's history after its writing is a sequence of recoveries, each of which re-edited the inheritance for its own emergency, and the sequence is the practical proof that a canon is an argument renewed by every generation that touches it. The first recovery was clandestine: through the nineteen thirties and forties the founding texts survived in the keeping of exiles, widows, party archives, and a handful of readers, while their authors recanted, fell silent, or died at borders, and the corpus existed as a rumour inside the movement that had condemned it. The second recovery followed the thaw of 1956, when the break with the official movement sent a generation searching for an uncontaminated Marxism and the texts came back into print as the classics of an alternative: the young Marx in the manuscripts, Lukacs in discussion editions, Gramsci in the party's own thematic volumes, read now as the property of the new lefts forming outside the apparatuses. The third recovery was the militant one of the nineteen sixties, which seized the corpus as a strategic arsenal: the English edition of the Prison Notebooks in 1971, prepared with a scholarly apparatus and received as a manual for the long war of position in advanced societies; the reissue of History and Class Consciousness in 1967, carrying its author's self-disowning preface in the same binding as the essays the students underlined; the translation programmes that put the Frankfurt corpus, the Italian debates, and the French duel into paperbacks within a few years. Each militant reading was a productive misreading in part, since strategists were mined from texts written in despair, but the miners found real metal, and the concepts' second career in the movements dates from this recovery rather than from their composition.

The fourth recovery was the codification itself, the moment in 1976 when the whole inheritance was gathered, named, and anatomised, which had the double effect of fixing the canon for the academy and provoking the counter-scholarship that has been correcting the fix ever since. The fifth recovery, still in progress in the field's scholarship, is the archaeological one: the critical editions, the correspondence volumes, the archival biographies, the recovery of the excluded continents and the forgotten economists, the slow replacement of the received portraits by documented ones. This guide belongs, self-consciously, to the fifth recovery, and its method has been its argument throughout: that the tradition is best honoured not by defending its label or by prosecuting it, but by restoring to view everything the label's successive editors, friendly and hostile, found it convenient to omit, and then letting the enlarged record bear the weight of whatever thesis can still stand on it. The defeat thesis stands, on the enlarged record, as the best explanation of the tradition's formation and an acknowledged failure as a measure of its worth; the category stands as a fertile construction whose construction must be confessed each time it is used; and the tradition stands, as it has stood since the first condemnation announced its existence, as the proof that the life of theory is longer, stranger, and less obedient than the life of the organisations that commission, condemn, and inherit it.

## Fascism, the founding trauma

No anatomy of this tradition is complete that treats fascism as background scenery, because fascism was the event its central concepts were built to explain and the force that physically scattered its authors across the earth. The official movement's analysis, fixed by the early nineteen thirties, defined fascism as the open terrorist dictatorship of the most reactionary elements of finance capital, a formula that made it the last stage of a dying system and licensed the catastrophic strategy of treating social democracy as the main enemy while the actual enemy marched. The Western tradition's analyses, produced in flight from the consequences of that error, are correspondingly various and correspondingly deep. Gramsci, writing before the March on Rome had finished unfolding, analysed the fascist phenomenon as a passive revolution and a Caesarism, a solution imposed from above on a society whose classes had deadlocked, and his prison notebooks kept returning to the question of what the liberal state had failed to hegemonise. Wilhelm Reich, from the borderland between the movement and psychoanalysis, asked the question the economists could not hear, why the masses had desired their own subjection, and answered it from the sexual and familial structure of the authoritarian character, in a book the movement's parties united to suppress. The Institute made the phenomenon a research programme: Franz Neumann's Behemoth, written in exile, anatomised the Third Reich as a polycratic chaos of competing empires rather than a monolith, an analysis that still anchors the historiography; the studies of authority and prejudice measured the fascist potential inside democratic populations; and the wartime masterwork of Horkheimer and Adorno finally widened the question until fascism became a possibility inside enlightenment civilisation itself, its barbarism the return of everything the administered world had repressed. A tradition that produced, under fire, the century's richest literature on the century's central catastrophe had not abandoned politics for philosophy. It had discovered, in the hardest way available, that the politics it inherited could not see the catastrophe coming, and it rebuilt its instruments accordingly. That rebuilding, with all the displacements, difficulties, and pessimisms this guide has catalogued, is the tradition, and its name, whatever its construction and its silences, has earned its place on the map.

One consequence of the trauma deserves explicit statement, since it explains a feature of the tradition that newcomers often misread as mere intellectual fashion. A generation formed by the discovery that civilisation could organise extermination through its most advanced instruments, bureaucracy, industry, broadcasting, and the applied sciences of man, was bound to interrogate the instruments themselves rather than merely their owners. The suspicion of technique, of administered culture, of reason narrowed to calculation, which runs from the Frankfurt wartime writings through the whole later tradition, is not a prejudice imported from romantic anti-capitalism, though romantic tributaries fed it. It is a conclusion drawn from evidence at the closest possible range, by analysts several of whom had measured, in their own survey data from before the catastrophe, the dispositions that the catastrophe then mobilised, and who lived to see their measurements become a death toll. The tradition's darkness has a bibliography and a body count behind it, and readers who keep both in view will find its most forbidding pages less forbidding, because they will know what the pages are about.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Western Marxism?

It is the name given to the body of Marxist theory written in continental Europe between the nineteen twenties and the nineteen seventies, running from Lukacs and Gramsci through the Frankfurt School to Sartre and Althusser. Its hallmarks are a concentration on philosophy, culture, and consciousness rather than on economics and statecraft, a growing distance from the workers' movements that earlier Marxism had served, and a migration of the work into universities and institutes. The name was not the members' own: it was applied to them from outside, first by a French philosopher in the nineteen fifties and then systematically by a British historian in the nineteen seventies.

### Q: Who coined the term Western Marxism?

Maurice Merleau-Ponty, the French phenomenologist, in his book Adventures of the Dialectic, published in 1955. He was analysing the young Lukacs, the Hungarian critic whose essays on reification had rebuilt Marxism around the idea of class consciousness, and he needed a word for that philosophically rich, unorthodox current as against the official doctrine administered from Moscow. The label began as one half of a contrast inside a book about something else, the fate of the dialectic, and it lay relatively quiet until Perry Anderson adopted it two decades later, widened its membership to a dozen figures, and gave it the systematic meaning that textbooks have transmitted since.

### Q: Which thinkers count as Western Marxists?

The conventional roster begins with the two founders of 1923, Georg Lukacs in Hungary and Karl Korsch in Germany, and with Antonio Gramsci, the Italian party leader who wrote his notebooks in a fascist prison. It continues with the Frankfurt School, Horkheimer, Adorno, Marcuse, and their associate Walter Benjamin, and with Ernst Bloch and Henri Lefebvre. The postwar French generation adds Lucien Goldmann, Jean-Paul Sartre in his Marxist phase, and Louis Althusser, while Italy contributes the anti-Hegelian school of Galvano Della Volpe and Lucio Colletti. The roster is a construction, not a membership roll: borderline cases are argued over, and the argument is part of the subject.

### Q: Why did Western Marxism turn to philosophy?

Because events had made the inherited theory's central expectation unintelligible without it. Capitalism had entered its deepest crisis, yet the working classes of the advanced countries had not become revolutionary; they had followed nationalist, fascist, or reformist banners instead. Explaining that outcome required a theory of consciousness itself: of how the commodity form shapes perception, how consent is organised through schools, churches, and newspapers, and how authority is installed in the character of the dominated. Those are philosophical questions, and the tradition pursued them with the instruments of Hegel, Kant, and eventually Freud because no other instruments seemed adequate to a problem that economics alone had demonstrably failed to settle.

### Q: Was Western Marxism a product of defeat?

On the most influential reading, yes, though the claim is about formation rather than worth. The revolutionary wave that followed the Russian Revolution broke in Germany, Hungary, and Italy; fascism then destroyed the labour movements of central Europe; and the surviving communist parties subordinated themselves to a foreign state. Theory produced inside that long emergency lost its practical addressee, retreated into institutes and exile, and took on the marks of its isolation: abstraction, difficulty, an obsession with consciousness, and a settled melancholy. The reading has one necessary qualification, which examiners reward: an explanation of the conditions that shaped these ideas is not a judgment on whether the ideas are true.

### Q: What did Perry Anderson argue about the tradition?

In Considerations on Western Marxism, published in 1976, Anderson argued that the tradition formed a single historical phenomenon with a recognisable anatomy. He listed its structural traits: the divorce between theory and political practice, the migration of interest from economics and politics toward philosophy and art, the relocation of Marxist work into universities, the esoteric character of its language, its recurrent returns to pre-Marxist thinkers such as Hegel and Spinoza, and its prevailing pessimism. He then explained the anatomy causally, as the deposit left by the failure of revolution in Western Europe and the isolation of theory from any workers' movement. The book also carried a polemical purpose, since its author was arguing for a different, more classical Marxism for his own country.

### Q: Did Western Marxism abandon economics?

Not entirely, and the exceptions are the most instructive part of the answer. Henryk Grossmann wrote his mathematical theory of capitalist breakdown while employed by the Frankfurt Institute itself. Paul Mattick developed an austere crisis theory from the council communist margins, and Ernest Mandel produced a vast economic periodisation of the postwar world for a living Trotskyist movement. What is true is narrower: the tradition's prestige and posterity concentrated in its philosophy, aesthetics, and theories of culture, while its economists worked at its edges. The familiar claim that economics was abandoned is partly an artefact of how the category was drawn, since defining the tradition as the philosophical current excludes the economists in advance.

### Q: Why is Western Marxism so hard to read?

Several causes compound. Some of the writing was produced under censorship or party discipline, and learned protective indirections that outlived the danger; Gramsci's notebooks, written past a prison censor, are the extreme case. Some of it reflects a deliberate philosophical conviction that a society misrepresenting itself cannot be described in the unexamined language that society supplies, so the prose strains its concepts on purpose. Some of it is the ordinary dialect of the universities into which the work migrated, where difficulty is a professional currency. And some of it, as the tradition's own analyst observed, is the mark of a missing audience: writers addressing a movement must be plain, while writers addressing posterity can afford to wait for their readers.

### Q: Is Western Marxism a useful category at all?

Used knowingly, yes; used as a photograph, no. The category organises a century of difficult writing into an intelligible story with a causal spine, and its central observations, the separation from practice, the philosophical displacement, the geography of exile, are documented patterns that scholars who reject its author's politics still rely on. Its dangers are equally documented: its boundaries were drawn by an analyst with an argument of his own, its omissions are systematic, and its central thesis tempts readers to explain concepts away instead of answering them. The sound practice is to keep the frame and print its construction on its face, restoring the figures and continents it leaves out.

### Q: Does the label exclude the non-European world?

By construction, it does, and the exclusion is the label's most consequential silence. In the very decades the category covers, José Carlos Mariátegui in Peru was fusing Marx with indigenous communal traditions, M. N. Roy in India was arguing the colonial question with Lenin's own congresses behind him, and C. L. R. James in the Caribbean was writing one of the century's classic Marxist histories, of the Haitian Revolution. None of them appears on the standard map, not because their work lacked depth or cultural range, but because the map's adjective was inherited from a contrast inside Europe. The point is about the category, not the world: Marxist theory in those decades was planetary, and the label describes one province of it.

### Q: How does the defeat thesis grade a thinker it was not designed for?

By bending rather than breaking, provided the grader specifies which trait is being tested. Gramsci is the standard exercise: a party leader writing for militants, he should, on a crude reading, resemble the classical generation, and in his strategic concreteness he does; yet his questions are the defeat's questions, posed from a cell, in a philosophical idiom saturated with Croce and Machiavelli. Sartre supplies the complementary exercise, a figure with no party and no chair whose vast attempt to reunite theory with concrete history was abandoned unfinished. Such cases show the thesis working as a claim about predicaments and gradients, not as a stamp that every career must either bear or refute.

### Q: What should a student separate between the tradition's periods?

Four phases reward separation. A founding phase, from the postwar revolutionary crisis into the early nineteen thirties, when the questions were open and parties still mattered. A catastrophe phase, through fascism, exile, and world war, when the darkest masterpieces were composed in flight or hiding and the theme became domination itself. A consolidation phase after the war, when survivors became professors, manuscripts were edited, and academic forms replaced movement forms. And a diffusion phase, from the nineteen sixties, when the humanist and structuralist camps fought their duel and the concepts escaped into feminism, cultural studies, and the wider academy. Arguments about the tradition usually fail by grading one phase with another phase's evidence.

### Q: Why did philosophy displace economics inside the tradition?

Displacement followed the audience. A movement preparing for power commissions programmes, statistics, and strategy; it asks its economists questions and punishes useless answers. When the addressee vanished, the demand vanished with it, and the surviving institutions, universities, endowed institutes, the exile's desk, commission what they reward: commentary, method, the monograph on a predecessor's categories. Philosophy also offered the one tribunal still in session, since a theory denied verification by events could still win arguments against rival readings of Marx. Add the genuine intellectual emergency about consciousness, which economics could not formulate, and the displacement is overdetermined: the tradition did philosophy because philosophy was what its situation asked of it and what its questions required.

### Q: How does the tradition map order the schools?

By predicament before doctrine. The map in this guide arranges the figures by country and period, then reads across each row for the concept contributed, the pre-Marxist ancestry recruited, and the relation to an organised movement. Read down the movement column and the tradition's master pattern appears without commentary: a commissar and a party leader at the top, an endowed institute keeping its distance in the middle, a professor in internal dissent and a fellow traveller in cycles of rupture at the bottom. The schools, Frankfurt, the Italian anti-Hegelians, the Parisian camps, then fall into place as local settlements of one shared emergency rather than as beads on a chronological string.

### Q: What separates Western Marxism from the Marxism that preceded it?

The classical generation, from Marx and Engels through Luxemburg and Lenin, wrote inside ascending movements, held theory and organisation in single careers, and expected the economy's contradictions to deliver the revolutionary subject in due course. The Western generations inherited the contradictions without the ascent. Their characteristic figure is therefore not the party theoretician but the professor, the exile, the prisoner, or the independent man of letters; their characteristic subject is not the crisis but the consciousness that fails to respond to it; and their characteristic product is not the programme but the treatise on method, culture, or ideology. The separation is one of historical situation before it is one of doctrine, which is why the same writer can cross it when the situation changes.
