Arthur Brooke's *Romeus and Juliet* is the poem Romeo and Juliet was built from, and the debt is larger than the play's fame usually admits. Published in 1562 as *The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet*, Brooke's long narrative in rhyming couplets supplies nearly everything a plot summary of the play would list: the feud, the feast, the window courtship, the secret marriage, the Friar, the Nurse, the street killing, the banishment, the forced match, the sleeping potion, the failed message, and the tomb. Shakespeare's originality in this tragedy is real, but it is the originality of transformation working inside a borrowed frame. The verdict this article defends is plain: Brooke gave the play its architecture entire, its cast entire, and even the moral problem the drama refuses to settle, and any account of the play that begins with Shakespeare inventing the story begins in error.

That verdict needs the poem itself, read as a work rather than mined for parallels. Brooke's poem runs to some three thousand lines, moves at a procession's pace through months where the play hurries through days, and speaks in two voices at once: a storyteller who lingers over his lovers and a preacher who has condemned them in his own preface before the story starts. Reading the poem whole changes what the play looks like. Scenes that feel like pure invention turn out to have germs in Brooke's couplets. Effects that seem to belong to the stage alone, the compressed calendar above all, stand out against Brooke's leisure as deliberate choices. And the poem's own strengths, its married middle, its Fortune-haunted narration, its sententious Friar, become visible as part of the inheritance rather than as scaffolding to be kicked away once the building stands.

Restoring Brooke also restores the play's first audience. The men and women who watched the tragedy in the 1590s did not meet its story as news. Many of them knew it the way Brooke had fixed it in English a generation before, in a poem reprinted within their own lifetimes, and they watched the drama the way readers watch an adaptation of a book they own: measuring, comparing, registering each compression and invention against a text in the memory. Source study is sometimes treated as an archaeology of the play, a digging beneath it. For this play it is closer to a reconstruction of its original auditorium. Brooke was not merely what the playwright read. Brooke was what the audience had read, and the play's boldest effects, the five-day calendar, the silenced tomb, Mercutio enlarged into a hinge and killed on it, were composed for spectators in a position to feel them as choices. The poem's recovery therefore changes the criticism of the play at its root: the drama's originality was never exercised in a vacuum, and it was never meant to be invisible. It was meant to be noticed, against Brooke, by people who knew Brooke.

## Who Was Arthur Brooke, and What Exactly Did He Publish in 1562?

Arthur Brooke is one of the most consequential minor figures in English literature: a writer remembered for a single book, whose single book underlies the most famous love tragedy in the language. Almost nothing secure is known of his life. He moved in the Inns of Court world that produced much early Elizabethan drama and verse, he wrote in the moralizing idiom that world favoured, and he died young, drowned in 1563 while crossing to the English expedition at Le Havre, when the ship carrying him went down. The poem he left behind was barely a year old. Its author never saw what it would become, because what it became depended on a playwright who was an infant, if born at all, when Brooke died.

The book itself appeared from the press of Richard Tottell in 1562, entered in the Stationers' Register that year, and reprinted in 1587, within Shakespeare's own working lifetime. Its title page is a compressed lesson in how Tudor books advertised borrowed goods: *The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet, written first in Italian by Bandell and nowe in Englishe by Ar. Br.* The credit line names Matteo Bandello, whose Italian novella stands at the head of the story's line, and reduces Brooke to initials, as if the English versifier were a tradesman delivering foreign merchandise. The title page is not false, exactly, but it is incomplete in a way that matters for understanding the poem. Brooke did not work from Bandello's Italian. He worked from Pierre Boaistuau's French version, published in the *Histoires Tragiques* of 1559, and the poem's texture, its expansions, its moral weather, all passed through that French filter before Brooke's couplets received them.

Boaistuau had already reshaped the Italian tale, enlarging its speeches, heightening its rhetoric, and strengthening its frame of moral reflection. Brooke took over that enlarged version and Englished it, adding a preface of his own and a narrator's running commentary that is his most personal contribution. The full descent of the story, from the Italian novelle through the French retelling to Brooke's poem and the prose rival version by William Painter, is mapped in [the source family tree of the play's origins](/2025/09/12/sources-of-romeo-and-juliet-family-tree/), and the French link in that chain has its own study in [Pierre Boaistuau and the French tradition behind the story](/2025/10/24/pierre-boaistuau-french-tradition/). Brooke's place in the line is the crucial one for the play, because his poem, and not the Italian or French versions, is the text whose order of events, whose phrasing, and whose emphases the drama follows scene by scene.

## Brooke and Boaistuau: What the English Poem Took From the French

The relation between Brooke and Boaistuau is close enough to be called translation and loose enough to be called authorship, and fixing its exact temperature matters, because everything claimed for Brooke's originality has to survive the comparison. Boaistuau's *Histoire Tragique* of Romeo and Juliet was itself an adaptation with an agenda. Working from Bandello's novella, the French writer expanded the lovers' interviews into formal rhetorical exchanges, gave the Friar long discourses on the properties of his sleeping powder and the philosophy of his art, multiplied the narrator's reflections on Fortune, and framed the whole as a cautionary history of disordered love. Brooke, rendering that French prose into English couplets, kept the expansions. The poem's long speeches, its herbal Friar, its wheel of Fortune, its sententious digestion of every crisis into proverb: all of this is already present in the French, and the honest verdict on Brooke's poem begins by surrendering those territories to his predecessor.

What Brooke adds is nonetheless decisive, and it operates at the level of address. His preface has no French equivalent in kind: where Boaistuau moralizes from inside the narration, Brooke steps outside the book altogether and prosecutes his lovers in propria persona, in prose, before the verse begins, recruiting the tale into an English argument about friars, confession, and disobedience that no Italian or French writer of the story had any reason to make. The anti-Catholic charges in that preface, the "superstitious friars", the auricular confession named "the key of whoredom and treason", are Brooke's own polemical signature, written for a readership three years into the Elizabethan settlement. The poem behind the preface is more Catholic in its furniture than the preface can afford to admit, a Verona of friars, confessions, and monastic counsel, and the friction between the book's frame and its contents is an English friction, Brooke's personal contribution to the tale's long migration.

Brooke also naturalizes. The French text's Italian names settle into English spellings; the narrative's distances, seasons, and ceremonies are re-imagined at an English scale; and the verse form itself, poulter's measure, is a native Tudor instrument with no counterpart in Boaistuau's prose. Translation in couplets is never neutral carriage. Every rhyme forces a small decision, every fourteen-syllable line demands padding or compression, and across three thousand lines those small decisions accumulate into a distinct poetic personality: more proverbial than the French, more legalistic in its judgments, more patient in its descriptions of ceremony and dress. Source scholars who have set the texts side by side, as Bullough did in his parallel presentation, find Brooke following Boaistuau's sense with a fidelity that makes the departures legible: the preface, the sharpened moral asides, the English colouring. The poem is a translation the way a harbour is a translation of a river: the water is the same water, and everything about the arrival is new.

One further consequence of the French mediation deserves emphasis, because it corrects a persistent error in popular accounts. The play's debt is often described as a debt to an Italian story that Shakespeare somehow obtained. The documentary chain runs otherwise. What reached England was a French rhetorical enlargement of an Italian novella, versified by an Englishman who credited the Italian on his title page and worked from the French on his desk. The play's Verona, its philosophizing Friar, its Fortune-haunted narration, its lovers trained in formal oratory: these are Franco-Italian goods that passed through Brooke's customs house, and the stamps on them are his.

The mediation also explains a feature of the poem that puzzles readers who come to it from the play: its amplitude of discourse. Bandello's novella is swift and ironic, a tale told at a raconteur's pace. Boaistuau slowed it into rhetoric, and Brooke, whose couplets could not hurry in any case, completed the transformation into something like a debate with a plot attached, in which every crisis generates its oration and every oration its sententia. The play's famous speed is therefore a double reversal, of Brooke's metre and of the whole Franco-English tradition of expansion behind him, and it registers as speed precisely because the tradition had taught its audience to expect the tale at a walking pace. Compression is only audible against an established tempo. Brooke, and Boaistuau behind him, established the tempo the drama exists to break.

Two features of the 1562 book deserve emphasis before its contents are examined. The first is its completeness as an act of cultural transfer. Before Brooke, the story of the Veronese lovers existed for English readers not at all; after Brooke, it existed in English verse, in a form any literate playgoer or playwright could absorb entire. The poem did for the tale what a port does for cargo: it landed the story in England, in English, with a moral customs declaration attached. The second feature is the preface's casual report that Brooke had himself seen the same argument presented on a stage. A play on the lovers existed before Brooke wrote, and is lost. That single remark, easy to skim past, complicates every simple story of origins: the poem is a source for Shakespeare, but it is also a survivor of an earlier theatrical handling of the same material, one more reminder that the tale kept migrating between page and stage long before the famous version fixed it.

The publisher's list sharpens the point. Richard Tottell built his house on two pillars, the law books that made his fortune and the vernacular verse that made his name, above all the *Songes and Sonettes* of 1557, the miscellany that introduced Surrey and Wyatt to print and taught English readers what a love poem in their own language could be. Brooke's narrative belonged to the second pillar: a long, morally warranted love story in English couplets, from the press that had defined English love poetry for the reading public five years earlier. The association is not decoration. A poem about love's rhetoric, issued by the publisher of the age's canonical love lyrics, entered the culture with its credentials in order, and the young men of the Inns of Court, Brooke's own milieu, were precisely the readers who bought such books, marked their sententiae, and carried their stories into the theatres where some of them also wrote. The poem's path from Tottell's shop to the public stage runs through that milieu, and it is shorter than the distance between 1562 and the 1590s makes it look.

## Poulter's Measure: The Gait of Brooke's Poem

Brooke tells his story in poulter's measure, the couplet form built from a line of twelve syllables followed by a line of fourteen, rhyming in pairs, repeated for the length of the poem. The name was explained by Tudor commentators through the poulterer's habit of counting eggs by dozens with an extra bird thrown in at intervals, and the form has the same swinging irregularity: a shorter line, then a longer line that overshoots it, couplet after couplet, like a man walking with one stride deliberately longer than the other. Fourteeners on their own would later split into the ballad's common metre; yoked to alexandrines in Brooke's pattern, they give the poem a loping, processional gait that no reader can hurry.

The gait is the meaning, in part. Poulter's measure is an end-stopped, sententious form. Its long lines invite proverbs, balanced clauses, and moral tags, and its rhymes click shut every second line like the lid of a box. A story told in this measure cannot easily do speed, secrecy, or interruption. It can do pageant, procession, ceremony, and lament, and Brooke's poem is full of all four. Where the play's blank verse will learn to fracture, overlap, and accelerate, Brooke's couplets proceed. The months his story consumes suit the metre's patience: this is a poem built for a world in which events ripen rather than detonate.

Brooke handles the form with more variety than its reputation suggests. The couplets carry plain narration, but they also carry set speeches, and the poem is generous with direct address: Romeus pleads, Juliet reasons, the Friar counsels, the Nurse bustles, all in extended orations that the narrator frames and then resumes. These speeches matter for the source question, because they are the poem's proto-drama. Again and again, a passage of Brooke reads like a scene waiting for a stage: a grievance stated, an answer returned, a decision taken. The playwright who later worked over this poem did not have to invent its people's voices from nothing. He had to free them from the couplet's procession and let them interrupt each other.

The metre also explains the poem's most distinctive pleasure, its slowness at the centre. Because poulter's measure cannot hurry, Brooke's lovers are granted the one luxury the play will deny them: duration. They meet, they marry, and then, astonishingly to anyone who knows only the play, they live as husband and wife for months, visiting by night, known to the Nurse and the Friar, in a settled secret domesticity that the poem treats as the story's still middle rather than as a pause before disaster. The couplets rock that middle like a cradle. When violence finally arrives, it arrives in a poem that has had time to build a life worth losing, and the loss is measured accordingly.

Brooke did not invent the measure, and his contemporaries had opinions about it. George Gascoigne, writing on English versification a decade later, treated poulter's measure as a known quantity with a known gait, and George Puttenham's *Arte of English Poesie* would catalogue it among the stanza resources of the language. The form carried associations its first readers could hear: it was the measure of moralized narrative and of stately complaint, the tempo of a story that expected to be weighed rather than witnessed. Choosing it was itself an interpretation of the tale. A story in poulter's measure announces that it will proceed by ceremony and reflection, that its pleasures will be the pleasures of recognition and judgment, and Brooke's poem honours the announcement for three thousand lines. The drama that later took the tale over had to change the measure before it could change anything else: blank verse, unrhymed and interruptible, is the sound of a world where events arrive faster than morals can be drawn from them, and the distance between the two metres is the distance between the two works in miniature.

There is a further consequence in the couplets' appetite for the proverb. A form that closes every second line with a rhyme rewards sentences that sound like conclusions, and Brooke's poem is dense with lines that detach into wisdom: observations on youth and age, on counsel taken and refused, on the governance of appetite, on Fortune's bookkeeping. The poem reads, in places, like a commonplace book that has been given a plot, and that quality endeared it to a century that read with a pen in hand, marking sententiae for reuse. The play preserves the habit while dispersing it among speakers, so that what in Brooke is the narrator's single proverbial voice becomes in the drama a contest of proverbial voices, Friar against Nurse, Prince against Capulet, each sententia answered by an event.

## The Preface That Condemns Its Own Heroes

Before the poem begins, Brooke addresses his reader in prose, and the address is one of the most quoted documents in the play's prehistory, because it condemns in advance the very lovers the poem is about to make engaging. Brooke presents the story as an example to be shunned. His lovers, he writes, are a pair "thralling themselves to unhonest desire, neglecting the authority and advice of parents and friends, conferring their principal counsels with drunken gossips and superstitious friars (the naturally fit instruments of unchastity)". The sentence continues through their whole career, "attempting all adventures of peril for th'attaining of their wished lust, using auricular confession (the key of whoredom and treason) for furtherance of their purpose". This is not neutral plot summary. It is a charge sheet, and every item in it is a verdict: desire is dishonest, parents are authority, the Nurse is a drunken gossip, the Friar is a superstitious instrument, and confession itself is a key to whoredom and treason.

The anti-Catholic edge is deliberate and of its moment. Brooke writes as an Elizabethan Protestant for readers formed by the same settlement, and his preface recruits the Italian tale into a domestic polemic: the machinery of the old religion, friars, confession, secret counsel, is what allows the sin to proceed. The poem that follows is more complicated than its preface, as poems usually are, but the preface never goes away. It stands at the poem's gate like a customs officer, instructing the reader to enjoy nothing without condemning it first. The full argument between that gatekeeping voice and the drama that inherited the story is taken up in [the comparison of Brooke's moralising preface with the play's treatment](/2026/02/27/brooke-moralising-preface-vs-shakespeare/), and no reading of Brooke is complete without it.

The preface is also a small treatise on how a Tudor translator justified his labour. Brooke presents the story as profitable example rather than as delight, promises the reader a spectacle of disordered love punished, and defends the vernacular enterprise itself: an Italian tale, Englished, made to do English moral work. The defence was conventional in form and sincere in function, since the poem's market depended on readers who bought verse narratives as improvement as much as entertainment. What distinguishes Brooke's performance of the convention is its prosecutorial energy. Most translators' prefaces of the period gesture at a moral and move on. Brooke itemizes. His lovers are charged count by count, desire, disobedience, bad counsel, perilous attempts, abused confession, so that the preface reads less like an advertisement than like the indictment to which the poem will be the evidence and the ending the sentence. That legal shape, charge, evidence, judgment, is the poem's deep structure in miniature, and it explains why Brooke's Verona feels, from the first page, like a place where everyone is already on trial.

### Why does Brooke keep stopping the story to lecture his reader?

Because the poem is built as an exemplum, a story whose telling and whose sermon are the same job. Brooke inherits a continental tale and an English didactic habit at once, and his narrator interrupts to gloss, to warn, and to draw the moral before the reader can draw a kinder one.

The lectures license the pleasure: sin may be shown at length provided the showing keeps apologizing for itself.

The apology, though, keeps slipping. Brooke's narrator moralizes over the lovers, yet the verse lavishes its best energies on their speeches, their stratagems, and their griefs, and the sheer duration of the married middle gives the condemned pair a dignity the preface's categories cannot contain. This doubleness is not hypocrisy so much as structure. Brooke needs the lovers at fault, or his example collapses; he needs them sympathetic, or his poem does. The tension between the preface's friar, a superstitious instrument of unchastity, and the poem's Friar Laurence, a learned, cautious, and largely benevolent counsellor, is the same tension in miniature: the doctrine condemns what the narrative keeps pardoning. Shakespeare did not create that doubleness. He inherited it, dissolved the preface into a Chorus, and let the drama carry both voices at once.

The preface also contains Brooke's most tantalizing sentence for theatre historians, his remark that he had seen the same argument lately set forth on stage, and with more commendation than he could look for from his own version. The remark is offered as modesty, the versifier deferring to a livelier medium, but its evidentiary value is large: a Romeo and Juliet play existed in England before 1562, performed, applauded, and now wholly lost. Brooke's poem therefore stands in a double relation to the stage. It is a source the later drama feeds on, and it is a record, at one remove, of an earlier drama that fed on the tale before it. The story's English life was theatrical before it was Shakespearean.

## The Story as Brooke Tells It

Brooke opens in Verona, a city governed by Prince Escalus and divided between two great houses, the Capellets and the Montagues, whose feud the poem treats as the standing condition of civic life. Into that division the narrator introduces Fortune, whose wheel governs the poem's weather, and then Romeus, a young Montague of beauty and promise, wasting in the approved Petrarchan fashion for a lady who disdains him. Brooke lingers over this first, hopeless attachment, because it establishes his hero as a practised lover before Juliet appears: Romeus enters the feast already fluent in love's rhetoric, already sick of its conventional disease, and the poem, unlike the play, gives the disease a long first act of its own.

### What does Brooke's long opening on Fortune and Verona establish before the lovers appear?

It establishes the poem's two causal systems before either lover acts. Verona is shown divided against itself, a civic fact no one chose, and Fortune is installed above the city as the power that turns private folly into public catastrophe, so that blame and pity both have somewhere to go.

Every later event will be filed under one system or the other, and the poem's refusal to choose between them is set in this opening the way a key signature is set at the head of a score. The feud is the standing condition; the wheel is the standing motion; the lovers enter a machine already built and already turning.

The feast falls in the Christmas season at the great Capulet's house, a revel to which Romeus comes masked with his companions, half hoping to see the lady who scorns him. What he sees is Juliet, the Capulet daughter, sixteen years old in Brooke's telling, and the poem's centre of gravity shifts at once. Brooke gives the meeting its full ceremonial weight: the dancing, the watching, the first words, and a small physical detail that later readers always notice, the coldness of Mercutio's hand. Mercutio is present in Brooke as a courtier of the feast, a gallant whose hand, taken in the courtesies of the evening, feels to Juliet like ice. He is a ornament of the revel, nothing more. No death waits for him in Brooke's poem, no curse, no hinge of tragedy. He dances, he is remarked on, and the story passes him by, and that passing is itself one of the surest measures of how much the play would eventually build on Brooke's ground plan without Brooke's blueprint for this figure.

The courtship proceeds by the poem's slow degrees. Romeus haunts the Capulet house, speaks with Juliet at her window across intervening nights, and wins her consent, and the two, impatient of parental authority exactly as the preface charged, carry their counsels to Friar Laurence. Brooke's Friar is a figure the preface's polemic cannot accommodate: a Franciscan of learning and experience, skilled in the properties of herbs and stones, a natural philosopher in a habit, who hears the lovers' confessions, weighs their case, and marries them in hope that the union will reconcile the houses. The marriage is secret, solemnized, and then, in the poem's boldest divergence from the play's tempo, lived in.

### Why is Brooke's Friar Laurence a master of herbs, powders, and natural causes?

Because Brooke, following his French source, imagines the Friar as a man whose religion and whose natural knowledge are one profession. His cell is a stillroom as much as a chapel, stocked with the plants and powders his order's medicine required, and his counsel grows from the same habit of mind as his pharmacy.

Observe the nature of a thing, then apply the preparation that nature allows: the maxim governs his pharmacy and his matchmaking alike. The potion, when it comes, is botany before it is plot.

### How does Brooke make the secret wedding the still centre of his poem?

By giving the marriage what the play never can: time to exist. Brooke's lovers are husband and wife for the space of three months, meeting by night with the Nurse's connivance, and the poem settles into that secret domesticity as into a held chord, the story's middle state rather than a pause before disaster.

The wedding is not a snatched sacrament between a ball and a brawl. It is the life the lovers actually get, and everything after it is measured as the destruction of something established rather than the interruption of something begun.

The destruction begins in the street. Tybalt, who has marked Romeus at the feast and nursed the insult of his presence there, forces the quarrel that Brooke narrates as a straight affray between two enemies: harsh words, drawn weapons, and Tybalt dead at Romeus's hand. No friend falls first, no intervention miscarries, no curse is spoken over the square. The poem's causality here is almost legal in its plainness. A feud produces a challenge, a challenge produces a killing, a killing produces a sentence, and Prince Escalus, weighing the circumstances, banishes Romeus to Mantua on pain of death. Romeus departs by night after a last visit to his wife, and the poem's long middle breaks into its long descent.

Brooke gives the exile its own duration, and the duration is an interpretation. His Romeus in Mantua is not a man in a waiting room; he is a husband in a foreign city, keeping a servant, keeping his marriage's secret, measuring his banishment against the term the sentence allows and against the reconciliation the Friar's diplomacy keeps promising. The poem visits him there across the months, and the visits build the tragedy's cruellest precondition: a life continuing. Romeus eats, sleeps, hopes, and grows accustomed to exile, so that when the false report arrives it kills not a frantic boy at the end of a five-day crisis but a settled man whose patience has been spent in advance. His purchase of the poison is correspondingly deliberate, a transaction conducted with the apothecary in full view of its illegality, and Brooke, characteristically, lets the law's categories do the emotional work: the exile buys death the way the city buys anything, across a counter, from a poor man, for gold.

In Verona, grief curdles quickly into policy. The Capellets, mourning Tybalt, press forward a marriage for Juliet with Count Paris, a young nobleman whose suit the poem presents as honourable, advantageous, and wholly unwanted. Juliet, already a wife, refuses in the only registers open to her, delay, tears, and finally the Friar's cell, where Brooke's confessor-physician produces his great device: a powder, to be taken in drink, that will cast her into a sleep so like death that she will be carried to the Capel vault and mourned as a corpse, while word goes to Mantua and Romeus returns to take her from the tomb when she wakes. Brooke's potion works on a grander scale than the play's. Its sleep lasts three days, long enough for burial, for mourning, for the whole theatre of a funeral to complete itself around a living woman.

Then the poem performs the failure on which everything turns, and it performs it quietly. The Friar's letter to Mantua is entrusted to a brother of his order, who travels to the city, falls in with friars under suspicion of plague infection, and is detained among them, the letter undelivered in his keeping. No villainy, no storm, no interception: a sick city's reasonable precaution, and a message that arrives nowhere. Romeus hears the news of Juliet's death instead from his own servant, buys poison from a Mantuan apothecary whose poverty Brooke notes as plainly as the play will, and rides for Verona and the Capel tomb.

### Why does Brooke's Paris mourn at the funeral instead of dying at the tomb?

Because Brooke never sends him there. His Paris belongs to the daylight world of contracts, funerals, and family policy, and the poem keeps him inside that world to the end, grieving at Juliet's burial as a disappointed bridegroom in the company of her kin.

The tomb in Brooke belongs to the lovers, the Friar, and the watch. Paris has no errand in it, no flowers to strew in private, and so no death, and the play's addition of his corpse to the vault's tally is one of the clearest signs of a later hand thickening the catastrophe.

The tomb scene in Brooke is the poem's longest held breath. Romeus forces the vault, finds Juliet laid among her ancestors in her winding sheet, and delivers over her body the lament the poem has been saving its strength for. He drinks his poison. Then, in the poem's most consequential arrangement of timing, Juliet wakes while Romeus is still dying, and the two hold the conversation the play will compress into horror: reproaches against Fortune, explanations, farewells exchanged between a living woman and a man feeling the poison work. He dies in her arms. Juliet, finding no poison left for herself, takes his rapier and stabs herself, and dies upon the body, and the Friar, arriving with the watch too late for anything but knowledge, finds the vault's arithmetic complete.

### How does Brooke end his poem once the lovers are buried?

With judgment, distributed with a notary's care. The Friar confesses the whole history before the Prince, and the poem then sentences its survivors according to the moral each has earned, closing the account its prose preface opened in its first pages.

The sentences are itemized. The Nurse, who carried the counsels, is banished from Verona. The apothecary who sold the poison is hanged. The Friar, his confession accepted and his purpose judged well meant, is permitted to withdraw to a hermitage a short distance from the city, where he lives out his days and dies. The two houses, instructed by the Prince and by the spectacle of the vault, are reconciled, and the lovers are buried together in a monument the city raises to them. Brooke's narrator closes over the tomb with the moral the preface promised, though by then the poem has spent three thousand lines complicating it.

## The Courtship Set Pieces: Feast and Window in Brooke

Brooke builds his courtship from two great set pieces, the feast and the window, and both repay slow reading because the play's two most famous scenes are their direct descendants. The feast in Brooke is a Christmas revel, a season of licensed disorder in a great house, and the poem renders it as ceremony before it renders it as plot: the hall, the music, the order of the dancing, the masquers admitted under the season's customs. Romeus enters that ceremony as a spectator of his own misery, positioned to watch the lady who scorns him, and Brooke's management of the moment is quietly masterful. The eye that came to feed on one face is arrested by another. Juliet is discovered the way the poem discovers everything, in procession, among the dancers, her beauty registered first as a public fact, witnessed by the room, before it becomes a private catastrophe for the man watching.

The poem lingers where the drama will hurry. Brooke allows the feast its full social mechanics: the taking of hands in the dance, the exchange of courtesies, the small change of glances and words by which two strangers in a feud become conspirators. Mercutio's icy hand belongs to this mechanics, a dancer's detail, the kind of observed trifle that certifies a narrator's presence in the room. When Romeus and Juliet first speak, they speak inside the feast's forms, and the forms are the poem's point: Verona's public rituals, dancing, masking, courtesy, are the only channels through which the feud's children can meet at all, and the tragedy begins because a channel built for ceremony is made to carry conspiracy.

The window scenes reverse the feast's terms. Where the feast is public, plural, and choreographed, the window is private, singular, and improvised, and Brooke stretches the courtship across repeated nights of it, Romeus in the garden or the street below, Juliet above, the architecture of the Capulet house turned into an instrument of assignation. The repetition matters. In Brooke, the window is not a single miraculous interview but a habit, a courtship with a rhythm, visits that lengthen as confidence grows, speeches that develop an argument across nights: his suit, her cautions, the marriage condition, the plan. The poem's critics sometimes call this slackness. It is better called courtship as process, the slow manufacture of trust between two people who begin as names in a feud and end as husband and wife, and it gives the marriage a foundation in negotiated knowledge that the play, for all its greater intensity, must ask its audience to take on credit.

Set side by side, the two set pieces also display Brooke's range as a narrative poet. The feast is his pageant mode, all surface and ceremony; the window is his rhetorical mode, all argument and plea. Between them stands the Friar's cell, his third great interior, where the courtship is converted into a contract. Feast, window, cell: appetite, negotiation, sacrament. The play preserves the sequence and the architecture exactly, compressing each room's business into a fraction of Brooke's space, and the compression is legible as such only because Brooke's rooms are so fully furnished to begin with.

## Romeus in Brooke: The Petrarchan Patient Who Becomes a Husband

Brooke's Romeus is the poem's most fully inhabited figure, and his arc is the reverse of the one popular memory assigns the hero. He begins as a type, the languishing Petrarchan lover of the sonnet tradition, sighing at a window that never opens, composing his misery in the approved postures for a lady whose disdain Brooke presents as a settled fact of nature. The poem dwells on this phase with a patience the play abandons, because Brooke is interested in the disease as a social ritual: Romeus performs his hopelessness before his companions, is rallied for it, consults the Friar about his affliction, and wears his love as young Verona expected a gentleman to wear it. The first love is a career. Juliet ends it not by outshining it but by answering it, and Brooke marks the cure in the hero's altered manner: the practised sighs give way to negotiation, strategy, and risk.

The marriage transforms him a second time. Brooke's Romeus as husband is a figure the play has no room for: a man managing a secret household, timing visits, relying on the Nurse's logistics and the Friar's counsel, enjoying a settled intimacy that the poem describes without prurience and without apology, whatever the preface said. His competence in this middle state is the poem's quiet argument against its own frame. The preface's Romeus is a thrall of dishonest desire; the narrative's Romeus is a son-in-law in hiding, conducting a marriage under siege conditions with considerable presence of mind. When the catastrophe comes, it therefore destroys a functioning husband, not a distracted suitor, and the poem's grief has a domestic weight the drama must replace with speed.

Even his violence is differently drawn. Brooke's Romeus kills Tybalt in an affray Tybalt forces, and the poem treats the killing with legal rather than psychological interest: provocation, combat, death, judgment. There is no dead friend to avenge, no moment of refused peace curdling into fury, and so no guilt of the play's kind to confess in a tomb. Brooke's Romeus in the vault is instead a penitent of Fortune, a man reading his life backward for the moment the wheel turned, and his long death, with Juliet awake beside him, is an audit rather than an explosion. The play's Romeo, defying the stars and drinking to his wife in one gesture, is this auditor compressed into a single act of will.

## Juliet in Brooke: Sixteen, Married, and Arguing Her Case

Brooke's Juliet is the poem's surprise, because the preface promises a cautionary puppet and the verse delivers a strategist. She enters at sixteen, older than the play's heroine by nearly three years, and Brooke's extra years purchase her a rhetoric: she debates her suitor at the window rather than merely receiving him, weighs the dishonour of a stolen marriage against the dishonour of a loveless one, sets conditions, and extracts from Romeus the commitment to marry before she grants the meetings that follow. The courtship in Brooke is a negotiation between two intelligences, and Juliet conducts her side of it with a lawyer's instinct for sequence: confession first, marriage next, favour after. The poem's moral frame calls this disobedience. The poem's verse calls it, unmistakably, competence.

Her married conduct extends the portrait. For three months she runs the practical side of a secret household from inside her father's house, managing the Nurse, timing the visits, sustaining the fiction of obedience while living as a wife, and Brooke allows the strain of that double life its full weight before the Paris suit makes it unbearable. Her resistance to the forced match is likewise procedural before it is passionate: delay, excuses, tears deployed as instruments, and at last the Friar's cell, where she arrives not as a penitent but as a client with a problem, demanding of her confessor the device his profession owes her. Brooke's Juliet threatens, bargains, and consents to the potion with her eyes open, having extracted from the Friar the full terms of the stratagem, the sleep's duration, the burial, the message, the waking, in an interview the poem renders as a contract's negotiation.

In the tomb she completes the arc. Waking to a husband dying beside her, she does not dissolve into lament; she cross-examines him, reproaching the Fortune that misdirected the letter, demanding the story whole, and only when the account is closed and Romeus dead does she convert grief into the poem's final act of agency, taking his rapier and ending her life on her own terms and timing. Brooke's official moral requires her to be a warning. His verse keeps making her a protagonist whose decisions, including the disastrous ones, are argued before they are taken. The play's Juliet, compressed, younger, and quicker, inherits that argumentative spine entire: the balcony negotiation, the potion soliloquy's itemized fears, the refusal to be managed, all have their first drafts in Brooke's couplets, where a sixteen-year-old conducts her tragedy like a case she intends to win.

## The Nurse in Brooke: The Go-Between the Preface Condemns

No figure in the poem is more harshly judged by its frame and more warmly treated by its narrative than the Nurse. The preface files her under a sneer, one of the "drunken gossips" with whom the lovers improperly took counsel, a stock figure of mid-Tudor moralizing, the old woman as corruption's courier. The poem then employs her for three thousand lines as the lovers' indispensable minister, and the employment keeps refuting the file. Brooke's Nurse is the marriage's civil service. She carries the first messages, brokers the meetings, knows the Friar's hours and the household's rhythms, manages the secret's logistics across three months of nightly visits, and sustains, on her own credit with both parties, an institution that exists nowhere in law. The poem cannot do without her competence, and its narrator, whatever the preface instructed, keeps recording that competence with something close to gratitude.

Brooke gives her a past as well as a function. Like her descendant in the play, she is a woman whose own losses stand behind her service to another woman's child, and the poem's brief notices of her history do the same work as the play's great Act 1 reminiscence: they make the go-between a person whose loyalty has a biography. Her counsel, when she gives it, is the counsel of experience inside a system she understands better than any principal in the poem. She knows what households forgive and what they never forgive, what a father's anger costs and how long it lasts, and her advice across the poem tracks that knowledge rather than any abstract morality. The preface calls her a gossip because the preface needs a villain of counsel. The narrative knows she is the only adult in Verona whose counsel is based on evidence.

Her ending is therefore the poem's most pointed act of judgment. When the discovery comes, the Nurse is banished, sentenced for the office she performed, while the Friar who devised the stratagems retires to a hermitage and the fathers who made the secret necessary reconcile over the tomb. Brooke's closing ledger prices her service as crime and the Friar's as error, a distribution that says more about the poem's hierarchies, of gender, of station, of cloth, than about the conduct it punishes. The play inherits the figure and softens the sentence into silence: its Nurse is simply left, unheard, in a house of the dead, her last counsel refused. Between Brooke's exile and the drama's abandonment lies a whole history of what each age found unforgivable in the woman who carried the messages.

## Brooke's Slow Catastrophe: Exile, Pressure, and the Failed Letter

The poem's second half repays attention as a study in how catastrophe behaves when it is not in a hurry. Brooke's machinery of ruin is administrative. A sentence of banishment, lawfully pronounced and lawfully limited, separates the spouses. A family's grief for Tybalt seeks settlement in a marriage contract. A friar devises a pharmaceutical solution and communicates it by the ordinary postal channel of his order. A public health precaution detains the post. Each step is reasonable; several are kind; the sum is annihilation. Where the play concentrates its disasters into hours so that accident and intention blur, Brooke spaces his across weeks, and the spacing changes the moral physics: in the poem, there was time for every disaster to be averted, messengers to be doubled, parents to relent, friars to travel in person, and the horror is that the ordinary world, proceeding at its ordinary pace, simply failed to avert any of them.

The failed letter is the catastrophe in miniature, and Brooke's handling of it is a small masterpiece of causal honesty. His messenger is not robbed, bewitched, or murdered. He goes to Mantua on his order's business, lodges with brethren who fall under suspicion of plague, and is shut in with them by the health authorities until the suspicion clears, the letter in his keeping the whole time, a few streets from its destination. Brooke underlines no irony and preaches no sermon over the episode; he simply lets a reasonable quarantine do what in the play requires a soliloquy's worth of fate. The episode also reveals the poem's trust in systems: friars carry letters because friars always carry letters, and when the system pauses, no one in Brooke's world thinks to improvise another. The play keeps the mechanism almost exactly, a measure of how completely its author trusted Brooke's causal design at the story's most fragile joint.

Pressure, in the poem, is likewise a matter of calendars rather than confrontations. The Paris marriage advances by negotiation and announcement; Juliet's resistance is a campaign of delays fought with the weapons of a dutiful daughter, illness, tears, and pious pretexts, until the Friar's powder offers the one delay that cannot be argued with. Brooke's Capulet is less a tyrant than an executor, settling his house after a bereavement, and the poem's tragedy of the marriage market is correspondingly colder than the play's: no single speech of rage detonates it, only the steady paperwork of a family arranging its future over a daughter who is already a wife. Readers formed by the drama sometimes find this second half slack. It is better described as forensic. Brooke is building the record from which his closing judgments will be read, and the record's completeness, every kindness and every precaution entered in evidence, is what makes the verdict at the tomb feel less like fate than like an audit the whole city failed.

## Brooke's Verona: Law, Medicine, and the Machinery of a City

A quality of Brooke's poem that parallel-hunting misses is its civic realism. Brooke's Verona runs on institutions, and the poem keeps showing them at work: a prince whose authority is legal before it is personal, a watch that patrols and reports, physicians and friars who practise a recognizable natural medicine, apothecaries who stock poisons under commercial rather than magical logic, health officers who can shut a suspected house against the world. The tragedy in Brooke is not visited on a fairy-tale city of lovers and fathers. It is processed, at every stage, by the ordinary machinery of a governed place, and the machinery's smooth functioning is part of the horror. Nothing in the poem's Verona is broken. The law sentences correctly, the medicine works exactly as promised, the quarantine is prudently imposed, the funeral is properly conducted. The catastrophe is what the machinery produces when two of its citizens route their private lives around it.

Escalus embodies the pattern. Brooke's prince is a judge more than a presence: he weighs the affray's circumstances, distinguishes the provoked killing from murder, fixes exile as the measured sentence, and at the end presides over a distribution of penalties and pardons that reads like a court's ledger. The poem's closing judgments, Nurse banished, apothecary hanged, Friar retired, houses reconciled under supervision, are the acts of this judicial imagination, and they give Brooke's ending its distinctive flavour of settlement rather than catharsis. The play keeps the prince and the ledger, "Some shall be pardoned, and some punished" (5.3.308), but surrounds the accounting with gold statues and a father's rhetoric, gilding a frame that Brooke leaves as plain oak.

The poem's medicine deserves the same attention. Brooke's Friar is credible to his century because his art is the century's art: simples gathered at the right hours, powders compounded, effects predicted from the known virtues of plants, a sleeping draught whose operation is described with a practitioner's confidence in dose and duration. There is no enchantment in the poem's potion and no devil in its pharmacy; there is technique, and technique's characteristic risk, that a preparation can be perfect and its circumstances fatal. The Mantuan apothecary completes the picture from below: a poor man in a poor shop, for whom the sale of poison is an economic decision taken under the pressure of want, and Brooke, like the drama after him, pauses over the man's poverty long enough to make the transaction a judgment on the city that keeps such shops necessary.

Even the plague machinery, on which the whole plot pivots, is drawn from administrative life. Cities of Brooke's Europe lived with pestilence as a recurring emergency, and the detention of travellers from suspected houses was routine governance, not exotic misfortune. Brooke's messenger is stopped by the most ordinary force in his world, public health, and the poem's refusal to gild the stoppage with storm or sorcery is a kind of realism the drama respected enough to copy almost unaltered. Taken together, these systems explain the poem's peculiar tone, less a romance than a report: the sense, unique to Brooke among the tale's early tellers, that the lovers died not in a story but in a jurisdiction.

The watch deserves a sentence of its own in this inventory, because Brooke uses it the way a court uses a clock. His Verona is patrolled, and news in the poem travels at the speed of its institutions: the affray is reported, the sentence promulgated, the funeral published, the tomb discovered by officers making their rounds. Privacy, in such a city, is a temporary failure of administration, and the lovers' secret survives exactly as long as the machinery fails to notice it. The poem's deepest realism is this sense of a watched world, in which a marriage can be hidden for three months but not forever, because forever is not a category the machinery recognizes.

## The Tomb in Brooke: The Long Farewell

Brooke's tomb sequence is his poem's summit, and it is built on a principle the play would reverse: duration. Everything in the vault takes the time it would take. Romeus travels from Mantua with the news unconfirmed and the poison bought, forces the Capel monument where Juliet lies among her ancestors in the place prepared for her house, and finds the funeral already cold, the torches, the inscriptions, the new corpse of Tybalt in the family's earth. Brooke inventories the vault the way his poem has inventoried every ceremony, because the inventory is the dread: a husband reading the architecture of the tomb he is about to join, in a poem that has never once hurried a ritual. His lament over the body is the longest single speech the narrator allows, an oration with topics, Fortune arraigned, the stars arraigned, his own haste arraigned, the poison praised as the one faithful counsellor left to him, and it ends, as orations in Brooke do, in action prepared by rhetoric: he drinks.

Then Brooke performs the timing decision on which the whole source relation turns. Juliet's three days expire inside her husband's dying. She wakes to a living man, poisoned, lucid, and able to explain, and the poem grants the pair the scene the entire story has been promising and withholding in every version since: a conversation in the tomb, conducted with full knowledge, between the two people the machinery was built to separate. She reproaches the fortune of the letter; he recounts the report that killed him, the servant's news, the apothecary, the ride; each absolves the other; the farewell is negotiated like their marriage was, in argued exchanges, until the poison finishes its work in the middle of the settlement and he dies with the account nearly closed. Nothing in the poem is more Brookean than that "nearly": even at the summit, his lovers get duration without completion, time to speak and not time enough.

Juliet's death follows with the poem's characteristic deliberation. She examines the means at hand as she once examined the Friar's stratagem, finds no poison remaining, and takes Romeus's rapier, the weapon that killed Tybalt and opened the catastrophe, as the instrument that closes it. Brooke narrates the act without flinching and without haste, and then holds the scene open for its witnesses: the Friar arriving with the watch, the discovery, the bodies removed, the city summoned to judgment. The drama's version of this sequence, compressed into a single scene of mounting horror, gains its celebrated terror by subtraction, one voice silenced, then the other, the explanations hoarded by a friar who arrives to speak them over corpses. Brooke's version gains a different power by addition, and the comparison should be stated fairly: his tomb is not a failed draft of the play's. It is a completed alternative, an elegy where the drama builds a trap, and the two endings measure each other the way the two poems of the lovers' story, narrative and drama, have measured each other ever since.

Two rows deserve a second look, because they mark the extremes of the relation. The marriage row shows the debt at its most interpretive: Brooke's three months of lived union are the poem's central affirmation, the thing his whole slow structure exists to grant, and the play's refusal of those months, its substitution of a single parting at dawn for a season of nights, is the tragedy's central deprivation, the thing its whole fast structure exists to enforce. Between the row where nothing was changed and the row where everything was changed lies the complete map of the source relation: a plot inherited entire, and a meaning rebuilt by deciding how much time love would be allowed.

## A Protestant Poem in a Catholic City: Brooke's Religious Texture

The strangest fact about Brooke's poem, held up against its preface, is how Catholic its world remains. The preface prosecutes friars and confession in the idiom of the new settlement; the poem then builds its entire machinery out of the old religion's offices. Its lovers are shriven by a friar, married by a friar in a cell, counselled through confession, rescued by a friar's pharmacy, and finally explained to their prince by that friar's public confession of the whole affair. Remove the sacramental infrastructure and no plot remains. Brooke's Verona is a city where the Church's personnel are the only competent adults, and the poem depends on their competence at every joint, even while its front door carries a notice warning readers against the profession.

The tension is not a blunder; it is the record of a translation between worlds. Boaistuau's France and Bandello's Italy supplied a story whose institutions were unselfconsciously Catholic, and Brooke, Englished and Protestant, could neither replace those institutions nor endorse them, so he did both things at once: condemned them in the frame, relied on them in the tale. The result is a poem whose theology is split exactly where its morality is split, and the split is historically eloquent. Brooke's first readers lived inside the same arrangement, attending a reformed church in a landscape still furnished with friaries in living memory, and the poem's double dealing with its friar, superstitious instrument in the preface, indispensable pastor in the verse, mirrors their own negotiated settlement with the past.

The Friar's confession at the end concentrates the irony. The preface had named auricular confession "the key of whoredom and treason"; the poem closes by having its friar confess, fully and publicly, and that confession is the instrument by which truth enters the city, sentences are measured, and the houses are reconciled. Brooke's plot acquits what Brooke's preface arraigns. The play inherits the acquittal and drops the arraignment almost entire, keeping a Friar whose failings are of nerve and timing rather than of doctrine, and replacing the confessional machinery with a final narrative delivered, fittingly, as testimony. What began as a Protestant translator's embarrassment becomes, across the source relation, one of the tragedy's enabling mercies: a world in which someone can still tell the whole truth at the end, and be believed.

## Brooke's Narrator: Fortune, Blame, and the Voice Between Reader and Story

Half of Brooke's poem is its narrator, and the narrator is the poem's most original possession. Boaistuau gave Brooke a story; the voice that keeps interrupting it, apostrophizing Fortune, lecturing the lovers, pitying them, and hauling every event back to the wheel, is Brooke's own instrument. The poem opens under Fortune's governance and never escapes it. Events in Brooke arrive doubled, caused at once by the wheel's turning and by the choices of the people the wheel crushes, and the narrator holds both causal systems open simultaneously, blaming the lovers' haste in one passage and lamenting Fortune's malice in the next, without ever feeling obliged to reconcile the two accounts.

This doubleness is the poem's deepest bequest to the play. The Prologue's "star-crossed" lovers and "death-marked" love translate Brooke's wheel into astrology, while the drama's tissue of rash decisions, missed timings, and refused counsels preserves Brooke's other system, the moral one, in dramatic form. Readers who argue whether the play is a tragedy of fate or of character are arguing inside a fork that Brooke's narration built. His poem is already both, uncomfortably, on every page: the preface insists the lovers chose their ruin, and the verse insists the ruin was written in the turning of the world. The play did not resolve Brooke's contradiction. It dramatized it so successfully that four centuries of criticism have mistaken the contradiction for Shakespeare's own invention.

The narrator's second function is intimacy management. Brooke's voice mediates between the reader and a story whose pleasures it officially distrusts, apologizing, glossing, and moralizing at the thresholds, then withdrawing for pages at a time while the lovers speak for themselves in speeches of real rhetorical power. Juliet in particular benefits from her narrator's absences. Brooke's Juliet argues, schemes, reproaches Fortune, and faces the tomb with a deliberation the preface's charge sheet never predicts, and the poem is at its best when its telling forgets its sermon. That forgetting, repeated across three thousand lines, is precisely the crack through which the play entered: a story whose official moral and whose actual sympathies pull in opposite directions is a story asking to be rewritten by someone willing to let the sympathies win.

The narrator's instrument, at the level of the line, is the sententia, the proverb-shaped sentence that poulter's measure invites and that Brooke distributes like milestones. His verse is salted with generalizations on youth, haste, counsel, women, fortune, and the governance of appetite, each one plausible, each one immediately complicated by the narrative it interrupts. This habit, too, is an inheritance. The play's most proverbial voice is the Friar's, and his couplets of counsel, "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast" (2.3.94) among them, are Brooke's narrative sententiae reassigned from a narrator to a character, given a cell, a stillroom, and a plot to ruin. What the poem spreads across its telling voice, the drama concentrates in its counsellor, and the concentration is fateful: wisdom that in Brooke merely comments on the action becomes, in the Friar's mouth, an agent inside it, dispensing the very devices the proverbs warn against. The migration of the sententiae from narrator to Friar is one of the quietest and most consequential transfers in the whole source relation.

## How Close Does the Play Stay to Brooke?

The closeness is, by the standards of Renaissance source use, extraordinary. Geoffrey Bullough, printing the poem as the play's principal source in his *Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare*, observed that the drama follows Brooke's narrative order with minimal rearrangement, episode answering episode from the feast to the vault, and the observation has survived every subsequent audit. Shakespeare, who elsewhere plundered chronicles and novellas with cheerful structural violence, here behaves almost like an adapter under contract: the sequence is kept, the cast is kept, the settings are kept, and even the proportions of the middle, courtship, marriage, catastrophe, map onto the poem's proportions once the time scale is compressed.

Verbal debt runs underneath the structural debt. Brooke's lovers speak in set speeches whose arguments the play inherits: the window wooing, in which Romeus pleads for grace and Juliet weighs danger, reputation, and desire, expands into the orchard dialogue whose heart, "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks? / It is the east, and Juliet is the sun" (2.2.2-3), dresses Brooke's moonlit petitioner in imagery the poem only sketches. Brooke's Friar counsels patience and mean-keeping in sententiae the play will sharpen into "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6.9). Brooke narrates Juliet's terrors before the potion, her fear of waking among the bones of her ancestors with Tybalt's corpse beside her, and the play unfolds those narrated fears into the great soliloquy that begins "What if this mixture do not work at all?" (4.3.21). Passage by passage, the play reads as Brooke read aloud by genius: the same matter, at higher pressure.

The proportions tell the same story as the sequence. Brooke's poem divides its length into rough thirds: courtship and marriage; the married life and its destruction; exile, stratagem, and tomb. The play, for all its compression, preserves the triptych and even its hinges, placing its own centre of gravity in a wedding the audience never sees celebrated and letting its second half run downhill through the same stations, sentence, suit, potion, letter, vault, that Brooke's second half travels. Adaptation at this level of structural fidelity is rare in the period, when dramatists treated prose tales and chronicles as quarries for striking scenes rather than as architectures to inhabit. Shakespeare's procedure with Brooke is closer to translation between media than to plunder: the poem is carried across into drama the way Brooke carried Boaistuau across into verse, with losses and gains at every border and the design intact. That is why the source relation has proved so durable a subject. It is the cleanest case in the canon of a great play with a legible parent, and the parent, read whole, keeps explaining the child.

The departures are real, and they are significant, and they belong by design to the companion study: the full inventory of invention, the raising of Mercutio from a cold-handed courtier to the tragedy's hinge, the compression of months into days, the wedding night cut to a single parting, Paris sent to die in the vault, is set out in [what Shakespeare changed from Brooke, the companion source study](/2025/09/26/what-shakespeare-changed-from-brooke/). What belongs here is the proportion. The departures are renovations inside a standing house. The play's most famous strokes of construction, the tomb's timing above all, achieve their effects by tightening Brooke's arrangements, not by replacing them: Brooke already wakes Juliet while her lover dies, and the play's colder arrangement, "Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3.120) spoken to a wife who cannot hear it, is a revision of a scene Brooke composed, not a scene invented from nothing.

## Speeches in Embryo: Brooke's Orations and Their Afterlives

The closeness is easiest to feel at the level of the individual speech, where Brooke's poem repeatedly behaves like a draft of the drama written in the wrong metre. Brooke's lovers, formed by Boaistuau's French rhetoric, do not converse so much as orate in alternation, and those orations are the play's scenes in embryo. The window wooing is the type case. In the poem, Romeus below the Capulet windows delivers a formal plea, rehearsing his service, his suffering, and his suit in ordered periods, and Juliet answers in kind, her reply weighing the danger of the place, the dishonour of a stolen contract, and the condition on which favour may follow, that marriage come first. Every element of the play's orchard negotiation is present in that exchange: the risk, the oath problem, the demand for marriage as earnest, the parting protracted by reluctance. The drama's contribution is to break the two orations into dialogue, letting plea and answer interrupt, overlap, and correct each other in real time, and to hang on the exchange the imagery, moon, stars, nightingale, that Brooke's couplets mention only in passing.

The Friar's speeches undergo the same transformation. Brooke's Laurence discourses at length: on his art, on the powder's operation, on the moral management of haste, in the measured periods of a man accustomed to the confessional's authority. The play disperses that single discursive voice across scenes and functions, soliloquy in the stillroom, counsel in the cell, rebuke, consolation, and final narration, but the voice's habits remain Brooke's: definition first, proverb second, application last. Even the poem's narrative descriptions keep turning into the drama's stage poetry. Brooke narrates Juliet's fears on the potion's eve, the dread of waking in the vault among the bones and the new corpse of Tybalt, the fear of madness among the dead, and the play takes that narrated catalogue and gives it to Juliet herself as the soliloquy of Act 4 Scene 3, converting the narrator's summary into a mind auditing its own terror item by item. "What if this mixture do not work at all?" is Brooke's paragraph learned by heart and spoken in the first person.

Romeus's tomb lament is the last and fullest embryo. Brooke gives his hero a long formal threnody over the body he believes dead, structured as accusation, against Fortune, against himself, against the stars that governed the marriage, before the poison closes the speech. The play preserves the structure and spends it in a fraction of the lines, because the drama has taught its audience to hear in minutes what the poem teaches in pages. Reading the two laments in sequence is the single most instructive exercise the source relation offers: the same grief, the same topics in the same order, the same destination, at two temperatures. Brooke's oration is a river in winter, broad and slow and freezing at the edges. The play's is the same river in spate. Critics who call the poem a quarry are right about the stone and wrong about the building: the speeches stand in Brooke already shaped, and what the drama adds is weather.

The Nurse's speeches mark the opposite pole of the same process, expansion in reverse. Brooke's Nurse speaks in the poem's plainest register, short on oratory and long on errands, her eloquence a matter of timing and nerve rather than periods. The drama takes that plainness and builds from it the great comic arias of the later acts' first halves, the reminiscence, the breathless report, the counsel of despair, as if the playwright had heard in Brooke's functional go-between a voice waiting for its music. Between the Friar's compressed orations and the Nurse's expanded bustle, the whole economy of the adaptation stands revealed: what narrative frames, drama redistributes, giving the poem's commentary to its counsellors and its silences to its servants, until every line of Brooke's three thousand has found either a voice or a grave in the play.

## What the Critics Argue About Brooke

Geoffrey Bullough's account remains the foundation. In the first volume of *Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare* (1957), Bullough printed Brooke's poem entire, set it beside the play, and concluded that it was the principal source in the strong sense: the text Shakespeare worked from, followed in sequence, echoed in phrase, and departed from chiefly by compression and intensification. J. J. Munro's earlier edition of the poem for the Shakespeare Library (1908) had already made the modern text available and argued the poem's centrality; Bullough consolidated the case with a completeness that made it the default position of source scholarship.

The disagreement that matters concerns whether Brooke alone is enough. Jill L. Levenson, editing the play for the Oxford Shakespeare, judged that Shakespeare also knew William Painter's prose version of the tale in *The Palace of Pleasure*, and that a small residue of details sits closer to Painter's telling than to Brooke's couplets. On her account the debt is Brooke-led but not Brooke-only: the playwright worked chiefly from the poem while drawing occasional touches from the prose retelling published a few years after it. René Weis, in the Arden Third Series edition, treats Brooke as the narrative bedrock while giving the preface's lost stage play its due, a reminder that the transmission was never a simple line from one book to one stage.

Adjudicated, the dispute resolves in Bullough's favour with Levenson's caution absorbed. The sequence-level fidelity, the inheritance of Brooke's distinctive arrangements, the married months, the three-day sleep, the plague-detained messenger, the dying conversation in the vault, are agreements with Brooke specifically, several of them absent or differently shaped in Painter, and they are too many and too structural to be coincidence or common tradition. Painter explains, at most, a residue. The working text on the playwright's desk, in whatever form memory held it, was Brooke's poem.

A second disagreement runs underneath the first, about the poem's own worth. An older critical habit treated Brooke as a plodding versifier whose couplets matter only because genius later quarried them. Bullough's own treatment, though source-focused, is more respectful, and later source scholarship has followed him in reading the poem as evidence with interpretive weight: its choices, the consummated marriage, the months of union, the Fortune frame, shape what the story could mean to its first theatrical audiences, who knew the tale from Brooke and measured the play against him. The adjudication here is equally clear. The couplets are uneven, the moralizing is intrusive, and the narrative design is nonetheless serious, coherent, and in its central arrangement, the lovers granted a marriage before they are granted a tomb, quietly daring. Scaffold is the right metaphor for Brooke only if a scaffold is understood as part of the architecture: it determines where the building can stand.

The two disagreements connect, and their connection is the real state of the question. If Brooke were a negligible poem, the fidelity of the play to it would be a curiosity, genius condescending to a crib. Because the poem is a serious narrative with its own argued reading of the tale, the fidelity becomes an interpretation in its own right: the play is, among other things, a sustained critical response to Brooke, agreeing with his architecture, disputing his tempo, dissolving his sermon into drama, and letting his lovers testify where his preface had prosecuted them. Editors who print the play with Brooke in the introduction, as both Levenson and Weis do, are acknowledging that response as part of the text's meaning. The source is not behind the play like a foundation under a house. It is beside the play like an elder sibling, plainer, slower, more censorious, and unmistakably of the same family.

## What Brooke's Medium Could Not Give

A fair account of the debt also fixes its boundary, and the boundary is drawn by Brooke's medium rather than by his talent. Narrative verse in poulter's measure, however accomplished, works under three incapacities that no amount of skill within the form can repeal. It cannot do simultaneity: the poem's people must speak in turn, at length, framed by a narrator, so the electricity of interruption, of two minds striking sparks in a single shared line, is unavailable to it. It cannot do embodiment: Mercutio in Brooke is a hand's temperature in a dance because narrative can spare him a sentence, where a stage must give him a body, a voice, and therefore, fatally, a destiny. And it cannot do the unspoken: Brooke's narrator must say what his people mean, gloss their motives, and price their choices, where a performed scene can let a pause, a costume, or a corpse carry the meaning past language altogether.

Naming these incapacities matters because they locate the play's originality with precision. The drama's genius, in this source relation, is concentrated exactly where the poem's medium is blind: in dialogue that thinks faster than narration can report, in minor figures promoted by the physics of casting, in meanings entrusted to silence. Shakespeare did not out-narrate Brooke. He changed the instrument, and the change of instrument is why the same story, in the same order, with the same people, produces a different tragedy. The poem's defenders do Brooke no service by claiming for his couplets the virtues of a stage they never sought. His virtues are the narrative ones, duration, judgment, ceremony, the long view, and they are the virtues the play traded away for speed. The trade was worth making. The poem is the record of its price.

## The Poem in Print: From Tottell's Quarto to Bullough's Source Volume

Brooke's poem had two Tudor lives and a long scholarly afterlife, and the shape of that history explains how completely it could disappear into its own offspring. The 1562 quarto from Tottell's press addressed the general market for verse narrative; the 1587 reprint, appearing a quarter-century later, shows the poem holding that market into the very years when the London stage was learning what to do with it. A reader of the play's first audiences who wanted the story at length, in English, in a form a household could own, had exactly one place to go, and the poem's presence in that culture is the background against which the drama's fidelity makes sense: the play did not need to explain its plot, compress its exposition, or apologize for its marvels, because Brooke had already naturalized the whole tale for the audience the play inherited.

Then the poem suffered the fate of successful sources: it was absorbed. Once the drama became the canonical form of the story, the couplets were read, when read at all, as the play's quarry, and centuries of editors mined them for parallels while the poem as a poem, with its married middle, its forensic second half, and its double-voiced narrator, went largely unread on its own terms. The recovery was scholarly and slow. J. J. Munro's edition of 1908 restored a reliable text and argued the poem's claims in its own introduction. Bullough's *Narrative and Dramatic Sources* in 1957 fixed the poem's status institutionally, printing it entire as the principal source and making its study a standard part of the play's scholarship. Source study since Bullough, including the editorial work of Levenson and Weis in their editions of the play, treats the poem as primary evidence: not a curiosity behind the tragedy but a document inside its meaning, recording what the story was before the drama decided what it could become.

That evidentiary status cuts both ways, and the poem is the better for it. Read as a source, Brooke explains the play. Read as a poem, Brooke corrects the play's admirers, who mistake compression for inevitability and the drama's inventions for the story's essence. The married months, the waking farewell, the banished Nurse, the hanged apothecary, the hermit Friar: these are not errors the play corrected but choices the play declined, and a story culture that knows both versions possesses something richer than either, a controlled experiment in what tempo, sympathy, and moral framing do to identical events. The poem in print, from Tottell's quarto to Bullough's parallel columns, is the laboratory record of that experiment.

## The Verdict: What Brooke Gave the Play

Brooke gave the play its plot entire, in order, with its beginning, middle, and end already joined. He gave it the cast entire: the feuding houses, the Prince, the lovers, the Nurse, the Friar, Tybalt, Paris, Mercutio in embryo, the apothecary in his poverty. He gave it the double causation, Fortune and folly, wheel and will, that the criticism of the play has debated ever since as if the debate began in 1595 rather than in 1562. He gave it speeches in germ, fears in narration waiting to become soliloquies, and a Friar whose sententiae needed only a stage to become proverbial. And he gave it the moral problem in its sharpest form, a preface that condemns what a poem cannot help loving, because the play is that same division of voice with the preface dissolved into a Chorus and the love allowed, at last, to testify in its own defence.

The series thesis holds: the cliché forgets Brooke, and restoring him shows the scaffold Shakespeare built upon. But the scaffold metaphor, pressed, understates the gift. Scaffolds come down. Brooke never came down. His poem is inside the finished building, beam after beam, visible wherever the plaster of the play's verse is thin: in the plot's bones, in the cast's roster, in the Friar's proverbs, in the vault's terrible timing. To read Brooke after the play is to discover that the most famous love story in English is also one of its most faithful adaptations, and that the fidelity, not the invention, is where this particular genius chose to work.

The final measure of the gift is pedagogical as much as literary. Every question a reader brings to the play, why the story moves in this order, why a friar holds its machinery, why its lovers are married before they are doomed, why its ending distributes punishments like a court's, has a first answer in Brooke, and the answer is never trivial. Brooke explains the play the way a constitution explains a state: not by exhausting its life, but by supplying the arrangements inside which its life became possible. The poem deserves its readers on those terms, and on its own: a young man's single book, drowned with its author within a year of printing, that went on to carry the most durable love tragedy in the language on its couplets. Posterity kept the tragedy and misplaced the carrier. The carrier, recovered, turns out to have been carrying more than the plot. It was carrying the meaning's first draft.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch Brooke concordance, and what does it show about the play's debt?

The InsightCrunch Brooke concordance is the side-by-side table in this article aligning Brooke's plot points, in his order, with the corresponding arrangements in the play. Its evidence is architectural rather than verbal. Row after row shows the same sequence of load-bearing events, the feast, the window courtship, the secret marriage, the street killing, the banishment, the forced match, the potion, the failed letter, the tomb, differing mainly in timing and pressure: three months of marriage compressed into days, a three-day sleep shortened to forty-two hours, a waking farewell in the vault rewritten as soliloquy over a corpse. The concordance demonstrates that the play's debt to Brooke is a debt of design, the hardest kind to disguise and the kind only a true source supplies.

### Q: Who published Romeus and Juliet, and what kind of book did it first appear as?

Richard Tottell published the poem in London in 1562, and the entry in the Stationers' Register dates from the same year. The book was a standalone English verse narrative, a substantial quarto of narrative poetry running to some three thousand lines in rhymed couplets, offered to general readers rather than to scholars. Tottell was a major trade publisher of law books and of popular verse miscellanies, and the poem belonged to his list of vernacular entertainments with a moral warrant. Its format matters to its afterlife: a complete, purchasable, English telling of the Italian tale, priced and sized for the same reading public that filled the playhouses a generation later. The poem was reprinted in 1587, evidence that the first edition found its market.

### Q: How did Arthur Brooke die, and why does his early death matter to the poem's history?

Brooke drowned in 1563, barely a year after his poem appeared, when the ship carrying him to the English expedition at Le Havre was lost during the crossing. He was a young man, a member of the Inns of Court world that produced much early Elizabethan writing, and the poem was effectively his whole surviving monument. His early death matters for two reasons. First, it froze the poem as a single-authored, unrevised text: no later edition carries an older Brooke's second thoughts. Second, it severed the poem from its maker so completely that the work circulated as common property, a story in the air, available for a playwright born around the year of the drowning to absorb as raw material rather than as a rival's property.

### Q: What does Brooke's title page claim about where the story came from?

The title page announces *The Tragicall Historye of Romeus and Juliet* as "written first in Italian by Bandell and nowe in Englishe by Ar. Br." The claim is a half-truth arranged for prestige. Matteo Bandello did publish the Italian novella from which the tale ultimately descends, and naming him gave the English book a distinguished continental pedigree. But Brooke's actual working source was Pierre Boaistuau's French version of 1559, itself an enlargement of Bandello, and the title page silently skips the French intermediary. Brooke himself is reduced to initials, a translator's modesty that also understates his contribution, since the preface, the narrator's commentary, and the English moral frame are his own additions to whatever his sources gave him.

### Q: Whose French version did Brooke translate, and what did he take from it besides the plot?

Brooke translated Pierre Boaistuau's retelling in the *Histoires Tragiques* of 1559, the first of Boaistuau's tragic histories Englished in the period. From Boaistuau he took far more than the plot. Boaistuau had already expanded Bandello's compact novella with long rhetorical speeches for the lovers, heightened the role of Fortune in the narration, and wrapped the tale in moral reflection, and Brooke inherited all three enlargements along with the events. The chain explains the poem's distinctive doubleness: an Italian story, given French rhetoric and a French taste for sententious expansion, then given an English Protestant preface and an English narrator's voice. When scholars compare the play with Brooke, they are comparing it with a tale already twice reshaped before Brooke's couplets received it.

### Q: What is poulter's measure, and why does Brooke's line keep changing length?

Poulter's measure is the couplet form in which a line of twelve syllables is followed by a line of fourteen, the pair rhyming, repeated for the whole length of a poem. Tudor commentators derived the name from the poulterer's practice of counting poultry by the dozen with extra birds allowed at intervals, and the form behaves like its name: a standard measure, then a longer measure that overshoots it. Brooke sustains the alternation for some three thousand lines. The effect is a loping, processional gait, heavily end-stopped, in which rhymes click shut every second line and long sentences unroll in balanced clauses. It is a metre for pageant, ceremony, and lament, and its patience suits a story Brooke allows to ripen across months.

### Q: How does Brooke's sixteen-year-old Juliet suit the moral his poem wants to teach?

Brooke's Juliet is sixteen, and the age is calibrated to his didactic purpose. She is old enough, in the poem's terms, to be marriageable and therefore fully responsible for choosing a husband against her parents' authority, which is the disobedience the preface prosecutes. She is also young enough for her passion to read as the error of inexperience rather than the settled wickedness of an adult, which keeps her pitiable inside a frame that condemns her. The age does double duty throughout the poem: it sharpens the charge of filial rebellion, since a daughter of sixteen owes obedience in Brooke's moral economy, and it softens the sentence, since the narrator can grieve for a girl ruined by her first exercise of a will she was too young to govern.

### Q: How much time passes inside Brooke's poem between the wedding and the tomb?

Months pass, and the poem insists on the duration. Brooke's lovers are married in secret and then live as husband and wife for the space of three months, meeting by night under the Nurse's management, before the street quarrel kills Tybalt and the sentence of banishment ends their domestic arrangement. Further stretches follow: the exile in Mantua, the pressure of the Paris match, and a potion whose sleep alone lasts three days. The whole action breathes at a seasonal pace in every way unlike the play's five days of crisis. The span is one of the poem's defining choices, because it converts the marriage from an episode into a life. When catastrophe comes in Brooke, it destroys something that has existed, at length, and the elegy is earned by the calendar.

### Q: What happens to Mercutio in Brooke's poem, and where does the poem leave him?

Almost nothing happens to Mercutio in Brooke's poem, and the almost is the point. He appears at Capulet's feast as a young courtier among the revellers, and Brooke gives him exactly one memorable property: his hand, taken by Juliet in the courtesies of the evening, is as cold as ice, a detail she remarks on in the poem's passing comedy of the dance. He has no Queen Mab speech, no quarrel with Tybalt, no death, and no curse. The poem leaves him at the feast, alive and unimportant, a gallant in the background of the meeting he will one day dominate in another work. No single comparison measures the play's inventiveness against its source more cleanly than the distance between Brooke's cold-handed dancer and the figure who dies cursing both houses.

### Q: Does Brooke's narrator blame Fortune or the lovers for the catastrophe?

He blames both, alternately, and never reconciles the two verdicts. The preface and the moralizing passages assign the ruin to choice: the lovers disobeyed parents, trusted a friar and a gossip, and pursued dishonest desire, and their deaths are the wages of those decisions. The narrative frame assigns the ruin to Fortune, whose wheel the poem invokes from its opening and whose malice the narrator laments over each reverse, as if the lovers were victims of a cosmic mechanism no prudence could have escaped. Brooke holds the systems in suspension for three thousand lines. The doubleness is not carelessness. It is the poem's engine, and the play inherits it entire, translating the wheel into stars while preserving the tissue of rash, blameworthy choices underneath.

### Q: What punishment does Brooke give the Nurse at the end of the poem?

Brooke banishes her from Verona. When the Friar confesses the whole history before the Prince, the Nurse's part in the machinery, carrying messages, managing the night visits, concealing a marriage from the parents she served, is exposed, and the poem's closing judgments assign her exile as the penalty for her office as go-between. The punishment is precisely fitted to Brooke's moral scheme. In the preface she is one of the "drunken gossips" with whom the lovers improperly conferred their counsels, and the ending converts that polemic into law: the counsellor is expelled from the city she corrupted. Her sentence also completes the poem's pattern of graded retribution, in which each accessory is weighed and priced according to the moral the narrator has been keeping in escrow since the preface.

### Q: Where does Brooke send his Friar Laurence once the truth is known?

To a hermitage. Brooke's Friar, arrested in the discovery's wake, makes a full confession before the Prince, setting out the marriage, the potion, and the failed letter, and the poem judges his intentions more leniently than his office. He is permitted to withdraw from Verona to a hermit cell a short distance from the city, where he spends the remainder of his life in penitential retirement and eventually dies. The sentence is a characteristic Brooke compromise between the preface and the poem: the preface's "superstitious friar" would deserve prison or worse, but the narrative's learned and well-meaning counsellor earns exile of a gentler, monastic kind, removed from the city his counsel failed while spared the apothecary's rope and the Nurse's banishment.

### Q: Why does Brooke mention having seen the story acted on a stage?

Because he had, and the remark does double work in his preface. Brooke tells his reader that he had seen the same argument lately set forth on stage, and presented with more commendation than he could hope for his own version, a graceful act of modesty that advertises the poem by association with a theatrical success. For later scholarship the sentence is evidence of something larger: a play on the Veronese lovers existed in England before 1562, performed and applauded, and is now entirely lost. The poem thus sits between two stages, one behind it and one ahead. It records an earlier dramatization it cannot reproduce, and it furnishes the text from which the famous dramatization would be built a generation later, by a writer working from the page Brooke left.

### Q: What are the two feuding families called in Brooke's poem?

Brooke calls them the Capellets and the Montagues, names he adapted, through Boaistuau's French, from the Cappelletti and Montecchi of the Italian tradition reaching back to Luigi da Porto. The spellings are worth attention because they show translation as domestication in progress: the Italian houses are being naturalized into English mouths, their names trimmed toward forms an English reader could pronounce and an English actor could speak. Brooke also keeps the Italianate frame around them, Verona, Prince Escalus, Count Paris, a Mantuan exile, while the family names settle into the shapes the play would make permanent. By the time the story reached the stage in its famous form, Brooke's anglicized spellings had done their quiet work, and the houses sound less like imports than like neighbours.

### Q: What do Brooke's lovers say to each other in the tomb before they die?

They hold the conversation the play refuses them. Brooke times the potion so that Juliet wakes while Romeus is still alive and feeling the poison work, and the poem gives the pair a full dying exchange: she reproaches Fortune and demands to know why he did not wait or send word, he explains the failed message and his despair at the report of her death, and the two take a conscious farewell, arranging their deaths as deliberately as they once arranged their marriage. He dies in her arms; she takes his rapier and kills herself upon his body. The scene is the poem's emotional summit, and its existence reframes the famous version's cruelty. The play's tomb, in which Romeo speaks to a wife who cannot answer, is a revision of Brooke's dialogue into soliloquy, and the revision gains its horror precisely by withholding what Brooke grants.

### Q: How long does Brooke's sleeping powder keep Juliet in her deathlike sleep?

Three days. Brooke's Friar prepares a powder, to be taken in drink, whose effect is a sleep indistinguishable from death lasting three full days, and the scale is integral to the poem's design. Three days allow the entire public theatre of bereavement to complete itself: the discovery, the lamentation, the funeral, the burial in the Capel vault among her ancestors, and the city's mourning, all performed over a living woman. The longer sleep also suits the poem's slower calendar, in which messages travel at a walking pace and a friar can be detained by plague precautions without the plot's clock screaming. The play's forty-two hours, by contrast, turn the same device into a countdown, and the difference between three days and forty-two hours is the difference between Brooke's elegy and the drama's suspense.

### Q: What did Brooke himself add to the story he translated?

Brooke added the frame, the voice, and the English moral weather. His sources supplied events; Brooke supplied the prose preface with its prosecutorial reading of the lovers, the narrator who interrupts to apostrophize Fortune, gloss conduct, and draw morals, and the anglicizing of names, places, and sentiments for an Elizabethan Protestant readership. He also adjusted his heroine's age downward from his French source and expanded the lovers' married life into the poem's long middle, choices that alter the tale's meaning as surely as any added episode. The additions are easy to underrate because they are additions of commentary rather than incident, but they govern how every incident reads. A translator who writes the preface, staffs the narration, and sets the moral temperature of a story has done more than carry it across a border.

### Q: Why does Brooke's narrator keep addressing Fortune as if Fortune were a person?

Because the poem's cosmology requires an agent for everything choice cannot explain. Brooke's Fortune is the medieval wheel-turner in full personification, addressed, reproached, and pleaded with across the poem, and she absorbs the causality that the moral scheme cannot use: the detained messenger, the servant's fatal report, the timing of a waking. Apostrophizing her lets the narrator hold two truths at once, that the lovers acted badly and that the world conspired, without dissolving either into the other. The device also flatters the poem's sententious style, since Fortune's turns license proverbs the way weather licenses talk of rain. When the play translated the wheel into a pair of star-crossed lovers, it kept the personification's function while changing its machinery, and the habit of blaming the sky began with Brooke's addresses to his goddess.

### Q: Whom does Brooke's Romeus love before he ever sees Juliet?

A lady of Verona who disdains him, and Brooke gives that first attachment a substantial stretch of poem. His Romeus enters the story as a practised Petrarchan sufferer, sighing, sleepless, and fluent in the rhetoric of hopeless devotion, wasting for a mistress whose cruelty the poem treats as a fixed condition of his youth. The episode does structural work the play compresses into Rosaline's name. It establishes love as Romeus's native element before Juliet gives the element an object worthy of it, and it lets Brooke mark the difference between the two loves by kind: the first is a sickness of convention, performed in solitude, while the second, for all the preface's condemnation, is mutual, argued, and answered. Romeus goes to Capulet's feast half hoping to suffer in his mistress's sight. He leaves it cured of the wrong disease.

### Q: When was Brooke's poem reprinted, and how has the poem survived since?

The poem was reprinted in 1587, within a few years of the period in which the famous play was taking shape, which means the source and the drama circulated in the same London book world. After that edition the poem survived as scholars' property. J. J. Munro edited it for the Shakespeare Library in 1908, giving modern readers a reliable text, and Geoffrey Bullough printed it entire in the first volume of *Narrative and Dramatic Sources of Shakespeare* in 1957, where it stands as the play's principal source with the parallels set out. Its survival has therefore been double: as a Tudor poem in its own right, uneven, sententious, and intermittently powerful, and as the indispensable document of the play's making, the one book without which the architecture of Romeo and Juliet cannot be explained.
