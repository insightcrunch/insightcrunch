The operative question answered on July 1, 2020 was precise in form and large in consequence. What legal instrument replaced the North American Free Trade Agreement for the United States, and on what terms did the replacement take the place of the older arrangement? The answer was not a treaty in the constitutional sense, and it was not a mere executive announcement that an existing bargain had been revised. The replacement rested on a statute, enacted through the ordinary legislative process, that approved a negotiated text, approved a later amendment to that text, and made the changes in domestic law that the revised bargain required. As an act of implementation, it translated that commitment into rules that federal agencies, courts, importers, exporters, workers, and firms could apply within the legal system of the United States. The terms of replacement were the terms written into the approved text as amended, no more and no less, and the path to enactment left marks on the substance of the bargain itself.

The formal identity of the measure can be stated in one clear pass. The statute was the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement Implementation Act, Public Law 116-113, codified in the Statutes at Large at 134 Stat. 11. It began in the House of Representatives as H.R. 5430, with a Senate companion designated S. 3052, and it belonged to the 116th Congress. President Donald Trump signed the measure on January 29, 2020. The act approved the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement, which the three governments had signed at Buenos Aires, Argentina, on November 30, 2018, as amended by the Protocol of Amendment signed on December 10, 2019. The pact entered into force on July 1, 2020, replacing NAFTA. That sequence, signature in 2018, amendment in 2019, approval by statute in 2020, and entry into force in the middle of 2020, contains the central institutional story of the legislation. A signed international text did not pass into domestic law untouched. Between signature and approval, the text changed, and the change occurred because legislators used the leverage that the governing procedure, properly understood, had left in their hands.

The namable claim belongs near the front of any account of the statute. The legislature amended a signed agreement. Trade promotion authority, the procedure under which the renegotiation proceeded, is often described as a device that removes congressional influence from trade bargaining, because it promises an expedited vote and denies legislators the chance to amend an implementing bill on the floor. The USMCA episode showed the opposite possibility inside the same design. Influence did not disappear. It moved to an earlier and less visible point in the process, to the interval between the conclusion of negotiations and the introduction of an implementing bill, when the executive branch still needed a majority willing to take the measure up and pass it. During that interval, the House Democratic majority declined to schedule the bill until enforcement concerns had been addressed, and the executive branch returned to the other two governments to secure changes in a formal protocol. The protocol altered obligations on labor and environmental enforcement, added a facility specific rapid response labor mechanism that had not appeared in the signed text, repaired the formation of dispute settlement panels, and removed commitments on data protection for biologic medicines.

That reading explains why the procedure cannot be reduced to a slogan about speed. The rules that governed the renegotiation combined executive leadership in negotiation with congressional control over objectives, consultation, and final approval, and [the procedure and its limits in the history of trade promotion authority](/2020/08/15/trade-promotion-authority-explained/) supply the longer background against which the events of 2017 to 2020 took their shape. The executive branch negotiated with foreign governments, set the diplomatic calendar, and decided when a text was ready for signature. Congress, acting through the statute that authorized the fast track treatment, had already defined the objectives that negotiators were instructed to pursue and the consultation that had to accompany the work. Congress also retained the decisive final act, because an agreement of this kind had no domestic force as an approved commitment of the United States until both chambers passed an implementing bill and the President signed it. The statute was the product of that divided authority, and its amended form recorded the points at which divided authority became substantive influence.

The legal character of the instrument reinforces the same point. The USMCA was a congressional-executive agreement, approved by ordinary majorities in both chambers under trade promotion authority, rather than a treaty ratified after the advice and consent of two thirds of the Senate. The implementing bill passed the House on December 19, 2019, by a vote of 385 to 41, and it passed the Senate on January 16, 2020, by a vote of 89 to 10, without amendment in the Senate. By the time the roll was called, the contested work had already been done, first in the renegotiation with Mexico and Canada and then in the domestic negotiation between the administration and House Democrats that produced the protocol. Organized labor, represented most visibly by the AFL-CIO and most major American unions, supported the amended pact after having opposed NAFTA, while the United States Chamber of Commerce and other business groups also supported the amended text. The pharmaceutical industry, by contrast, opposed the removal of the biologics commitment. 

Comparison with the predecessor sharpens the contrast. The NAFTA Implementation Act, Public Law 103-182, signed on December 8, 1993, had passed the House by 234 to 200 and the Senate by 61 to 38 in November 1993. The USMCA margins reflected a different sequence, in which skepticism had been brought inside the drafting process before passage and the price of passage had been paid in textual amendments rather than in a close vote. Entry into force supplied the final element of identity. Canada gave royal assent to its implementing bill on March 13, 2020, and the United States notified completion of its internal procedures on April 24, 2020. Canada was the last party to notify, and the pact entered into force on July 1, 2020, the first day of the second month after that last notification. On that date NAFTA ceased to supply the governing framework and the USMCA supplied it instead. 

## The Renegotiation Demand and the Inherited Procedure

The demand that produced the statute arose from a campaign commitment stated repeatedly during the election of 2016. Donald Trump, as a candidate, pledged to renegotiate NAFTA on terms more favorable to the United States or to withdraw from the pact if renegotiation failed to produce an acceptable result. After taking office, President Trump and administration officials maintained that position, describing NAFTA in public statements as a defective bargain for American workers and manufacturers and presenting renegotiation as a test of whether the continental framework could be retained in revised form. Those characterizations belonged to the candidate and to the administration that advanced them, and the record treats them as positions held and argued by their proponents rather than as findings established by the statute itself. A president elected after promising either a new bargain or an exit had created an expectation that the status quo would not simply continue, and the governments of Mexico and Canada had to plan around a negotiating partner whose opening position included a stated willingness to leave. Two parties sought, in broad outline, to preserve a framework on which their exporters and supply chains depended. 

Withdrawal was more than rhetoric because the older pact contained its own exit clause. Article 2205 of NAFTA permitted a party to withdraw from the agreement six months after giving written notice to the other parties. The provision mattered for three reasons. It made the threat of departure credible in a strictly legal sense, since the instrument that the administration criticized also supplied the mechanism for leaving it, and no amendment or foreign consent was required to start the clock. The six month interval also created a defined period of uncertainty during which firms, workers, and governments would face the prospect of restored tariffs and unsettled rules, a prospect that itself exerted pressure on the parties that wished to preserve continental arrangements. Renegotiation offered revision while keeping the basic architecture of integrated production and preferential market access. Withdrawal offered revision by termination, with the replacement left to later improvisation or to separate bilateral arrangements that no party had drafted. The administration used the notice power as leverage by holding it in reserve rather than by issuing notice at the outset. 

The choice to renegotiate rather than replace followed from that structure of incentives. A wholly new arrangement, built outside the NAFTA framework, would have required the parties to settle every question of market access, origin, investment, and dispute settlement without a working baseline, while supply chains organized around the existing rules faced an open ended transition. Renegotiation allowed the governments to treat the older text as a draft to be revised, retaining elements that functioned to the satisfaction of the parties and concentrating bargaining effort on the provisions that one or more parties wished to change. For Mexico and Canada, renegotiation offered the prospect of continuity under revised terms, a result preferable in their stated positions to the disruption of termination. The shared decision was narrower than agreement on substance. It was a decision about form, that the continental settlement would be amended within a trilateral negotiation rather than dismantled and rebuilt from separate pieces, and that decision held even when the talks narrowed for a time to a bilateral track between the United States and Mexico in the summer of 2018.

The procedure that governed the American side of the renegotiation had been enacted before the demand for renegotiation arose. The Bipartisan Congressional Trade Priorities and Accountability Act of 2015, the trade promotion authority statute of that year, supplied the framework under which the administration conducted the talks and under which Congress later considered the implementing bill. Congress set negotiating objectives in advance and required the executive branch to consult with legislators during the negotiations, and in return Congress promised that an implementing bill for an agreement concluded within the authority would receive expedited consideration, with limited debate and a vote without floor amendments. The design answered a diplomatic problem that foreign governments had long identified. A partner asked to make concessions at the bargaining table wanted assurance that the legislature of the United States would not reopen the finished text line by line and unravel the balance of concessions after signature. The up or down vote supplied that assurance at the final stage. 

Negotiating objectives performed work that became visible only when the talks reached their difficult stages. By writing objectives into the 2015 statute, Congress instructed the executive branch on the subjects that a qualifying agreement had to address and the direction that American negotiators were expected to take on matters that included market access, rules of origin, labor and environmental obligations, intellectual property, and dispute settlement. The objectives instead set the standard against which legislators later judged the signed text. When House Democrats argued in 2019 that the signed text lacked enforcement machinery adequate to its labor obligations, the argument drew strength from the structure of the 2015 statute, which had treated labor commitments as part of the expected content of a trade agreement rather than as decoration outside the enforceable core. Consultation added a second channel of influence that operated while bargaining continued. The 2015 statute obliged the executive branch to consult with the committees of jurisdiction, principally Ways and Means in the House and Finance in the Senate, and with advisory groups of members, before and during negotiations and before signature. Consultation gave legislators and staff access to negotiating texts and to the officials conducting the talks, and it created repeated occasions on which congressional concerns could be registered while positions remained fluid. It guaranteed familiarity, and familiarity shortened the distance between a complaint about the text and a drafted amendment capable of answering the complaint.

### How did a procedure built for speed produce delay and amendment?

Trade promotion authority set a timetable and barred amendments on the floor, but it also set negotiating objectives, required consultation, and left the House in control of whether an implementing bill reached a vote at all. A majority unwilling to schedule the bill held leverage that no floor amendment could have supplied.

The answer turns on a gap that the expedited procedure never closed. Fast track rules governed the consideration of an implementing bill after the President transmitted it, guaranteeing prompt committee and floor action and protecting the bill from amendment during that consideration. The rules did not compel the House to want the bill, and they did not transfer the power of the Speaker to decide when, or whether, a measure around which no consensus existed would be brought forward. In the political circumstances of 2019, that retained power was decisive. The House had passed under Democratic control in the election of 2018, and Speaker Nancy Pelosi designated a working group of House Democrats to negotiate with Trade Representative Lighthizer over the concerns that the majority identified in the signed text. The working group process ran through 2019 after the administration transmitted the draft text and the draft Statement of Administrative Action on May 30, 2019. The House majority declined to schedule the implementing bill until enforcement concerns had been met to its satisfaction. The executive branch faced a choice between a signed text that could not reach a vote and an amended text that could, and it chose amendment.

Formal negotiations had opened in August 2017, under the objectives and procedures of the 2015 statute, and the path to signature illustrated both the reach and the limits of trilateral bargaining under deadline pressure. The three governments announced agreement in stages during the autumn of 2018. The United States and Mexico reached a text in late August 2018, and Canada joined the arrangement on September 30, 2018, preserving the trilateral form that the choice to renegotiate had contemplated. Signature followed at Buenos Aires on November 30, 2018. Mexico, which had negotiated through a change of administration at home in 2018, obtained the continuity of a continental framework and accepted obligations, including labor law changes, that reached deeper into domestic practice than NAFTA had reached. Canada, which joined after the bilateral text between its two partners had taken shape, accepted a settlement that preserved the trilateral structure and state to state dispute settlement while conceding new access in protected sectors such as dairy, where the Office of the United States Trade Representative estimated that new tariff rate quota access would rise to about 3.6 percent of the Canadian dairy market. Signature, however, settled only the international stage. 

Mexico moved first among the three parties, and its early actions became part of the conditions that shaped the American debate. Mexico ratified the original agreement in June 2019, before either of its partners had completed approval, and on May 1, 2019 Mexico enacted a major labor law reform that implemented a constitutional reform adopted in 2017. The reform replaced conciliation boards with labor courts, established secret ballot votes for union decisions, and required the legitimation of existing collective bargaining contracts through worker votes. House negotiators in Washington treated the Mexican reform as necessary but not sufficient. The distinction captured the enforcement problem at the center of the 2019 standoff. A change in Mexican law, however substantial on paper, did not by itself guarantee that workers at particular facilities could organize and bargain without interference, and the signed text supplied only state to state procedures of the ordinary kind for labor disputes. The protocol answered with the burden shifting presumption for labor and environmental claims, with strengthened language on violence against workers and forced labor, with benchmarks and monitoring for the Mexican reform, with labor attachés posted in Mexico, and with the rapid response mechanism for covered facilities.

Canada occupied a different position in the sequence, less central to the enforcement dispute and more important to the timing of entry into force. The Canadian government had joined the text in September 2018 after resisting, in its public positions during the talks, efforts to dismantle the trilateral framework and to weaken dispute settlement between the parties. In the event, the settlement preserved a state to state chapter and eliminated investor state dispute settlement between Canada and the United States, an outcome that aligned with positions Canada had defended in other negotiations. The Mexican Senate approved the protocol on December 12, 2019, by a vote of 107 to 1, two days after signature in Mexico City on December 10, 2019. The House of Representatives passed the implementing bill a week later, on December 19, 2019, after the Ways and Means Committee had reported it, and the Senate followed in January 2020 after approval by the Finance Committee. Canada gave royal assent to its implementing bill on March 13, 2020, and its notification, the last of the three, set the date from which entry into force was calculated. 

The conditions that produced the statute can therefore be stated as a chain of dependencies. A campaign commitment created the demand and made withdrawal a credible alternative. An exit clause in the older pact gave the alternative legal form and a six month horizon. A procedure enacted in 2015 supplied objectives, consultation, and an expedited final vote, and in doing so defined both the assurances that made foreign concessions possible and the standards that legislators later used to judge the signed text. A change in control of the House in 2018 placed the scheduling power in the hands of a majority whose labor constituency demanded enforcement machinery that the signed text lacked. A labor reform in Mexico supplied the domestic foundation on which that machinery could be built, and a year of negotiation between the House working group and the Trade Representative produced the protocol that closed the gap between signature and passage. Without the campaign commitment, no renegotiation opens in 2017. Without the House majority of 2019 and its refusal to schedule, the signed text of 2018 reaches a vote unamended and the rapid response mechanism never enters the bargain. Public Law 116-113, read against its conditions, was the point at which those separate forces converged in a single text, and the amended agreement that entered into force on July 1, 2020 carried the imprint of each force in its provisions.

## A Signed Text, a Withheld Vote, and a Second Negotiation

The statute that entered into force on July 1, 2020 had been negotiated twice. The first negotiation produced a signed trilateral text in November 2018 that the House of Representatives refused to bring to a vote. The second negotiation, conducted largely between the House Democratic majority and the Office of the United States Trade Representative during 2019, produced an amendment protocol in December 2019 that changed the labor, environmental, pharmaceutical, and dispute settlement provisions of the signed text and, in doing so, transformed a narrow and contested bargain into a measure that passed both chambers by overwhelming margins. The sequence matters because the implementing bill that Congress approved in Public Law 116-113 was not the bill that would have implemented the November 2018 text alone. It implemented the pact as amended, and the amendment was the price of passage.

Formal authority for the renegotiation came from the Bipartisan Congressional Trade Priorities and Accountability Act of 2015, the trade promotion authority statute under which the executive branch negotiated subject to objectives set by Congress and under which an implementing bill, once introduced, received expedited consideration without amendment on the floor. That framework shaped every stage that followed. Because the expedited procedure depended on the administration observing consultation and notification requirements, and because the final bill could not be amended in either chamber, the decisive bargaining over the content of the implementing legislation occurred before introduction, in discussions among the administration, the House majority, and the governments of Mexico and Canada, rather than in markup amendments or floor debate after a bill appeared.

Formal negotiations opened in August 2017 among the United States, Mexico, and Canada to modernize and replace the North American Free Trade Agreement. The talks proceeded under the negotiating objectives in the 2015 statute, and for the first year they advanced without a concluding breakthrough that the three governments could announce jointly. The structure of the negotiation itself created pressure at several points. Mexico faced a change of administration during the talks, Canada and the United States disputed a range of sectoral questions, and the United States administration pressed for substantial changes to rules of origin, dispute settlement, and the duration of the pact. The fact sheet record is sparse on the internal exchanges of that first year, and the public milestones were the opening itself and the bilateral turn that followed in the late summer of 2018.

In late August 2018 the United States and Mexico announced an understanding that resolved the principal bilateral issues between those two parties and supplied the core of a broader trilateral settlement. For a period the possibility remained open in public discussion that the result might proceed as a bilateral arrangement, but the statutory and diplomatic architecture pointed toward a three party outcome, and Canada remained in negotiation with the United States on the questions that concerned Canadian interests most directly, including dairy access, dispute settlement, and cultural and procurement matters. On September 30, 2018 Canada joined the understanding, converting the bilateral text into a trilateral one and preserving the continental structure that had governed North American trade under the predecessor pact since the nineteen nineties. The three governments announced agreement in the autumn of 2018 on that basis.

Representatives of the three countries signed the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement on November 30, 2018 in Buenos Aires, Argentina, on the margins of an international summit meeting held in that city. Signature completed the first stage of negotiation as a diplomatic matter. It did not, by itself, change United States law, approve the pact for the United States, or bring any provision into force. Under the American constitutional arrangement for congressional-executive agreements of this kind, approval required an ordinary statute passed by both houses of Congress and signed by the President, and the trade promotion authority procedure supplied the route for that statute. The signed text therefore entered 2019 as a concluded negotiation awaiting implementing legislation that had not been introduced, in a House that had just passed to Democratic control in the midterm elections of November 2018.

The administration transmitted the draft text to Congress during 2019 and followed it with the draft Statement of Administrative Action on May 30, 2019. The statement of administrative action is the executive branch account, required under the trade promotion authority framework, of how the administration proposed to administer the pact and what changes to existing administrative practice the implementing bill would involve. Its transmission on May 30, 2019 marked a formal step toward introduction of a bill. It did not produce introduction. Between transmission of the draft statement and introduction of H.R. 5430 in December 2019 lay more than six months of negotiation that the formal timetable alone cannot explain, because the obstacle was not drafting. The obstacle was that the House majority would not schedule a bill whose enforcement provisions the majority judged inadequate.

An independent assessment reached Congress during the same interval. The United States International Trade Commission issued its report in April 2019, evaluating the likely economic effect of the pact as signed. The Commission estimated that the text would raise United States real gross domestic product by 68.2 billion dollars, an increase of 0.35 percent, and would raise employment by about 176,000 jobs, an increase of 0.12 percent, relative to a baseline in which the predecessor agreement remained in place. The Commission attributed the largest effects to services and to the sectors governed by the new rules of origin. Those figures entered the congressional debate as estimates by the Commission against a NAFTA baseline, not as guarantees, and they described the November 2018 text rather than the amended text that Congress eventually approved, because the protocol that altered the pact was not negotiated until December 2019, eight months after the Commission reported.

Mexico moved first among the three legislatures. Mexico ratified the original agreement in June 2019, approving the November 2018 text before the United States or Canada had acted and before the Protocol of Amendment existed. Mexican action rested in part on a domestic transformation that the House negotiators in Washington watched closely. On May 1, 2019 Mexico enacted a major labor law reform implementing a 2017 constitutional reform. The statute replaced conciliation boards with labor courts, required secret ballot union votes, and required the legitimation of existing collective bargaining contracts through votes by the workers covered by them. The reform addressed, in Mexican domestic law, the structural complaint that had run through congressional criticism of the predecessor pact for a generation, namely that Mexican workers had lacked effective means to organize independent unions and to reject contracts negotiated without their participation.

### Why Did the House Delay a Vote for a Full Year?

The House Democratic majority withheld the vote because the signed text, in the working group's judgment, lacked enforceable labor and environmental obligations, preserved a biologics exclusivity period the group opposed, and retained a dispute system a respondent could block by refusing to appoint panelists, defects Mexico's reforms alone did not cure.

Speaker Nancy Pelosi designated a working group of House Democrats to negotiate enforcement changes with United States Trade Representative Robert Lighthizer through 2019, and the existence of that channel defined the politics of the withheld year. The majority did not reject the pact outright, and the working group did not present itself as seeking to reopen every chapter. Pelosi's negotiators instead pursued a narrower and more consequential course. They treated Mexican labor reform of May 1, 2019 as a precondition that was necessary but not sufficient, and they pressed the administration to secure changes to the text itself, to the implementing bill, and to the monitoring machinery that would accompany both. Lighthizer, for the administration, engaged the working group rather than forcing a vote under the expedited timetable, a choice that acknowledged the arithmetic of the House. A bill brought to the floor over the objection of the Speaker would not have been scheduled at all, and a bill defeated or indefinitely postponed would have damaged the pact in all three capitals.

The working group demanded, first, that the labor and environment chapters be enforceable in practice rather than declaratory in form. Under the signed text, a party complaining of a labor or environmental violation had to prove that the violation occurred in a manner affecting trade and investment, a threshold that congressional Democrats argued, based on experience under earlier trade agreements, a complaining party would struggle to meet and that a respondent could contest at length before any panel reached the substance. The negotiators sought reversal of that burden, so that a violation would be presumed to affect trade and investment unless the responding party demonstrated otherwise. They sought the same treatment for failures to address violence against workers, and they objected to language that required a complaining party to show a sustained or recurring course of action or inaction in cases of such violence. They also pressed to close language on forced labor that had allowed a party to adopt measures the party itself considered appropriate, wording the working group treated as self judging and therefore as a loophole in the obligation to prohibit imports of goods produced with forced labor.

Behind the general demand for enforceability lay a specific facility level concern. A state to state proceeding, even under improved rules, operates at the level of governments and proceeds over years, and the working group pressed for a mechanism that could reach a single workplace in Mexico where freedom of association and collective bargaining had been denied, and that could do so on a timetable measured in months. That demand eventually produced the facility specific rapid response labor mechanism, which the signed text had not contained at all and which arrived only through the protocol, as annexes to the dispute settlement chapter directed at covered facilities in Mexico in priority sectors. The implementing act that followed the protocol added supporting machinery on the American side, including the Interagency Labor Committee for Monitoring and Enforcement, benchmarks for Mexican labor reform implementation, labor attachés posted in Mexico, an Independent Mexico Labor Expert Board to monitor the reform and advise Congress and the executive branch, and 180 million dollars to support labor reform and technical assistance in Mexico. Those elements belonged to the settlement of 2019 rather than to the bargain of 2018.

Two further demands completed the working group's program. On pharmaceuticals, the negotiators opposed the provision of the signed text that would have required at least ten years of data protection for biologic medicines. The United States maintained twelve years of such protection under domestic law, Canada eight years, and Mexico five years, and the working group objected to locking a ten year floor into a trade agreement where it would constrain future congressional choices and extend protection in the partner countries. Removal of the biologics commitment, and of a separate commitment of at least three years of exclusivity based on new clinical information for previously approved products, became part of the price of House support. The pharmaceutical industry opposed the biologics removal, a position attributed to that industry in the record of the debate, while the AFL-CIO and most major American unions, organizations that had opposed the predecessor agreement, supported the amended pact, as did the United States Chamber of Commerce and other business groups. On dispute settlement, the working group demanded a system that could not be blocked. Practice under the predecessor pact had shown that a responding party could frustrate panel formation by withholding agreement on panelists, leaving a meritorious complaint without a tribunal. The negotiators required a repair that guaranteed establishment of a panel on request and selection of panelists by lot where the parties could not agree, supported by standing rosters.

The second stage of negotiation concluded in Mexico City. On December 10, 2019 the three governments signed the Protocol of Amendment, the instrument that revised the November 2018 text in the respects the Washington negotiation had produced. The protocol shifted the burden on trade and investment effects in labor and environmental cases, removed the sustained or recurring qualifier for violence against workers, tightened the forced labor obligation, added the rapid response annexes, repaired panel formation under the dispute settlement chapter, removed the biologics data protection requirement and the new use exclusivity commitment, clarified the regulatory review exception in the intellectual property chapter, and revised the environment chapter to require each party to adopt, maintain, and implement laws fulfilling specified multilateral environmental agreements to which the party belonged, with expanded monitoring and enforcement machinery analogous to the labor changes. Two days after signature, on December 12, 2019, the Senate of Mexico approved the protocol by 107 to 1, completing Mexican approval of the amended text with a margin that mirrored the breadth of support the revised bargain had acquired.

Congressional action followed within a week, and the sequence from introduction to presidential signature occupied less than seven weeks. H.R. 5430, the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement Implementation Act, was introduced on December 13, 2019, the day after Mexican Senate approval of the protocol, and the Committee on Ways and Means reported the bill as H. Rept. 116-358. The House passed the bill on December 19, 2019 by 385 to 41, Roll Call 701. The scale of that margin is best understood against the vote that had carried [the implementing statute that carried the predecessor agreement](/2020/06/15/nafta-implementation-act-1993-guide/) a generation earlier, when the House had approved the North American Free Trade Agreement Implementation Act on November 17, 1993 by 234 to 200 and the Senate had followed on November 20, 1993 by 61 to 38. Between 1993 and 2019 the coalition for a North American trade statute had widened from a divided chamber, in which the predecessor bill survived by thirty four votes in the House, to a chamber in which only forty one members voted against approval. The protocol, and the labor support that the amended text attracted, accounted for a large part of that difference.

The Senate acted without altering the bargain the House had accepted, as the expedited procedure contemplated. The Committee on Finance approved the bill on January 7, 2020. The Senate passed H.R. 5430 on January 16, 2020 by 89 to 10, Record Vote 14, without amendment. Senate passage without amendment preserved the text negotiated among the House majority, the administration, and the two partner governments, and it avoided any need for a conference or a second House vote that might have reopened questions settled in December. The bill was presented to the President on January 22, 2020, and President Donald Trump signed it on January 29, 2020, enacting Public Law 116-113, 134 Stat. 11. American approval was complete at signature, although the pact itself awaited completion of internal procedures by Canada and formal notification by all three parties before it could enter into force.

Canada completed the parliamentary stage last. The Parliament of Canada gave royal assent to its implementing bill on March 13, 2020. Under the entry into force provision, the pact was to take effect on the first day of the second month after the last party had notified completion of its internal procedures. Canada notified last, after royal assent. The United States notified completion of its internal procedures on April 24, 2020. With the notifications of all three parties recorded, entry into force followed on July 1, 2020, the date on which the agreement replaced the predecessor pact as the governing instrument for North American trade among the three countries. No step in that final sequence required further congressional action. The statute signed on January 29, 2020 had already approved the amended text, appropriated and authorized the machinery the implementing act created, and made the changes to United States law that the pact required, so that the July 1, 2020 transition operated by force of the notifications and the calendar the three governments had set.

The breadth of the final votes also reflected the unusual alignment that the second negotiation had assembled. Organized labor, whose opposition had defined much of the congressional politics of North American trade since the early nineteen nineties, stood on the supporting side of the amended text. The AFL-CIO supported the amended agreement, and most major American unions took the same position, a reversal that the working group negotiations had been designed in substantial part to produce. Business organizations arrived at support by a different route. The United States Chamber of Commerce supported the amended agreement, as did other business groups whose interest lay in preserving continental supply chains and in securing updated rules for digital trade, agriculture, and manufacturing under a durable statute rather than facing continued uncertainty over the future of the predecessor framework. Opposition persisted at the margins and from identifiable quarters. The pharmaceutical industry opposed the removal of the biologics provision, and the forty one House votes and ten Senate votes against the bill recorded dissent that the December settlement had narrowed without eliminating. A coalition that holds the principal labor federation and the principal business federation on the same side of a trade vote is rare in congressional practice, and the roll calls of December 2019 and January 2020 registered exactly that alignment.

The procedural character of the passage deserves equal weight. Under trade promotion authority, Congress surrendered the ordinary power to amend the implementing bill in exchange for consultation during negotiation and for a guaranteed vote within a set period after introduction. In the case of H.R. 5430 that bargain operated in an inverted form. Consultation, in the shape of the working group, did not merely advise the negotiators while talks with Mexico and Canada proceeded. It continued after signature, after transmission of the draft text, and after transmission of the draft Statement of Administrative Action on May 30, 2019, and it produced changes to the international text itself through a protocol negotiated with the partner governments. The implementing bill that the Committee on Ways and Means reported in H. Rept. 116-358 therefore embodied two settlements at once, a trilateral settlement among governments reached in November 2018 and revised in December 2019, and a domestic settlement between a House majority and an administration of the opposing party reached across the same December days. Senate passage without amendment on January 16, 2020 protected both settlements simultaneously. Presentation on January 22, 2020 and signature on January 29, 2020 then converted the combined result into law in a single enactment, Public Law 116-113, whose approval section embraced the agreement as amended by the protocol rather than the text that had been signed in Buenos Aires fourteen months earlier.

The two stage history left a mark on the statute itself. Because the decisive congressional negotiation occurred before introduction, under a procedure that barred floor amendment, the compromises of 2019 appear in the enacted measure as a coherent package rather than as a patchwork of adopted and rejected amendments. The labor enforcement machinery, the monitoring committees, the benchmarks for Mexican reform, the funding for technical assistance, and the approval of a text stripped of the biologics commitment all entered law together on January 29, 2020. A reader of the public law who begins with the November 2018 signature and moves directly to the January 2020 signature misses the year that explains the document. During that year Mexico reformed its labor law, an American Speaker held a signed continental agreement off the floor of the House, a working group rewrote the enforcement core of the bargain in negotiation with the Trade Representative, and three governments signed a protocol that each legislature then approved by margins the original text had shown no prospect of commanding.

## What the Protocol Rewrote

The text signed in Buenos Aires on November 30, 2018, did not survive to ratification in its original form. Mexico ratified that version in June 2019, and the administration in Washington transmitted a draft implementing bill and a draft Statement of Administrative Action during 2019, yet the House Democratic majority declined to schedule the measure until a set of enforcement concerns had been answered. Speaker Nancy Pelosi designated a working group of House Democrats to negotiate with United States Trade Representative Robert Lighthizer through 2019, and the product of that negotiation was the Protocol of Amendment signed in Mexico City on December 10, 2019. The Congressional Research Service, in its summary of the amendment, treated the protocol as a set of targeted revisions rather than a reopening of the whole bargain, and that characterization fits the document. Tariff schedules, market access commitments, and most chapter architecture carried over intact. What changed was the machinery by which obligations could be enforced, the treatment of a small group of pharmaceutical provisions, and the apparatus for watching whether Mexico carried through a domestic labor reform that the House negotiators had treated as necessary but not sufficient. Each revision answered a specific objection that had blocked a vote, and each operated by altering a burden of proof, deleting a qualifying phrase, or adding a procedure that the signed text had lacked.

The most consequential technique in the protocol was burden shifting, and its clearest application concerned the phrase that had governed labor and environment disputes under the signed text. Chapter 23 on labor and Chapter 24 on environment both conditioned a successful complaint on proof that a violation had occurred in a manner affecting trade and investment between the parties. In form the phrase looked like a jurisdictional filter, a way of confining the chapters to conduct with a commercial dimension. In practice, as the House negotiators argued through 2019, the phrase placed a demanding evidentiary task at the threshold of every case. A complaining government first had to establish the underlying violation, for example a failure to protect freedom of association or a failure to enforce an environmental law, and then had to establish a second proposition about economic effect, namely that the particular failure, as distinct from the general condition of an industry, had affected trade or investment flows. That second proposition invited dispute about causation, about the size of a cost advantage gained by nonenforcement, and about whether goods from a specific workplace or sector had entered commerce between the parties in a way traceable to the violation.

The difficulty compounded because the information needed to prove trade effects often sat with the responding government or with employers under its jurisdiction, and the signed text gave the complaining party no presumption to bridge that gap. The result, in the assessment pressed by House Democrats and by the AFL-CIO during the 2019 negotiation, was a pair of chapters that stated strong substantive obligations while making a formal case hard to assemble. Proving a pattern of underenforcement across an economy was a different undertaking from proving the trade effect of an individual violation, and the signed formulation pushed complainants toward the narrower and harder showing.

### Did the Protocol Make Labor Violations Easier to Prove?

Yes. The protocol presumed that a covered labor or environmental violation affected trade and investment between the parties, and a responding government had to demonstrate otherwise to defeat that presumption. The change reversed the signed text, under which a complaining government carried the burden of proving trade effects first.

The presumption did not eliminate the need to prove a violation itself, and the distinction matters for understanding how a panel was expected to work after July 1, 2020. A complaining party still had to identify the obligation, present evidence that the responding party had failed to meet it, and persuade a panel on the merits of that failure. What the protocol removed was the separate threshold contest over commercial effect. Once a violation was established, effect on trade and investment followed as a presumed consequence, and the responding party bore the burden of demonstrating that the particular violation had no such effect. A respondent could attempt that demonstration with evidence about the sector, the destination of production, or the absence of trade between the parties in the goods or services concerned, but silence or general assertion no longer sufficed to keep the case at the threshold. The labor chapter and the environment chapter received the same treatment, a parallel construction the Congressional Research Service recorded in its account of the protocol, and the parallel was deliberate in design. House negotiators had argued that environmental nonenforcement could confer a cost advantage in the same manner as labor nonenforcement, by allowing a producer to avoid pollution control spending or waste handling costs that a competitor in another party had to bear, and a presumption confined to one chapter would have left the other chapter subject to the original proof problem.

A second labor revision removed a qualifier that had narrowed the obligation concerning violence against workers. The signed text had tied a claim about a failure to address violence against workers to proof of a sustained or recurring course of action or inaction. That formulation borrowed language familiar from provisions about a pattern of nonenforcement, and it fitted cases in which a government tolerated repeated antiunion violence over an extended period. It fitted poorly, the House negotiators contended, where a single severe episode, or a short series of threats and assaults connected to an organizing campaign, chilled the exercise of associational rights at a decisive moment. Under the signed wording, a responding government could concede that violence had occurred and that authorities had failed to respond adequately, yet argue that the failure lacked the duration or repetition the qualifier demanded. The complaining party would then have to assemble a record across workplaces and across time before a panel could reach the underlying failure, even where the connection between violence and the suppression of a union campaign was direct.

Deletion of the sustained or recurring requirement changed the unit of analysis. A failure to address violence against workers could be presented as a violation on the facts of the episode or episodes at issue, without an additional showing that the failure formed part of a longer course of conduct, and the burden shifting presumption on trade and investment applied to that claim as it applied to other labor claims. Mechanically, the revision lowered the volume of historical evidence a complainant needed, shortened the period a panel had to survey, and reduced the opportunity for a respondent to defeat a claim on a temporal technicality unrelated to whether workers had in fact been left unprotected. The revision also aligned the violence provision more closely with the way organizing actually unfolded in a contested workplace, where intimidation concentrated in the weeks before a representation vote could determine the outcome long before a pattern spanning years could be documented. The AFL-CIO, which had opposed the North American Free Trade Agreement and supported the amended pact, treated stronger treatment of violence against workers as part of the enforcement package that distinguished the revised text from its predecessor, and House negotiators presented the deletion as a response to testimony about violence directed at independent union organizers in Mexico.

A third tightening concerned forced labor, and it turned on three words in the signed text. The original formulation permitted a party to adopt measures it considered appropriate against goods produced with forced labor. Language of that kind is self judging in effect, because the party imposing or declining to impose a measure becomes the judge of its own compliance, and a panel asked to review the obligation has little standard against which to measure the party's choice. A government that took a narrow step, or that explained inaction as a considered judgment, could argue that the text had committed the assessment to its own discretion. The protocol tightened the formulation so that the obligation to prohibit imports of goods produced with forced labor was not self judging, closing what the Congressional Research Service summary described as a perceived loophole. The mechanical consequence was to make the commitment reviewable on ordinary terms. A panel could ask whether a party maintained a prohibition, whether the prohibition reached the goods the chapter covered, and whether the party had applied it, without deferring at the outset to that party's own characterization of appropriateness. The change did not specify a single enforcement model or a single penalty, and it left each party to administer its import laws through its own customs apparatus, but it removed the textual basis for treating the commitment as a matter of unreviewable preference.

The fourth change added an entirely new instrument rather than revising an existing sentence. The facility specific rapid response labor mechanism entered the pact through the protocol as Annex 31-A for the United States and Mexico and Annex 31-B for Canada and Mexico, both attached to Chapter 31 on dispute settlement. Nothing corresponding to the mechanism had appeared in the November 2018 text. Its presence in the protocol reflected a judgment by the House negotiators and the Office of the United States Trade Representative that state to state litigation over general patterns, even with a favorable burden of proof, moved too slowly to remedy a denial of freedom of association or collective bargaining at a single plant before the organizing effort collapsed and the workforce dispersed. The annexes therefore created a track aimed at covered facilities in Mexico in priority sectors, with a short review window, an expedited panel, verification that could include a visit to the site, and remedies directed at goods or services of the facility concerned rather than at the trade of a whole country. The full procedure belongs to a separate account of the mechanism, and the point for the protocol is narrower. By placing the annexes inside Chapter 31 through an amendment agreed in December 2019, the three governments converted a negotiating demand into treaty text before any implementing bill passed, so that the obligation, the timetable, and the facility level remedy all rested on the consent of Mexico as a party rather than on a unilateral condition imposed afterward by one legislature.

Repair of ordinary state to state dispute settlement formed the fifth item, and the background lay in experience under the North American Free Trade Agreement. Under that predecessor, a responding party could frustrate the formation of a panel by withholding agreement on appointments, because the process depended on the parties selecting panelists within set periods and a respondent that preferred delay had an incentive to let those periods lapse. Cases could stall before any hearing on the merits, and the defect became a standing illustration, in congressional debate over successive trade agreements, of a dispute system that a determined respondent could disable without ever defending its measure. The protocol addressed the defect at the formation stage. A Chapter 31 panel was to be established on request of the complaining party, standing rosters of panelists were maintained in advance, and where the parties could not agree on appointments, selection could proceed by lot from the roster. Selection by lot removed the veto that silence or refusal had supplied. A respondent retained every right to argue jurisdiction, to contest the facts, and to challenge the legal reading offered by the complainant, but a respondent could no longer prevent a panel from existing.

That repair interacted with the burden shifting revisions to give the protocol its internal coherence. A presumption on trade effects had limited value if a complainant could not obtain a panel to apply it, and a panel system that formed reliably had limited value if the threshold burden made meritorious claims impractical to plead. House negotiators presented the dispute settlement repair as a general fix rather than a labor specific concession, since the same blocking technique had threatened disputes under any chapter, and the Office of the United States Trade Representative accepted the roster and lot mechanism as part of the December 2019 package.

The pharmaceutical revisions moved in the opposite direction from the enforcement revisions, deleting commitments the signed text had contained rather than adding new ones. The most contested deletion concerned biologic medicines, a category that included many complex therapies produced in living cells. The signed text had required at least ten years of data protection for biologics, a period during which a follow on producer could not rely on the originator's regulatory data to obtain marketing approval. The protocol removed that requirement in full. No exclusivity commitment for biologics remained in the pact after the deletion, and each party was left to apply its own domestic law. For the United States that domestic term stood at twelve years, for Canada at eight years, and for Mexico at five years, figures the Congressional Research Service set out in explaining the effect of the removal. Because the United States term already exceeded ten years, deletion did not shorten protection under United States law, and the implementing act made no change to the domestic statute on that point. What deletion changed was the international obligation. The United States no longer bound its partners to a ten year floor, and a future Congress retained freedom to alter the twelve year domestic term without breaching the pact, a freedom that a treaty floor would have constrained indirectly by locking a minimum into an international commitment.

Positions on the deletion divided along familiar lines and were stated openly by named holders. The pharmaceutical industry opposed the removal, arguing in its public statements during the December 2019 debate that a ten year commitment had been part of the negotiated balance and that weakening data protection discouraged investment in biologic research. House negotiators stated a different reasoning. In the account of the negotiation recorded during the House debate, the negotiators held that a ten year treaty commitment would have tied the hands of Congress on the domestic price and access questions surrounding biologic medicines, and that a trade agreement did not need to export a protection period that exceeded the terms Canada and Mexico applied under their own law. Speaker Pelosi and members of the working group presented the deletion as a correction that kept the domestic twelve year term intact while removing an international lock that could have been invoked against later domestic legislation.

Two smaller pharmaceutical and intellectual property items accompanied the biologics deletion. The signed text had contained a commitment of at least three years of exclusivity based on new clinical information for previously approved products, covering new indications, new formulations, and new methods of administration, sometimes described as new use exclusivity. The protocol removed that commitment as well. Under the signed wording, a producer that generated clinical data supporting a second use for an existing medicine could obtain a period in which a competitor could not rely on that data for the new use, even though the underlying product had long been approved for another purpose. Removal left the treatment of such data to domestic law in each party, as with biologics, and eliminated a provision that House negotiators had grouped with the biologics term as an extension of protection beyond what access policy required. The same set of revisions clarified the regulatory review exception, a provision of the kind associated with the Bolar doctrine in United States practice. The clarification confirmed that third parties could use, sell, or import a patented product for the purpose of generating information to support an application for marketing approval. Without such an exception, a generic or follow on producer would have to wait until patent expiry before beginning the testing and dossier preparation that approval required, adding the length of the regulatory process to the effective period of exclusivity. The clarified text allowed that preparatory work to proceed during the patent term, so that a competing product could be ready for approval when protection ended, while leaving the patent owner free to enforce the patent against commercial sales made before expiry.

Environment chapter revisions followed the pattern established for labor, combining a substantive specification with expanded machinery for watching compliance. The protocol revised Chapter 24 to require each party to adopt, maintain, and implement laws fulfilling specified multilateral environmental agreements to which that party belonged. The specification named, among the agreements covered, the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species, the Montreal Protocol on substances that deplete the ozone layer, and the MARPOL convention on pollution from ships. Naming particular agreements mattered because a general exhortation gave a panel little to apply, whereas an obligation tied to laws fulfilling a listed agreement allowed a complainant to identify the domestic statute that implemented the commitment and to test whether that measure had been maintained and applied. The burden shifting presumption extended to environment disputes, and the protocol expanded monitoring and enforcement machinery for the chapter in a manner analogous to the labor changes.

The final group of protocol commitments concerned verification inside Mexico, and it rested on a sequence of Mexican domestic actions that preceded the December 2019 signature. Mexico amended its constitution in 2017 to require labor courts in place of conciliation boards, secret ballot votes on union representation and contracts, and a process for legitimating existing collective bargaining agreements. Mexico enacted a major labor law reform on May 1, 2019, to implement that constitutional change. House negotiators treated enactment as a precondition for serious discussion of the implementing bill, and then treated enactment alone as insufficient, because the reform required new courts, new registries, and votes in a very large number of workplaces where established unions held contracts that workers had never ratified in a secret ballot. The protocol and the implementing act therefore added benchmarks for Mexico's labor reform implementation, commitments on monitoring, and machinery that allowed the United States to observe progress against dated expectations rather than relying on general assurances. Benchmarks in this setting functioned as a shared checklist. A benchmark identified an element of the reform, such as the operation of the new labor justice institutions or the conduct of legitimation votes, against which the parties could measure whether implementation proceeded on the schedule the reform itself contemplated.

Personnel and committees gave the benchmarks an institutional home. The implementing act provided for labor attachés posted in Mexico by the United States, officials whose assignment was to follow reform implementation on the ground and report through the Office of the United States Trade Representative and the Department of Labor. The act also created the Interagency Labor Committee for Monitoring and Enforcement, cochaired by the Trade Representative and the Secretary of Labor, and the Independent Mexico Labor Expert Board, charged with monitoring Mexico's labor reform and advising Congress and the executive branch. The implementing act provided one hundred eighty million dollars to support labor reform and technical assistance in Mexico, a figure stated in the statute and recorded by the Congressional Research Service. Mexico's Senate approved the protocol on December 12, 2019, by a vote of one hundred seven to one, two days after signature in Mexico City. Canada gave royal assent to its implementing bill on March 13, 2020, the United States notified completion of its internal procedures on April 24, 2020, and the pact entered into force on July 1, 2020, with the protocol text as the operative agreement among the three parties.

Taken together, the revisions explained why a bill that could not obtain a House vote in the form signed in 2018 passed the House on December 19, 2019, by three hundred eighty five to forty one, and the Senate on January 16, 2020, by eighty nine to ten. The protocol had rewritten the conditions under which promises about workers, about violence in organizing campaigns, about forced labor, and about environmental laws could be tested, and it had paid for part of that rewriting by surrendering pharmaceutical commitments that the signed text had secured. The implementing act that President Donald Trump signed on January 29, 2020, approved the pact as amended, so that the law of the United States rested on the December text from the first day of operation.

## Enforcement at the Level of the Single Factory

The labor provisions of the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement occupy a different structural position from the labor arrangements that accompanied the North American Free Trade Agreement. Under the earlier framework, labor cooperation rested in a side accord apart from the commercial bargain at the center of the pact, with a limited path toward sanctions. Under the text that entered into force on July 1, 2020, labor obligations sit inside the core of the pact itself, in Chapter 23, subject to the state to state dispute settlement machinery of Chapter 31 and supplemented by a facility specific mechanism with no counterpart elsewhere in the text. The House Democratic working group that negotiated with United States Trade Representative Robert Lighthizer through 2019 treated enforceable labor commitments as a condition for scheduling the implementing bill, and the Protocol of Amendment signed on December 10, 2019 in Mexico City supplied the enforcement additions that the signed text of November 30, 2018 had lacked. That placement also connected trade enforcement to [the longer statutory history of American labor standards legislation](/2015/05/15/us-labor-legislation-since-1950/), which had developed through domestic statutes long before labor chapters entered trade negotiations.

Chapter 23 requires each party to adopt and maintain in its statutes and regulations internationally recognized labor rights, to enforce those rights effectively, and to refrain from weakening protections in order to attract trade or investment. The rights include freedom of association and the effective recognition of the right to collective bargaining, the elimination of all forms of forced or compulsory labor, the effective abolition of child labor and a prohibition on the worst forms of child labor, and the elimination of discrimination in respect of employment and occupation. The chapter further addresses acceptable conditions of work with respect to minimum wages, hours of work, and occupational safety and health. The language of adoption and maintenance reaches both the content of a statute book and its durability. National law remains the primary instrument of protection, while the pact supplies an external discipline against legislative retreat and administrative neglect.

Effective enforcement forms the second pillar. A party may not fail to enforce its labor laws through a sustained or recurring course of action or inaction in a manner affecting trade or investment between the parties. As signed in November 2018, the text required the complaining party to prove that a violation had occurred in a manner affecting trade and investment. The Protocol of Amendment reversed that allocation of proof for the labor chapter and for the environment chapter alike. Under the amended text, a panel presumes that a failure to comply with a labor obligation affects trade and investment unless the responding party demonstrates otherwise. The Congressional Research Service described the change as a burden shifting presumption. The protocol made a parallel change for violence against workers. The signed text had required proof that a failure to address violence or threats against workers for exercising labor rights occurred as part of a sustained or recurring course of action or inaction. The protocol removed that language and applied the same presumption, allowing a claim to rest on a failure to address violence without first establishing that the failure formed part of a repeated course of conduct.

A prohibition on derogation completes the general architecture. A party may not waive or otherwise derogate from its labor laws in a manner that weakens or reduces adherence to the rights the chapter protects, where the waiver or derogation affects trade or investment. The provision addresses a competitive fear that had accompanied North American integration since the NAFTA debates of the early 1990s, namely that governments competing for export production and foreign investment would tolerate substandard conditions, or affirmatively relax protections, in order to lower production costs. Together the two duties close off the most direct routes by which a party might seek advantage through labor costs, lowering the written standard for commercial reasons or leaving the written standard untouched while declining to apply the standard in practice.

The forced labor provision carries the chapter from workplace regulation into customs practice. Chapter 23 obligates each party to prohibit the importation of goods into its territory that were mined, produced, or manufactured wholly or in part by forced or compulsory labor, including forced or compulsory child labor. The obligation converts a labor standard into a border measure, enforced at the point of entry rather than through proceedings in the country of production alone. The protocol tightened the provision in a respect negotiators regarded as significant. The signed text had permitted a party to adopt measures the party considered appropriate against goods produced with forced labor, language that left the judgment largely to the party itself. The amended text removed the self judging quality of that formulation, so that the duty to prohibit such imports stands as an obligation subject to the ordinary disciplines of the pact. The Congressional Research Service identified the tightening as the closing of a perceived loophole.

Annex 23-A translates the general chapter into obligations specific to Mexico concerning worker representation in collective bargaining. The annex requires Mexico to provide in its law for workers to engage in concerted activities for collective bargaining or protection and to organize, form, and join the union of their choosing without interference from employers, and it addresses the establishment of independent unions free from employer domination or control. Annex 23-A speaks directly to a pre reform pattern in much of Mexican manufacturing, collective contracts negotiated between employers and unions that many workers had never chosen. The annex contemplates that collective bargaining contracts and union leadership contests rest on the free and secret vote of the workers concerned, that workers have access to the contracts that govern their employment, and that an independent and impartial body registers unions and contracts and verifies elections. The obligations are specific to Mexico because the diagnosis that produced the annex was specific to Mexico, concerning institutions and practices that the other two parties did not share in the same form. Under the annex, union leaders are elected by free, personal, and secret ballot, and existing collective bargaining contracts undergo legitimation, a process under which workers covered by a contract vote, again by secret ballot, on whether to approve the agreement under which they work. A contract that fails legitimation loses effect under the framework the annex describes.

Mexico enacted the implementing legislation for that transformation on May 1, 2019, giving statutory form to a constitutional reform adopted in 2017. The House negotiators in Washington treated enactment of the Mexican reform as necessary but not sufficient for approval of the implementing bill, a judgment the Congressional Research Service recorded in its account of the 2019 negotiations, and the sequence explains why the protocol followed the Mexican statute by seven months. The Mexican Senate approved the protocol on December 12, 2019 by a vote of 107 to 1, two days after signature in Mexico City. The most consequential structural change in the May 2019 law concerned the forum for labor disputes. The reform replaced the conciliation and arbitration boards, tripartite bodies that combined administrative and adjudicative functions and operated under the executive branch at the federal and state levels, with labor courts situated within the judiciary. The boards had attracted criticism from Mexican reformers for delay and for proceedings in which the distinction between conciliation and judgment blurred. Labor courts under judicial authority were designed to separate adjudication from administration and to place decisions in the hands of judges. Alongside the courts, the reform created new institutions for conciliation and for the registration of unions and collective contracts, assigning registration to a specialized federal center rather than to the bodies that heard disputes. Placing registration in an independent registry addressed a point at which the older system had allowed paper unions to acquire contractual standing without a demonstrated mandate from workers.

Democratic procedure inside unions forms the second structural change. The May 2019 law required that union leaders be elected through the personal, free, and secret vote of the membership, and it extended voting requirements to the approval of collective bargaining contracts and to the legitimation of contracts already in force. The legitimation program confronted the accumulated stock of contracts concluded under the older system by requiring each existing contract to be submitted to a vote of the workers it covers within the period the reform prescribed. The registry verifies that each legitimation vote occurred under conditions that allow a genuine choice, and rival organizations gained procedures to test majority support in a workplace. Read together, the election, approval, and legitimation provisions replace a system in which representation could rest on documentary title with a system in which representation must demonstrate the consent of the represented, expressed through ballots that employers and union officials alike are barred from observing or directing. The runway for implementation was long. Replacing boards with courts across a federal system required the creation of judicial institutions at the federal level and in each state, the appointment and training of judges, and the establishment of conciliation centers and registry offices capable of handling elections and contract votes at national scale. Mexico faced that runway while the pact moved toward entry into force on a fixed timetable, taking effect on July 1, 2020 regardless of the pace at which state level courts opened or legitimation votes proceeded. The protocol and the implementing act responded with benchmarks for implementation, money, monitoring, and personnel stationed in Mexico, and the design distinguishes between the obligation, which bound Mexico from entry into force, and the transformation of practice, which all three governments understood would proceed institution by institution and workplace by workplace over the years the reform schedule allowed.

### How Does a Complaint About a Single Facility Move From Petition to Remedy?

A party that holds a good faith belief that workers at a covered facility in Mexico have suffered a denial of rights asks Mexico to review the matter. Mexico may investigate itself within a fixed period, and if that review fails to resolve the concern, a panel verifies the facts and determines whether a denial occurred.

The facility specific rapid response mechanism, set out in Annex 31-A for the United States and Mexico and in Annex 31-B for Canada and Mexico, was added entirely by the Protocol of Amendment. The mechanism did not exist in the text signed on November 30, 2018, and its appearance in the December 2019 protocol marks the distance between the original bargain and the bargain the House of Representatives approved on December 19, 2019 by a vote of 385 to 41. The rapid response mechanism inverts the characteristics of ordinary dispute settlement under Chapter 31, which proceeds over measures of general application. The mechanism works as follows in design. A claim concerns one facility rather than a national law. The inquiry concerns two specified rights rather than the whole labor chapter. The timetable runs in days and months rather than in the longer periods of ordinary panels. The remedies fall on the goods or services of the particular facility whose workers suffered the denial. The AFL-CIO, which had opposed the North American Free Trade Agreement, supported the amended pact, and the federation's support rested in significant part on the judgment that facility level enforcement answered the central failure of the earlier side accord, under which complaints about particular workplaces produced consultations without consequences for the production that benefited from a violation.

Coverage defines the reach of the mechanism. A covered facility is a facility in Mexico in a priority sector, a category the annexes define to include sectors producing manufactured goods, sectors supplying services, and sectors engaged in mining. Coverage attaches to the facility rather than to the enterprise as a whole at the first instance, so that a company operating several plants faces inquiry into the plant where a denial is alleged, with consequences for other plants arising only through the escalation provisions that follow repeated determinations. The mechanism covers only two rights, freedom of association and the right to collective bargaining, as those rights appear in the obligations of Annex 23-A. A denial of rights, the term the annexes use, means a denial of those two rights to workers at a covered facility. Other labor failures, however serious, such as violations concerning child labor, discrimination, wages, hours, or safety, proceed if at all through the general obligations of Chapter 23 and ordinary dispute settlement, not through the expedited facility procedure.

The procedure begins with a request grounded in good faith. A party that believes in good faith that a denial of rights has occurred at a covered facility may request that the respondent, Mexico, review whether a denial exists. The good faith standard sets the threshold at belief rather than at proof. On receipt of a request, Mexico has 10 days to decide whether to agree to conduct its own review of the alleged denial. That short election window respects the primacy of Mexican institutions under the reform, giving the new labor authorities, the registry, and the courts the first opportunity to examine conditions in a Mexican workplace and to secure remediation through domestic processes. The window also prevents delay from defeating the mechanism at its first step, because refusal or silence carries a defined consequence, namely that the requesting party may move directly toward a panel. If Mexico agrees to conduct a review, the annexes allow a period of 45 days from the original request for Mexico to investigate and to seek remediation of any denial the investigation confirms. If Mexico declines to conduct a review, if Mexico conducts a review and concludes that no denial exists, or if a review confirms a denial but remediation does not follow, the requesting party may ask for the establishment of a rapid response panel.

The panel consists of three panelists drawn from standing rosters that the parties maintain for the mechanism, a design the protocol reinforced by ensuring that panel formation cannot be frustrated by a failure to appoint. Panelists verify the existence or nonexistence of a denial of rights, and the annexes authorize verification that includes on site visits to the covered facility, so that the panel may examine records, observe conditions, and hear from workers and management in the workplace itself rather than relying exclusively on documentary submissions exchanged between capitals. On site verification is the evidentiary heart of the mechanism, because disputes over representation typically turn on facts that paper records can obscure, such as whether workers knew a contract existed, whether a vote occurred under observation, or whether a union claiming title to a contract has any presence among the workforce. After verification, the panel issues a determination of whether a denial of rights exists. The timetable for the panel stage is expedited and measured in months rather than the years of ordinary dispute settlement, and the determination, rather than a negotiated recommendation, supplies the trigger for remedies.

Remedies follow the determination in a ladder calibrated to repetition. Upon a determination that a denial of rights exists, the requesting party may impose remedies that include suspension of preferential tariff treatment for goods manufactured at the covered facility, meaning that the facility's goods lose the benefit of the preferential rates the pact provides, and the imposition of penalties on goods or services of the covered facility. The remedies fall on the output of the plant rather than on unrelated trade. If a second determination of a denial of rights is made against a facility of the same entity, remedies may extend to other covered facilities of the same entity producing the same or related goods or services, widening the consequence from a single plant to the entity's comparable operations. Upon a third determination against facilities of the same entity, entry of goods from that entity may be denied outright, the most severe step in the ladder, reserved for a pattern in which two prior determinations failed to produce durable compliance. Remedies imposed under the annexes remain in place until the parties agree that the denial of rights has been remediated, so that removal of a remedy depends on demonstrated correction accepted by both governments rather than on the passage of a fixed period.

Both annexes run against facilities in Mexico alone, and the asymmetry is textual rather than accidental. Annex 31-A pairs the United States with Mexico. Annex 31-B pairs Canada with Mexico on the same terms. In each annex the definition of a covered facility is confined to facilities in the territory of Mexico. The reason lies in the source of the rights enforced. The mechanism enforces the representational obligations of Annex 23-A, and Annex 23-A imposes its specific duties concerning independent unions, secret ballots, and contract legitimation on Mexico alone, because those duties answer conditions in the Mexican system that the 2017 constitutional reform and the May 2019 statute set out to transform. Facilities in the United States and Canada remain subject to the general obligations of Chapter 23 and to ordinary dispute settlement for labor failures, but the expedited facility procedure, with on site verification and plant level trade remedies, was constructed as an instrument for monitoring a single country's institutional transition, accepted by that country in the protocol as part of the settlement that also delivered American funding and technical assistance for the reform Mexico had chosen under its own constitution.

The Implementation Act built domestic machinery in the United States to operate the labor provisions, beginning with the Interagency Labor Committee for Monitoring and Enforcement. The statute created the committee and charged it with monitoring Mexico's implementation of its labor reform and with assessing whether Mexico was meeting the obligations of the labor chapter and Annex 23-A. The committee is co chaired by the United States Trade Representative and the Secretary of Labor, a pairing that joins the agency responsible for trade negotiations and enforcement with the agency responsible for labor standards and for the technical knowledge that workplace level claims require. The committee serves as the receiving and evaluating point for information about conditions in Mexican workplaces, whether that information arrives from the labor attachés in Mexico, from the expert board the statute also created, from Mexican authorities, or from petitions submitted by interested parties in the United States.

The petition process gives private parties a formal route into that machinery. Under the statute and the procedures the committee adopted, an interested party may submit a petition alleging a denial of rights at a covered facility in Mexico, and the committee considers the petition and decides whether the allegation warrants a request for review by the United States under Annex 31-A. The Interagency Labor Committee issued interim procedural guidelines for petitions on June 30, 2020, one day before entry into force, so that the petition route existed in published form from the first day the mechanism could operate. The interim guidelines describe the information a petition should contain, the manner of submission, and the course of committee consideration. No petition had been filed and no invocation had occurred by July 1, 2020, and the system is properly described as created and ready rather than as tested. While a complaint concerning a facility proceeds, the Implementation Act also allows the suspension of liquidation for entries of goods from a facility under complaint, meaning that the final assessment of duties on those entries is held open while review continues, preserving the practical value of a later remedy and preventing importers from locking in preferential treatment for shipments produced while a denial is under examination. Congress paired enforcement with assistance, providing 180 million dollars to support labor reform and technical assistance in Mexico. The funding supports the institutions that conduct elections and verify contracts and the administrative competence on which a Mexican review within the 45 day period depends. The statute also created the Independent Mexico Labor Expert Board, a body of experts charged with monitoring and evaluating Mexico's implementation of its labor reform and with reporting its assessments to Congress and to the executive branch, including to the Interagency Labor Committee. The statute further funded labor attachés posted in Mexico, officials based in Mexican territory whose duties include monitoring labor conditions, gathering information about workplaces and reform implementation, and liaising with Mexican authorities, unions, and employers. Together, the committee in Washington, the expert board reporting to Congress, and the attachés in Mexico form a monitoring structure that mirrors the logic of the labor bargain itself, general obligations in a core chapter, specific obligations for the Mexican transition, and a facility level remedy for the cases in which monitoring and domestic review fail to secure the two rights on which the design converges.

## Content Rules, Investor Claims, an Expiry Date, and the Digital Chapter

The substantive chapters of the agreement reached well beyond tariff schedules. Tariffs between the three countries had already fallen to low levels under the predecessor pact, so the commercial weight of the new text sat in the conditions attached to preferential treatment, in the rights granted to investors, in the machinery governing how long the bargain would last, and in rules for forms of commerce that the predecessor had barely addressed. A vehicle could cross a border free of duty only if its content, its steel, and the wages paid to the workers who built it satisfied thresholds written into Chapter 4 and its automotive appendix. An investor disappointed by a government measure faced a far narrower path to arbitration under Chapter 14 than the path open under the predecessor. The whole arrangement carried an expiry mechanism under Article 34.7 that no earlier American trade agreement had contained in the same form. Chapter 19, on digital trade, regulated transmissions, data, and source code.

The automotive rules of origin formed the most intricate part of the package, because the North American vehicle industry had organized itself around the predecessor rules for more than two decades. Production chains crossed the three borders repeatedly, with engines, transmissions, wiring, stampings, and final assembly divided among plants in the United States, Mexico, and Canada according to cost and specialization. A rule of origin determined whether the finished vehicle counted as North American enough to enter a partner market without duty. Under the predecessor, the regional value content threshold for passenger vehicles and light trucks stood at 62.5 percent. The new text raised that threshold to 75 percent, phased in over three years after entry into force, with longer alternative staging available for a limited number of vehicles.

The regional value content test measured the share of a vehicle value attributable to originating materials and regional production, so raising the threshold tightened every calculation beneath it. A producer relying on electronic components, specialty parts, or subassemblies from outside North America had to find regional substitutes, bring production inside the region, or accept that a vehicle might lose preferential treatment and face the ordinary tariff. The three year phase in recognized that supply contracts, tooling, and plant investment could not be rearranged on the day of entry into force on July 1, 2020. The longer alternative staging for a limited number of vehicles served the same purpose for models whose production cycles made rapid adjustment especially difficult. Support for the higher threshold came from the Office of the United States Trade Representative, which presented the increase as a means of anchoring more vehicle value, and the employment attached to that value, inside North America.

Layered on top of the content threshold sat a requirement with no counterpart in the predecessor text. The labor value content rule required that at least 40 percent of a passenger vehicle value, and 45 percent of a truck value, come from production in facilities paying an average base hourly wage of at least 16 dollars. The test did not ask only where parts originated. The test asked what the workers who made a defined share of the vehicle had been paid. A producer could accumulate qualifying value through high wage production work, and portions of the requirement could be satisfied through credits for high wage technology expenditures and high wage assembly expenditures, so engineering, research, and assembly activity carried out at qualifying wage levels counted alongside direct production. The structure mattered. The wage floor was not a general labor standard enforced against a government through the labor chapter. The wage floor operated inside the rule of origin itself, as a condition a vehicle had to meet before preferential tariff treatment attached. The design imported a labor standard into a rule of origin for the first time, converting wage levels in particular plants into a determinant of tariff treatment for finished goods.

The intention behind that conversion was plain in the structure of the rule, even apart from any statement of purpose. Mexican assembly wages had long stood far below American and Canadian levels, and the predecessor text had treated the resulting cost difference as an ordinary feature of regional production that firms could exploit freely while still claiming preferential access. The labor value content rule narrowed that freedom. A vehicle built predominantly with low wage labor could accumulate regional value content and still fail, because a defined share of its value had to originate in facilities meeting the 16 dollar average. The rule therefore pushed in two directions at once. Production could move toward higher wage facilities in the United States or Canada, or wages and qualifying high wage activity in Mexico could rise toward the threshold, and either movement satisfied the arithmetic. The AFL-CIO, which had opposed the predecessor agreement and supported the amended agreement, treated enforceable labor commitments and mechanisms that reduced the incentive to chase low wages as central tests of the renegotiation.

A third automotive requirement governed the metals inside the vehicle. At least 70 percent of a vehicle producer purchases of steel and aluminum had to originate in North America. The steel component carried an additional discipline with a longer runway. Steel had in due course to be melted and poured in North America, and a seven year runway applied to that melt and pour requirement, so steel that passed through regional finishing operations without regional melting and pouring had a defined period before it ceased to count. The distinction between purchase origin and melt and pour origin closed a route by which steel produced substantially outside the region could acquire originating status through limited regional processing. Alongside the metals rule, the appendix required that core parts themselves meet origin requirements, so a vehicle could not reach the threshold on the strength of regional final assembly while its principal components arrived from outside the region without themselves qualifying.

The attributed readings of the automotive package divided along the line between distribution and cost. The Office of the United States Trade Representative presented the combined thresholds as a rebalancing toward regional production and higher wage employment, and organized labor, including the AFL-CIO, supported the amended agreement of which those thresholds formed a part. The contrary reading, pressed by vehicle producers and parts suppliers whose associations warned Congress during the ratification debate about compliance burdens, treated the same thresholds as a compounding administrative and sourcing problem. That industry reading emphasized that a producer had to track regional value, wage based value, credit eligible expenditures, steel purchase origin, and core parts status simultaneously, for each model, across suppliers that themselves had to certify the origin and wage characteristics of their own inputs. Complexity at that scale carried cost even where a vehicle ultimately qualified, and the industry reading held that some production might lose preferential treatment or shift in ways that raised consumer prices rather than returning employment to the United States.

Chapter 14 rewrote the relationship between foreign investors and host governments, and the rewrite ran in the opposite direction from the automotive chapter. Where the automotive rules added conditions, the investment chapter subtracted rights that investors had held under Chapter 11 of the predecessor. Investor state dispute settlement, the mechanism by which a private investor could bring a claim directly against a host government before an international arbitral tribunal and seek monetary damages, disappeared entirely for one pairing and contracted sharply for another. Between the United States and Canada, investor state dispute settlement was eliminated. Between Canada and Mexico, no such settlement existed under this agreement at all, although those two countries retained investor state dispute settlement between themselves under the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans Pacific Partnership. The elimination for the northern pairing reflected a judgment, embedded in the text, that investors in two countries with developed court systems and long records of reciprocal investment did not require a private arbitral route around domestic courts.

Between the United States and Mexico, Annex 14-D preserved a narrowed form of arbitration rather than abolishing it. Claims were limited to three grounds, national treatment, most favored nation treatment, and direct expropriation. An investor could allege that a host government had treated the investment less favorably than domestic investments or than investments from other countries, or that the government had taken the investment directly. Claims resting on the broader fair and equitable treatment standard, and claims for indirect expropriation based on regulatory measures said to have destroyed investment value without a formal taking, fell outside Annex 14-D. The narrowing removed the two categories that had generated the most expansive readings of investor rights under the predecessor. Annex 14-D also imposed a procedural condition with substantial practical weight. An investor had first to litigate in domestic courts for 30 months before arbitrating, unless that litigation would be obviously futile. The waiting period reversed the logic of the predecessor mechanism, which had allowed investors to bypass domestic courts almost entirely. Under the new annex, domestic courts received the first opportunity to correct a measure, and arbitration operated as a residual remedy after a defined interval rather than as a first resort.

Annex 14-E carved a deliberate exception into that narrowing for a defined class of investments. Investors party to covered government contracts in specified sectors retained access to arbitration on the fuller set of Chapter 14 standards. The named sectors were oil and natural gas, power generation, telecommunications, transportation, and certain infrastructure. The distinction produced a two tier structure inside a single chapter. A manufacturer investing in Mexico without a covered government contract faced the narrowed claim list and the 30 month domestic litigation condition. An energy or telecommunications investor holding a covered contract faced neither restriction to the same degree.

Annex 14-C managed the transition between the two regimes. The annex preserved claims under Chapter 11 of the predecessor for legacy investments, meaning investments made while the predecessor was in force and existing at its termination, for three years after the predecessor terminated. An investor whose capital had been committed under the older and broader set of rights did not lose those rights on July 1, 2020, the day the new agreement entered into force. The three year window allowed claims resting on the legacy investment to be brought under the predecessor standards within the interval, after which the window closed and the Chapter 14 framework governed without the legacy overlay. The annex answered those arguments with a fixed period rather than an indefinite preservation, balancing reliance against the stated intention of the three governments to move beyond the predecessor investment regime.

The reason the transition required such care was that Chapter 11 of the predecessor had become, over two and a half decades, the most contested feature of that agreement. The AFL-CIO and allied labor and environmental organizations criticized the mechanism on the ground that it granted foreign investors a private right of action unavailable to domestic firms, allowed tribunals to award damages against public measures adopted for health, environmental, or labor purposes, and placed the resulting financial risk on taxpayers who had no seat in the proceeding. In that critical reading, the threat of a claim could chill regulation even where a government ultimately prevailed, because the cost and exposure of arbitration entered the calculation before a measure was adopted. The United States Chamber of Commerce and business organizations defending the mechanism answered that investment protection served a different function. In the defense offered by the Chamber and by investors who relied on the predecessor chapter, enforceable protection against discrimination and uncompensated taking gave firms the confidence to commit capital in countries whose courts or politics might not protect foreign property reliably, and the remedy compensated investors rather than overturning the host government measure itself. Between those two attributed positions sat the compromise that Chapter 14 embodied. Complete abolition applied only where both governments accepted the adequacy of each other's courts. A narrowed mechanism survived for the southern pairing. Fuller protection survived for contract sectors.

The sunset mechanism in Article 34.7 gave the agreement a defined lifespan of a kind that trade agreements ordinarily lack. The agreement terminates 16 years after entry into force, on July 1, 2036, unless each party confirms in writing, through its head of government, that the party wishes to continue the agreement for a new 16 year term. The default position was therefore termination rather than continuation. A trade agreement normally continues until a party takes the affirmative step of leaving it, so inertia favors survival. Article 34.7 reversed the inertia. Silence, disagreement, or simple failure to complete the confirmation process in any one of the three capitals would set the agreement on a path to expiry, and the affirmative act required for survival had to come from each head of government personally, in writing, rather than from a trade ministry acting in the ordinary course of administration.

### What happens if a party refuses to confirm continuation at the joint review?

If any party withholds confirmation at the joint review, the pact does not end at once. The parties instead conduct joint reviews every year afterward, and termination follows ten years after the review at which confirmation was withheld if confirmation never comes during that interval.

The machinery that led to that result began with a joint review of the operation of the agreement, conducted by the Free Trade Commission on the sixth anniversary of entry into force, July 1, 2026. At that review each head of government could confirm continuation, and confirmation by all three parties produced a new 16 year term. If any party did not confirm at a joint review, the parties moved to annual joint reviews afterward, returning to the question every year rather than waiting for another fixed anniversary. If confirmation never came, the agreement terminated 10 years after the date of the joint review at which confirmation was withheld, giving a decade of run off during which trade conducted in reliance on the agreement could be adjusted, renegotiated, or unwound. The 10 year run off prevented that pressure from becoming an abrupt rupture, because the termination date generated by a withheld confirmation sat a full decade beyond the review that generated it.

Article 34.6 supplied a separate and more familiar device that should not be confused with the sunset. Any party could withdraw from the agreement on six months written notice. Withdrawal was an individual act, available at any time, effective for the withdrawing party on a short timetable, and carried over from standard practice in earlier agreements including the predecessor. The sunset operated differently in every respect. The sunset required no act of withdrawal by any party, applied to the agreement as a whole rather than to one party participation, and unfolded across review dates and run off periods measured in years rather than months. A party that wished to leave quickly had Article 34.6.

The attributed arguments for and against a built in expiry divided over the value of certainty. The Office of the United States Trade Representative, which negotiated the clause, presented the review and sunset structure as a discipline against complacency, a guarantee that the three governments would periodically examine whether the bargain served their interests and would renegotiate terms that economic change had overtaken, rather than allowing an outdated text to govern indefinitely through inertia. The opposing reading, advanced by the United States Chamber of Commerce and by business organizations that had supported ratification while criticizing the sunset device, held that investment in regional supply chains depended on confidence that the rules would endure beyond any single political cycle. In that reading, a plant built to satisfy a 75 percent content rule had a working life extending across the 2026 review and toward the 2036 termination date, and an investor asked to commit capital against rules that might lapse on a withheld confirmation would demand a higher return, delay the commitment, or place the plant elsewhere.

Chapter 19, on digital trade, addressed a sector that the predecessor text had left almost untouched, for the straightforward reason that the commerce in question barely existed in commercial form in the early nineteen nineties, when the predecessor was negotiated. Chapter 19 prohibited customs duties on electronic transmissions, so a transmission crossing a border could not be taxed at the border as though the transmission were a physical good in a container. The chapter guaranteed nondiscriminatory treatment of digital products, so a party could not favor domestic software, media, or other digital products over the corresponding products of another party. The chapter protected cross border transfer of information by electronic means, and barred a party from requiring data localization, meaning the use of computing facilities inside its own territory, as a condition of doing business there. The chapter also protected against forced disclosure of source code, so a government could not demand access to the source code of software as a condition of importation, distribution, or sale, subject to the exceptions the text itself specified. Paperless trading provisions and open government data provisions completed a chapter concerned with the administrative as well as the commercial infrastructure of digital exchange.

One provision of the chapter had no precedent in an American trade agreement. Chapter 19 included an interactive computer services provision limiting liability for third party content, the first such provision in a United States trade agreement. The provision carried into trade law a principle drawn from domestic American law, that a provider of an interactive computer service should not bear liability as the publisher of content supplied by users of the service. The digital chapter as a whole supported the claim, made for the renegotiation from its opening, that the agreement modernized the predecessor rather than merely adjusting its tariffs and content rules.

Three shorter subjects completed the substantive coverage, and each illustrated a different kind of bargain. Canadian dairy access was a concession extracted within a system of supply management that the agreement otherwise left in place. Canada opened new tariff rate quota access for American dairy products, access that the Office of the United States Trade Representative estimated would rise to about 3.6 percent of the Canadian dairy market. Canada also agreed to eliminate the Class 6 and Class 7 milk pricing classes within six months of entry into force. The pricing class elimination removed a mechanism rather than merely enlarging a quota, and the six month deadline placed that removal at the front of the implementation calendar, in the second half of 2020, rather than in a distant staging period.

Government procurement illustrated a narrowing rather than an expansion of coverage. The procurement chapter applied only between the United States and Mexico. Canada stood outside the bilateral procurement obligations of the chapter and relied instead on the World Trade Organization Agreement on Government Procurement, as had been the position under the predecessor in its final years. The arrangement preserved an existing allocation of obligations rather than disturbing it, because Canada relied on the multilateral procurement agreement for government purchasing access while the United States and Mexico pairing retained a dedicated chapter between themselves.

The macroeconomic policies and exchange rate chapter, Chapter 33, addressed the subject that trade agreements had traditionally left to finance ministries and to the International Monetary Fund. The chapter carried transparency and consultation commitments on currency practices, requiring disclosure and discussion of exchange rate policies, interventions, and reserves, without trade sanctions of the ordinary kind behind those commitments. A party that considered another party currency practices objectionable received a right to information and to consultation rather than a right to impose countermeasures under the agreement. The enforcement available for that chapter, however, stopped at transparency and consultation, so the chapter functioned as a disclosure regime attached to a trade agreement rather than as a sanction backed prohibition on currency manipulation. Read together, the three shorter subjects showed the range of the whole text, from a quantified opening in a protected farm market, through a procurement carve out that changed nothing, to a currency chapter whose significance lay in its existence rather than in the remedies it supplied.

## A Modest Tariff Revision Carrying a New Enforcement Architecture

Any honest assessment of the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement Implementation Act has to separate two questions that public argument through 2019 and into 2020 compressed into one. The first concerns how much the terms of North American trade changed on July 1, 2020, when the agreement entered into force and replaced the North American Free Trade Agreement. The second concerns how much the machinery for enforcing those terms changed on the same date. The answers point in opposite directions. Measured by duties at the border and by market access, the revision was modest, in places deliberately conservative. Measured by obligations in the core text, presumptions governing proof, remedies against a single workplace, and the calendar imposed on the agreement itself, the revision was fundamental. Public Law 116-113 did both things at once.

The counter-reading deserves statement at full strength, because it rests on facts that supporters of the amended text did not seriously dispute. By the time formal talks opened in August 2017, most trade among the United States, Mexico, and Canada already crossed borders duty free under the predecessor, whose tariff eliminations had been completed years earlier. A renegotiation beginning from that baseline had limited room to liberalize through tariff cuts, since the principal schedules already stood at zero. Goods that entered duty free on June 30, 2020 entered duty free on the following day, under a different legal instrument and different origin and enforcement rules, but at the same rate. Congressional supporters of the amended text who described continuity as an achievement, and business organizations that prized continuity, were describing a real feature of the settlement.

The market access gains confirm that modest scale. Canada opened new tariff rate quota access for American dairy products, and the Office of the United States Trade Representative estimated that access would rise to about 3.6 percent of the Canadian dairy market. Canada also committed to eliminate the Class 6 and Class 7 milk pricing classes within six months of entry into force. Neither element restructured Canadian dairy policy, which retained supply management. The automotive provisions show the same character in another form. Regional value content for passenger vehicles and light trucks rose from 62.5 percent to 75 percent, phased in over three years, and labor value content and steel and aluminum requirements added further qualification conditions. Those provisions operated as thresholds for a preference rather than openings of closed markets. They altered sourcing and production calculations, but a threshold is a condition on a benefit that already existed.

On enforcement, the text that conserved the tariff schedules rebuilt nearly every mechanism by which obligations could be invoked, proved, and remedied. Under the original signed text, a complaining party pressing a labor or environmental claim had to prove that a violation occurred in a manner affecting trade and investment, placing the hardest element on the party alleging the breach. The Protocol of Amendment of December 10, 2019 reversed that burden. A panel presumes a violation affects trade and investment unless the responding party demonstrates otherwise, and the reversal applied to both the labor chapter and the environment chapter. The protocol removed language requiring proof that a failure to address violence against workers occurred in a sustained or recurring course of action or inaction. It tightened the forced labor commitment so that the obligation to prohibit imports of goods produced with forced labor was not self-judging. It revised the environment chapter to require each party to adopt, maintain, and implement laws fulfilling specified multilateral environmental agreements, including the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species, the Montreal Protocol, and the MARPOL ship pollution convention. In the amended text such obligations stood inside the agreement itself, carrying a presumption drafted to make them usable.

The facility specific rapid response labor mechanism carried that transformation furthest, through an instrument with no precedent in any earlier trade agreement. The protocol added it as Annex 31-A, pairing the United States with Mexico, and Annex 31-B, pairing Canada with Mexico. It was not part of the text signed in November 2018. It covered two rights only, freedom of association and collective bargaining, at covered facilities in Mexico in priority sectors producing manufactured goods, supplying services, or engaged in mining. A party holding a good faith belief that a denial of rights had occurred could request a review by Mexico, which held 10 days to agree to conduct its own review and, if it did, 45 days from the request to investigate and seek remediation. Where Mexico declined review, found no denial, or failed to achieve remediation, the requesting party could ask for a panel of three panelists drawn from rosters. The panel could verify conditions, including through on-site verification, on a timetable measured in months rather than years. Remedies were aimed at a single workplace: suspension of preferential tariff treatment for goods manufactured at the covered facility, or penalties on goods or services of the facility. A second determination could extend remedies to other covered facilities of the same entity producing the same or related goods or services. A third determination could bring denial of entry for goods from that entity outright. That facility level focus, absent from earlier agreements, is what labor organizations identified as a change in kind.

### Did the Implementing Act Change North American Trade or Preserve It?

The answer depends on the dimension measured. On tariffs, the act largely preserved duty free trade and adjusted market access at the margins. On enforcement, it rebuilt the machinery for labor, environment, and disputes, created facility level remedies, and set a scheduled review that the predecessor lacked entirely.

Two further provisions completed the enforcement picture, and both addressed weaknesses the predecessor had exposed in practice. Under the earlier arrangement, a responding party could frustrate panel formation by withholding agreement on composition, leaving a complaint filed and stalled. The protocol ensured that a Chapter 31 panel is established on request and that panelists can be selected by lot where the parties cannot agree, with standing rosters maintained. The sunset provision worked on the same logic at a longer horizon. Under Article 34.7 the agreement terminates 16 years after entry into force, on July 1, 2036, unless each party confirms in writing, through its head of government, continuation for a new 16-year term. On July 1, 2026, the sixth anniversary, the Free Trade Commission conducts a joint review. If any party withholds confirmation at a joint review, the parties hold joint reviews every year afterward, and termination follows 10 years after the review at which confirmation was withheld if confirmation never comes. The predecessor carried no such expiry date, and the amended text thereby built renegotiation into the calendar.

The pharmaceutical provisions show that the protocol moved substance, not merely procedure. The signed text would have required at least 10 years of data protection for biologic medicines, against domestic terms of 12 years in the United States, 8 years in Canada, and 5 years in Mexico. The protocol removed that requirement altogether, leaving no biologics exclusivity commitment in the agreement and each party under its own law. It also removed a commitment of at least three years of exclusivity based on new clinical information for previously approved products, covering new indications, formulations, and methods of administration. The pharmaceutical industry opposed the biologics removal, registering a genuine loss from its stated position, since a commitment in a signed text was withdrawn before the implementing bill reached the House floor. Congressional supporters of the amended text presented the deletion as correction of an overreach. Both descriptions attach to the same words removed from the same pages.

Attribution clarifies where each reading found its institutional home. The AFL-CIO and most major American unions, which had opposed the predecessor, supported the amended agreement, reversing a generation of organized labor opposition to continental trade liberalization on the terms offered in 1993. Labor organizations supplied the most consequential endorsement of the enforcement reading. Business organizations supplied the strongest endorsement of the continuity reading. The United States Chamber of Commerce and other business groups supported the amended text, and their stated interest centered on preserving an integrated continental market and avoiding disruption from withdrawal or tariff uncertainty. Congressional supporters of the amended text presented the protocol as the instrument that converted an unacceptable draft into an enforceable settlement without discarding the market the draft governed. The pharmaceutical industry stood apart, opposing the final text for what the protocol had taken out rather than what it had put in.

The vote margins record how those constituencies combined, and they require explanation because the alignment was, on its face, improbable. H.R. 5430 passed the House on December 19, 2019 by 385 to 41 and the Senate on January 16, 2020 by 89 to 10. The President received the bill on January 22, 2020 and signed it on January 29, 2020. A renegotiation demanded by a president of one party, conducted amid open conflict between the executive and the House majority of the other party, cleared that House by a margin approaching unanimity. The predecessor's implementing act had passed the House in November 1993 by 234 to 200 and the Senate by 61 to 38. The margin of 385 to 41 is explained not by agreement on first principles but by exchange: each side purchased the element it valued most with a concession the other valued more, and constituencies whose opposition had nearly defeated earlier measures were brought inside the coalition before the vote.

The protocol was the price of that coalition, and the chronology shows the price exacted in sequence. The administration transmitted the draft text and then the draft Statement of Administrative Action in 2019, and the House Democratic majority declined to schedule the implementing bill until enforcement concerns were met. Speaker Nancy Pelosi designated a working group of House Democrats to negotiate with United States Trade Representative Robert Lighthizer through 2019. That negotiation, conducted while Mexico's Senate had already ratified the original agreement in June 2019, produced the protocol signed in Mexico City on December 10, 2019, nine days before the House vote. Mexico's Senate approved the protocol on December 12, 2019 by 107 to 1, accepting amendments to a text Mexico had already ratified. The protocol was the condition on which a House majority agreed to move a bill it had the votes to leave unscheduled, and its contents track that majority's stated concerns: burden of proof, violence against workers, the facility level mechanism, panel formation, and the biologics commitment. The implementing bill followed on December 13, 2019.

Labor support converted that transaction into a durable coalition, because its absence had been the defining weakness of continental trade politics since 1993. Mexico had enacted a major labor law reform on May 1, 2019, implementing its 2017 constitutional reform by replacing conciliation boards with labor courts, requiring secret ballot union votes, and requiring legitimation of existing collective contracts. House negotiators treated that reform as necessary but not sufficient, reasoning that a Mexican statute without enforcement machinery in the agreement would rest entirely on Mexican administrative capacity and political will. The protocol supplied the external machinery. The implementing act supplied supporting institutions: an Interagency Labor Committee for Monitoring and Enforcement, co-chaired by the Trade Representative and the Department of Labor, which issued interim procedural guidelines for petitions on June 30, 2020; an Independent Mexico Labor Expert Board to monitor the reform and advise Congress and the executive; labor attachés posted in Mexico; and 180 million dollars for labor reform and technical assistance in Mexico. With that structure in place, the AFL-CIO and most major unions endorsed an agreement of a kind their organizations had opposed for a generation, depriving opponents of the argument that the workers subject to the competition rejected the terms. Business organizations completed the coalition, receiving the continuity on tariffs and supply chains they had sought.

The costs each side accepted are as much a part of the assessment as the benefits each claimed. The executive accepted enforcement machinery it had not sought. The text signed in November 2018 contained no rapid response mechanism, retained the complainant's burden on the trade effects element, left panel formation exposed to obstruction, and carried a 10 year biologics commitment negotiated as an achievement. The administration signed a protocol revising every one of those positions. It accepted monitoring of a partner's labor institutions, with American officials posted on that partner's territory, and benchmarks attached to that partner's domestic reform as continental obligation. The House majority accepted an agreement whose tariff core it had not written. Its working group amended the enforcement architecture and removed the pharmaceutical commitments its members opposed, but the majority then voted overwhelmingly for a text preserving the market access structure of a renegotiation it had not directed. That text retained automotive thresholds and digital trade provisions negotiated by an administration of the opposing party, and it carried the sunset mechanism that administration had championed. Each side paid in a currency the other valued, and the payment is legible between the text of November 2018 and the text of December 2019.

Against that record, the inventory of what the statute did not do is equally specific. It did not restore tariffs on continental trade, and no schedule reimposed duties on the broad categories of goods that had moved duty free under the predecessor. It did not dismantle the integrated supply chains through which components crossed both borders repeatedly during production. Producers organized around continental sourcing faced new origin thresholds, wage based content calculations, and steel and aluminum sourcing conditions, including a melt and pour requirement taking effect over a seven year runway. Those conditions raised the cost of qualifying for preferences and operated within a production system whose existence the text assumed and whose continuation business supporters had made their central objective. The act likewise left the predecessor's basic market access architecture standing. The chapter structure, the preference system, and arrangements such as a government procurement chapter applying only between the United States and Mexico, with Canada relying on the World Trade Organization's Agreement on Government Procurement, carried forward with amendment rather than replacement.

Other questions the statute left deliberately unsettled, and the currency chapter illustrates the pattern. Chapter 33, on macroeconomic policies and exchange rate matters, carried transparency and consultation commitments on currency practices, without trade sanctions of the ordinary kind. No mechanism in the text makes a finding on currency practices trigger the agreement's remedies, because the parties committed to disclosure and discussion rather than enforceable disciplines backed by retaliation. The commitment stopped short of enforcement, so the currency question remained unsettled beyond transparency. The review machinery exhibits the same deferral in structural form. The joint review of July 1, 2026, the annual reviews after a withheld confirmation, and the termination dates attached to them are commitments about future decision points rather than decisions. The statute created occasions on which the three governments must confront the agreement's record, and a consequence if confirmation never arrives. It did not determine what those governments would find. Its output lies in the future by design.

The labor experiment carries the deepest dependence on events outside the statute's own operation. The rapid response mechanism, the benchmarks, the attachés, and the expert board presuppose a transformation inside Mexican institutions: labor courts functioning in place of conciliation boards, secret ballot votes conducted and honored, collective contracts submitted to legitimation by the workers they cover, and officials able to act against resistance to the new rules. The Mexican reform of May 1, 2019 supplied the legal framework, and the implementing act supplied money, monitoring, and a remedy aimed at facilities where the framework failed. What the American statute could not supply was the transformation itself. Congress could appropriate 180 million dollars, station attachés, create a monitoring board, and authorize suspension of liquidation of entries from a facility under complaint while review proceeded. The experiment rests on Mexican institutional implementation that the statute funds and monitors but cannot itself perform.

The final limit on assessment is temporal, and at the vantage of July 1, 2020 it is absolute. The agreement entered into force on the article's date, so every claim about its economic effects available on that date was a projection rather than a measurement. The most authoritative projection in the official record came from the United States International Trade Commission, which estimated in April 2019 that the agreement would raise United States real gross domestic product by 68.2 billion dollars, or 0.35 percent, and employment by about 176,000 jobs, or 0.12 percent, relative to a baseline in which the predecessor remained in place. The Commission placed the largest effects in services and in the sectors governed by the new rules of origin. Both sides quoted the figures in debate, treating a model based estimate produced before the protocol was negotiated as though it measured the enacted text. The estimate addressed the signed text of 2018, and no equivalent official estimate of the amended text stood in the record at entry into force. The enforcement machinery distinguishing the enacted text was the element least susceptible to that estimation, since the effect of a rapid response panel, a reversed burden of proof, or a repaired dispute process depends on whether officials invoke the instruments and how facilities and governments respond. None of that had occurred by July 1, 2020. No petition had been decided, no panel had been formed under the repaired procedures, and no joint review had convened. The statute enacted on January 29, 2020 will be judged, on the schedule its own text prescribes, by machinery that had not operated when the agreement took effect: a tariff settlement of continuity, an enforcement settlement of genuine novelty, a coalition built by exchange, and consequences reserved by design to a future the statute scheduled but did not settle.

The statute took a defined place in the sequence of North American trade law. The North American Free Trade Agreement Implementation Act of 1993 had built the first continental framework on a close vote and on labor and environmental arrangements placed outside the core bargain. Public Law 116-113 replaced that framework on July 1, 2020 with a text that kept the duty free core of continental trade, placed labor and environmental obligations inside the agreement, added a facility specific remedy for denials of association and bargaining rights in Mexico, narrowed investor claims, rebuilt panel formation, and attached a review and termination calendar that the predecessor had lacked. Continuity in market access and novelty in enforcement therefore stood side by side in a single enactment, and the sequence from signature in 2018 through amendment in 2019 to approval in 2020 explained how that combination arose.

Readers placing the statute in the longer run of postwar bargaining will find that [the history of American trade legislation since 1950](/2020/10/15/us-trade-legislation-since-1950/) sets the implementing act against the earlier statutes on authority, objectives, and approval that shaped the procedure of 2015 and the negotiations that followed. That placement mattered because the statute was built to be revisited. The joint review scheduled for July 1, 2026, the sixteen year term running to July 1, 2036, the annual reviews that followed a withheld confirmation, and the three year window for legacy investment claims all turned dates in the text into future tests of the bargain. A reader who tracked those dates against the provisions that generated them held the measure of the act as its authors wrote it, as an approval of a negotiated text, as a set of changes to domestic law, and as a calendar of decisions that the three governments reserved for later years under rules fixed on January 29, 2020.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the USMCA implementation act do?

The United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement Implementation Act approved the agreement signed on November 30, 2018, as amended by the Protocol of Amendment signed on December 10, 2019, and enacted the changes to United States law that the agreement required. President Donald Trump signed the measure on January 29, 2020 as Public Law 116-113. The act created monitoring machinery for labor enforcement, funded assistance for labor reform in Mexico, and approved terms covering rules of origin, investment, digital trade, and dispute settlement. The agreement entered into force on July 1, 2020, replacing the North American Free Trade Agreement as the governing framework for trade among the three countries.

### Q: How did USMCA pass so overwhelmingly?

The implementing bill passed after a second negotiation changed the signed text. The House Democratic majority declined to schedule the bill during 2019 until enforcement concerns had been met, and a working group of House Democrats negotiated changes with United States Trade Representative Robert Lighthizer. The Protocol of Amendment of December 10, 2019 strengthened labor and environmental enforcement, added a facility specific labor mechanism, repaired panel formation, and removed a biologics commitment. The AFL-CIO and most major American unions supported the amended agreement, as did the United States Chamber of Commerce and other business groups. The House passed the bill on December 19, 2019 by 385 to 41, and the Senate passed the bill on January 16, 2020 by 89 to 10.

### Q: What did the December 2019 protocol change in USMCA?

The Protocol of Amendment signed in Mexico City on December 10, 2019 revised the signed text in targeted respects. A panel gained a presumption that a labor or environmental violation affected trade and investment unless the responding party demonstrated otherwise. Language requiring proof of a sustained or recurring pattern for violence against workers was removed, and the forced labor obligation was tightened so that the duty was not self judging. The protocol added the rapid response labor annexes, repaired panel formation, deleted a commitment of at least ten years of data protection for biologic medicines, deleted a new use exclusivity commitment, clarified the regulatory review exception, strengthened the environment chapter, and added benchmarks for labor reform in Mexico.

### Q: What is the rapid response labor mechanism in USMCA?

The rapid response labor mechanism appears in Annex 31-A for the United States and Mexico and Annex 31-B for Canada and Mexico, both added by the Protocol of Amendment to Chapter 31. The mechanism covers facilities in Mexico in priority sectors and protects two rights, freedom of association and collective bargaining. A party with a good faith belief in a denial of rights may ask Mexico to review the facility. Mexico had 10 days to agree to a review and 45 days from the request to investigate and seek remediation. If review failed, a panel of three panelists could verify conditions, including on site, and determine whether a denial existed. Remedies included suspension of preferential tariff treatment for goods from the facility.

### Q: Does USMCA have a sunset clause?

Article 34.7 gave the agreement a term of 16 years from entry into force. The agreement entered into force on July 1, 2020, so the term ran to July 1, 2036 unless each party confirmed in writing, through its head of government, a wish to continue the agreement for a new 16 year term. On July 1, 2026, the sixth anniversary, the Free Trade Commission was scheduled to conduct a joint review of the operation of the agreement. If any party withheld confirmation at a joint review, the parties were to hold joint reviews every year afterward. If confirmation never came, the agreement terminated 10 years after the date of the joint review at which confirmation was withheld.

### Q: What auto content does USMCA require?

Chapter 4 and its automotive appendix raised regional value content for passenger vehicles and light trucks from 62.5 percent under the predecessor to 75 percent, phased in over three years after entry into force, with longer alternative staging for a limited number of vehicles. Labor value content required at least 40 percent of a passenger vehicle value and 45 percent of a truck value to come from production in facilities paying an average base hourly wage of at least 16 dollars, with portions satisfiable through credits for high wage technology and assembly expenditures. At least 70 percent of a vehicle producer steel and aluminum purchases had to originate in North America, and core parts had to meet origin requirements.

### Q: Did USMCA add a digital trade chapter?

Chapter 19 addressed digital trade, a subject the predecessor text had left almost untouched. The chapter prohibited customs duties on electronic transmissions, guaranteed nondiscriminatory treatment of digital products, and protected cross border transfer of information by electronic means. The chapter barred a party from requiring data localization as a condition of doing business and protected against forced disclosure of source code. Paperless trading and open government data provisions completed the chapter. Chapter 19 also included an interactive computer services provision limiting liability for third party content, described in the record of the negotiations as the first such provision in a United States trade agreement, carrying a principle from domestic American law into trade law.

### Q: When did USMCA enter into force?

The agreement entered into force on July 1, 2020. Entry followed completion of internal procedures by all three parties and notification of that completion, with the agreement taking effect on the first day of the second month after the last notification. Canada gave royal assent to its implementing bill on March 13, 2020 and notified last. The United States notified completion of its internal procedures on April 24, 2020, after President Donald Trump signed the implementing act on January 29, 2020. Mexico had ratified the original agreement in June 2019 and approved the protocol in December 2019. On July 1, 2020 the agreement replaced the North American Free Trade Agreement.

### Q: Why was the biologics exclusivity provision removed?

The signed text had required at least ten years of data protection for biologic medicines. The Protocol of Amendment removed that requirement in full, and no exclusivity commitment for biologics remained in the agreement. Each party followed domestic law, set at twelve years in the United States, eight years in Canada, and five years in Mexico. House negotiators objected to locking a ten year floor into a trade agreement, because a treaty floor would constrain later congressional choices on domestic questions surrounding biologic medicines even though the domestic term of twelve years stayed intact. The pharmaceutical industry opposed the removal, arguing that the commitment had formed part of the negotiated balance and that weaker protection discouraged investment.

### Q: Why does investor state dispute settlement end between the United States and Canada?

Chapter 14 eliminated investor state dispute settlement between the United States and Canada. Under the predecessor, a private investor could bring a claim directly against a host government before an international arbitral tribunal and seek monetary damages. The elimination reflected a judgment embedded in the text that investors in two countries with developed court systems and long records of reciprocal investment did not require a private arbitral route around domestic courts. No investor state dispute settlement existed between Canada and Mexico under the agreement either, although those two countries retained such settlement between themselves under the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans Pacific Partnership. Investors in the northern pairing retained access to domestic courts of the host country.

### Q: Can investors bring legacy claims under the predecessor agreement?

Annex 14-C preserved claims under Chapter 11 of the predecessor for legacy investments, meaning investments made while the predecessor was in force and existing at its termination, for three years after the predecessor terminated. The predecessor terminated when the new agreement entered into force on July 1, 2020, so the annex opened a three year window in which a claim resting on a legacy investment could be brought under the predecessor standards. After that window closed, the Chapter 14 framework governed without the legacy overlay. The fixed period balanced reliance by investors whose capital had been committed under the older and broader set of rights against the intention of the three governments to move beyond the predecessor investment regime.

### Q: What does the sixteen dollar wage requirement in the auto rules mean?

The labor value content rule required that a defined share of a vehicle value come from production in facilities paying an average base hourly wage of at least 16 dollars. At least 40 percent of a passenger vehicle value and 45 percent of a truck value had to meet that description. A producer could accumulate qualifying value through high wage production work, and portions of the requirement could be satisfied through credits for high wage technology expenditures and high wage assembly expenditures. The requirement operated inside the rule of origin itself, as a condition a vehicle had to meet before preferential tariff treatment attached, rather than as a general labor standard enforced against a government through the labor chapter.

### Q: What does the melted and poured rule require for steel?

The automotive appendix required at least 70 percent of a vehicle producer steel and aluminum purchases to originate in North America. For steel, an additional discipline applied. Steel had in due course to be melted and poured in North America, and a seven year runway applied to the melt and pour requirement from entry into force on July 1, 2020. The distinction between purchase origin and melt and pour origin closed a route by which steel produced substantially outside the region could acquire originating status through limited regional processing, such as finishing work, without regional melting and pouring. Steel that passed through regional finishing operations alone had a defined period before that steel ceased to count toward the requirement.

### Q: What dairy access did the agreement give the United States in Canada?

Canada opened new tariff rate quota access for American dairy products. The Office of the United States Trade Representative estimated that access would rise to about 3.6 percent of the Canadian dairy market. Canada also agreed to eliminate the Class 6 and Class 7 milk pricing classes within six months of entry into force, a deadline that fell in the second half of 2020. The pricing class elimination removed a mechanism rather than merely enlarging a quota. Canada retained its system of supply management for dairy, so the concession operated as an opening inside a protected system that the agreement otherwise left in place, rather than as a restructuring of Canadian dairy policy as a whole.

### Q: Can a country withdraw from the agreement?

Article 34.6 permitted any party to withdraw from the agreement on six months written notice. Withdrawal was an individual act, available at any time, and effective for the withdrawing party on that short timetable. The provision differed from the sunset mechanism in Article 34.7 in every respect. Withdrawal required an affirmative act by one party and applied to that party participation, while the sunset required no act of withdrawal, applied to the agreement as a whole, and unfolded across review dates and runoff periods measured in years. A party that wished to leave quickly had Article 34.6, a device carried over from standard practice in earlier agreements, including the predecessor agreement between the three countries.

### Q: What did the agreement change about environmental enforcement?

The Protocol of Amendment revised Chapter 24 to require each party to adopt, maintain, and implement laws fulfilling specified multilateral environmental agreements to which that party belonged. The listed agreements included the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species, the Montreal Protocol, and the MARPOL convention on pollution from ships. The protocol applied the same burden shifting presumption used for labor claims, so a panel presumed that an environmental violation affected trade and investment unless the responding party demonstrated otherwise. Naming particular agreements gave a panel a defined obligation to apply, tied to the domestic statute that implemented each commitment. The protocol also expanded monitoring and enforcement machinery for the chapter in a manner analogous to the labor changes.

### Q: What labor reform did Mexico pass as part of the bargain?

Mexico enacted a major labor law reform on May 1, 2019, implementing a constitutional reform adopted in 2017. The reform replaced conciliation boards with labor courts situated within the judiciary and created institutions for conciliation and for registration of unions and collective contracts. Union leaders were to be elected through the personal, free, and secret vote of the membership. The reform required legitimation of existing collective bargaining contracts, under which workers covered by a contract voted by secret ballot on whether to approve the agreement under which they worked. House negotiators in Washington treated enactment of the reform as necessary but not sufficient, and the protocol and the implementing act added benchmarks, monitoring, and funding in response.

### Q: Can a country block a dispute panel under the agreement?

A responding party could not block a Chapter 31 panel by withholding agreement on appointments. Experience under the predecessor had shown that a respondent could frustrate panel formation by letting selection periods lapse, leaving a complaint without a tribunal. The Protocol of Amendment repaired that defect at the formation stage. A panel was established on request of the complaining party, standing rosters of panelists were maintained in advance, and where the parties could not agree on appointments, selection could proceed by lot from the roster. Selection by lot removed the veto that silence or refusal had supplied. A respondent retained every right to argue jurisdiction, contest the facts, and challenge the legal reading offered by a complainant before the panel.

### Q: Does the agreement ban data localization requirements?

Chapter 19 barred a party from requiring data localization, meaning the use of computing facilities inside its own territory, as a condition of doing business there. The chapter also protected cross border transfer of information by electronic means, prohibited customs duties on electronic transmissions, and guaranteed nondiscriminatory treatment of digital products. A government could not demand access to the source code of software as a condition of importation, distribution, or sale, subject to the exceptions the text specified. Paperless trading and open government data provisions completed the chapter. The data localization bar formed part of a wider digital settlement that the predecessor text had left almost untouched, because the commerce in question barely existed in commercial form in the early nineteen nineties.

### Q: How is the implementing statute cited?

The implementing statute is cited as the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement Implementation Act, Public Law 116-113, 134 Stat. 11, enacted by the 116th Congress. The measure began in the House of Representatives as H.R. 5430, with a Senate companion designated S. 3052. The Committee on Ways and Means reported the House bill as H. Rept. 116-358. The House passed the bill on December 19, 2019 and the Senate passed the bill on January 16, 2020. The bill was presented to the President on January 22, 2020, and President Donald Trump signed the measure on January 29, 2020. A citation to the public law number or to the Statutes at Large volume and page identifies the enacted statute.
