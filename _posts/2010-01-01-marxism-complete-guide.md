Almost every public argument about Marxism is unwinnable, and the reason is structural rather than political. One person is attacking a theory of history. The other is defending a critique of how profit arises inside a factory. A third has in mind a set of twentieth-century governments. A fourth is thinking about a method of literary interpretation taught in a university seminar. All four are using the same word, all four are talking about something real, and none of them is talking about the same thing. The argument cannot resolve because there is no shared proposition on the table.

That is the problem this guide exists to fix. What follows separates Marxism into six components that can be held, attacked, or defended independently, shows which text each rests on, states the strongest standing objection to each, and points to the article in this series that treats it in full. The claim is not that the six are unrelated. They were developed by the same two people over four decades and they lean on each other constantly. The claim is that they are separable: you can accept the theory of exploitation and reject the theory of history, and many serious economists have; you can accept the theory of ideology and reject the labour theory of value, and much of twentieth-century cultural theory did exactly that; you can find the analysis of capitalism powerful and regard the Soviet Union as a catastrophe with no bearing on the analysis at all, which was the position of a large part of the Western left for seventy years.

Once the components are separated, the standard conversational moves stop working. The claim that Marxism has been refuted by the collapse of a state apparatus stops being an argument and becomes a question about which component that collapse bears on. The claim that Marx predicted everything correctly stops being a slogan and becomes a demand to specify which prediction, in which text, at which level of strength. The first move in any serious discussion is to ask which of the six is actually in dispute. Call it the component-confusion rule: most arguments about Marxism fail not because the participants are unreasonable but because they never establish what the argument is about.

## What Marxism actually is, stated precisely

Marxism is a research tradition, not a doctrine. It originates in the writings of Karl Marx, who lived from 1818 to 1883, and Friedrich Engels, who lived from 1820 to 1895, and it consists of a linked set of theoretical claims about how societies are organised, how they change, how wealth is produced and distributed under capitalism, how ideas relate to material interests, and how political power is structured. The tradition includes the original writings, the disputes among their inheritors, the revisions those disputes produced, and the research programmes that grew out of them in economics, history, sociology, anthropology, geography, and literary criticism.

The word "tradition" is doing real work in that sentence. A doctrine has a fixed content that adherents accept or reject. A research tradition has a shared set of questions, a shared vocabulary, a set of characteristic hypotheses, and vigorous internal disagreement about which of those hypotheses is correct. Marxists have disagreed with each other, in print and at length, about whether the rate of profit tends to fall, whether the state is an instrument of a ruling class or a structure with its own logic, whether the theory of value is a theory of price determination or a theory of social form, whether revolution requires a vanguard party, whether a peasantry can be a revolutionary agent, whether the Soviet Union was socialist, whether Marxism has a moral theory, and whether the young Marx and the mature Marx wrote the same philosophy. Those are not marginal quarrels. They are the substance of the tradition, and any account that presents Marxism as a settled body of belief has misdescribed the thing it is describing.

That said, a tradition without content is not a tradition. Four commitments recur across almost every version, and they are what make the disagreements internal rather than a case of unrelated people sharing a label. The first is that the way a society organises the production of its material life is explanatorily fundamental to the rest of its structure, not merely one factor among many. The second is that the central productive relationships in class societies are relationships of appropriation, in which one group lives on labour performed by another, and that this is a structural feature rather than a moral aberration. The third is that the conflict generated by those relationships is the principal engine of large-scale historical change. The fourth is that the prevailing ideas of a period are not free-floating but stand in a systematic relationship to those productive arrangements and to the interests they serve. Take away any one of the four and you have something else. What varies enormously across the tradition is how strongly each is asserted and by what mechanism it is supposed to work.

### Is Marxism the same thing as communism?

No. Marxism is a body of social and economic theory; communism names both a projected form of society without class division and, in ordinary usage since the twentieth century, the political movements and states that claimed the label. A person can accept Marxist analysis and reject communist politics, and many academics do. Treating the two words as interchangeable is the commonest error on the subject.

The distinction matters more than it looks. Marx and Engels used communism to name a possible future society in which the productive resources of society are held in common and the division of labour no longer assigns people to lifelong roles. They wrote very little about how such a society would be institutionally organised, and Marx explicitly refused to supply blueprints, dismissing the writing of recipes for the cookshops of the future as a task he would not undertake. The twentieth-century use of communism, referring to the parties and states of the Soviet type, is a later and much more specific thing, and it carries institutional commitments to a vanguard party, to democratic centralism, and to a particular model of central planning that are absent from Marx entirely. When someone says that communism has failed, they may be making a claim about the Soviet economic model, a claim about the political form of one-party states, or a claim about the moral desirability of common ownership. Those are three different claims requiring three different arguments, and only the third bears on anything Marx wrote at length.

## Where the six components come from in the texts

The six components are not evenly distributed across Marx's output, and knowing where each is developed is what separates a reader who can cite from a reader who can only gesture. The theory of history has its compressed canonical statement in the preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy, published in 1859, which runs to a few pages and contains the entire architecture: the correspondence between relations of production and a stage of development of the productive forces, the base and superstructure formulation, the fetters clause describing what happens when the relations turn from forms of development into constraints, the claim that consciousness must be explained by the material contradictions of life rather than the reverse, and the closing restraint that no social order disappears before the productive forces it can accommodate are exhausted. The first extended development is in The German Ideology, written between 1845 and 1846 and unpublished in Marx and Engels's lifetimes, which supplies the argument about consciousness and material life that the 1859 statement compresses.

The critique of political economy is the work of the last two decades of Marx's life and is concentrated in Capital, whose first volume appeared in 1867 and is the only volume Marx saw through the press. The Grundrisse, the notebooks written in 1857 and 1858 and not published until the twentieth century, contains the fullest treatment of several arguments that the finished volume compresses or drops, including the material on pre-capitalist economic formations and the celebrated passage on machinery and general social knowledge. The second and third volumes of Capital were assembled from manuscripts by Engels after Marx's death and published in 1885 and 1894 respectively, which is a fact of real consequence: an argument built on the third volume is built on an editor's arrangement of unfinished material, and any writer who cites it as though Marx had prepared it for publication has made a sourcing error a specialist will notice immediately.

The theory of class is dispersed rather than concentrated, which is why it generates so much confusion. There is the political statement of it in the Manifesto of the Communist Party of 1848, which opens on the history of hitherto existing society as a history of class struggles and which is a piece of movement writing rather than analysis. There is the historical application in The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte, written in 1852, which contains Marx's most sophisticated treatment of how classes acquire or fail to acquire political capacity, and which describes the French smallholding peasantry as forming a class in the sense of shared economic conditions while lacking the organisation and shared identity that would make them a political force. There is the analytical treatment scattered through Capital. And there is the famous unfinished chapter at the very end of the third volume, which poses the question of what constitutes a class and breaks off after roughly a page. Marx never completed a systematic theory of class, and the tradition has been arguing about how to complete it ever since.

The theory of ideology begins in The German Ideology, where the argument appears that the ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas, and it develops through the analysis of commodity fetishism in the first chapter of Capital, which is a different and more interesting argument: that under commodity production, relations between people take on the appearance of relations between things, and that this appearance is not a mistake in anyone's head but a real feature of how the system presents itself. The theory of the state and politics is genuinely underdeveloped in Marx and has to be assembled from the historical writings, from The Eighteenth Brumaire, from The Civil War in France of 1871 on the Paris Commune, and from the Critique of the Gotha Programme, written in 1875 and published by Engels in 1891, which contains the compressed remarks on the transition period and on distribution that later became the object of enormous exegetical labour.

The sixth component, the twentieth-century state projects, comes from a different order of source entirely: the political writings of Lenin, the institutional record of the Soviet Union and the states modelled on it, the Chinese revolution and what followed it, and the historiography of all of these. Marx died in 1883 and had no part in any of it. This guide treats that component as a component because refusing to treat it as one is dishonest, since it is what most people mean by the word, but it keeps the analytical question distinct from the historical one throughout.

### Which text should a beginner read first?

Not Capital. Start with the Manifesto for the political statement and the rhetoric, then the 1859 preface for the theory of history in compressed form, then Wage Labour and Capital or Value, Price and Profit for the economics in accessible form. Capital's first volume rewards a reader who arrives with that grounding and punishes one who does not.

## How the position developed across Marx's own writing

Treating Marx as the author of a single unchanging position produces bad readings in both directions, and the developmental picture is one of the highest-value things a reader can carry away from an orientation article. The early writings, principally the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844 and the material Marx and Engels produced in the middle of that decade, are organised around a philosophical anthropology: the concept of alienation, the idea of species-being, a conception of human beings as creatures who realise themselves through productive activity and who are deformed when that activity is taken from them. The vocabulary is Hegelian and Feuerbachian, and the argument is at least partly moral in structure even where Marx denies that it is.

The mature economic writings do not use that vocabulary. Alienation as a term recedes sharply after the mid-1840s, and Capital is organised around categories that are analytical rather than anthropological: the commodity, the value form, labour power, surplus value, constant and variable capital, the organic composition of capital. Whether this constitutes a break or a development is one of the genuine disputes in the literature. The strong break reading, associated above all with Louis Althusser writing in the 1960s, holds that an epistemological rupture separates the ideological early writings from the scientific mature work, and that reading the one through the other is a category error. The continuity reading, held by a large body of scholarship and given enormous impetus by the belated publication of the 1844 manuscripts in 1932 and of the Grundrisse in the middle of the twentieth century, holds that the same problem drives both bodies of work and that the later categories are the earlier concerns given precise economic content. The Grundrisse is the crucial evidence for the continuity side, because it is a mature text, written in 1857 and 1858, that uses the early vocabulary freely.

A reader does not have to settle that dispute, but a reader does have to know it exists, because a great deal of writing about Marxism is quietly committed to one side of it without saying so. An article that explains Marxism entirely through alienation and self-realisation has adopted the continuity reading. An article that explains it entirely through surplus value and the laws of motion of capital has adopted the break reading. The [full treatment of how the concept of history was worked out](/2010/01/15/historical-materialism-complete-guide/) across these texts belongs to the historical materialism pillar, which takes the 1859 preface clause by clause and shows how the theory hardened between the 1840s and the 1860s.

There is a second developmental fact of comparable importance and much less general awareness. In the last decade of his life Marx became markedly more cautious about the universality of his own scheme. In 1877 he wrote to the editorial board of a Russian journal objecting to the conversion of his sketch of Western European development into a universal historico-philosophical theory whose supreme virtue was that it stood above history. In 1881 he drafted several replies to Vera Zasulich on the question of whether the Russian peasant commune might provide a route to a socialist form that did not require Russia to pass through capitalist development first, and the drafts entertain that possibility seriously. The late Marx is not the confident stage theorist of the textbook summaries, and the two documents just named are the strongest available correction to the stagist misreading.

## The six components of Marxism, one at a time

What follows treats each component as a standalone claim: what it asserts, which text carries it, what it forbids, and the strongest objection currently standing against it. Each ends by routing to the pillar in this series that owns the topic in full, because the purpose of a head-term guide is orientation rather than adjudication.

### The theory of history

The claim is that the fundamental explanation of large-scale historical change lies in the development of society's productive capacities and in the conflict between those capacities and the social relationships organising their use. Societies develop their capacity to produce. The relationships that organise production, above all the relationships of ownership and control that determine who commands whose labour, at some point stop facilitating that development and start obstructing it. The resulting tension is resolved through a period of social transformation in which the obstructing relationships give way. The political, legal, and ideological arrangements of a society are to be explained by reference to this productive foundation rather than treated as autonomous.

What this component forbids is worth stating precisely, because a theory that forbids nothing explains nothing. It forbids explaining a durable institutional order purely by reference to the ideas of its participants, with no account of how the order reproduces itself materially. It forbids treating legal and political forms as the ultimate cause of the economic arrangements they codify. It forbids explanations of major social transformations that make no reference to the productive interests of any social group. Those are real prohibitions, and a good deal of ordinary historical writing violates them.

The standing objection is a problem about mechanism rather than about evidence, and it is the sharpest thing said against historical materialism in the modern literature. The theory says, in effect, that relations of production obtain because they develop the productive forces. That is a functional explanation, of the form that a thing exists because of a beneficial effect it has. Functional explanations are legitimate in biology because natural selection supplies the mechanism connecting the effect to the persistence of the trait. Historical materialism has no equivalent mechanism specified. G. A. Cohen, defending the theory in 1978 in what is still the most rigorous reconstruction of it, argued that consequence laws can be legitimate even where the mechanism is not yet known. Jon Elster, attacking in 1985, argued that a functional explanation without a specified mechanism is not an explanation at all, and that Marxist historiography routinely slides into treating the beneficial consequence as though it were itself the cause. The objection lands. The reply is not empty, but the mechanism gap is real, and honest defenders concede it. The [complete guide to historical materialism](/2010/01/15/historical-materialism-complete-guide/) sets out six versions of the theory from strongest to weakest and shows which version any given attack or defence is actually addressing.

### The critique of political economy

This is the analytical core of the mature work, and it is a critique in a specific sense: not a complaint about capitalism but an attempt to show that the categories in which classical political economy described the economy conceal the social relationship generating the phenomena it describes. The argument runs through the analysis of the commodity as a unity of use value and value, the derivation of the money form from the exchange relation, and then the central move: the distinction between labour and labour power. What the worker sells is not labour but the capacity to labour, over a defined period, at a price determined like other commodity prices by what it costs to reproduce it. What the purchaser obtains is the use of that capacity, and the value it can create in the working day exceeds the value of the capacity itself. The difference is surplus value, and it is the source of profit, interest, and rent alike.

The consequence is that exploitation, in Marx's usage, is a technical rather than a moral term. It names a quantitative relationship between necessary and surplus labour time, and it obtains whether or not the wage is fair, whether or not the employer is decent, and whether or not the worker feels aggrieved. This is the single most misunderstood point in the entire tradition. Marx is not primarily saying that workers are paid too little. He is saying that a system in which labour power is a commodity produces a surplus appropriated by the owners of the means of production, and that this is what the system is, not a defect in it.

The standing objections here are the most technically developed in any of the six. The transformation problem, opened by Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk in 1896, concerns the relationship between the value magnitudes of the first volume and the price magnitudes of the third, and whether Marx's own procedure for converting one into the other is internally consistent. The marginalist alternative, developed independently in the 1870s by Jevons, Menger, and Walras, offers a theory of price formation that requires no labour theory of value at all and that became the mainstream of economics. And there is the question of whether the labour theory of value is empirically testable and, if so, what the price and labour content correlation studies actually show. None of these is settled by assertion in either direction. The [complete guide to the first volume of Capital](/2010/06/01/capital-volume-one-complete-guide/) works through the argument chapter by chapter and separates what is required for the theory of exploitation from what depends on the more contested claims about price.

### The theory of class

The claim is that the members of a society are structurally divided by their relationship to the means of production, that this division generates opposed material interests, and that the resulting conflict is the principal dynamic of political life in class societies. Class in this sense is not a category of income, status, education, accent, or consumption. It is a relational position: whether you own productive assets sufficient to live on, whether you must sell your capacity to work in order to live, and whether you command the labour of others.

Two distinctions do most of the analytical work. The first is between class as an objective structural position and class as a collective political agent, which is the difference between a group of people who share a situation and a group of people who recognise the situation, organise around it, and act. Marx marks this in his own way in The Eighteenth Brumaire with the discussion of the French peasantry, though the familiar formulation of class in itself and class for itself is not his phrasing and derives from the later tradition, principally from Georg Lukács in the 1920s. Attributing that pair of terms to Marx directly is a small error that costs credibility with specialists and marks in examinations. The second distinction is between the two-class model of the Manifesto, which is a polemical simplification, and the far more differentiated analyses in the historical and economic writings, which are populated by landlords, financiers, the petty bourgeoisie, artisans, peasants, and a stratum Marx calls the lumpenproletariat.

The standing objection is that occupational and property structures in developed economies have not polarised in the way the theory anticipated. The middle strata expanded rather than being ground down, pension and share ownership complicated the ownership criterion, and the largest firms separated ownership from control in ways that complicate the identification of a capitalist class. Marxist class analysis responded with a substantial technical literature on contradictory class locations and on the distinction between economic ownership and possession, which is serious work rather than evasion, but the objection is a real one and the [complete guide to class in Marxist theory](/2011/09/01/marxist-theory-of-class-guide/) states it at full strength before assessing the replies.

### The theory of ideology

The claim is that systems of belief stand in a systematic and explicable relationship to the material organisation of society, and that this relationship is not one that the holders of the beliefs typically perceive. There are at least three distinct versions of the claim in the tradition and they are frequently run together to nobody's benefit.

The first is the interest version from The German Ideology: the class that controls material production also controls mental production, so the ideas that circulate as general truths tend to be ideas serving the interests of that class. The second is the appearance version from the first chapter of Capital, which is more subtle and more interesting: under generalised commodity production, the social relationships between producers genuinely appear as relationships between things, because that is how the exchange of products actually presents them. This is not false belief imposed by anyone. It is a real appearance generated by a real structure, which is why Marx calls it a fetishism rather than an error. The third is the reproduction version developed in the twentieth century, above all by Louis Althusser, in which ideology is less a body of ideas than a set of practices and institutions through which people are constituted as subjects who fit the positions the system requires.

Two corrections belong here. The phrase false consciousness, universally attributed to Marx in secondary literature, does not appear in Marx. It appears in a letter Engels wrote to Franz Mehring in 1893, and its subsequent career is largely independent of both. And the version of the theory that treats ideology as deliberate deception practised by a conscious ruling class on a duped population is the weakest version available and is not what the interesting texts argue. The standing objection is the charge of condescension: that ideology critique claims privileged access to the real interests of people who disagree with the analyst, and that it makes disagreement into evidence of the condition it diagnoses, which is unfalsifiable. That objection has force and is taken seriously inside the tradition. The [complete guide to the Marxist theory of ideology](/2012/02/01/marxist-theory-of-ideology-guide/) separates the versions and assesses each.

### The theory of the state and politics

The claim, in its simplest form, is that the state in a class society is not a neutral arbiter standing above competing interests but is structured by, and functions to reproduce, the prevailing relations of production. The Manifesto puts this at its most compressed and most vulnerable, describing the executive of the modern state as a committee managing the common affairs of the whole bourgeoisie, which is a slogan rather than an analysis. Marx's own historical writing is much more sophisticated. The Eighteenth Brumaire analyses a regime that is not the direct instrument of any class and that acquires substantial autonomy precisely because the contending classes have fought each other to a standstill. The Civil War in France treats the Paris Commune of 1871 as evidence about the institutional form a workers' state might take, and it is the closest Marx comes to institutional prescription.

This component is the least developed in Marx and the most developed after him, which is why the twentieth-century literature on it is so much larger than the primary material. The instrumentalist and structuralist positions, argued out between Ralph Miliband and Nicos Poulantzas in the 1960s and 1970s, remain the standard axis of the debate: whether the state serves capital because its personnel are drawn from and connected to the capitalist class, or because its structural position within a capitalist economy constrains what any government can do regardless of who staffs it. The standing objection is that the theory has no adequate account of the constitutional state, of the extension of the franchise, of the welfare settlement, or of the persistence of political liberties that a purely functional account of the state would not predict. The [complete guide to the Marxist theory of the state](/2012/12/01/marxist-theory-of-the-state-guide/) treats the positions in full.

### The twentieth-century state projects

The sixth component is different in kind from the other five, and treating it as though it were another theoretical claim is the source of a great deal of bad argument. It consists of the record of states that governed in the name of Marxism: the Soviet Union from 1917 to 1991, the People's Republic of China from 1949, and the states of Eastern Europe, East and Southeast Asia, Cuba, and elsewhere that adopted variants of the same model. That record includes industrialisation from a low base, mass literacy campaigns, and improvements in some measured health and education indicators. It also includes famine, mass deportation, forced labour camps, political executions, and the suppression of dissent, on scales that historians continue to estimate and dispute using archival material that became accessible only in stages.

This series reports that record accurately and without euphemism, and it also refuses to let the record settle questions it cannot settle. The reason is a rule that holds in both directions. A theory is not vindicated by a regime's achievements and is not refuted by its crimes; both require their own arguments. If a Soviet literacy campaign succeeded, that is a fact about a policy and its implementation, not a confirmation of the labour theory of value. If a famine occurred under a collectivisation drive, that is a fact of the first importance about that policy, about the political system that made it possible, and about what the pursuit of a transformation programme through an unaccountable state apparatus can produce, and it is a fact any serious person must reckon with. Whether it also refutes the 1859 preface is a separate question requiring a separate argument, and the argument has to be made rather than assumed.

The connective question, which is the interesting one, is whether the theory contains features that made such outcomes more likely: whether a theory that treats the state as an instrument to be captured, that has no developed theory of political accountability, and that treats moral constraints as ideological, is one whose adoption predictably tends in a particular institutional direction. That is a real argument, it is made by serious critics, and it is answered by serious defenders. It is the argument that matters, and it is not conducted by counting either the literacy statistics or the death tolls, which is why this guide asserts no figure for either. The [complete guide to the critiques of Marxism](/2019/08/01/critiques-of-marxism-complete-guide/) is where that argument is conducted at length.

## Marx, Marxism, and the gap between them

The word Marxism postdates Marx. It came into general use after his death in 1883, principally through the German Social Democratic Party and then through the Russian movement, at a moment when a body of writing that Marx had left substantially unfinished was being converted into a platform capable of organising a mass party. That conversion is not a footnote. It is the single most consequential fact about how the subject reaches a modern reader, because almost everything most people know as Marxism was formalised in the two decades after Marx stopped writing, by people with a political organisation to build.

Engels reports that Marx, confronted with the doctrines of certain French adherents, remarked that if that was Marxism, then he himself was not a Marxist. The remark surfaces in Engels's correspondence, in letters to Eduard Bernstein and to Conrad Schmidt, and the honest way to handle it is to attribute it exactly as it comes down: reported by Engels, not documented as an utterance. Presented that way it is still worth having, because it records that the gap between Marx and the systematising of Marx was visible to both of them while Marx was alive.

Engels's own popularising work did much of the systematising. Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, published in 1880 and extracted from his longer polemic against Eugen Dühring, is the text through which the theory reached the largest number of readers in the nineteenth century, and it presents the material with a confidence and a completeness that the sources do not always support. Engels's later correspondence complicates this considerably. In letters to Joseph Bloch in 1890, to Conrad Schmidt in the same year, to Franz Mehring in 1893, and to Heinz Starkenburg in 1894, he insists that neither he nor Marx ever claimed the economic element was the only determining one, and describes the historical process as one in which political, legal, and ideological forms exert reciprocal influence. Whether those letters constitute a retreat from an earlier determinism or a restatement of what the 1859 preface already said is a live scholarly question, and the answer matters, because the two readings license quite different versions of the theory.

Lenin's essay of 1913 on the three sources and three component parts of Marxism supplies the organising scheme that dominated twentieth-century teaching: German classical philosophy, English political economy, and French socialism, synthesised into a single doctrine. It is a lucid piece of exposition and a useful heuristic, and it is not Marx's own account of what he was doing. Presenting it as a self-description, which competing summaries do routinely, imports a specific and politically shaped reading as though it were neutral description. Whenever a source presents Marxism as having exactly three parts, it is reproducing Lenin.

### Did Karl Marx ever have a job?

Yes. Marx worked as a journalist for most of his adult life, editing newspapers in Germany in the 1840s and serving through the 1850s and into the early 1860s as a European correspondent for an American newspaper, which was his principal source of income for years. He was also supported financially by Engels, who worked in his family's Manchester firm.

The claim that Marx never worked circulates widely and is straightforwardly false, but the more interesting point is what the journalism did to the theory. Marx's newspaper work required him to write regularly about British politics, Indian administration, Chinese trade, the American civil war, European diplomacy, and the commercial crises of the 1850s, and the resulting body of writing is a large part of his output. The commercial crisis of 1857 is what prompted him to begin the notebooks now published as the Grundrisse, in the belief that the crisis might be the decisive one and that he had better get his economics onto paper. The [intellectual biography of Karl Marx](/2013/05/01/karl-marx-intellectual-biography/) treats the life in the detail this orientation guide deliberately avoids, organised around the ideas rather than the calendar.

## The standard misreadings, named and corrected

A guide that only states positions leaves the reader defenceless against the versions already circulating. Six misreadings do most of the damage, and each has a specific correction.

The first is that Marxism and communism are interchangeable words, addressed above. The second is that Marxism is a doctrine rather than a research tradition, which produces the peculiar spectacle of a critique that refutes one Marxist position by citing a different Marxist position, as though internal disagreement were embarrassing rather than normal. Every research tradition contains disagreement; the presence of Marxists who reject the falling rate of profit no more refutes Marxism than the presence of economists who reject rational expectations refutes economics.

The third is that Marx designed the Soviet system. He did not, and the textual position is unusually clear. Marx wrote almost nothing prescriptive about post-capitalist institutions, and the little he wrote is scattered and hedged: some remarks in the Critique of the Gotha Programme about distribution in an early phase and a later one, some observations on the Commune, a refusal in the afterword to Capital to write recipes for the cookshops of the future. The institutional apparatus of the twentieth-century socialist states, meaning the vanguard party, democratic centralism, the nomenklatura, the central planning ministry, and the five-year plan, was developed between the 1900s and the 1930s by people responding to conditions Marx never analysed. This does not make the connective question uninteresting. It makes the direct-design claim false.

The fourth is that Marxism abolishes personal property. The Manifesto addresses this objection directly and the answer is that the target is bourgeois property, meaning productive property that yields command over the labour of others, not the possessions a person uses. The distinction is between property in the means of production and property in articles of consumption, and it is drawn explicitly in the text that is most often cited to deny it. This is the single easiest correction to make and the one that most reliably changes a conversation.

The fifth is that Marx was an armchair theorist with no contact with the world he described, dealt with above. The sixth is that Marxism is only about economics. The theory of ideology and the theory of the state do independent work, and a great deal of the twentieth-century tradition, from Gramsci through the Frankfurt School to the cultural studies that came out of Birmingham, is concerned with precisely the questions that a purely economic reading cannot accommodate. Reducing the tradition to its economics is a move that critics and enthusiasts make with equal frequency and equal inaccuracy.

### Does Marxism mean the government owns everything?

No. Marx's target is private ownership of the means of production, and state ownership is one possible alternative among several, not the position itself. The tradition contains sustained arguments for cooperative, communal, and associational forms, and a substantial body of Marxist writing treats state ownership under an unaccountable bureaucracy as a different problem rather than a solution.

## The strongest objection to Marxism as a whole

Under the rule that governs this series, the objection is stated at full strength, in the terms its own defenders use, before any reply.

The objection is that Marxism is a framework so elastic that it accommodates every outcome and therefore forbids none. When revolution occurs in an advanced industrial economy, the theory is confirmed. When it occurs instead in an agrarian one, the theory is confirmed by way of a supplementary account of uneven development and the weakest link. When it fails to occur anywhere, the theory is confirmed by way of hegemony, ideological state apparatuses, and the incorporation of the working class. When workers become more prosperous, this is the temporary result of imperial superprofits. When they become poorer, this is immiseration proceeding on schedule. Every possible observation has an available Marxist reading, and a framework with that property is not a scientific theory but a hermeneutic, comparable to psychoanalysis in the same respect, which is exactly the comparison Karl Popper drew when he made this objection its most famous form.

The objection has a second and sharper edge. Where Marxism did stake specific predictions, its record is poor in exactly the places it committed most. The predicted polarisation of the class structure into two camps did not occur in the developed economies. The predicted immiseration of the working class, on any straightforward reading of that claim, did not occur. Revolutions in the advanced economies did not occur; the ones that did occurred in economies the theory identified as unready. The tendency of the rate of profit to fall has not produced the terminal crisis. A defender who reclassifies each of these as a tendency subject to counteracting influences is performing exactly the manoeuvre the first part of the objection describes.

That is the objection at full strength. Three things can be said in reply, and it is worth being clear that they mitigate rather than dissolve it.

The first is that each component does forbid things, as set out above, and those prohibitions are real and are violated by ordinary historical and economic writing. A framework that forbids the explanation of durable institutional orders by ideas alone, that forbids treating the legal form of property as its own foundation, and that forbids treating profit as a return to a factor without asking who performs the labour, is not a framework compatible with any observation whatever.

The second is that the elasticity charge, applied consistently, would indict a great deal of social science, including most macroeconomics, most of the sociology of institutions, and every theory of history. Auxiliary hypotheses are how research programmes handle anomalies, and the question is whether the auxiliaries are independently motivated and generate new predictions or whether they are purely protective. That is a question to be answered case by case rather than by a blanket verdict, and the answer differs sharply across the six components. The account of uneven development is a serious body of analysis with independent content. Some of the immiseration reclassifications are frankly protective.

The third is that the prediction failures should be conceded plainly rather than managed. They are failures. The honest position is that the analytical apparatus of the critique of political economy has proved more durable than the historical forecasts built on it, that the theory of ideology and the theory of the state have been rebuilt substantially since Marx, and that the theory of history in its strong form has fewer defenders inside the tradition than outside critics generally realise. Whether what remains is enough is precisely the question, and it is argued out at length in the [complete guide to the critiques of Marxism](/2019/08/01/critiques-of-marxism-complete-guide/) rather than settled here.

## Where Marxism operates in contemporary scholarship

A reader trying to judge whether any of this is live needs to know where the tradition currently does work, and the honest answer is that it is unevenly distributed and largely detached from party politics.

In history, the influence is deepest and least controversial. The British Marxist historians who worked from the 1940s onward, including Christopher Hill on the English revolution, Eric Hobsbawm on the long nineteenth century, E. P. Thompson on the formation of the working class, and Rodney Hilton on medieval agrarian society, reshaped the discipline's sense of what counts as a historical subject, and history from below is now a mainstream methodological commitment held by historians with no interest in the politics. The transition debates about how feudalism gave way to capitalism, running from the Dobb and Sweezy exchange of the 1950s through the Brenner debate opened in 1976, remain the reference points for anyone working on European agrarian change.

In sociology, class analysis, the sociology of work, and the study of social reproduction carry Marxist categories, often under other names. In geography, the analysis of uneven spatial development and the production of space is a substantially Marxist research programme. In anthropology, the articulation of modes of production supplied the framework for a generation of work on colonial and postcolonial economies. In literary and cultural studies, the influence is so thoroughly absorbed that its origin is frequently invisible: the assumption that a text stands in an explicable relation to the social conditions of its production is a Marxist assumption that survived long after the theory of value was abandoned by the people using it.

In economics, the position is more marginal and more contested. Marxian economics exists as a heterodox research programme with its own journals and its own quantitative literature on profit rates, labour shares, and the empirical relation between prices and labour content. It sits outside the discipline's mainstream, and describing it otherwise would be inaccurate. What is worth noting is that several concepts developed inside the tradition, including the analysis of technological unemployment, the study of labour process and workplace control, and the treatment of crises as endogenous to accumulation rather than as external shocks, have counterparts in mainstream work that arrived at them independently.

### Why is Marxism still taught in universities?

Because a large body of consequential social science was built with these categories, and a student who cannot read it is cut off from major work in history, sociology, geography, and cultural studies. Teaching a framework is not endorsing it, and the same departments teach Weber, Durkheim, Hayek, and Foucault under exactly the same rationale.

## What Marxism is not: the neighbours it gets confused with

Orientation requires boundaries as much as content, and several traditions sit close enough to Marxism that they are routinely absorbed into it by people who mean something looser than they say.

Social democracy in its modern sense is the position that capitalism should be regulated, taxed, and supplemented by a welfare state rather than replaced. Historically it grew out of the same movement, and the parties that carry the name were Marxist in their founding programmes, which is why the confusion has a genuine pedigree. The decisive break came with the revisionist controversy at the end of the nineteenth century, in which Eduard Bernstein argued that the empirical trends Marx expected were not materialising and that the movement should pursue reform openly rather than as a stage towards a revolution it no longer expected. Rosa Luxemburg's reply is the classic statement of the opposing case. What distinguishes the mature social democratic position from Marxism is not gradualism as such, since gradualist Marxisms exist, but the abandonment of the claim that the wage relation is the source of the problem. A politics that seeks a fairer distribution of the product without disputing the relation that generates the product is not a slower Marxism; it is a different analysis.

Anarchism shares the objection to the existing property order and rejects the Marxist account of the state. The dispute is old and specific: it broke the First International in the early 1870s in the confrontation between Marx and Mikhail Bakunin, and the substance of it was whether the seizure of state power could be a route to a stateless society or whether an apparatus of that kind reproduces domination regardless of who holds it. Bakunin's prediction that a revolutionary state would produce a new governing stratum wearing the vocabulary of liberation is among the most cited passages in the anarchist tradition precisely because of what followed in the twentieth century. Whether the Marxist reply survives that record is a live argument and not one this guide settles.

Utopian socialism, meaning the tradition of Saint-Simon, Fourier, and Owen, is what Engels defined Marxism against in the 1880 pamphlet. The distinction he drew was between socialism arrived at by designing a better arrangement and socialism derived from an analysis of the tendencies of the existing one. The distinction flatters the second term, and it is Engels's framing rather than a neutral one, but it identifies something real: Marx's refusal to describe the future society is a methodological commitment, not an oversight.

The phrase cultural Marxism requires separate handling because it is a term with two distinct careers and confusing them produces bad writing. There is a descriptive scholarly usage referring to the turn of Western Marxist thought towards culture, ideology, and consciousness, associated with Lukács, Gramsci, and the Frankfurt School, adopted because the predicted revolutions in the advanced economies had not occurred and the reasons appeared to lie in the domain of consent rather than coercion. That usage names a real intellectual development. There is also a polemical usage in contemporary political argument, in which the phrase designates an alleged deliberate programme to undermine social institutions, and that usage carries a documented history running through mid-twentieth-century propaganda that a writer should know about before deploying the phrase. The two usages are not the same, and an article that treats the second as though it were the first, or vice versa, has misled its reader. A journalist writing this phrase should establish which sense a source intends before quoting it.

## The internal divisions of the tradition

Knowing the main currents is what allows a reader to place any Marxist text they encounter, and the tradition divides along lines that recur.

The Second International period, roughly from the 1880s to 1914, produced the orthodoxy against which everything later reacted: a confident, evolutionary reading in which capitalism's collapse was assured by economic development and the task of the movement was organisation and patience. Karl Kautsky is the representative figure. The revisionist controversy split it and the First World War destroyed it, when the parties of the International voted for war credits in their respective countries and the internationalist claim collapsed in a week.

Leninism, developed between the 1900s and 1920s, answered the question of what to do in a country the orthodox reading said was not ready. Its distinctive contributions are the theory of the vanguard party as a disciplined organisation of professional revolutionaries, the theory of imperialism as the stage at which capitalism exports its contradictions and the chain breaks at its weakest link, and the practice of democratic centralism. Whether these constitute a development of Marx or a break with him is one of the largest disputes in the literature and is not resolvable by textual citation alone, since Marx wrote nothing about party organisation of that kind.

Western Marxism, a term that came into use after the Second World War, describes the current that developed in Central and Western Europe from the 1920s in response to the failure of revolution outside Russia and the political defeats that followed. Lukács, Antonio Gramsci, Karl Korsch, and later the Frankfurt School are the principal figures. Its characteristic move is the turn towards philosophy, culture, and the mechanisms of consent, and Gramsci's concept of hegemony, developed in prison notebooks written in the late 1920s and 1930s under censorship conditions that shape their vocabulary, is the most influential single contribution of this current to social science generally.

Structuralist Marxism in France in the 1960s, above all Althusser, attempted to purge the humanist and Hegelian vocabulary and to reconstruct the theory as a science of structures, producing the concepts of overdetermination, relative autonomy, and ideological state apparatuses. Analytical Marxism, from the late 1970s, took the opposite tack: it accepted the standards of argument of anglophone analytic philosophy and mainstream social science, subjected the central claims to those standards, and rebuilt what survived. G. A. Cohen, Jon Elster, John Roemer, and Erik Olin Wright are the principal names, and the group's most striking feature is how much it discarded, including the labour theory of value in Roemer's case and the functional method in Elster's.

Post-Marxism, from the 1980s, retains the concern with power and social antagonism while abandoning the claim that class is the privileged axis, and the tradition regards it as either a necessary development or an abandonment depending on who is asked. Alongside all of these run the traditions that developed outside Europe with their own problems to solve, in India, China, Latin America, and Africa, and treating those as applications of a European theory rather than as intellectual production in their own right is a mistake this series avoids.

### How do you tell which kind of Marxist a writer is?

Ask three questions of the text. Does it treat the theory of history as a strong causal claim or a weak constraint? Does it hold that class has explanatory priority over other social divisions? Does it defend the labour theory of value as a theory of price, as a theory of social form, or not at all? The answers place almost any writer.

## The vocabulary problem

A reader who tries to enter this material through ordinary usage will be defeated by the vocabulary, because a dozen of the key terms mean something narrower or simply different in technical use than in general English, and several were coined by later writers and back-projected onto Marx.

Capital, in technical use, is not money and not machinery. It is a social relation: value engaged in a process of self-expansion through the purchase of labour power and means of production. A machine sitting in a bankrupt factory is not capital in this sense, which is why the tradition insists that capital is a relation rather than a thing. Exploitation names a quantitative relation between necessary and surplus labour, not unfairness. Value is not price and is not usefulness; the tradition distinguishes use value, exchange value, and value itself, and collapsing them makes the first chapter of Capital unreadable. Class is a relational position, not an income band. Ideology, in the technical sense, is not a synonym for political opinion. Dialectic, whatever else it means, does not mean the thesis, antithesis, and synthesis triad, which is not Marx's formulation and derives from a schematisation of Hegel by Heinrich Moritz Chalybäus that both Hegel and Marx would have disowned.

Several terms are later coinages. Dialectical materialism is not Marx's phrase; it belongs to Georgi Plekhanov and the Second International and was codified in a specific and rigid form in the Soviet period. Class in itself and class for itself is Lukácsian. False consciousness is Engels's, once, in a letter. Base and superstructure is Marx's, but appears far less often than its centrality in secondary literature would suggest, and its German original, Uberbau, is ordinary architectural vocabulary rather than a technical coinage, which is part of why the metaphor invites a rigidity the argument does not require. The [glossary of key Marxist terms](/2010/05/15/marxism-glossary-key-terms/) sets out the whole vocabulary with the distinctions ordinary explainers collapse, and it is the article to keep open while reading anything else in this series.

Translation choices matter more here than in most subjects. The two standard English versions of the first volume of Capital, the older Moore and Aveling translation and the Fowkes translation, render key terms differently, and a reader comparing quotations from the two will find apparent disagreements that are artefacts of translation. Aufhebung, which carries simultaneous senses of cancelling, preserving, and raising, is variously rendered as supersession, sublation, or abolition, and the choice can reverse the sense of a passage about the future of the state or of philosophy. Where a translation choice materially changes meaning, a careful writer names both renderings rather than pretending one is definitive.

## Using the component map: three worked cases

The map earns its place only if it changes what a reader can do with a live argument. Three cases show the procedure.

Take the claim that Marxism was refuted in 1989. Locate it. The collapse of the Eastern European states and, two years later, of the Soviet Union is a fact about the sixth row of the map. What follows from it for the other five rows is not obvious and has to be argued rather than assumed. A defender who responds that the Soviet Union was never socialist is making a substantive claim, not dodging: there is a serious literature, running from Trotsky's analysis of a degenerated workers' state in the 1930s through the state capitalist analyses developed by Tony Cliff and others from the 1940s, arguing that the Soviet formation did not satisfy the criteria the theory itself specifies. That literature can be assessed. What the defender may not do is invoke it and then also claim the achievements of the same states as evidence for the theory, which is a move that occurs frequently and is straightforwardly inconsistent. And the critic has a strong follow-up available: if no actually existing case counts, on what evidence could the theory ever be assessed? That is the real argument, it belongs in the sixth row, and it is a good deal more interesting than the exchange it replaces.

Take the claim that Marx was wrong because workers became richer. Locate it. This is a claim about the theory of class and, behind it, about specific predictive statements in the critique of political economy. The first question is what the immiseration claim actually asserts, and here the textual work matters: there is a reading on which it concerns absolute living standards, a reading on which it concerns the share of the product going to labour relative to capital, and a reading on which it concerns the condition of the worker relative to the accumulating wealth confronting them. The three are empirically distinct, they are supported to different degrees by the text, and only the first is straightforwardly refuted by rising real wages in the industrialised economies. A serious version of the objection specifies which reading it targets and shows that the textual support for that reading is strong. A serious reply concedes that the strongest reading fails and shows what the weaker readings still commit the theory to. Neither side can proceed by citing an aggregate wage series and declaring the matter closed.

Take the claim that Marxist analysis explains the modern platform economy. Locate it. This sits in the second row and partly in the third, and the analytical question is whether the categories fit without being stretched past usefulness. Some fit cleanly: the distinction between labour and labour power is directly applicable where a firm purchases availability rather than output, and the analysis of how technical change raises the productivity of labour without raising its price is directly applicable to algorithmic work allocation. Others strain: where the worker owns the principal instrument of production, the classical criterion of separation from the means of production applies awkwardly and requires supplementing with an account of dependence that Marx does not supply. An honest application says which is which. An application that reports only the clean fits has not tested the framework, and an application that reports only the strains has not used it.

The procedure generalises. Locate the claim in a row, establish what the row's text actually says, identify which reading of it the argument targets, and ask what evidence would bear on that reading specifically. Most public argument about this subject skips all four steps.

## What the tradition predicted and what happened

A reader deciding how much weight to give any of this deserves a plain statement of the record, which means separating claims that were tested from claims that were not, and resisting the temptation to score the whole tradition in a single verdict.

Several expectations were not borne out on any straightforward reading. Revolution did not occur in the most advanced capitalist economies, and where socialist revolutions did occur, they occurred in economies with small industrial working classes, which is close to the inverse of what the theory of history would lead one to expect. The class structure of developed economies did not polarise into two camps; intermediate strata expanded substantially. The proletarianisation of the peasantry proceeded very unevenly and in some regions barely at all across generations. The internationalism of the working class did not survive the test of 1914, and this was the failure that shattered the Second International and prompted the theoretical reconstruction that followed.

Several claims have proved analytically durable without settling into confirmation or refutation. The concentration and centralisation of capital, meaning the tendency of production to concentrate in progressively larger units and of ownership to consolidate, is a claim the historical record supports well, though it is not a claim unique to this tradition. The endogeneity of crisis, meaning the argument that downturns arise from the ordinary operation of accumulation rather than from external shocks or policy error, has been vindicated to a degree that mainstream economics has partly absorbed under other descriptions. The claim that technical change is shaped by the imperatives of control over the labour process as well as by efficiency is now supported by a large empirical literature in the sociology of work, much of it produced by researchers with no allegiance to the tradition.

Several claims remain genuinely contested with serious evidence on both sides. Whether the rate of profit exhibits a secular tendency to fall depends on measurement decisions about capital stock valuation, depreciation, and the treatment of unproductive labour, and different defensible decisions produce different answers, which means the dispute is substantially a dispute about measurement rather than about the world. Whether relative prices track labour content closely enough to support the labour theory of value is likewise the subject of a quantitative literature whose results turn on the treatment of turnover, of capital goods, and of the aggregation level chosen. Reporting either as settled in either direction misrepresents the state of the evidence.

The honest summary is that the analytical apparatus has outlasted the forecasts. Categories developed to explain a nineteenth-century industrial economy continue to be used productively by researchers in several disciplines, while the specific historical trajectory the theory sketched did not occur. Whether a research tradition can survive that combination indefinitely is a fair question and it does not have an obvious answer.

### Is Marxism still relevant?

That depends entirely on which component is meant. The theory of history in its strong form has few defenders even inside the tradition. The analysis of how surplus is extracted, the concept of class as a relational position, and the account of ideology remain in active research use across history, sociology, geography, and cultural studies, frequently by people who reject the politics entirely.

## For students and exam candidates

Marxism is examined at almost every level and in several disciplines, and what earns marks varies less across them than students expect. Sociology papers typically want the theory of class, the base and superstructure model, and an evaluative comparison with a Weberian or a functionalist account. Politics and political theory papers typically want the theory of the state, the account of revolution, and an assessment of whether the framework survives the twentieth-century record. Philosophy papers typically want historical materialism as a thesis about explanation, alienation, or the question of whether Marxism contains a moral theory. Competitive examinations covering political science and international relations typically want the whole apparatus compressed, with the ability to distinguish Marx from Lenin from Gramsci carrying a disproportionate share of the marks.

The one distinction that earns marks on any of these is the separation this guide is built around. An answer that treats Marxism as a single position and then evaluates it will be marked as a mid-range answer whatever else it contains. An answer that specifies which component is under discussion, states what that component claims, and evaluates that claim on evidence appropriate to it, is doing what the mark scheme calls analysis. Writing that the theory of history in its strong form faces the mechanism objection while the account of surplus extraction faces the transformation problem, and that these are separate difficulties bearing on separate claims, demonstrates command in two sentences.

The standard trap is the twentieth-century record. Candidates who have been taught to include an evaluative point routinely reach for the Soviet Union and write a paragraph that assumes the record settles a theoretical question. The paragraph reads as an opinion rather than an evaluation, and it costs marks with careful markers. The move that works instead is to name the connective question explicitly: to say that the record raises the question of whether the theory's silence on political accountability made that institutional outcome more likely, and that answering it requires an argument about the theory rather than a description of the events. Structuring an answer on this subject, and the mark logic behind each move, is the business of the [exam answer guide for historical materialism](/2010/05/01/historical-materialism-exam-answers/), which supplies the skeletons this orientation guide deliberately does not.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

Every class on this subject arrives having conflated Marx with the Soviet Union, and the conflation is not a failure of preparation; it is what the surrounding culture supplies. Teaching against it directly, by asserting that Marx did not design the Soviet system, tends to produce polite disbelief, because the assertion sounds like special pleading on behalf of a figure the students already suspect the teacher of liking.

The intervention that works is the six-component map used as an exercise rather than as a handout. Give the class a set of statements drawn from ordinary public argument, both hostile and sympathetic, and ask them to assign each to a component. Statements such as the claim that Marxism ignores human nature, that Marxism has been tried and failed, that Marxism explains why the media covers strikes the way it does, and that Marxism says value comes from labour will distribute across four different rows, and the distribution is visible to the class within a few minutes. The point makes itself, which is the only way it lands with students who suspect the teacher of a position.

The extract that resolves the residual scepticism is the passage in the Manifesto answering the objection about property, because it demonstrates in the primary text, in Marx's own defensive posture, that the target is productive property rather than personal possessions. Students who have been told that Marxism means confiscating their belongings can see the objection being anticipated and answered by the author himself, which is more persuasive than any amount of teacherly correction. For a politically mixed class, the balance rule that holds is the one this series follows: state each position in the terms its own defenders use before evaluating it, and be visibly willing to state what the framework fails to explain. A class that sees the teacher concede the prediction failures will accept the account of the analytical strengths.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

Three verification habits prevent most of the errors that appear in professional writing on this subject.

Verify attribution between Marx and Engels before citing. A substantial share of what circulates as Marx is Engels, including the systematising of the theory in Socialism: Utopian and Scientific, the phrase false consciousness, and much of the material in the second and third volumes of Capital as they stand. The direction of error runs both ways: Engels is credited with softening a determinism that the 1859 preface did not contain, and Marx is credited with a completeness that only Engels's editing produced. Where a claim rests on the third volume, say that the volume was assembled posthumously from manuscripts.

Verify quotations against a named work and section, and treat the most quotable lines with the most suspicion. Marxism attracts fabricated quotations at a rate matched by few subjects. The line about democracy being the road to socialism, attributed to Marx across an enormous number of publications, has no located source in his writings. The opium passage from the introduction to the critique of Hegel's philosophy of right is genuine, but it is almost always quoted with the surrounding sentences removed, and those sentences change its sense considerably, since the passage is describing religion as the expression of and protest against real suffering before it reaches the phrase everyone quotes. A quotation that is doing rhetorical work in a piece should be checked in a full text rather than in another article.

Verify the status of the text you are citing. The Theses on Feuerbach are notes, published by Engels after Marx's death and edited by him. The German Ideology was not published in the authors' lifetimes and its famous first part survives as manuscript bundles whose ordering is an editorial decision rather than an authorial one. The Grundrisse is a set of private notebooks. The 1844 manuscripts were unpublished and untitled by Marx. None of this makes those texts unusable, and all of them are indispensable, but citing a notebook as though it were a considered published position is the sourcing error that a specialist reader notices first. Readers building a working archive of this material can [save and annotate these guides and build a citation-linked reading list free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html), which is the practical way to keep quotation cards attached to their sources rather than floating loose in a notes file.

## How the components lean on each other

Separability is not independence, and a guide that stopped at the map would leave the reader with six pieces and no sense of why they were ever assembled. The connections are worth stating because they are what make the tradition a tradition rather than a filing system.

The theory of history supplies the frame in which the critique of political economy is a critique of something historically specific rather than of economic life in general. Without it, the analysis of capital is an account of one economy among possible economies with no reason to expect it to end. With it, capitalism becomes a mode of production with an origin, a dynamic, and conditions under which its characteristic relations become obstacles rather than engines. This is why the transition question, meaning how the previous order gave way to this one, carries the weight it does in the tradition: an account of how capitalism arose is the strongest available evidence that it is the kind of thing that can pass.

The critique of political economy supplies the theory of class with its criterion. Without an account of how surplus is pumped out of the direct producers, class is a stratification scheme like any other, and the choice of the ownership axis over the income axis or the status axis is arbitrary. With it, the ownership axis is privileged for a reason: it is the axis along which the appropriation of surplus labour is organised, which is what makes the interests opposed rather than merely different. This is also why Marxist class analysis resists the sociological instinct to add dimensions until the model fits the survey data, since adding dimensions without a criterion returns the analysis to arbitrary stratification.

The theory of class supplies the theory of ideology with its explanandum. The interesting question about prevailing beliefs is not that they exist but that they are systematically related to a structure of interests that they do not describe. And the theory of ideology supplies the theory of the state with the account of consent it needs, because a state that reproduced a class order by coercion alone would be a very different and much less stable object than the constitutional states the tradition actually has to explain. Gramsci's hegemony is precisely the concept produced at that junction, and it is no accident that it emerged from a Marxist trying to explain why the revolutions in Western Europe failed.

Run the connections in the other direction and the elasticity objection reappears in a sharper form: a framework in which each component supplies the interpretation the next one needs is one that can be difficult to test at any single point, because every anomaly can be reassigned to a neighbouring component. The tradition's better practitioners are alert to this, which is why the analytical Marxists insisted on stating each claim in a form that could be assessed alone. The map in this guide is built on the same instinct.

## The questions readers actually arrive with

A great deal of the demand for information about this subject arrives in the form of blunt questions, and answering them on substance without adopting the framing is a service that encyclopedic sources do not perform.

Whether Marxism killed millions is a question about the sixth row, and the honest answer separates three things. The states that governed in this name produced mass death through famine associated with collectivisation drives, through penal and deportation systems, and through political killing, and the historical scale of this is a matter for archival research where estimates vary by method, by which categories are included, and by which archives are available. That much is a historical fact and should never be softened. Whether the theory produced those outcomes is a distinct question requiring an argument about the theory, and a serious version of that argument exists and is made by serious critics: it concerns the absence of any developed account of political accountability, the treatment of moral constraint as ideological, and the instrumental conception of the state. Whether Marxism as an intellectual tradition is thereby refuted is a third question, on which the tradition's own internal critics, from the Frankfurt School to the state-capitalism theorists, have said more than most external critics realise.

Whether Marxism ignores human nature is a question that misfires on the texts. Marx has a theory of human nature, in the early writings especially, built around the claim that human beings are creatures who produce their means of life and realise themselves in that activity, and the concept of species-being carries it. What the theory denies is that the specific dispositions displayed under a specific set of social arrangements, competitiveness and acquisitiveness above all, are the fixed content of that nature rather than its form under those arrangements. That is a contestable claim and the evidence from behavioural and evolutionary research bears on it, but it is a different claim from the one the objection attacks.

Whether Marx was right about anything is a question that dissolves once the components are separated, since the answer differs by row and the person asking usually has one row in mind. Whether a professor teaching Marxism is a Marxist is a question about pedagogy rather than about theory, and the answer is that teaching a framework and holding it are different activities, as the same course's treatment of Hobbes or Malthus demonstrates. Whether Marxism is when the government does things is a joke that has become a genuine source of confusion, and it is worth answering plainly: state activity as such has no relation to the theory, which is a theory about who owns productive assets and who commands whose labour, and by that criterion a heavily regulated economy with private ownership of the means of production is capitalist and always was.

Whether one has to read Capital to understand Marxism is a question about effort, and the answer is that a reader who wants the theory of history, the theory of ideology, or the theory of the state can get a long way without it, while a reader who wants to make or assess any claim about exploitation, value, or crisis cannot avoid at least the first nine chapters. The [complete guide to the first volume](/2010/06/01/capital-volume-one-complete-guide/) exists to make that first encounter survivable.

## What would count as evidence against the framework

Any framework worth taking seriously can say what would embarrass it, and the elasticity objection has force precisely because the tradition has often declined to answer. The answer differs by component, which is another use for the map.

For the theory of history in its strong form, the disconfirming evidence would be a durable social order in which the relations of production persistently obstructed the development of productive capacity without generating either transformation or collapse, over a period long enough to exclude the fetters clause's own hedge about exhaustion. Long stagnations that end in conquest rather than internal transformation are the awkward cases, and the theory's defenders handle them by distinguishing the possibility of transformation from its necessity, which is a legitimate move that also weakens the claim considerably. The weak version, in which material conditions constrain what is historically possible without determining what occurs, is close to unfalsifiable and close to something most historians would accept, which is the trade-off the ladder makes visible.

For the critique of political economy, the disconfirming evidence is more tractable than the reputation of the subject suggests. If profit could be shown to arise systematically in a fully competitive economy with no surplus labour performed, the account of its source would fail. If relative prices proved to bear no relation to labour content beyond what the correlation of any two aggregates produces, the value theory in its price-explaining version would fail. The literature on both of these exists, it is quantitative, and it disagrees with itself about the treatment of capital stock, turnover, and unproductive labour, which is why nobody should report either result as settled.

For the theory of class, the disconfirming evidence would be the demonstration that position in the relations of production has no systematic relation to political behaviour, life chances, or organisational capacity once other variables are controlled. This has been tested extensively in the sociological literature and the results are mixed in an interesting way: property and authority relations continue to predict a great deal, and continue to predict less than the theory in its strong form requires.

For the theory of ideology, the falsifiability question is the objection rather than a side issue, and the honest position is that the interest version is testable in principle, the appearance version is a claim about social form rather than a causal hypothesis and is assessed by whether it makes the phenomena intelligible, and the reproduction version is the hardest to state in a form that could fail. For the theory of the state, the disconfirming evidence would be durable state action against the systematic interests of capital, sustained rather than episodic, and the argument over whether the postwar welfare settlements constitute exactly that has been running for decades without resolution.

Stating this much is not a concession extracted under pressure. It is what distinguishes a research tradition from a worldview, and the parts of the tradition that have taken the question seriously, particularly the analytical current from the late 1970s, produced the sharpest work of the last half-century by doing so.

## A reading path through the tradition

A reader who wants to go further should proceed by component rather than by chronology, because the chronological path leads through the Hegelian apparatus of the early writings and loses most people permanently.

Begin with the Manifesto, read in a single sitting, treated as a political document with a rhetorical purpose rather than as an analytical text. It supplies the vocabulary, the historical sweep, and the section answering objections that is more useful than the famous opening. Follow it with the 1859 preface, which is short enough to read three times and dense enough to require it, and which contains the theory of history in the form every subsequent argument addresses. Then read Wage Labour and Capital or Value, Price and Profit, both of which originated as addresses to workers' organisations and both of which present the economics in a form Marx designed to be followed by people without technical training.

At that point the reader has a choice determined by the component they care about. For the economics, the first volume of Capital is unavoidable, and the workable approach is to read the first chapter twice, accept that the section on the value form will not fully resolve on a first pass, and then proceed through the chapters on the labour process, surplus value, the working day, machinery, and the historical material on so-called primitive accumulation, which is the most readable long stretch in the book. For the theory of history, The German Ideology in a good edition, with the awareness that its famous first part is an editorial construction from manuscripts, and Engels's 1890s correspondence for the authorised qualifications. For the theory of the state, The Eighteenth Brumaire, which is also the best-written thing either author produced, and The Civil War in France. For the early philosophical material, the 1844 manuscripts, read with the knowledge that Marx did not publish or title them.

Beyond Marx, the sequence that gives the most return is Lenin on imperialism and on the state, Luxemburg on reform and revolution, Gramsci on hegemony in a scholarly selection rather than a movement anthology, and one text from the analytical current to see the claims tested against the standards of contemporary social science. A reader who has done that has the map in this guide filled in with primary material and can assess almost anything written about the subject.

Working through a sequence of that length rewards a system for keeping quotations attached to their sources and notes attached to the component they bear on, which is exactly the failure mode that turns six months of reading into a folder nobody can use.

## Does Marxism contain a moral theory?

The question is not decorative, because it determines what kind of argument a critic has to make. If the framework is a moral indictment of capitalism, it can be answered with an alternative moral theory. If it is an analysis with no evaluative content, moral objection is beside the point and the argument has to be conducted on the analysis. Marx's own position is genuinely difficult, and both readings can be supported from the texts.

The case for saying there is no moral theory is strong and is what Marx repeatedly says. He treats justice as a concept internal to a mode of production, so that a transaction is just if it corresponds to the prevailing relations of production, which means the wage bargain is entirely just by capitalist standards and the charge of injustice is confused. He mocks the socialists who appeal to eternal principles of fairness, and the Critique of the Gotha Programme is scathing about the phrase concerning a fair distribution of the proceeds of labour. Exploitation, as noted, is a technical measure. On this reading the framework is a science of a social formation, and the reason to oppose capitalism is not that it is wrong but that it is transient and that a particular class has an interest in its supersession.

The case for saying there is a moral theory rests on the texts themselves being unignorably evaluative. The chapters of Capital on the working day and on machinery are not neutral prose, and no reader has ever mistaken them for it. The early writings are built on a conception of human flourishing that is doing straightforwardly normative work: alienation is a deprivation, and calling it one requires a standard. The account of what a post-capitalist society makes possible, sparse as it is, describes the conditions for the development of individual capacities in terms that are unintelligible as pure description.

The literature has produced several attempted reconciliations. One holds that Marx rejects justice as a criterion while operating with a different evaluative concept, of self-realisation or of freedom, which is not a matter of distribution at all. Another holds that Marx is inconsistent, condemning capitalism in terms his own theory forbids him, and that the inconsistency is real rather than apparent. A third holds that the internal conception of justice permits an immanent critique, in which capitalism is measured against the standards it proclaims, which is a form of moral argument that requires no external principles. Each of these has serious defenders and the dispute is unresolved.

The practical consequence is worth carrying. A critic who argues that Marxism has no adequate account of rights, of individual liberty, or of the constraints on what may be done to persons in pursuit of a social goal is making an objection the tradition has struggled to answer, and it is a more serious objection than most of the economic ones, because it bears directly on the connective question about the twentieth-century record. A defender who responds by citing the moral force of the chapters on the working day has conceded that the framework carries evaluative commitments, which is the concession the objection needs.

## The tradition outside Europe

An orientation guide that stopped at the European currents would misdescribe where most of the tradition's adherents have lived and most of its practical problems were worked out. Three regional developments matter enough to name here, each treated in full elsewhere in this series.

In Russia and then the Soviet space, the governing problem was that the theory of history appeared to forbid what the movement wanted to do. An agrarian economy with a small industrial working class was, on the orthodox reading, not ready, and the entire theoretical apparatus of Leninism was built to answer that objection: the vanguard party as the organisational solution to a working class too small and too new to act alone, the theory of imperialism as the account of why the decisive break might come at the periphery rather than the centre, and the doctrine of uneven and combined development as the general statement of why a latecomer economy does not repeat the sequence of the pioneers. Whether these are developments of Marx or departures from him is the question the whole subsequent literature turns on.

In China, the governing problem was the peasantry. The theory identified the industrial working class as the revolutionary agent and treated the peasantry as a class incapable of independent political action, which was an awkward starting point in a society that was overwhelmingly agrarian. The Chinese tradition's distinctive theoretical production, including the strategy of protracted rural struggle and the analysis of contradictions among the people, was generated by that problem, and the older question of whether the Asiatic mode of production described the region's history acquired a political charge it never had in Europe. The [complete guide to Marxism in China](/2018/05/01/marxism-in-china-complete-guide/) covers the lineage and its record.

In Latin America, the governing problem was that the region's economies were not developing along the trajectory the theory described, and the analysis produced in response, dependency theory and the structuralist economics associated with it, argued that underdevelopment was not a stage that preceded development but a condition produced by the same process that developed elsewhere. That analysis reached far beyond the tradition and was taken up by economists and policymakers with no Marxist commitments at all, which is one of the clearer instances of the framework producing something the mainstream absorbed.

In India, the tradition had to accommodate caste, a structure of social division that the class categories do not capture and that cannot be treated as a survival destined to dissolve under capitalist development, since it demonstrably has not. The resulting body of work on the relation between caste and class, on agrarian relations and the mode of production in Indian agriculture, and on the subaltern historiography that grew partly out of and partly against this tradition, is a substantial intellectual production in its own right rather than an application of a European theory to a local case, and treating it as the latter is a habit this series avoids.

In the anticolonial movements of Africa and the Caribbean, the governing problem was that the theory's account of the revolutionary agent had been derived from societies with developed industrial proletariats, and the societies in question did not have them. What they had were peasantries, urban populations displaced by colonial economic policy, and a small intelligentsia. The theoretical production that followed included C. L. R. James's treatment of the Haitian revolution as a movement of enslaved plantation workers who constituted something closer to a modern proletariat than the metropolitan categories suggested, and Frantz Fanon's argument about the political capacities of populations the orthodox reading dismissed. A second and equally consequential body of work concerned Marx's own writings on colonialism, which contain passages describing British rule in India as destructive and simultaneously as an unwitting instrument of social transformation, and which have been read as evidence of a developmental teleology the tradition later had to shed. Whether Marx revised that position in his last decade, in the same period that produced the 1877 letter and the Zasulich drafts, is a genuine scholarly question that the publication of his late ethnological notebooks reopened.

The general lesson from all five is that the framework has repeatedly been most productive where it did not fit, because the misfit forced the development of new concepts. That is what a research tradition is supposed to do, and it is a better argument for the framework's seriousness than any of the confirmations its defenders usually reach for.

## What is settled and what remains disputed

Some things about this subject are not in serious dispute among people who have read the material, and saying so is useful, because a reader arriving from public argument will have been told that everything is contested.

It is settled that Marxism is not a single doctrine, that the word postdates Marx, that the five-stage theory of history is a later formalisation rather than Marx's own scheme, that dialectical materialism is not Marx's term, that false consciousness is not his phrase, that class in itself and class for itself is not his phrasing, that Marx wrote almost nothing prescriptive about post-capitalist institutions, that the target of the property argument is productive property, that Marx worked for a living, that the second and third volumes of Capital are posthumous editorial constructions, and that the theory of the state is the least developed part of the original corpus. Every one of these is contradicted routinely in general-audience writing, and a reader who holds them can identify an unreliable source in a paragraph.

It remains disputed whether the early and mature writings represent a continuity or a break; whether the theory of history is best stated in a strong technological version, a strong class-conflict version, or a weak constraint version, and whether any of them survives the mechanism objection; whether the labour theory of value is a theory of price determination, a theory of the social form of the product, or neither; whether the transformation of values into prices is solvable and, if so, at what cost to the original claims; whether the rate of profit exhibits a secular tendency to fall, which is substantially a dispute about measurement; whether the state is best analysed instrumentally or structurally; whether Leninism developed or broke with Marx; whether the Soviet formation satisfied the theory's own criteria for a post-capitalist society; and whether class retains explanatory priority over other axes of social division. These are not disputes between Marxists and their critics. Most of them are disputes conducted inside the tradition, in its own journals, with the sharpest positions on both sides frequently held by people who consider themselves Marxists.

One further thing is worth saying about the shape of that list. The settled items are almost all negative, meaning they concern what the tradition does not contain and what Marx did not write, while the disputed items are almost all substantive claims about how societies work. That asymmetry is not an accident of presentation. It reflects the condition of a subject whose secondary literature accumulated faster than its textual scholarship for the better part of a century, so that clearing away what was never there is a precondition for arguing about what is. A reader who has done the clearing is in a position most participants in public argument about this subject never reach.

What a reader can do with all of this, having reached the end, is place any claim about Marxism in a row of the map, name the text that bears on it, state the strongest objection to it, and identify whether the argument they are in is about theory or about record. Those four moves are what the rest of this series is built to support, and the natural next step is the [complete guide to historical materialism](/2010/01/15/historical-materialism-complete-guide/), which takes the first row and works the 1859 preface clause by clause into six claims arranged from strongest to weakest.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Marxism in simple terms?

Marxism is a tradition of social and economic analysis originating with Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, built on the claim that the way a society organises the production of its material life shapes its class structure, its politics, and its prevailing ideas. Its most developed part is an account of how profit arises under capitalism: workers sell their capacity to work, employers use that capacity to produce more value than the wage costs, and the difference is the source of profit. Around that sits a theory of historical change driven by conflict between productive capacities and the social relations organising them, a theory of class as a relational position rather than an income band, and an account of how prevailing beliefs relate to material interests. It is a research tradition with genuine internal disagreement rather than a fixed doctrine, which is why summaries that present it as a single set of beliefs mislead.

### Q: What are the main components of Marxism?

Six components can be held or rejected independently: a theory of history, a critique of political economy, a theory of class, a theory of ideology, a theory of the state and politics, and the twentieth-century state projects that governed in the tradition's name. The first five are theoretical claims traceable to specific texts, principally the 1859 preface, the first volume of Capital, The Eighteenth Brumaire, The German Ideology, and The Civil War in France. The sixth is a historical record rather than a theoretical claim, and treating it as one is the source of most bad argument on the subject. The components lean on each other, but accepting one does not commit you to the others: economists have accepted the analysis of exploitation while rejecting the theory of history, and cultural theorists have used the theory of ideology while abandoning the labour theory of value entirely.

### Q: Is Marxism a political theory or an economic theory?

Both, and also a theory of history and a theory of ideology, which is why the question keeps producing unsatisfying answers. The mature work is overwhelmingly economic: Capital is a treatise in political economy and the concepts that carry the analysis are economic categories. But the theory of history is a claim about explanation in the social sciences generally, the theory of ideology has had its largest influence in cultural and literary study, and the theory of the state is political theory in the ordinary sense. The most accurate description is that Marxism is a general framework for social analysis whose most developed application is to the capitalist economy. Disciplines that use it have tended to take the component they need and leave the rest, which is why a historian, a literary critic, and a heterodox economist can all describe themselves as working in this tradition while sharing very little.

### Q: Did Karl Marx ever call himself a Marxist?

Engels reports that Marx, presented with the doctrines of certain French followers, said that if that was Marxism then he was not a Marxist. The remark appears in Engels's correspondence, in letters to Eduard Bernstein and to Conrad Schmidt, and the careful way to use it is to attribute it exactly as it survives: as Engels's report rather than as a documented utterance from Marx. The underlying point is solid regardless of the anecdote's status. The word Marxism came into general use after Marx's death in 1883, and the systematising of his unfinished writings into a doctrine capable of organising a mass party happened in the two decades afterwards, largely in the German and Russian movements. The gap between what Marx wrote and what Marxism became is a matter of chronology, not of pedantry.

### Q: Who founded Marxism and when did it begin?

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels developed the body of thought, from their collaboration beginning in the mid-1840s through Marx's death in 1883 and Engels's editorial and popularising work until his own death in 1895. Dating the beginning depends on what counts. The philosophical anthropology of the 1844 manuscripts, the materialist conception of history in The German Ideology of 1845 and 1846, the political statement in the Manifesto of 1848, and the mature economics of Capital in 1867 are four distinct starting points for four distinct components. Marxism as a named position, with a doctrinal content and organised adherents, dates from after Marx's death, principally through the German Social Democratic Party and the Russian movement, and a great deal of what is taught as Marxism was formalised in that later period rather than by either founder.

### Q: What did Marx predict would happen to capitalism?

He expected concentration of ownership into progressively larger units, recurrent crises arising from the ordinary operation of accumulation rather than from external shocks, a growing and increasingly organised working class, and eventually a transformation in which the relations of production become obstacles to the productive capacities they had developed. He also expected the class structure to polarise and revolution to occur first in the most industrially advanced economies. The record is mixed and should be stated without either side's spin: concentration and endogenous crisis hold up well and are not claims unique to this tradition, while polarisation did not occur, revolutions happened in economies the theory identified as unready, and the transformation did not arrive. Whether the failures are fatal depends on which version of the theory is being defended, since the strong and weak versions stake very different amounts.

### Q: Why is Marxism still taught in universities?

Because a large body of consequential scholarship was produced with these categories and cannot be read without them. Social history as practised since the mid-twentieth century, the sociology of class and of work, economic geography, the anthropology of colonial economies, and most of the theoretical apparatus of cultural studies all carry Marxist assumptions, frequently held by researchers with no interest in the politics. A student who cannot follow a class analysis is cut off from major work in four or five disciplines. Beyond that, teaching a framework and endorsing it are separate activities, and departments that teach Marx also teach Weber, Hobbes, Hayek, Durkheim, and Foucault on the same rationale. The subject also functions well pedagogically because its central claims are clear enough to be argued against, which is a property many theoretical frameworks lack.

### Q: What is the difference between Marx and Marxism?

Marx is a nineteenth-century writer who left a large body of published work, a much larger body of unpublished notebooks and correspondence, and several central projects unfinished at his death. Marxism is what a movement made of that material, beginning in the 1880s and continuing through the twentieth century, and it includes a substantial amount that Marx did not write and would not necessarily have recognised: the five-stage theory of history, dialectical materialism as a codified philosophy, the vanguard party, the tripartite division into three sources and three component parts, and the phrase false consciousness. Reading the tradition backwards onto the author is the single commonest error in secondary writing, and the correction is procedural: before attributing anything to Marx, ask which work it appears in and whether the work was published in his lifetime.

### Q: Does Marxism abolish personal property or private property?

Private productive property, meaning ownership of the means of production that yields command over the labour of others. Not personal possessions. The Manifesto raises this objection against itself and answers it directly, distinguishing property that is the product of a person's own labour and used by them from property that functions as capital, and stating that the target is the latter. The confusion is understandable because the English word property covers both, while the argument turns entirely on the distinction. What follows from the distinction in institutional terms is a separate question, and the tradition contains arguments for state ownership, for cooperative ownership, for communal forms, and for combinations, with substantial internal criticism of state ownership under an unaccountable bureaucracy as reproducing the problem in a new form.

### Q: Why is Marxism so controversial?

Three reasons operating together. The framework makes claims about interests, which means it tells people that beliefs they hold sincerely are systematically related to a social position they did not choose, and that is an inherently provocative thing to say. States that governed in its name produced famine, mass repression, and political killing on a scale that historians continue to document, and any tradition carrying that association will be argued about in terms borrowed from that record. And the vocabulary is genuinely technical while sounding ordinary, so exploitation, capital, class, and ideology are heard in their everyday senses and generate arguments about positions nobody holds. Separating the components dissolves a surprising amount of the heat, because most of the fiercest exchanges turn out to concern the sixth component while being conducted in the vocabulary of the first five.

### Q: Can you accept part of Marxism and reject the rest?

Yes, and most people who use the framework professionally do exactly that. The analytical Marxists of the late twentieth century subjected the central claims to the standards of mainstream social science and discarded a great deal, including the labour theory of value in some cases and the functional method of explanation in others, while retaining the analysis of class and exploitation. Historians use the materialist approach to explanation without committing to any position on value theory. Cultural theorists use the theory of ideology while treating the economics as a separate matter. This is normal behaviour within a research tradition rather than a compromise, and treating partial acceptance as inconsistency assumes the doctrinal picture of the subject that this guide is written to displace.

### Q: Is Marxism the same as socialism?

No. Socialism is a broad family of positions favouring social ownership or control of productive resources, and it includes traditions with no relation to Marx: religious socialism, guild socialism, Fabianism, cooperative movements, and the utopian socialists Engels defined the tradition against. Marxism is one current within that family, distinguished by a specific analysis of how capitalism works and how historical change occurs, and by its derivation of socialist conclusions from that analysis rather than from moral principle. The distinction runs in one direction only: all Marxists are socialists in the broad sense, most socialists historically have not been Marxists. Where the confusion causes real damage is in political argument, where a critique of one current is presented as a refutation of the family.

### Q: How much of Marxism was actually written by Engels?

More than most readers assume, and the question matters for citation. Engels co-authored the Manifesto and The German Ideology, edited and published the second and third volumes of Capital from Marx's manuscripts after his death, published the Theses on Feuerbach from Marx's notebooks, and wrote the popularising works through which the theory reached its largest nineteenth-century audience, including Socialism: Utopian and Scientific and the longer polemic it was extracted from. He also wrote the correspondence of the 1890s that supplies the standard qualifications of economic determination, and the phrase false consciousness appears in one of those letters. Whether Engels systematised Marx faithfully or introduced a positivist rigidity is a long-running scholarly dispute. What is not disputed is that a great deal circulating as Marx is Engels, and writers should check.

### Q: What should a teacher check before assigning material on this subject?

Whether the resource separates the theoretical claims from the twentieth-century record, whether it attributes later formulations to Marx, and whether it states the strongest objection at full strength. A resource that presents the five-stage theory of history as Marx's own, that uses false consciousness as a Marxian term, or that treats the collapse of the Soviet Union as a refutation of the labour theory of value will produce answers that lose marks and will also expose the teacher to reasonable complaints about balance from either direction. The safest resources are those that concede the prediction failures explicitly and state what the framework fails to explain, since a class that sees the account conceding things trusts the account. Checking the vocabulary against a proper glossary before the first session prevents most of the rest.

### Q: Which claims about Marx should a writer never repeat without checking?

Any quotation that is doing rhetorical work, and three specific items. The line about democracy being the road to socialism, attributed to Marx across an enormous quantity of published writing, has no located source. The opium passage is genuine but is almost always quoted with the surrounding sentences removed, and those sentences change its sense from dismissal to something considerably more ambivalent. And the remark about not being a Marxist survives as Engels's report rather than as documented speech. Beyond quotations, check whether the text cited was published in Marx's lifetime, since the notebooks, the 1844 manuscripts, The German Ideology, and the later volumes of Capital all have complicated editorial statuses that a specialist reader will know and an unwary writer will not.

### Q: Do you have to be a communist to use Marxist analysis?

No, and the assumption that you must is one reason the framework is used more widely than it is acknowledged. Analytical tools do not carry political commitments intrinsically: a historian can explain the enclosure of common land through the interests of landholders without endorsing any programme, and a sociologist can use a relational concept of class without holding a view about the future of the wage relation. The tradition's own history supports this, since several of its most rigorous twentieth-century figures rejected substantial parts of the political inheritance while continuing to work with the categories. The reverse also holds: adopting the political conclusions does not require accepting the analysis, and a great deal of socialist politics historically has rested on moral argument with no Marxist content at all.

### Q: Is Marxism a religion?

The comparison is old and is worth taking seriously enough to answer rather than dismissing. The structural parallels are real: a comprehensive account of history with a direction, a canonical body of texts subject to exegesis, foundational figures, schisms, heresies, and, in the twentieth-century parties, institutional machinery for policing orthodoxy. Anyone who has read the polemical literature of the Second and Third Internationals will recognise the genre. But resemblance is not refutation, and the same structural features can be found in any long-lived intellectual tradition with institutional carriers, including several that nobody calls religions. The question that matters is whether the central claims are stated in a form that could be assessed against evidence, and the answer differs by component, which is the analysis this guide sets out rather than a verdict on the analogy.

### Q: What is the shortest honest definition of Marxism?

A tradition of analysis holding that the organisation of material production is explanatorily fundamental to social structure, that class societies rest on the appropriation of surplus labour from direct producers, that the conflict this generates drives large-scale historical change, and that prevailing ideas stand in a systematic relation to these arrangements. Everything else is elaboration, qualification, or dispute. The definition is honest because it states what is common across the tradition rather than picking one current and presenting it as the whole, and because it makes no claim about the twentieth-century states, which are a separate subject requiring separate evidence. A definition shorter than that will be a slogan, and a definition longer than that will have smuggled in a position that a substantial part of the tradition rejects.
