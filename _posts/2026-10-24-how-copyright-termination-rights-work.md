## A Grant Signed Decades Ago Can Be Ended by Statute

Copyright termination rights allow an author, or statutory heirs after the death of the author, to end a prior transfer or license of copyright by serving a formal notice within a statutory window, even when the grant states that the transfer is perpetual. Section 203 of the Copyright Act governs many grants made on or after January 1, 1978, while Section 304 governs many earlier grants, and each provision sets a clock, a notice procedure, and a class of persons entitled to act. Termination is a timed statutory override of contract - inalienable, yet confined by strict clocks, notice formalities and a derivative works exception. The override operates despite any agreement to the contrary, including an agreement to make a will or to make a future grant, because Congress placed the power in the statute rather than in private bargaining. The power does not erase the past. Exploitation that occurred under the grant before the effective date of termination remains lawful, payments already made are not returned, and derivative works prepared under authority of the grant receive special protection. Termination instead recaptures the remaining rights in the work for the period after the effective date, so that the author or the heirs can license the work again, retain the rights, or transfer the rights to a different publisher, producer, or distributor. The mechanism sounds simple in summary and proves demanding in practice. A claimant must identify the correct statutory track, calculate the opening and closing of a window measured in years, determine every person whose consent is required, prepare a notice that identifies the grant and the work with precision, serve the notice on the grantee or a successor, and record the notice with the Copyright Office before the effective date. An error in the window, in the required signatures, or in the contents of the notice can defeat the entire effort, and litigation over musical compositions, novels, and motion picture source material has repeatedly turned on such defects. Courts therefore treat termination as a statutory privilege bounded by the text of Sections 203 and 304, not as a general fairness doctrine. The Second Circuit and the Ninth Circuit have both enforced timely notices for valuable catalogs while rejecting claims that fell outside the statutory categories, and the pattern confirms the thesis. The right is powerful because a contract cannot waive the right in advance, and the right is narrow because Congress defined the eligible grants, the eligible works, the eligible claimants, and the period for action with unusual specificity. Understanding termination therefore begins with sequence rather than sentiment. The first inquiry asks when the grant was executed and by whom. The second inquiry asks whether the work qualifies. The third inquiry asks who holds the termination interest and whether the required share of that interest has joined the notice. The fourth inquiry asks whether the notice was served far enough in advance and recorded in time. Only after each inquiry is answered does the derivative works exception determine what a former grantee may continue to exploit. The sections that follow take each element in order, beginning with the reason Congress created an exit from grants that authors had signed freely and that ordinary contract law would have enforced according to the terms written on the page.

## Why Congress Built an Exit Into Copyright Grants

Congress built termination into the Copyright Act of 1976 because the market for a new work rarely reveals the value of the work at the moment of transfer. House Report 94-1476, the principal committee report for the 1976 Act, explains that authors frequently face unequal bargaining power and that the eventual success of a work is inherently unforeseeable at signing. A novelist with no track record, a songwriter without a hit, or an illustrator dependent on a single publisher negotiates against a firm that spreads risk across a large catalog. The firm can absorb failure across hundreds of titles, while the author bears the entire consequence of underpricing one successful work. Under ordinary contract principles, a bad bargain remains binding, and a grant for the full term of copyright could lock an author into a rate set before any audience existed. Congress concluded that a second opportunity to share in the value of a successful work served the constitutional purpose of copyright, because the prospect of recapture gives authors a renewed incentive and gives heirs a measure of protection against a transfer made under economic pressure. The reasoning in House Report 94-1476 also reflects a judgment about duration. The 1976 Act extended the term of copyright substantially, moving protection for works created on or after January 1, 1978 to the life of the author plus 50 years in the original design of the Act, and Congress treated the added value of a longer term as a benefit that should not pass entirely to a grantee under an old agreement. Termination gives the author a share in that extended value by allowing renegotiation at a defined point. The design is deliberately delayed. A grantee receives a long period of secure exploitation, measured at 35 years for most grants under Section 203, before the right matures, so investment in publication, recording, or filming rests on a predictable interval. The delay balances the competing interests rather than choosing one side outright. Publishers argued during the legislative process that unrestricted recapture would chill investment, while authors argued that no recapture would make the longer term a windfall for transferees. The statutory compromise answers both arguments with a clock. Equally deliberate is the inalienable character of the right. Section 203 provides that termination may be effected notwithstanding any agreement to the contrary, and Section 304 contains parallel language for earlier grants. Without that clause, a standard publishing contract could extract a waiver at signing, when the author holds the least leverage, and the protection would vanish in the very transactions that motivated the protection. Congress had seen a similar pattern under the renewal system of the Copyright Act of 1909, where advance assignments transferred the renewal term before the author could assess the value of the work. The termination provisions therefore repeat a lesson learned across seven decades of legislation. The provisions also protect family members who did not sign the original grant. When an author dies before the window opens, the interest passes to a widow or widower, children, and grandchildren under a statutory scheme, on the theory that the economic consequences of an early bargain fall on dependents as heavily as on the author. The result is a federal reallocation of rights inside a private transaction, justified in the legislative record by bargaining inequality, unforeseeable value, and the enlarged term that Congress itself created.

## The Renewal System That Taught Congress a Lesson

The Copyright Act of 1909 divided protection into an original term of 28 years and a renewal term of 28 years, and that division shaped the bargaining problem that termination later addressed. The renewal term was intended as a second estate for the author. If the author survived into the renewal period, the author could reclaim the work or negotiate a new grant on better terms, and if the author died, specified family members could claim renewal. In practice, publishers sought assignments of the renewal term at the same time as the original grant, often in the first contract for a work whose value remained unknown. The author received one payment or one royalty structure for both terms, and the protective purpose of renewal depended on whether courts would enforce an assignment made before the renewal right had vested. The Supreme Court answered that question in Fred Fisher Music Co. v. M. Witmark and Sons, 318 U.S. 643 (1943). The Court held that an author could assign the expectancy in the renewal term and that such an assignment bound the author if the author survived into renewal. The decision treated the renewal expectancy as assignable property and gave effect to the contract, while leaving protection for statutory successors when the author died before renewal, because in that event the renewal passed to the family class rather than through the assignment. Fisher therefore preserved a partial safeguard and defeated the broader one. A living author who had signed early remained bound, exactly the outcome the two-term structure had sought to prevent. Congress responded in the Copyright Act of 1976 by replacing renewal for new works with a single unitary term and by creating termination as a right that an advance agreement cannot defeat. The contrast between an assignable expectancy under Fisher and an inalienable termination right under Sections 203 and 304 is the central lesson of the earlier system. A later decision exposed a related problem for derivative works based on material in a renewal term. In Stewart v. Abend, 495 U.S. 207 (1990), the Supreme Court considered the motion picture Rear Window and the underlying story by Cornell Woolrich, first published as It Had to Be Murder. The author had assigned motion picture rights, the author died before renewal, and the renewal interest passed to the executor, who granted rights to a different party. The Court held that continued exhibition of the film infringed the renewal rights because authorization from the original grant did not carry forward once the renewal term belonged to the successors and no new grant authorized the use. Stewart confirmed that successors take the renewal term free of a derivative work arrangement when the author does not survive to renew, a rule distinct from the derivative works exception that Congress wrote into termination. The two cases frame the historical arc. Fisher showed that contract could consume a protective second term during the life of the author. Stewart showed that succession at renewal could disrupt even a completed and celebrated film. Congress designed termination to avoid both extremes, by making the right inalienable during life and after death, and by protecting derivative works prepared under a grant even after the grant ends. The history explains the structure, because termination is neither a renewal claim nor a rescission claim, but a new statutory device built from the failures of the device that came before the 1976 Act.

## Two Statutory Tracks for Two Eras of Grants

Section 203 and Section 304 divide termination by the date and character of the grant, and selecting the wrong track invalidates the analysis from the first step. Section 203 covers a transfer or license of copyright, or of any right under copyright, executed by the author on or after January 1, 1978, other than a grant for a work made for hire and other than a grant by will. For such grants, the window generally opens 35 years after execution of the grant. If the grant covers the right of publication, the window opens at the earlier of 35 years after publication of the work under the grant or 40 years after execution of the grant. The window remains open for 5 years, and termination takes effect only on a date within that window stated in a notice served in advance. Section 304(c) covers a different population. The provision applies to a transfer or license of the renewal copyright, or of any right under the renewal copyright, for a work subsisting in a first or renewal term on January 1, 1978, where the grant was executed before that date by the author or by specified statutory persons. For those grants, the window opens 56 years after copyright was originally secured or on January 1, 1978, whichever is later, and the window lasts for 5 years. Section 304(d) addresses a narrower gap created by the Sonny Bono Copyright Term Extension Act of 1998, which added 20 years to subsisting copyrights. Where the termination right under Section 304(c) expired before the extension and the author or another grantor did not exercise the right, Section 304(d) supplies a further window opening 75 years after copyright was originally secured, again lasting for 5 years. The distinction matters because a pre-1978 grant cannot be terminated under Section 203, and a post-1977 grant by an author cannot be terminated under Section 304. Courts apply the tracks by grant date and work status. Later amendments illustrate how Congress adjusts the surrounding statute without collapsing the two tracks, and a reader tracing those amendments encounters the [Digital Millennium Copyright Act guide](/2017/08/01/digital-millennium-copyright-act-1998/) among the guides that map the structure of the amended Act. Both tracks share core features. Each makes termination available notwithstanding an agreement to the contrary. Each requires advance written notice, service on the grantee or a successor in title, and recordation of a copy of the notice with the Copyright Office before the effective date. Each protects derivative works prepared under authority of the grant before termination, allowing continued utilization of such a work under the terms of the grant after termination, while barring preparation of new derivative works from the recaptured rights. The tracks differ in grantor as well. Section 203 is confined to grants executed by the author, whereas Section 304(c) reaches certain grants executed by a widow or widower, children, an executor, or next of kin acting under the renewal provisions. The difference reflects the renewal system, under which persons other than the author could hold and transfer renewal rights. A careful analysis therefore records four facts before any notice is drafted - the date copyright was secured, the date the grant was executed, the identity of the grantor, and the date of publication under the grant - because those four facts select the track, set the clock, and determine whether a termination right exists at all.

## The First Element Is a Grant Executed by the Author

The first element under Section 203 is execution of the grant by the author, and the identity of the grantor separates terminable grants from transfers that remain outside the provision. A transfer signed by the author during life, whether an assignment of the full copyright or a license of a single exclusive right, falls within the statute if the remaining conditions are met. A nonexclusive license also falls within Section 203, although the practical value of terminating such a license differs from the value of recapturing an exclusive right, because a nonexclusive licensee never held the power to exclude others. By contrast, a grant by will is expressly excluded, because a testamentary transfer reflects a deliberate disposition at death rather than a bargain struck under the pressures described in House Report 94-1476. A transfer by a person other than the author also falls outside Section 203. If an author leaves copyright to a spouse and the spouse later licenses the work, that license is not a grant executed by the author, and the heirs of the author cannot terminate the license under Section 203. Section 304(c) is broader on this point for the older era, because renewal rights could be held and transferred by statutory successors, and the provision therefore reaches grants by specified family members and representatives for works under the 1909 Act system. The boundary requires precision about authorship itself. In a joint work, a grant executed by one joint author transfers only the interest of that author, and termination by that author or by the statutory successors of that author reaches only the share conveyed, unless the joint authors executed a joint grant governed by the special rule for grants involving more than one author. For a grant executed by two or more authors of a joint work, termination requires action by a majority of the authors who executed the grant, and the framework follows the execution pattern rather than the ownership pattern alone. The distinction between copyright and neighboring fields sharpens the point, as shown by a [comparison of patent, copyright and trade mark](/2019/12/15/patent-copyright-trademark-comparison/) that places transfer rules for each regime side by side. Patent law permits assignment under 35 U.S.C. Section 261 without a statutory termination right for an inventor, and trademark law restricts assignment of a mark without the associated goodwill under 15 U.S.C. Section 1060, so neither field supplies an analogue that can be imported into copyright. The grant element also excludes transfers by operation of law in many contested settings, and courts examine the instrument rather than the label placed on the instrument by the parties. A document called a submission agreement or a receipt may still effect a transfer if the language conveys a copyright interest, while a document that conveys only physical copies conveys no copyright under Section 202 and leaves nothing to terminate. The inquiry is therefore documentary and personal at once - documentary because a qualifying instrument must exist, and personal because the hand that executed the instrument must be the hand of the author for Section 203 to apply. Failure on either branch ends the claim before the clock is even calculated, which is why grantor identity appears first in the statutory sequence and why careful estates preserve signed instruments across decades.

## The Second Element Is a Work Outside Work Made for Hire

The second element excludes a work made for hire, a category defined in Section 101 of the Copyright Act and litigated across publishing, music, software, and film. If a work qualifies as a work made for hire, the employer or the commissioning party is considered the author for purposes of the Act, and no termination right arises under Section 203 or Section 304(c) for a grant of such a work. The exclusion follows from the authorship premise of termination. Congress created the right for a human author who transferred rights in the work of that author, not for a corporation deemed to be an author by statutory definition. Section 101 supplies two branches. The first branch covers a work prepared by an employee within the scope of employment. The second branch covers a specially ordered or commissioned work, but only if the work falls within one of nine enumerated categories and only if the parties expressly agree in a written instrument signed by both parties that the work is a work made for hire. The nine categories include a contribution to a collective work, a part of a motion picture or other audiovisual work, a translation, a supplementary work, a compilation, an instructional text, a test, answer material for a test, and an atlas. A commissioned novel, a commissioned song outside an audiovisual context, or a commissioned photograph for general use does not become a work made for hire merely because a contract uses that phrase, because the subject matter falls outside the enumerated list. The employee branch turns on the relationship between the creator and the hiring party. In Community for Creative Non-Violence v. Reid, 490 U.S. 730 (1989), the Supreme Court held that determination of employee status under the first branch follows the general common law of agency, and the Court listed factors including the skill required, the source of the tools, the location of the work, the duration of the relationship, the right to assign additional projects, the discretion over hours, the method of payment, the role in hiring assistants, the treatment of the work for tax purposes, and the provision of employee benefits. No single factor controls, and the Court concluded that the sculptor in Reid was an independent contractor rather than an employee. The classification decided ownership of the sculpture at issue and illustrates why the same classification decides terminability in later disputes. An independent contractor engaged for a single project presents the harder case, and the written instrument requirement for the second branch becomes decisive when the category fits. Litigation over sound recordings illustrates the stakes of the category list. Recording artists and labels have disputed whether a sound recording qualifies as a contribution to a collective work or as part of an audiovisual work, and the dispute matters because an affirmative answer combined with a signed writing would remove the recording from termination altogether. Courts therefore resolve the work made for hire question before reaching dates or notices, because a work inside the definition ends the termination inquiry, while a work outside the definition proceeds to the remaining elements of grant, window, notice, and shares. The element protects the coherence of the statute, since a right designed to correct the bargain of a human author has no application where the Act assigns authorship to the hiring party from the moment of creation.

## Who Holds the Termination Interest

Ownership of a termination right follows a statutory scheme rather than the will of the author or the ordinary rules of descent alone, and the scheme determines whose signatures a valid notice requires. While the author lives, the author holds the termination interest and may exercise the right alone for a grant executed by that author alone. After the death of the author, the interest passes outside probate to the classes named in Sections 203 and 304, and a devise of copyright in a will does not redirect the termination interest to a different person. The distinction between ownership of copyright and ownership of the termination interest surprises many estates, because the two interests can rest in different hands at the same time. A publisher may hold copyright under a grant, an heir may hold a remainder share of copyright subject to the grant, and the statutory class may hold the power to end the grant on a future date.

### Who can terminate a grant after an author dies?

After an author dies, the termination interest passes to a surviving spouse, children, and grandchildren as statutory classes defined by Section 203 and Section 304. A spouse without children holds the entire interest, while a spouse with children holds one-half and the children and grandchildren share the remaining one-half per stirpes.

The shares continue to govern action after the class is identified. Where a spouse and children survive, exercise of the right requires the persons who together own more than one-half of the total termination interest, a majority-in-interest rule stated in Section 203 and repeated in Section 304(c). A spouse holding one-half cannot act alone in that configuration, because one-half is not more than one-half, and at least one child or another holder must join. Where no spouse survives, children and grandchildren holding more than one-half of the interest may act. A deceased child is represented by the children of that child, who take the share of the parent per stirpes and who act by a majority among themselves for that share. The rule prevents a minority from recapturing rights against the judgment of the larger share, while also preventing a grantee from blocking termination by securing a refusal from a single holder of a small fraction. The restriction preserves a structured opportunity for renegotiation with the original grantee without giving that grantee a veto over termination itself. When the author leaves no spouse, no children, and no grandchildren, the interest passes to the executor, administrator, personal representative, or trustee of the author under the statutory order, and if none of those representatives exists or acts, the interest passes to the next of kin. Each tier is statutory rather than elective. A termination notice must therefore identify the class, calculate each share, and gather signatures for more than one-half of the interest, and a notice signed by the wrong class or by an insufficient share fails even when the window and the contents are correct. Cases involving the heirs of Superman co-creator Jerry Siegel and the heirs of other prominent writers turned in part on class membership and share calculations, confirming that succession under Sections 203 and 304 is a federal question governed by the definitions in the statute, including the definitions of widow, widower, children, and grandchildren, rather than by family preference or by a private settlement that contradicts the statutory proportions.

## How Joint Authorship and Heirs Change the Vote

A grant signed by one author presents a simple question of who may terminate, because the terminating party and the granting party are the same person during the life of that author. Joint authorship removes that simplicity. Section 203 provides that where a grant was executed by two or more authors of a joint work, termination may be effected by a majority of the authors who executed the grant. The statute counts authors, not shares of copyright ownership, and it does not require unanimity. Two of three joint authors may therefore terminate over the objection of the third, while one of two joint authors cannot, because one of two is not a majority. The same majority principle appears in Section 304 for older grants, although the older provision frames the vote around the class of persons who own the termination interest rather than around the living authors alone. In both settings the inquiry begins with identification of every person who executed the grant, because a signature on a publishing agreement, a recording contract, or an assignment determines membership in the voting group.

Death changes the composition of that group without extinguishing the right. Where an author is dead, the termination interest of that author passes to the statutory successors named in Sections 203 and 304. A surviving widow or widower, the children of the author, and the grandchildren of the author through a deceased child take in the proportions the statute prescribes. If a widow or widower survives together with children or grandchildren, the spouse owns one half of the termination interest of the deceased author and the children and grandchildren together own the other half. If no spouse survives, the children and grandchildren together own the entire interest. If neither a spouse nor descendants survive, the executor, the administrator, the personal representative, or the next of kin of the author owns the interest, in that sequence of authority. The scheme is deliberately closed. Friends, collaborators who did not execute the grant, creditors, and later assignees of the copyright do not enter the class merely because they hold an economic stake in the work.

Distribution among descendants follows a per stirpes pattern, a term the statute explains through shares rather than through labels alone. The share of a deceased child passes to the children of that deceased child, who divide among themselves the portion their parent would have taken. A living child takes a personal share and also stands as the root of a branch for grandchildren only if that child has died before the termination is effected. The Copyright Act states the consequence in concrete terms for action: where a termination interest is owned by more than one person, action may be taken by a majority of the owners of that interest. Within a family branch, therefore, a separate majority calculation may be necessary before the branch can be counted toward the larger majority of authors or owners. Practitioners and scholars, including the treatise Nimmer on Copyright, treat these layered majorities as one of the most demanding features of the system, because a notice signed by an impressive number of relatives may still fail if the signers do not together represent the required majority of each deceased author interest. The vote is arithmetic applied to a family tree and to a signature page, and both documents must be read together before any notice is served.

## The Section 203 Clock and Its Five Year Window

### How many years must pass before a grant can be terminated?

A grant governed by Section 203 becomes terminable at the end of 35 years from the date of execution, and termination may be effected at any time during the five year period that follows, subject to the separate computation for grants covering publication rights.

The date of execution is the anchor for the ordinary Section 203 calculation. Execution means the signing of the grant by the author, and Section 203 applies only to grants executed by the author on or after January 1, 1978. A grant signed before that date belongs to the older regime of Section 304, even if the work itself was created later in some unusual sequence or the agreement was performed over many years. Where several authors signed on different days, or where amendments restated an agreement, the record of signing becomes central, because a difference of months at the front end moves the entire window at the back end. The five year window is not a grace period that can be extended by agreement, and it is not a period that pauses while the parties negotiate. Once the window closes without an effective termination, the grant continues according to its terms for the remaining life of the copyright, and no second window opens under Section 203 for the same grant.

Precision matters because the window interacts with the notice deadlines considered in later sections. A termination inside the window requires a notice served years in advance, so the practical planning horizon begins well before the window opens. An author or a successor who waits until the window has nearly closed may find that the earliest lawful effective date has already passed beyond the end of the window, leaving no available date at all. The statute permits termination despite any agreement to the contrary, including an agreement to make a will or to make a future grant, and that override is the reason the clock cannot be reset by private drafting.

The window also has a defined relationship to derivative works prepared under the grant. Termination returns the rights covered by the grant, but a derivative work prepared under authority of the grant before termination may continue to be utilized under the terms of the grant after termination. A motion picture based on a novel, a translation prepared and published under the grant, or a sound recording derived from a composition may therefore survive termination in its existing form, while the preparation of new derivative works after the effective date belongs to the owner of the recaptured rights. That distinction shapes the commercial value of the clock. The approaching end of 35 years gives the terminating party leverage to negotiate a new grant on revised terms, a use Congress anticipated, but a new grant signed before termination takes effect carries its own statutory limits, and a replacement grant generally becomes effective only after the effective date of termination if it is to rest on the recaptured rights. The calendar, the contract file, and the derivative work record must align, because Section 203 rewards the party who treats the window as a fixed statutory interval rather than as a flexible bargaining suggestion. The five year span is generous for filing and negotiation, yet unforgiving once it has run.

## The Publication Grant Rule Inside Section 203

Section 203 contains a second computation for a grant that covers the right of publication of the work, and that computation displaces the simple measure of 35 years from execution in many book, music, and periodical transactions. For such a grant, the five year termination period begins at the end of 35 years from the date of publication of the work under the grant, or at the end of 40 years from the date of execution of the grant, whichever term ends earlier. The comparison is between two complete periods, not between two starting points, and the earlier ending period controls. A work published promptly after signing will usually reach termination under the 40 year measure, because 35 years from a publication date only two years after execution would end later than 40 years from execution. A work left unpublished for a long interval presents the opposite pressure in appearance only, since the statute still caps the wait through the 40 year alternative. The rule prevents a grantee from postponing termination indefinitely by delaying publication, a danger Congress addressed directly in the structure of the provision.

Identifying a publication grant requires attention to the rights actually conveyed. The statutory phrase covers a grant that includes the right of publication, a right associated with distribution of copies or phonorecords of the work to the public. A publishing agreement for a novel plainly falls within the description, as does a music publishing agreement that authorizes printed and recorded dissemination of a composition. A grant limited to a single derivative use, such as motion picture adaptation alone, or a license for public performance alone, raises a different classification question, and classification determines which clock governs. Litigation and commentary have therefore focused on the text of the grant, the bundle of rights transferred, and whether publication was one component of a larger transfer. Nimmer on Copyright collects the debate and treats the presence of the publication right within a broader grant as the operative fact, rather than the label the parties placed on the document. Labels such as agreement, license, or assignment do not control the statutory analysis, because Section 203 reaches a transfer or license of copyright or of any right under copyright, however the parties styled the transaction.

Worked illustrations show why the distinction changes real dates. If a grant was executed in 1980 and the work was published under the grant in 1981, the period measured from publication ends in 2016 and the period measured from execution ends in 2020, so termination may be effected during the five years beginning in 2016. If the same grant led to publication in 1990, the publication measure would end in 2025, while the execution measure ends in 2020, and the earlier date again controls, opening the window in 2020. The publication date itself must also be established from evidence, including copyright registration records, deposit copies, publisher catalogs, and the first authorized distribution of copies. The publication grant rule thus joins contract interpretation to the statutory definition of publication, and both inquiries must be completed before a notice date can be selected with confidence.

## The Section 304 Clock for Older Works

Works already under copyright when the Copyright Act of 1976 took effect follow a different clock, set out in Section 304, because their grants were made under the Copyright Act of 1909 and its system of an initial term and a renewal term. Section 304(c) addresses a grant executed before January 1, 1978 by an author or by the statutory renewal claimants named in the provision, covering the renewal term or an extension of the copyright. For such a grant, termination may be effected at any time during a five year period beginning at the end of 56 years from the date copyright was originally secured, or beginning on January 1, 1978, whichever date is later. The reference point is the date copyright was secured, commonly through publication with notice or through registration for an unpublished work, rather than the date the grant was signed. A song copyrighted in 1960 therefore reaches the start of the Section 304(c) window in 2016, regardless of whether its publishing grant was signed in 1960, in 1965, or in another year before 1978.

Section 304(d) supplies a second opportunity for a defined and narrower class. The Copyright Term Extension Act of 1998, associated in public debate with Sonny Bono, added 20 years to the terms of subsisting copyrights, extending protection for works by individual authors to the life of the author plus 70 years. Where the termination right under Section 304(c) had expired before the extension was enacted, and the grant had not already been terminated, Section 304(d) permits termination during a five year period beginning at the end of 75 years from the date copyright was originally secured. The provision reaches only the rights covered by the grant that arise under the added extension years. Congress used the new window to give authors and successors a share in the windfall of the longer term, so that the benefit of the extension would not pass entirely to grantees under old contracts signed when no such extension existed.

The class of persons who may terminate under Section 304 overlaps with the class under Section 203 but reflects the renewal structure of the older law. The author, if living, acts directly. If the author is dead, the termination interest belongs to the widow or widower, the children, and the grandchildren per stirpes, with the same general proportions between a surviving spouse and descendants that govern under Section 203, and with executors and next of kin taking when no member of the named family class survives. Certain grants under Section 304 were executed not by the author but by a widow, a widower, children, or next of kin as renewal claimants, and the provision assigns the vote for those grants to the class that executed them or to its surviving members under the statutory formula. A work made for hire stands outside both regimes, because the employer is considered the author for copyright purposes and no author grant exists to terminate. The derivative works exception also applies under Section 304, preserving continued use of a derivative work prepared under the grant before termination. Together, the 56 year and 75 year measures form a calendar keyed to the age of the copyright itself, a design that suits works whose commercial lives and contract histories began long before the termination right was created in 1976.

## Notice as the Mechanism That Makes Copyright Termination Real

### When must a termination notice be served?

A termination notice must be served not less than two years and not more than ten years before the stated effective date of termination, and a copy must also be recorded in the Copyright Office before that effective date arrives to complete the statutory process.

Termination under Sections 203 and 304 is not self executing. The passage of 35 years, of 56 years, or of 75 years creates an opportunity, and only a notice of termination served in compliance with the statute converts that opportunity into a change of ownership on a stated effective date. The effective date must fall within the five year window described in the preceding sections. A notice naming a date before the window opens is premature, and a notice naming a date after the window closes is late, even if service itself occurred years earlier. The service interval of two to ten years before the effective date forces planning on a long horizon. Service ten years before an effective date near the end of a window may require action before the window has even opened, a sequence the statute permits because the controlling dates are the effective date and the interval between service and that date, not the opening of the window alone. Families and authors who begin the search for heirs, contracts, and copyright records only after the window opens often discover that the service interval has consumed much of the usable time.

Service means delivery of the signed written notice to the grantee or to the successor in interest of the grantee. Where the identity or address of the grantee cannot be ascertained after reasonable inquiry, the regulations of the Copyright Office permit alternative methods tied to publication and to the records of the Copyright Office, and the file of the search becomes part of the proof of compliance. Recordation is a separate step from service. A copy of the notice as served must be recorded in the Copyright Office before the effective date of termination, and recordation serves as constructive notice of the facts stated in the recorded document when the document identifies the work and the document or registration number associated with the work. The Copyright Office records the document without adjudicating whether the termination is legally effective, a distinction the Office states in its recordation practice and in its circulars on termination. Recordation therefore proves that a paper was presented and recorded, not that a court would sustain the termination against a challenge.

The notice system also fixes the moment after which bargaining positions change. A grant may be renegotiated at any time, but an agreement for a further grant made after the notice of termination has been served is effective only if it is made after the effective date of termination, apart from a limited statutory allowance for an agreement between the author or successors and the original grantee reached after service of the notice. After the effective date, the recaptured rights belong to the terminating party, who may retain them, license them, or transfer them under a new grant that will itself be subject to termination in due course.

## What a Valid Notice Must State and Where It Goes

The contents of a notice of termination are prescribed by regulation rather than left to the preference of the sender. Section 203 and Section 304 each direct that the notice must comply in form, content, and manner of service with requirements prescribed by regulation of the Register of Copyrights. The governing regulation is codified at 37 Code of Federal Regulations 201.10, and compliance work begins with its text. The regulation calls for a clear identification of the grant being terminated, including the date of the grant and the parties to it, the title of the work and the identity of the authors, a description of the rights covered by the grant that are being terminated, and the effective date of termination. A notice that describes a catalog in general language without identifying the grant and the works with adequate precision invites a challenge that the grantee could not determine which rights were claimed to revert.

The regulation also addresses the persons signing, the facts about authorship and survival that support the authority of the signers, and statements concerning a grant executed by a person other than the author under the older provisions. A complete notice therefore reads in part like a chain of title memorandum. It names the work, it names the grant, it states whether the author survives, and where the author does not survive it identifies the class through which the signers claim. The notice must be served on the grantee or on the successor of the grantee, at the address determined under the regulation, and proof of service should be preserved with the same care as the notice itself. Certified mail receipts, courier records, correspondence acknowledging receipt, and Copyright Office recordation receipts together form the evidentiary packet that a later court, a later purchaser, or a later licensee will ask to inspect when the recaptured rights are exploited or sold.

Recordation in the Copyright Office gives the notice a public location. The Office maintains the recorded document among its public records, indexed so that a searcher examining the title of the work can discover the claim of termination. That public function explains the regulatory insistence on identifiers. A registration number links the notice to the registration record, a title links it to catalog searches, and the name of the author links it to authority files. Recordation before the effective date is a statutory condition, not a courtesy, and a notice served in time but recorded after the effective date fails the sequence the statute imposes. The distinction between service and recordation has generated disputes where one step was completed and the other was neglected, or where the copy recorded differed from the copy served. The safest evidentiary position, reflected in Copyright Office guidance and in the practice described by Nimmer on Copyright, is identity between the served copy and the recorded copy, with both steps completed well inside their deadlines. At the same time, recordation is ministerial. The Copyright Office does not try disputed questions of heirship, majority, or the classification of a grant, and its acceptance of a document for recordation leaves every substantive question open for agreement between the parties or for decision by a federal court if litigation follows.

## Mistakes in Notice and the Harmless Error Question

A termination notice is a formal document operating inside strict statutory dates, and small errors raise a large question: whether a defect defeats the termination or may be excused as harmless. The regulation at 37 Code of Federal Regulations 201.10 contains a harmless error provision. Errors in a notice that do not materially affect the adequacy of the information required by the regulation, or the ability of a reasonable person to understand the information and to identify the grant to which the termination applies, do not affect the validity of the notice. The standard is functional. It asks whether the reader of the notice was misled in a material way, not whether the sender achieved perfection in every field. The Copyright Office has applied that approach in its examination of notices submitted for recordation, and federal courts considering notice disputes have generally asked the same question of substance over form.

Other errors sit at the opposite pole because they concern the conditions that create the right itself. A notice that names an effective date outside the five year statutory window does not present a clerical slip; it asks for termination at a time the statute does not allow, and courts have treated selection of the effective date as a substantive element rather than as a detail of form. A notice that fails to identify the grant being terminated, or that identifies a different grant from the one the signers had power to terminate, leaves the grantee unable to know which rights are claimed, and that failure goes to the core of the regulatory standard. A notice signed by persons who do not together hold the required majority presents a defect of authority, not a defect of paperwork, and the harmless error rule does not manufacture signatures or enlarge the statutory class. Service on the wrong party, or a complete failure to record before the effective date, likewise concerns a statutory step rather than a statement inside the document. Litigated disputes have clustered around these boundaries, with grantees characterising contested errors as substantive and terminating parties characterising the same errors as harmless, a pattern Nimmer on Copyright documents across the case law without reducing it to a single mechanical test.

Between the poles lies the contested middle ground where most careful drafting effort belongs. A wrong registration number paired with a correct title, author, and grant date may mislead no one, while the same wrong number standing as the sole identifier of the work could mislead a searcher and a grantee alike. Courts approach such questions through the text of the notice, the surrounding records, and evidence of actual understanding, and outcomes turn on facts that cannot be generalized safely beyond the document in dispute. The same law also protects reliance by grantees and by later purchasers, who must be able to tell from a notice which rights revert and when. Harmless error doctrine holds those two policies in balance, forgiving the error that misleads no reasonable reader and refusing rescue where the error obscures the grant, the work, the authority, or the date on which ownership changes.

## What Reverts on the Effective Date

On the effective date stated in a valid notice, the statute provides that all rights under the Copyright Act that were covered by the terminated grant revert to the authors or to the other persons who own the termination interests. Section 203(b) states the general rule for grants under Section 203, and Section 304(c)(6) states a parallel rule for grants under Section 304(c). The reversion is automatic by operation of law once the procedural conditions have been met. No separate assignment, no court order, and no consent from the grantee is required to make the transfer back effective, although litigation sometimes becomes necessary when a grantee disputes whether a notice was valid, whether a work falls inside the grant, or whether a statutory exception applies.

The scope of reversion follows the scope of the grant. A grant that conveyed only North American book publication rights returns only those rights, while a grant that conveyed all rights in all media returns the full bundle described in Section 106, including reproduction, preparation of derivative works, distribution, public performance, and public display, to the extent those rights were included. Rights that the grant never conveyed remain where they were before termination and are not enlarged or reduced by the notice. Rights arising under foreign law are also outside the reversion, because Sections 203 and 304(c) operate on rights under the United States Copyright Act, a limitation the Supreme Court and lower courts have treated as central when a single agreement purported to assign rights worldwide for a single work.

After reversion, the reverted rights vest in the persons identified by the statutory succession scheme. Where an author survived to the effective date, the interest vests in that author. Where an author did not survive, the interest vests in the widow or widower, the children, and the grandchildren holding the termination interest under the statutory shares, subject to the rules for exercise by a majority of an interest. A co-author holds a separate termination interest in a joint work under Section 203, while execution-era grants under Section 304(c) are treated through the interest structure that Congress provided for pre-1978 works. The persons holding the interest after reversion hold it as owners under the Act and may license or assign it, subject to the limits described below.

Future grants receive special protection. Section 203(b)(4) and Section 304(c)(6)(D) provide that a further grant, or an agreement to make a further grant, of a reverted right is valid only if it is signed by the same number and proportion of owners required to terminate the original grant. A promise made before termination to grant the reverted rights after termination is generally ineffective, with one narrow exception for an agreement made with the original grantee or a successor in title after service of the notice. The design prevents a grantee from using superior bargaining power during the term of the old grant to lock up the reverted rights in advance and thereby defeat the second chance that Congress intended to provide. Courts applying the provision examine the date of the agreement, the identity of the parties, and the number of signatures, rather than the commercial fairness of the price offered.

Reversion does not erase facts created during the grant. Copies lawfully made and distributed remain lawful copies, ownership of a physical manuscript or master remains governed by property and contract principles distinct from copyright ownership, and obligations that accrued before the effective date remain enforceable according to their terms. The change is in control of the copyright itself. From the effective date forward, continued exploitation that requires a Section 106 right, and that is not sheltered by the derivative works exception or another statutory limit, requires authority from the owners of the reverted rights. That division between past lawful acts and future control explains much of the litigation that follows a notice, because catalogues built over decades often mix original works, derivative works, physical assets, and trademark claims that termination does not decide.

## The Derivative Works Exception and Its Limits

### What happens to a film made from a work whose grant was terminated?

A film completed under the grant before termination may continue to be distributed and exhibited under the terms of the grant after termination takes effect. The film remains a lawful derivative work, while the underlying rights revert, and no sequel, remake, or other new derivative work may be prepared from the reverted rights without a new grant.

The exception appears in Section 203(b)(1) for grants under Section 203 and in Section 304(c)(6)(A) for grants under Section 304(c). Each provision states that a derivative work prepared under authority of the grant before its termination may continue to be utilized under the terms of the grant after termination. The language protects reliance interests built during the grant period. A motion picture studio that financed an adaptation, a publisher that issued a translation, and a record producer that released an authorized arrangement do not lose the ability to exploit the specific derivative work that was prepared in time, merely because the underlying grant has ended. The derivative work keeps its own copyright in its new material, and the owner of that derivative work may continue the authorized uses on grant terms.

The limits are as important as the permission. The exception covers utilization of a derivative work already prepared, not preparation of additional derivative works after the effective date. A studio may continue to distribute the completed film, but the studio may not prepare a sequel, a remake, a new edit that amounts to a new derivative work, or a new adaptation of the reverted novel without authority from the owners of the reverted rights. A publisher may continue to sell the existing translation, but the publisher may not commission a revised translation that draws on the underlying work after termination. The distinction turns on whether the activity uses the finished derivative work as a fixed work or instead returns to the underlying work to create additional expression.

Preparation before termination is the threshold fact, and disputes often center on timing and completeness. Courts examine contracts, production records, fixation dates, and the point at which a derivative work existed in sufficiently permanent form to be perceived or reproduced. A mere idea, a treatment that was never developed under the grant, or a project announced before the effective date but created afterward does not receive the same shelter. The grantee bears the practical burden of showing that the specific work claimed under the exception was prepared under authority of the grant and before termination, because the exception is an exception to the general reversion rule and is read in light of that structure.

The leading royalty decision is Mills Music, Inc. v. Snyder, 469 U.S. 153 (1985). The case concerned a popular song first published in 1923 and a derivative arrangement of that song, and the question was whether royalties from continued use of the derivative work after termination flowed under the terms of the original grant, including a division that gave a share to the publisher, or whether the full amount belonged to the author family after reversion. The Supreme Court held that the terms of the grant continued to govern utilization of the surviving derivative work, so the royalty division in the grant remained effective for that use. The decision divided the Court and drew a dissent that would have read the exception more narrowly in favor of the terminating family. The holding remains the central authority on money flow for surviving derivative works.

Even within Mills Music, the exception did not transfer the underlying song back to the publisher or restore the terminated grant. It preserved a defined use on defined terms. Licenses for new uses of the underlying work, grants for media not embodied in the surviving derivative work, and control of the original composition or story passed to the termination interest holders. The boundary therefore runs between a finished derivative work and the source work that made it possible. That boundary is administrable in a single film or translation and harder in franchises, revised editions, remixes, and software, where later versions blend old material prepared under the grant with new material created afterward and where courts must separate protected utilization from unauthorized preparation.

## Why Derivative Works Were Allowed to Survive

Congress included the derivative works exception because termination without such a limit would have destroyed investments that the legal system had encouraged for decades. A grant in the middle of the twentieth century might support a motion picture costing many millions of dollars, a Broadway adaptation requiring extensive new music and staging, or a translation program spanning multiple volumes. If reversion of the underlying rights had also withdrawn authority to use the finished film or translation, the derivative owner would lose the value of original expression that the derivative author lawfully created, and distributors, theaters, and archives would face uncertainty about works already in circulation. The exception mediates between the author-protective purpose of termination and the reliance interest of parties who built a separate work while the grant was in force.

The structure reflects a distinction in copyright law between an underlying work and the new material in a derivative work. Section 103(b) provides that copyright in a derivative work extends only to the material contributed by the derivative author and does not imply any exclusive right in the preexisting material. Termination returns the preexisting material to the termination interest holders, but the derivative author retains rights in the contribution that the derivative author added, subject to the grant terms that authorized continued use. A film adaptation of a novel illustrates the division. The screenplay dialogue, performances, cinematography, and editing choices belong to the film as a derivative work, while the characters, plot, and expression taken from the novel remain part of the underlying work that reverts.

Legislative history discussed by courts in cases such as Mills Music shows a concern for continuity in established distribution arrangements. Congress expected that a derivative work in existence at termination would not vanish from commerce and that the economic bargain governing that work would continue for that work. At the same time, Congress withheld authority to prepare new derivative works after termination, so the grantee could not use a single early adaptation as a platform for perpetual control of the source. The compromise gives the derivative owner stability for the finished product and gives the author family control over the future of the underlying work, including the choice whether to license a new adaptation, on what financial terms, and to which producer.

The compromise also reduces a holdup problem in both directions. Without survival, the termination interest holders could block distribution of a completed film even though the film embodies substantial expression owned by others, and even though audiences, archives, and licensees had arranged their affairs around its availability. Without a limit on new derivative works, the original grantee could announce a slate of sequels and revisions shortly before the effective date and claim that the entire slate was sheltered, draining reversion of practical value. The statutory line at preparation before termination gives courts and parties a factual test tied to fixation and production records, rather than a test tied to announcements, intentions, or expenditures alone, although close cases still arise when a work was partly prepared before the date and completed afterward.

Critics of the exception have argued that grant terms preserved by Mills Music can perpetuate an unequal royalty division long after Congress meant to give the author family a fresh bargain. Defenders answer that the exception is confined to the derivative work that exists and that the family regains the larger asset, namely the underlying work and all rights outside that single derivative use. Both accounts appear in judicial opinions and commentary, and both take the statutory text seriously. The practical result is a split market after termination. The old grantee may continue exploiting the surviving derivative work under old terms, while the termination interest holders control the source work and may authorize competing editions or adaptations, subject to trademark law, contract terms, and the rule against preparing derivative works from the surviving derivative work without its owner consent.

## Work Made for Hire as the Border Dispute

Work made for hire determines whether termination exists at all. Sections 203 and 304(c) grant termination rights for grants made by an author, but a work made for hire is deemed authored by the employer or commissioning party under Section 201(b), and the employee or commissioned party is not treated as the author for that purpose. If a work qualifies as a work made for hire, there is no author grant to terminate and no termination interest to exercise. If the work does not qualify, the human creator is the author, a later assignment is a grant subject to the statute, and the termination window may open decades after creation. Classification therefore decides the outcome before notice periods, signature counts, or derivative works questions are reached.

Section 101 supplies two routes. The first covers a work prepared by an employee within the scope of employment. The second covers a specially ordered or commissioned work, but only where the work falls within one of the categories listed in the statute and the parties expressly agree in a signed written instrument that the work shall be considered a work made for hire. The categories include a contribution to a collective work, a part of a motion picture or other audiovisual work, a translation, a supplementary work, a compilation, an instructional text, a test, answer material for a test, and an atlas. A work outside those categories cannot become a work made for hire through the commissioned route, however clearly a contract uses that label, because the label is effective only when the statutory conditions are satisfied.

The leading employee test comes from Community for Creative Non-Violence v. Reid, 490 U.S. 730 (1989). The case concerned a sculpture created by James Earl Reid for an organization that advocated for people without housing. The Supreme Court held that employee status under the work made for hire doctrine is determined under the general common law of agency, not under a test based only on control over the final product or on the hiring party motivation. The Court listed factors drawn from agency law, including the skill required, the source of the tools and instruments, the location of the work, the duration of the relationship, the right to assign additional projects, the discretion over work hours, the method of payment, the role in hiring assistants, whether the work is part of the regular business of the hiring party, whether the hiring party is in business, the provision of employee benefits, and the tax treatment of the hired party. No single factor is decisive.

Reid rejected narrower approaches that lower courts had applied and grounded the inquiry in the relationship between the parties as a whole. A creator who works from a separate studio, supplies personal tools, receives payment by the project, receives no employee benefits, and is treated as an independent contractor for tax purposes points away from employee status, even where the commissioning party gave detailed instructions about subject matter. Conversely, a staff writer on a regular payroll, working at an office supplied by a publisher, with benefits and tax withholding, points toward employee status even where the writer exercised substantial creative discretion. Courts apply the Reid factors to publishing, music, software, and visual art, often on summary judgment records built from contracts, payroll documents, tax forms, and correspondence.

Freelance creators present the recurring border dispute because creative industries relied heavily on short engagements and assignment forms during the twentieth century. Comic book artists, session musicians, illustrators, and staff-adjacent writers often worked without the formal incidents of employment, yet publishers treated the output as company property from creation. Marvel Characters, Inc. v. Kirby, 726 F.3d 119 (2d Cir. 2013) illustrates the dispute in comics, where the Second Circuit applied an instance and expense analysis developed in earlier case law for works created before the 1976 Act took effect. The analysis asks whether the work was created at the instance and expense of the hiring party, a test distinct from the Reid agency factors that govern works under the 1976 Act. Confusing the two tests is a common error, and careful opinions separate pre-1978 works under the older presumption from later works under the statutory definition.

## Sound Recordings and the Work Made for Hire Fight

Sound recordings occupy an unsettled position in the work made for hire doctrine, and that position controls whether recording artists may terminate assignments of rights in albums made after 1977. A sound recording is a distinct work of authorship under Section 102, separate from the musical composition embodied in the recording. An album may therefore involve two layers of rights and two layers of termination analysis, one for the songs and one for the masters. Recording agreements typically assign the masters to a label and describe the recordings as works made for hire, but a contractual label alone satisfies the statute only if the commissioned work route applies or if the performers and producers qualify as employees within the scope of employment under the Reid agency test.

The commissioned work route created the central problem. Before late 1999, the list of categories in Section 101 for specially ordered or commissioned works did not name sound recordings as a freestanding category. Labels argued that an album could qualify as a compilation or as a contribution to a collective work, while artists argued that a master recording is neither category and that individual performers were not employees of a label under agency law. The classification mattered because an employee finding would make the label the statutory author of the masters and would remove Section 203 termination for the artists and producers who created them, leaving only the separate rights in the underlying compositions, where those compositions had not also been assigned on terms subject to termination.

In 1999, a technical amendment to the Copyright Act briefly listed sound recordings among the categories of specially ordered or commissioned works that could qualify as works made for hire. The amendment appeared in legislation described as technical in character and received little public debate before enactment. Recording artists, songwriters, and artist organizations objected that the change altered substantive rights by making it easier for labels to claim statutory authorship of albums, and hearings in 2000 examined whether the amendment should remain. Artists including prominent performers and representatives of recording artist groups told Congress that the provision threatened termination rights that would begin to mature for post-1977 recordings in the following decade. Labels answered that industry practice had long treated masters as label-owned works and that uncertainty would unsettle catalogue finance.

Congress repealed the listing in 2000. The repeal legislation removed sound recordings from the enumerated categories and stated that the amendment should not be read to imply that the earlier listing had settled the status of recordings for other periods. The repeal restored the prior statutory text but did not resolve the underlying classification question. After the repeal, a sound recording may still be a work made for hire if it was prepared by an employee within the scope of employment, or if it qualifies through another enumerated category on the facts of a particular project, a point labels continue to press through compilation and collective work arguments. Artists continue to answer that an album is a unitary recorded performance rather than a compilation of preexisting materials in the statutory sense, and that session arrangements lack the incidents of employment identified in Reid.

The result is contested status rather than a settled rule. Termination notices for albums from the late nineteen seventies and the nineteen eighties have forced labels and artists to litigate or negotiate over employee status, the role of producers, the contribution of featured and nonfeatured performers, and the effect of union agreements and pension fund payments on the agency analysis. Some disputes settle with revised ownership or licensing terms for the masters, while others proceed on the composition rights alone where the recording claim is uncertain. The 1999 and 2000 episode remains important because it shows Congress declining to resolve the contest by a categorical listing, leaving courts to apply Section 101 and Reid to recording relationships that vary by era, label, and studio practice.

## The Superman Litigation as a Worked Example

The Superman litigation shows termination applied to a foundational character created before the 1976 Act. Superman first appeared in Action Comics in 1938, created by writer Jerry Siegel and artist Joseph Shuster, who assigned rights to the predecessor of DC Comics for a small payment that became a standard illustration of the unequal bargain Congress cited when it enacted renewal and termination protections. The character generated comics, radio programs, films, television series, and merchandise over many decades, while Siegel and Shuster and later their families pursued claims for a greater share of the value. Because the original grants predated 1978, the termination claims proceeded principally under Section 304(c) rather than Section 203, with its longer timeline and its rules for grants executed before January 1, 1978.

In the late nineteen nineties, the heirs of Jerry Siegel served termination notices directed at the Superman material from Action Comics and related early works. The notices identified the grants, the works, and the effective dates as the statute requires, and DC Comics disputed their scope and effect. The dispute raised questions that recur in character litigation, including which elements of Superman were contained in the terminated material, which elements were created in later works that remained under DC control, which elements fell under the derivative works exception, and whether agreements signed after the original assignment affected the termination interest. Joanne Siegel and Laura Siegel Larson were central figures in the family side of the case, and the Shuster estate pursued related claims through a separate line of litigation that raised additional questions about agreements signed by family representatives.

In 2008, a federal court in California held the Siegel notice effective for early Superman material. The decision in Siegel v. Warner Bros. Entertainment Inc. addressed the contents of Action Comics No. 1 and other early publications and concluded that the termination recaptured rights in material from those works, subject to allocation questions, accounting issues, and limits for material created in later works or protected as derivative works. The court separated the early expression, including core elements associated with Superman and Clark Kent as they appeared in the early stories, from later additions developed over decades of publication. The ruling did not give the Siegel heirs control of every Superman work in circulation, and it did not withdraw authority to use derivative works prepared under the grants before termination, but it shifted ownership of the early material and altered the bargaining position of the parties in a franchise of exceptional commercial value.

Later proceedings and settlement covered other material and related claims. The parties resolved disputes concerning additional Superman works and Superboy material through agreements that avoided a final adjudication of every contested element, while separate appellate decisions addressed agreements involving the Shuster side and the effect of a 1992 agreement on statutory termination rights. The Ninth Circuit decision concerning DC Comics v. Pacific Pictures Corp. illustrated that a post-1977 agreement may in some circumstances supersede an earlier grant and change the termination analysis, a holding that turned on the specific documents and on whether the later agreement was itself a grant subject to a new termination period. That line of authority is fact specific and is distinguished in later cases where no such superseding agreement exists.

As a worked example, the Superman cases teach classification before valuation. Counsel and courts first identified the operative grants and their dates, then separated pre-1978 material under Section 304(c) from later works, then applied the derivative works exception to films and other adaptations, then addressed ownership shares among heirs. The character as a commercial symbol was never the legal unit of analysis. The units were identified works, identified grants, and identified expression within those works. That method explains why a ruling described in public discussion as a victory over Superman could in legal terms be a partial recapture of early material, a preservation of existing derivative works, and a settlement framework for the remainder, all at the same time and without contradiction.

## Music Catalogue Cases as a Worked Example

Music catalogues produce termination disputes at scale because a single career may involve hundreds of compositions, several publishers, co-writers with separate interests, and derivative recordings and arrangements that continue to earn royalties after a grant ends. A songwriter who assigned a share of compositions to a publisher in the nineteen seventies or nineteen eighties may hold a termination interest in that share alone, while a co-writer who signed a different agreement holds a different interest on a different schedule. Administration therefore requires song by song analysis of grant dates, copyright dates, notice windows, and ownership shares, and a catalogue level statement that a writer has recaptured a catalogue usually conceals a schedule in which some compositions have reverted, some remain under grant, and some are subject to notices that have not yet taken effect.

Scorpio Music (Black Scorpio) S.A. v. Willis, decided in the Southern District of California in 2012, is a leading illustration for a single co-writer share. Victor Willis, a co-writer of Village People compositions including works associated with the group recordings of the late nineteen seventies, served notices to terminate grants to Scorpio Music under Section 203. Scorpio argued in substance that Willis could not terminate alone because the compositions were joint works and because the grants should be treated as a single transaction requiring action by a majority of all authors. The court held that Willis could terminate his own grant of his undivided one third share and that termination of that share was effective, because Section 203 gives a joint author a separate right to terminate a grant of that author interest. The publisher argument that unanimity or majority action across all co-authors was required did not prevail on the grants at issue.

The Willis holding matters beyond the Village People catalogue. It confirms that a co-writer does not need permission from other co-writers to recapture the share that the co-writer granted, and it clarifies that a publisher cannot defeat termination by pointing to the inaction of writers who signed separate grants or who hold interests on other terms. After the effective date, Willis held his one third interest as an owner and could license that interest, while the remaining shares remained with their owners, creating the co-ownership accounting and licensing rules that govern joint works after a partial termination. A licensee seeking complete rights in a composition after such a termination must therefore assemble authority from all owners, including the recapturing writer, and a party that continues to treat the old publisher as owner of the full composition risks infringement liability for the reverted share.

Recorded music disputes add a second layer, because a jury or court may need to decide work made for hire status for masters while composition termination proceeds on a separate track. In 2024, a Florida jury verdict for members of 2 Live Crew applied Section 203 to early nineteen nineties grants, and the verdict should be stated carefully as a jury finding on work made for hire status in that case. The jury rejected the position that the relevant recordings and compositions were works made for hire that would have blocked termination, and it accepted the termination position advanced by the group members on the grants before the jury. A jury verdict decides the dispute between the parties on the evidence presented and does not by itself create an appellate rule for all recordings, a distinction that separates the 2 Live Crew result from a published appellate holding such as Reid or Kirby.

Taken together, the music cases show termination as an accounting and ownership exercise as much as a doctrine. Willis separated a one third composition share from a joint work catalogue. Mills Music separated royalty flow for a surviving derivative work from ownership of the underlying song. The 2 Live Crew verdict separated a fact finding about work made for hire status from a general rule for the recording industry. In each setting, the effective date changed who must sign a new license, who receives income attributable to the reverted share, and who may authorize a new derivative work based on the reverted material. Publishers and labels that maintain grant by grant records, notice calendars, and share level royalty systems can administer that change, while parties that treat a catalogue as a single undifferentiated asset face the disputes that the statute was designed to produce when the termination window opens.

## The Territorial Question and Foreign Rights

For decades the working assumption in much of the music and publishing trade held that a termination under the Copyright Act returned rights for the United States alone. Under that reading, a songwriter who terminated a 1960s grant took back the right to license the song at home, while the original publisher kept the right to license the same song in Canada, Britain, Germany, and every other market. The assumption rested on territoriality, the principle that copyright is a bundle of national rights created by national law, with each country defining ownership, duration, and transfer for exploitation inside its borders. The Berne Convention reinforced the habit by requiring national treatment rather than a single world copyright. Industry contracts reflected the division, collecting societies such as ASCAP and BMI administered domestic performance income, and foreign subpublishers accounted separately for overseas collections. Nothing in everyday practice forced a court to test whether the assumption matched the statutory text.

The test case concerned a single song and a grant made long before the modern catalog market. Cyril Vetter and Don Smith wrote Double Shot (Of My Baby Love), first released in 1966, and the rights passed through a chain of publishers that included Windsong Music Publishers and Resnik Music Group. Acting under Section 304(c), the termination provision for grants made before 1978, Vetter served notice to recapture the copyright. The dispute that followed as Vetter v. Resnik did not concern whether the notice was timely or whether the statutory heirs and shares had been identified correctly. The dispute concerned geography. Resnik and the defendant publishers maintained that termination restored American rights only, leaving foreign rights with the grantee for the rest of the term. Vetter maintained that the statute drew no border at all, so a valid termination ended the grant as a whole.

On January 12, 2026, the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit held for Vetter. The court reasoned that Section 304(c) speaks of recapturing rights under a copyright, and that the text contains no geographic limit confining recapture to the United States. Because Congress wrote other limits into the same subsection, including the derivative works exception and the exclusion of works made for hire, the absence of a territorial limit carried weight. The court also treated the remedial purpose of termination as relevant context, since confining recapture to one market would leave the author with only part of the second chance the statute was designed to supply. The holding was stated as plain statutory interpretation, not as a ruling on how French or Japanese law would treat a French or Japanese grant. Its subject was a grant governed by the Copyright Act of the United States, and its conclusion was that such a grant, once terminated, ended in full.

The judgment then traveled to the Supreme Court, and the manner of its ending matters as much as the result. On October 19, 2026, the Supreme Court affirmed the judgment of the Fifth Circuit by operation of 28 United States Code 2109. Four justices took no part in the consideration or decision of the case, and without participation by those four justices no quorum of qualified justices existed to hear and decide the appeal. Section 2109 directs that in such a circumstance the judgment under review is affirmed with the same effect as an affirmance by an equally divided court. An affirmance of that kind resolves the case between the parties and leaves the lower court judgment in force, but it creates no national precedent and expresses no view of the Supreme Court on the merits of the territorial question.

The geography of authority after October 19, 2026 is therefore uneven, and precision about that unevenness is part of the law. The worldwide reading of Section 304(c) is binding precedent within the Fifth Circuit, covering federal courts in Louisiana, Mississippi, and Texas, and district courts there must follow Vetter in a comparable case. Outside that circuit, Vetter is persuasive authority only, entitled to whatever weight a court in the Second Circuit, the Ninth Circuit, or any other circuit finds in the reasoning. A New York court remains free to adopt the older territorial reading, and a California court remains free to reject it. Until another circuit rules, or a quorum of the Supreme Court decides the question in a later case, catalog contracts touching foreign exploitation carry a split risk. Licenses drafted on the domestic-only assumption face challenge in one region and deference in another, and that regional difference, rather than any settled national rule, defines the practical position.

## How Other Countries Protect Authors Against Old Grants

Canada chose a mechanism built around death rather than a calendar window measured from the grant. Section 14(1) of the Copyright Act of Canada provides that when an author was the first owner of copyright and assigned or granted an interest otherwise than by will, the assignment or grant operates only for 25 years from the death of the author, and the copyright then reverts to the estate of the author for the remainder of the term. The device resembles termination in purpose, since a bargain made early in a career cannot consume the whole life of the work, but the design differs in almost every particular. The trigger is the death of the author and not a filing window. No notice has to be served on the grantee, no document has to be recorded, and no negotiation has to be opened before a deadline. The reversion arrives by operation of law, and the estate receives whatever years remain. Section 14(1) also carries its own exceptions, including special treatment for collective works and for photographs and portraits made to order, so the Canadian rule is a reversion for a defined class of grants rather than a universal reset.

Germany reached a comparable concern from the opposite premise, treating the author as the permanent center of the right. Under the Urheberrechtsgesetz, copyright itself is in principle not transferable during the life of the author, apart from succession, so a German author grants rights of use, or Nutzungsrechte, rather than assigning the copyright outright. Author contract law, strengthened by reforms in 2002 and again in later amendments, then polices the bargain around those use rights. Sections 32 and 32a give the author a claim to equitable remuneration and a further participation claim when the agreed payment becomes conspicuously disproportionate to the returns from the work, a device often called the bestseller clause. Section 41 permits recall of an exclusive right of use when the holder fails to exercise it or exercises it inadequately, after notice and a waiting period. Section 42 permits recall for changed conviction, where the work no longer reflects the beliefs of the author. None of these devices copies the American window and notice system. The German pattern instead combines inalienability at the level of the right itself, judicial adjustment at the level of price, and recall at the level of nonuse or conviction, distributing protection across the life of a contract rather than concentrating protection in a single recapture date.

India built protection for authors through assignment limits and a non-waivable share in later exploitation, rather than through a general termination window. The statute that organizes those limits began as the post-independence codification described in [Copyright Act 1957 explained](/2019/10/01/copyright-act-1957-explained/), and its assignment provisions restrict the duration and territorial scope that an assignment can claim when the instrument is silent, with a default period of five years and a default territory covering India under Section 19. Assignments also have to identify the work, the rights assigned, and the amount payable, and an assignment can lapse when the assignee fails to exercise the rights within the period the statute supplies. The balance shifted further toward authors of songs and screen works with the reforms traced in [Copyright Amendment Act 2012 and its non-waivable royalty right](/2019/10/15/copyright-amendment-act-2012-explained/), which inserted provisions into Sections 18, 19, and 33 protecting the right of the author of a literary or musical work included in a cinematograph film or sound recording to receive royalties, generally on an equal share basis, for utilization beyond the initial communication. That royalty right cannot be assigned except to a copyright society or to legal heirs, and a purported waiver or a transfer outside those channels does not defeat it.

The comparison clarifies the American design by contrast. Canada waits for death and then shortens every qualifying grant, Germany refuses full transfer and then supervises price and use, and India limits the reach of assignments and reserves a royalty stream that contract language cannot sign away. The Copyright Act of the United States does none of those things in that form. Ownership in the United States passes freely by contract, courts do not adjust a royalty rate because a song outperformed a modest advance, and nonuse alone does not end a grant. Protection arrives instead at one statutory interval, through Section 203 for later grants and Section 304 for earlier grants, and only for a party who identifies the window, prepares a compliant notice, serves the correct grantee or successor, and records the document in time. The foreign systems show that dissatisfaction with early bargains is a shared feature of copyright law across jurisdictions. The American answer remains distinctive for placing the entire weight of protection on timing and procedure, a design whose strengths and defects run through every later question in this field.

## The Procedural Route From Notice to Negotiation or Court

Procedure begins long before a notice is drafted, because termination is available only for a defined grant, a defined grantor, and a defined interval. The first task is reconstruction of the paper chain. A family or an author gathers the original publishing agreement or book contract, later assignments, probate records, wills, and corporate succession documents, and tests the chain against two threshold exclusions. A grant made by will cannot be terminated, and a work made for hire was never owned by the employee or commissioned party in the statutory sense, so no termination right arises from such a work at all. The date of the grant then sorts the file. A grant executed on or after January 1, 1978 belongs under Section 203. A grant executed before that date by an author or a statutory successor belongs under Section 304(c), and a narrow class of grants that missed an earlier chance may belong under Section 304(d). Each track carries a five-year window, and the window is computed from dates fixed by the statute, including the date of execution, the date of publication, and the end of the original or renewal term, depending on the track.

The table below settles which track or category governs a grant, which window or status applies to that category, and what effect a successful termination has on rights and on derivative works.

| Track or category | Grants or works covered | Window or status | Effect of termination |
|---|---|---|---|
| Section 203 grant | Grant executed on or after January 1, 1978 by the author | Five-year window opening 35 years after execution or after publication | Rights revert to the author or statutory successors |
| Section 304(c) grant | Grant executed before January 1, 1978 by the author or a successor | Five-year window opening 56 years after copyright or at the statutory alternative date | Rights revert to the author or statutory successors |
| Section 304(d) grant | Pre-1978 grant whose Section 304(c) window expired before October 27, 1998 | Five-year window tied to the added 20 years of term | Rights revert where the earlier window was missed |
| Grant made by will | Transfer of copyright directed by a will | Excluded by statute | Termination unavailable |
| Work made for hire | Work prepared by an employee or qualifying commissioned work | No author grant to terminate | Termination unavailable |
| Derivative work prepared under a grant | Film, translation, arrangement or other derivative work made before termination | Lawful use may continue after termination | Underlying rights revert while the derivative work stays usable |

Notice then converts a calendar calculation into a legal act. Under Section 203 and Section 304 alike, notice must be served not less than two years and not more than 10 years before the effective date chosen inside the window, and the content of the notice is governed by the regulation at 37 Code of Federal Regulations 201.10. A compliant notice identifies the grant, the work, the grantor, the parties who own the termination interest, the effective date, and the statutory basis for the claim. Service has to reach the grantee or a successor in interest, and where an address cannot be found after a reasonable search the regulation supplies alternative methods, including recordation and publication steps administered through the Copyright Office. A copy of the notice must be recorded in the Copyright Office before the effective date as a condition of effectiveness. Errors in this sequence are not treated as harmless paperwork issues. A notice served too late cannot be cured by agreement, a notice recorded after the effective date fails, and a notice omitting the required share of a joint work or a required signature exposes the whole filing to challenge.

Once a facially compliant notice is served and recorded, the route divides between negotiation and litigation. Many files move into negotiation because both sides hold something the other side values. The terminating party holds the reversion, while the outgoing grantee often holds administration systems, artwork, metadata, licenses in place, and knowledge of the market for the work. A declaratory judgment action remains available when the parties dispute validity, ownership shares, derivative status, or territorial scope, and federal courts decide such disputes on the documents and the statute rather than on the fairness of the original bargain. Accounting questions follow the effective date, since income attributable to exploitation after termination belongs to the new owner, subject to the derivative works exception and to any new agreement the parties sign. The procedural route is therefore unforgiving at the front end and flexible at the back end. Statute and regulation govern the notice with little room for repair, and contract governs almost everything the parties build on top of a termination that has taken effect.

## What Termination Changes in a Negotiation

Termination changes a negotiation first by changing who holds the default. Without a termination right, a publisher or label that owns a catalog under a valid grant negotiates from entitlement, and an author seeking better terms offers value, such as new works, extended cooperation, or settlement of a dispute, in exchange for concessions. After a valid notice fixes an effective date, the positions reverse. The grantee faces a date after which continued exploitation of the underlying work requires a new grant, and the author or the estate faces a choice among regranting to the incumbent, moving administration elsewhere, self-administering, or selling the recaptured interest. The statute does not set the price of the new bargain, and neither Section 203 nor Section 304 supplies a royalty rate, an advance figure, or a duty to negotiate in good faith. The right supplies leverage and a deadline. Market evidence supplies the numbers, through comparable deals, streaming and synchronization income, performance royalties tracked by ASCAP and BMI, and the remaining years of copyright term that a buyer or licensee can exploit.

The second change concerns information. A party preparing a termination has to assemble the chain of title, the copyright registration and renewal records, probate documents, and royalty statements, and that assembly often reveals gaps that affect value for both sides. A missing renewal filing, an unrecorded assignment, a will that divided a termination interest among children who disagree, or a songwriter share that was never administered can reduce what a buyer will pay and can complicate a regrant to the incumbent. Sophisticated catalog buyers respond with diligence requests, representations and warranties, escrows, and holdbacks tied to the outcome of a termination window. A seller who warrants that no termination notice has been served takes a different risk from a seller who discloses a notice and prices the catalog around the effective date. Estates experience the same discipline in reverse. An estate that serves notice without resolving internal shares may hold a right that no single faction can license, since grants after termination generally require the same proportion of owners who could terminate, and fragmentation can stall a deal that the statute made possible.

The third change is temporal, and it explains why negotiations often begin years before an effective date. Notice may be served as much as 10 years in advance, so a publisher holding a notice on a valuable catalog faces a long period in which investment decisions carry a known horizon. Spending on restoration of a film, remastering of recordings, or a new marketing campaign has to be weighed against the derivative works exception, which protects a derivative work prepared under authority of the grant before termination but does not authorize new derivative works after the effective date. A record company may continue selling a remaster prepared in time, while a film studio may continue exploiting a completed adaptation, and each incumbent will price that protected position in talks. Works that exist only as plans receive no protection, so a sequel, a new translation, or a fresh arrangement belongs to the negotiation rather than to the exception. The distinction pushes both sides to inventory derivative works with precision, title by title and version by version, before arguing about price.

The final change concerns the shape of any settlement. Because termination cannot be waived in advance, a settlement cannot simply declare the right extinguished. An agreement that purports to override the statute, made before a notice takes effect, collides with the rule in Section 203 and Section 304 that termination may be effected notwithstanding any agreement to the contrary. What parties can do, and regularly do, is bargain after the right has ripened. A terminating author may make a new grant to the same publisher on different terms, may grant administration only while retaining ownership, may divide rights by media or territory in a fresh instrument, or may license nonexclusively while a sale is explored. Each structure is a new contract built on a statutory reset, not a surrender of the reset itself. That distinction preserves the policy of the statute inside ordinary commercial practice. Negotiation decides where recaptured rights go and at what price, and the statute decides only that the old grant ends and that the decision belongs, for once, to the side that wrote, composed, or inherited the work.

## The Critique of Termination as a System

Criticism of termination begins with the gap between a simple promise and an intricate machine. The promise is that an author who sold rights cheaply before the value of a work was known receives a second opportunity after time reveals that value. The machine requires the author or a family to locate decades-old contracts, compute a window from dates that may involve execution, publication, and renewal, identify every holder of a termination interest, obtain the required proportion of signatures, serve the correct successor company, and record a compliant notice in the Copyright Office, all inside deadlines that cannot be extended by consent. A system designed for authors without leverage often rewards the families and estates with the strongest legal support, while an heir who learns of the right after the window closes receives nothing. Formal defects have defeated or threatened claims in disputes involving well-known books and songs, and the cost of a declaratory judgment action can consume a large share of the value at stake for a mid-level catalog. Defenders answer that a property rule needs clear steps and that certainty for buyers requires hard edges, but the critique retains force wherever a missed formality defeats the class of claimant the statute was written to protect.

A second line of criticism concerns fragmentation and holdout. Congress distributed termination interests among a surviving spouse, children, and grandchildren under a per stirpes scheme in Section 203 and Section 304, and termination requires action by a majority of the interest for a single author and by a majority for each joint author in a joint work. Family structures rarely map neatly onto that scheme after two generations. Estranged children, minor grandchildren represented by guardians, and estates in probate can leave a catalog with a valid termination and no workable licensing majority. Fred Fisher Music Co. v. M. Witmark and Sons and the later disputes over shares illustrated how succession law and copyright law intersect in ways neither system handles cleanly. Mills Music, Inc. v. Snyder added a different complication, holding that the derivative works exception preserved royalty arrangements for a derivative work song arrangement in a manner many songwriters regarded as a windfall for publishers. Each case was defensible on its text, and together the cases show a system whose outcomes turn on family law, contract drafting, and exception drafting as much as on authorship.

A third line of criticism questions the economic premise. Termination transfers value only when a work still earns money near the end of a 35-year or 56-year interval, and the great majority of books, songs, and articles earn little after a fraction of that span. For the small class of durable works, the right can produce a large transfer, and critics argue that the transfer often rewards heirs rather than creators, disturbs settled expectations of companies that invested in keeping a work available, and raises the price of catalog acquisitions for every other work in a portfolio. Steinbeck v. Penguin Group sharpened the contractual version of the dispute, when the Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit gave effect to a later agreement that replaced an earlier grant and thereby defeated a termination claim tied to the earlier grant, a result the dissent and many commentators treated as a route around inalienability. The Kirby litigation against Marvel, settled in 2014 before a Supreme Court decision, raised the work made for hire boundary that decides whether a famous creator held any termination interest at all. Critics on the other side answer that investment after acquisition is usually modest for back catalog, that heirs are a legitimate object of a family-support statute, and that a predictable recapture date can be priced into acquisitions from the start.

The most durable critique may be structural. Termination polices one moment, the making of an old grant, while the market for creative work has moved toward structures the statute reaches poorly. Employment relationships and carefully drafted commissioned work agreements place output inside the work made for hire doctrine, where no termination interest arises. New distribution revenue, such as interactive streaming income classified through labels and collecting arrangements, reaches authors through layers of contract that a recapture of the underlying composition or recording copyright only partly controls. A songwriter may terminate a publishing grant and still face a sound recording owned by a label under a separate analysis, since federal copyright in sound recordings and the state law history around recordings follow a distinct path. The critique therefore does not rest on a single defect. It describes a right of real force inside a narrowing field, administered through demanding procedure, distributed through family shares that complicate licensing, and defended most plausibly as a limited correction rather than as a general charter of author bargaining power.

## The Strongest Case Against a Worldwide Reading

The complication in the territorial debate deserves a full statement in the form its strongest advocates would choose, because the publishers who resisted a worldwide reading in Vetter v. Resnik were not defending a clerical preference. BMG and the supporting publishers argued from the structure of copyright itself. Copyright, on that view, is not a single global entitlement but a set of national rights, each created and bounded by the law of the country where protection is claimed. A statute of the United States can end a grant of United States rights, because those rights exist under the Copyright Act. Foreign rights exist under the Copyright Act of Canada, the Urheberrechtsgesetz of Germany, the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 of the United Kingdom, and the copyright statutes of other states, and those systems decide for themselves whether an assignment endures, reverts, or becomes subject to recall. A termination notice recorded in Washington, on this argument, should no more extinguish a British or German right than a British court order should rewrite title to a building in Louisiana. The principle has a name in conflict of laws, territoriality, and the publishers placed that principle at the center of the case rather than at the margin.

Reliance supplied the second pillar of the argument, and reliance in catalog publishing is measured in decades of conduct rather than in a single transaction. Publishers and subpublishers built foreign exploitation networks on the domestic-only understanding, paying advances, commissioning translations and local recordings, registering works with foreign collecting societies, and accounting to authors on the basis that the foreign share would remain administered by the grantee for the full term. Catalog buyers priced acquisitions on the same understanding, lenders took security against catalogs on the same understanding, and estates accepted settlements on the same understanding. A worldwide reading unsettles each layer at once, and it does so retroactively for grants signed in the 1950s and 1960s, when the parties could not have priced a recapture they had no reason to expect. Courts are generally cautious about readings that disturb settled property arrangements on a large scale without a clear statement from the legislature, and the publishers asked for that caution here. Section 304(c), the argument continued, contains no clear statement about foreign rights at all, and silence should be read against disruption rather than in favor of a global transfer that Congress never described in committee reports or floor debate in territorial terms.

The third pillar concerned governance and workability. Foreign exploitation is administered under foreign contracts, foreign collecting society rules, and in many markets foreign mandatory law protecting authors in ways that differ from the American model. If termination under Section 304(c) recaptures foreign rights, a court in the United States must then decide which foreign rights existed, whether a subpublisher grant was a derivative arrangement or an independent assignment, and how a French moral rights claim or a German recall claim interacts with an American effective date. The publishers argued that such questions belong to the courts and legislatures of the countries whose rights are at stake, and that the cleaner rule, termination of United States rights only, keeps each legal system master of the rights it created. That argument also drew support from the general presumption against extraterritorial application of statutes of the United States, a presumption the Supreme Court has applied in cases such as Morrison v. National Australia Bank to require a clear indication before a statute is read to govern conduct and rights abroad.

The thesis of this article narrows at exactly this point, and the narrowing should be stated without evasion. The statutory text argument that prevailed in the Fifth Circuit is a serious argument, grounded in the absence of a geographic limit in Section 304(c) and in the presence of express limits elsewhere in the same provision. The publishers held a serious counterargument, grounded in territoriality, reliance, and the presumption against extraterritorial effect, and that counterargument lost in one circuit rather than across the nation. After the affirmance of October 19, 2026 under 28 United States Code 2109, no national precedent settled the contest. The worldwide question therefore stays open outside the Fifth Circuit. Inside Louisiana, Mississippi, and Texas, Vetter governs and a valid termination reaches foreign rights. In New York, California, and Tennessee, where much catalog business is litigated and administered, the question awaits a court with authority to answer for that forum, and any account that treats the worldwide reading as established national law overstates what the cases hold.

## What Is Settled, What Remains Open, and Why the Right Endures

Several propositions stand on firm ground after nearly five decades under Section 203 and a longer period under the renewal and termination structure that Section 304 carried forward. A qualifying grant can be terminated during a five-year statutory window even when the contract says the grant is perpetual, irrevocable, or a waiver of any right to reclaim the work. The right cannot be surrendered by an agreement made before termination takes effect, because both provisions state that termination may be effected notwithstanding any agreement to the contrary, including an agreement to make a will or a future grant. The class of persons who may terminate is fixed by statute, moving from the author to a surviving spouse, children, and grandchildren in defined shares when the author is dead. A grant by will is outside the system. A work made for hire is outside the system. A derivative work prepared under authority of a grant before termination may continue to be used under the terms of the grant, while new derivative works after the effective date require permission from the owner of the recaptured rights. Notice, service, and recordation follow the regulation at 37 Code of Federal Regulations 201.10, and the Copyright Office maintains the public record on which buyers and licensees rely.

Other propositions remain open or only partly resolved, and an honest map separates the two columns. The territorial reach of termination is the largest open item after Vetter, binding as a worldwide rule in the Fifth Circuit and unresolved as national law. The boundary between a replacement agreement that creates a new grant and an impermissible advance waiver remains sensitive after Steinbeck, and outcomes turn on how a later instrument is drafted and characterized. The work made for hire boundary continues to decide major catalogs before any window is reached, through the employee test of Community for Creative Non-Violence v. Reid and through the commissioned work categories and writing requirements of Section 101. Valuation of recaptured rights in new media channels remains a matter of contract evidence rather than statutory rule, and fragmentation among heirs continues to produce valid terminations that are hard to license. The table in Section 24 organizes the settled tracks and exclusions in one place, and the open questions cluster around the edges of that table rather than at the center, in geography, characterization, and family shares.

The endurance of the right, despite demanding procedure and persistent criticism, rests on a judgment Congress made twice and declined to withdraw. In the Copyright Act of 1976, effective January 1, 1978, Congress treated the unequal bargaining position of authors as a structural fact rather than a private misfortune, and it answered with a recapture right that a contract could not remove. In the Sonny Bono Copyright Term Extension Act of 1998, Congress added years to the term and added Section 304(d) so that a class of older grants received a corresponding chance at the added years, confirming that termination was not an accident of one drafting era. Courts have enforced the right for songs, books, and characters, have refused enforcement where a statutory boundary placed a work outside the system, and have left the central mechanism intact through disputes over shares, notices, and derivative works. Legislatures in Canada, Germany, and India answered the same underlying concern through reversion, recall, remuneration, and reserved royalties, which suggests that the concern itself is a permanent feature of copyright markets rather than a temporary grievance of one generation of authors.

A restatement in closing therefore takes a different shape from a slogan about authors winning and publishers losing. Termination endures because a legal system that allows rights to be sold for the full term, at a price fixed before value is known, reserves one supervised opportunity to reopen the allocation after the market has spoken. That opportunity arrives late, lasts five years, demands exact compliance, protects derivative works already made, excludes grants by will and works made for hire, and leaves price to fresh negotiation. Decisions about a specific grant belong with a qualified professional. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does copyright termination mean?

Termination means a statutory power to end a transfer or license of copyright and to bring the transferred rights back to the author or to the statutory successors of the author. The power comes from sections 203 and 304 of the Copyright Act of 1976 rather than from a contract term. A grant made by an author can be ended during a defined window even when the grant states a longer term, a perpetual term, or an irrevocable term. When termination takes effect, future use of the work under the ended grant requires a new grant from the party holding the recaptured rights, and that party may license the work to a new publisher, producer, or distributor instead.

### Q: How is termination different from copyright expiring?

Expiration ends the copyright itself, while termination ends only a transfer or license and leaves the copyright alive under new ownership. When a copyright expires, the work enters the public domain and any person may copy, adapt, or distribute the work without permission and without payment. When a grant is terminated, the copyright continues for the remainder of its term, the recaptured rights vest in the author or the statutory successors, and other persons still need permission to use the work. Expiration follows the calendar of the copyright term, measured from creation, publication, or the death of the author. Termination follows the date of the grant and opens only during the statutory window tied to that grant.

### Q: Why did Congress create termination rights?

Congress created termination rights to address unequal bargaining power between individual authors and the publishers, studios, and labels that acquire copyrights. At the time of a first grant, the future value of a book, song, or other work is usually unknown, so an author may sell rights for a small payment before the market has tested the work. A second chance to own the rights decades later lets the author or the family of the author share in value that only became clear over time. The mechanism resembles the renewal structure of earlier law, but termination was designed as a safeguard that operates by statute and that a private bargain at the time of the grant cannot remove.

### Q: Can an author waive termination rights in a contract?

Termination cannot be waived by contract, because sections 203 and 304 provide that termination may be effected despite any agreement to the contrary. Language promising never to terminate, language assigning a future termination interest to a grantee, and language treating a later termination notice as a breach all fail against the statute. The rule reflects a judgment that the right would be worthless if the stronger bargaining party could demand a waiver as a standard condition of every grant. Parties remain free to negotiate new terms, to make a new grant after termination takes effect, and to settle a dispute over whether a grant is subject to termination or whether a notice was valid and timely.

### Q: What is the difference between Section 203 and Section 304 termination?

Section 203 covers grants made by the author on or after January 1, 1978, while section 304 covers grants made before that date under the earlier copyright system. Under section 203, the window opens thirty five years after execution of the grant, or, for a grant covering publication, at the earlier of forty years after execution and thirty five years after publication. Under section 304(c), the window opens fifty six years after copyright was secured, and section 304(d) gives a further window for rights that the earlier window missed. The two provisions also differ in the persons who may terminate, the treatment of derivative works, and the procedural details that govern notice. Both provisions nevertheless share the same core idea that a grant long in force may be ended during a single defined opportunity.

### Q: Does termination apply to works made for hire?

Termination does not apply to works made for hire, because the statute excludes such works from sections 203 and 304. In a work made for hire, the employer or the commissioning party that falls within a statutory category is treated as the author for copyright purposes, and the individual creator never holds the transferred rights that termination is meant to recapture. Classification turns on the employment relationship and, for specially ordered works, on a signed writing and a listed category, rather than on a label chosen by the parties. Disputes over termination therefore often begin with classification, since a finding that a work was made for hire ends the termination claim before any question of notice or timing is reached.

### Q: Who inherits a termination interest?

When an author has died, the termination interest passes by statute rather than by the will of the author. A surviving spouse owns the entire interest unless there are surviving children or grandchildren, in which case the spouse takes one half and the children and grandchildren take the other half per stirpes. When no spouse survives, the children and grandchildren take the whole interest per stirpes, meaning a deceased child share passes down to descendants of that child. If no spouse, child, or grandchild survives, the interest passes to the executor, administrator, personal representative, or next of kin under the statute. A termination decision then requires action by owners holding more than one half of the interest.

### Q: How does termination work for joint authors?

For a joint work, a grant made by two or more authors is terminated by the authors who executed the grant, and after the death of an author the share of that author is exercised by the persons holding the termination interest of that author. Section 203 requires a majority of the authors who executed the grant to join in a termination, so a single joint author acting alone cannot end a joint grant. Each share is counted by authorship rather than by the size of a royalty interest, and a deceased author share is exercised by the statutory successors as a group holding more than one half of that share. Grants made separately by joint authors raise separate questions about which grant a notice reaches.

### Q: What must a termination notice contain?

A termination notice must identify the grant being ended and state the effective date of termination, which must fall inside the statutory window. Under the regulation at 37 Code of Federal Regulations 201.10, the notice gives the name of each grantee whose rights are being terminated, the title of each work, the date of the grant and the date of copyright where relevant, and a brief statement of the relationship between the terminating party and the grant. The notice must be signed by the terminating party or by an authorized agent. Content rules are stated in the regulation, and service must reach the grantee or a successor in interest before the effective date chosen in the notice.

### Q: Where is a termination notice recorded?

A copy of a termination notice must be recorded with the United States Copyright Office before the effective date of termination, as 37 Code of Federal Regulations 201.10 requires. Recordation is a condition of the termination taking effect, separate from service of the notice on the grantee. The Copyright Office accepts the document for the public record, assigns recordation information, and returns a certificate, but recordation does not amount to a ruling that the notice is legally valid. A complete submission includes the notice copy, identifying information for indexing, and the recordation fee set by the Office. Parties often record early in the notice period so that a correctable defect in the submission can be addressed before the effective date arrives.

### Q: Can a defective notice still be valid?

A defective notice can still be valid when the defect is a harmless error and the notice, read as a whole, still identifies the grant, the work, and the effective date with reasonable clarity. Courts have treated errors in lists of works or in minor identifying details as harmless where the grantee received fair warning of the rights at stake and was not misled. Defects of substance are different: an effective date outside the window, service that misses the statutory deadline, or failure to record before the effective date can defeat a termination. The distinction reflects the purpose of notice, which is to give the grantee a clear and timely opportunity to plan for the end of the grant.

### Q: What rights return after termination takes effect?

After termination takes effect, the rights covered by the ended grant return to the author or to the statutory successors who exercised the termination. The returning rights include reproduction, preparation of derivative works, distribution, public performance, and public display, to the extent those rights were transferred in the grant. Ownership vests on the effective date, and from that date the former grantee holds no continuing right under the ended grant except where the statute preserves a specific use, most notably the derivative works exception. Rights that the author never transferred do not return because no transfer occurred, and rights in a separate work by another author stay with that author. Recaptured rights may then be held, licensed, or transferred again.

### Q: Can a derivative work keep being distributed after termination?

A derivative work prepared under authority of a grant before termination may continue to be used under the terms of the grant after termination takes effect. The exception covers a film based on a novel, a translation, an arrangement, or another authorized derivative work that already exists, and it allows continued distribution and performance of that existing work. The exception does not allow preparation of a new derivative work after termination, so a sequel, a remake, or a fresh adaptation requires a new grant from the owner of the recaptured rights. The line between exploiting an existing derivative work and creating a new one decides many disputes, especially where a franchise adds episodes, editions, or versions after a termination date.

### Q: Who receives royalties from a surviving derivative work?

Royalties from a surviving derivative work continue to flow as the original grant provides, a result settled by Mills Music, Inc. v. Snyder, 469 U.S. 153 (1985). In that case, a song had been assigned, a derivative use had been authorized, and termination later recaptured the copyright in the underlying song. The Supreme Court held that the derivative works exception preserved the terms of the grant for the existing derivative work, including the royalty allocation between publisher and songwriter estate. Termination therefore did not redirect the whole royalty stream to the terminating party. The grantee keeps the contractual share for the surviving derivative work, while rights outside that exception belong to the owner of the recaptured copyright.

### Q: Does termination apply to grants made by will?

Termination does not apply to a grant made by the will of the author. Sections 203 and 304 both exclude a transfer by will from the grants that may be terminated, so a beneficiary who takes copyright under a will holds the rights without exposure to a later statutory termination of that transfer. The exclusion sits beside the work made for hire exclusion as a boundary on the whole mechanism. A related rule governs the termination interest itself: after the death of the author, that interest passes under the statutory order of spouse, children, and grandchildren, and the will of the author cannot redirect the interest to a different person or defeat a share assigned by the statute.

### Q: Can an author make a new grant after termination takes effect?

An author or the statutory successors may make a new grant of recaptured rights after termination takes effect, and such a grant may be made to the former grantee or to a different publisher, studio, or label. A new grant is also possible through an agreement reached before the effective date when the agreement is made after service of the termination notice and satisfies the statutory conditions for such an agreement, including, in the section 203 setting, a rule for agreements with the original grantee. A promise made at the time of the original grant to return the rights in advance is treated differently, because the statute operates despite any agreement to the contrary and guards the termination power from advance surrender.

### Q: Why did the Superman copyright become a termination dispute?

The Superman dispute arose because Jerome Siegel and Joe Shuster created the character as young writers and granted rights to the publisher for a small payment before the scale of the franchise was known. Decades later, the heirs of Jerome Siegel served termination notices under section 304 for the pre 1978 grants, seeking to recapture rights in the early Superman material, including the first published stories. Litigation then addressed which elements came from the first publications, which elements came from later works made under the grant, and whether settlements signed by family members barred termination. The case became a public illustration of the policy behind termination: a grant made when value was speculative is revisited after value has been proved over a long period.

### Q: What did Mills Music v Snyder decide?

Mills Music, Inc. v. Snyder, 469 U.S. 153 (1985), decided how the derivative works exception interacts with royalty terms in a terminated grant. The songwriter Ted Snyder had assigned rights in a song, the publisher had authorized derivative uses, and the heirs later terminated the grant under section 304. The question was whether continued use of the derivative works carried the royalty split from the grant or sent all royalties to the heirs. The Supreme Court held that utilization of a derivative work under the exception continues under the terms of the grant, so the publisher retained the contractual share. The decision preserved the bargain for existing derivative works while leaving the underlying copyright recaptured for other uses after termination.

### Q: Does United States termination return foreign rights?

Whether termination returns foreign rights was long contested, because a grant often assigns rights for the whole world while the termination statute is a creature of United States law. In Vetter v. Resnik, the Fifth Circuit in 2026 held that termination recaptured worldwide rights where the grant had conveyed rights on a worldwide basis, reasoning that the statute would be weakened if a former grantee kept exploiting the work abroad under an ended grant. That holding binds district courts inside the Fifth Circuit and does not settle the question nationally, since other circuits have not adopted the same rule and a contrary rule remains possible elsewhere. Until a broader consensus or a Supreme Court decision emerges, the geographic reach of a termination depends in part on the forum hearing the dispute.

### Q: How does Canada handle reversion of copyright grants?

Canada handles reversion through section 14(1) of the Canadian Copyright Act, which returns an assignment or grant to the estate of the author twenty five years after the death of the author. The Canadian rule is triggered by death rather than by the date of the grant, so no notice window of the United States type controls the timing. Reversion covers an assignment or an exclusive license made by the author during life, subject to exceptions that include a grant made by will and collective works and certain uses in such works. After reversion, further dealing with the rights requires authority from the estate. The structure shows a different legislative balance: the interest of the family after death is protected automatically, without service or recordation of a termination notice.
