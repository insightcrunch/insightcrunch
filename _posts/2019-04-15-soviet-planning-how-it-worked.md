Soviet planning is the most argued about economic mechanism of the twentieth century and among the least described. Debates about whether planning can work proceed for pages without anyone saying what the planners in Moscow actually did between January and December of a plan year: what paper moved, who signed it, what numbers it carried, and what the people who received it did next. This article supplies the description. Its central finding can be stated in one sentence, and everything that follows is the evidence for it. Soviet planning did not compute an allocation of resources for the economy. It took the output that enterprises had already achieved, added an increment, argued about the size of the increment and the inputs that would accompany it, and called the result a plan. The method has a name in the literature, planning from the achieved level, and once the name is understood the famous arguments on both sides change shape, because both sides were largely arguing about a method the Soviet economy did not use.

![A planning office desk with material balance sheets, a plan cycle chart and ministry correspondence, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Neither of the two popular pictures survives the description. In one picture, a vast computer, or its paper equivalent, weighs every need in the society against every resource and issues a coherent set of instructions that factories then execute, so that the economy resembles a single firm drawn to national scale. In the other picture, the whole apparatus is a fiction draped over chaos, with officials inventing numbers and enterprises ignoring them. The record shows something more interesting than either. It shows a working administrative routine, repeated annually for decades, that genuinely coordinated a large part of a continental economy, that moved real resources toward the objectives the leadership prioritised, and that did so by bargaining over quantities rather than by calculating values. The routine had a stable internal logic. Its participants understood its rules intimately and behaved, within those rules, with consistent rationality. The failures of the system were not the failures of stupid men executing a senseless design. They were the predictable output of sensible men responding to the incentives the design placed in front of them, and the description below follows that logic stage by stage: the mechanics first, then the incentives the mechanics created, then the informal economy that grew up to make the formal one function, then the analysis that explained why shortage was the system's normal condition, and finally the reforms that tried, and failed, to change the method without changing the system.

### How did Soviet planning actually work?

by incrementing previously achieved output rather than computing an optimum. A central agency built physical balances for a few thousand aggregated commodity groups, iterating targets with ministries and enterprises, while an informal network of unofficial supply agents obtained the inputs the plan did not deliver.

That answer compresses the whole article, and each clause of it needs unpacking. Incrementing rather than computing: the target for a product in the coming year began from the tonnage, yardage, or unit count achieved in the year just ending, and the argument between the centre and the producer concerned the percentage to be added. The aggregated groups, a few thousand of them: the central balance sheet covered steel, coal, cement, grain, cloth, and their like as broad categories, not the millions of distinct specifications into which those categories dissolved at the factory gate. Iterating: the numbers went down the hierarchy as proposals and came back up as counter proposals, sometimes several times, before hardening into obligations. And the informal network: because the balances were aggregated and the specifications were not, enterprises chronically lacked the exact inputs their targets required, and a class of professional expediters existed to find those inputs outside the plan's own channels. A reader who holds those four clauses can already see why the standard debate misfires. The calculation objection, stated by Ludwig von Mises and developed by Friedrich Hayek, holds that without market prices a planning agency cannot compare the value of alternative uses of a resource and therefore cannot allocate rationally. The objection imagines an agency attempting to choose among uses. The Soviet agency, most of the time, was not choosing among uses at all. It was rolling forward an existing pattern of uses and arguing about its growth rate. Whether that defence of the record helps or hurts the case for planning is a question this article takes up in its own section, after the record itself is on the table.

## Soviet planning at the centre: Gosplan and the division of labour

The institution at the centre of the system was the State Planning Commission, known by its Russian abbreviation as Gosplan, established in 1921 as the body charged with drawing up a general plan for the national economy. It grew out of the commission that had produced the electrification plan of the revolutionary period, and it inherited from that origin a characteristic cast of mind: the economy was to be developed by identifying the decisive sectors, concentrating resources upon them, and treating the rest as following in their wake. Gosplan was never, however, the whole of the planning apparatus, and one of the first corrections the description requires is to the image of a single office deciding everything. Around and beneath it stood a set of distinct institutions, each holding one piece of the coordination task, and the seams between those pieces explain a great deal of what the system did and failed to do.

Gosplan itself drew up the plans: the long range perspective plans, the five year plans that set the strategy of a period, and the annual plans that carried legal force for enterprises. A separate state committee, Gossnab, handled material and technical supply, the actual allocation of producer goods among users through a system of funded commodities and supply orders, so that the right to receive steel or timber came not from the plan document alone but from an allocation certificate issued through the supply network. The Ministry of Finance controlled the money side, enterprise budgets, credits through the state bank, and the prices at which output was valued, while a state committee on prices fixed those prices administratively, sometimes leaving them unchanged for long stretches. The branch ministries in Moscow, each responsible for an industry such as ferrous metallurgy, chemicals, machine building, or light industry, stood between the centre and the enterprises, disaggregating the plan to their subordinate factories and aggregating the factories' requests back upward. Regional and republican bodies held smaller planning commissions of their own for local industry and services. The enterprise, at the bottom, was the unit that actually produced, and its director was the figure on whom the system's pressures converged.

This division of labour matters because no single participant ever saw the whole. Gosplan saw aggregated quantities. Gossnab saw allocation claims against available funds of goods. The ministry saw its branch's capacity and its branch's needs, and it saw rival branches only as competitors at the margin of the centre's attention. The enterprise director saw his own machinery, his own workforce, his own stockroom, and the specific assortment his customers demanded, none of which was visible in the aggregated categories three levels above him. Information in the system flowed in the form of reports and requests, and every participant who passed information upward also had interests that shaped what he passed. The description of planning as a hierarchy is therefore incomplete in a way that misleads. It was a hierarchy of bargaining, in which each level negotiated with the level above over the two numbers that defined its existence: the output it would be required to deliver and the inputs it would be authorised to receive.

### What did the centre actually decide, and what did it leave below?

The centre decided quantities for the aggregated groups it balanced, the distribution of investment among branches, and the priority ranking that governed whose claims were met when goods ran short. It did not decide the specification of most products, the assortment within a group, or the matching of a buyer to a seller, all of which settled lower down.

The boundary between what the centre decided and what it left below was not a defect that better administration could have removed; it was set by arithmetic. A modern industrial economy produces millions of distinct items once size, grade, dimension, and specification are counted, and no staff of planners, however enlarged, could construct balances for each of them. The literature's standard estimate, given in Alec Nove's account of the Soviet economic system and repeated across the scholarship, is that Gosplan itself drew up balances for roughly two thousand commodity groups, that Gossnab allocated a somewhat larger number of funded products, and that the ministries and supply organisations handled a far larger set below them, with the total of centrally touched categories amounting to an order of magnitude in the low tens of thousands at most. These counts are approximate orders of magnitude, documented in the scholarly literature and uncontested within it, and the article states them on that basis and no other. Against them stood the millions of specifications in which production actually occurred. The plan, in other words, governed the economy the way a map at small scale governs a coastline: the great features were fixed by it, and everything intricate was settled by people on the ground, using the map as a constraint and a claim ticket rather than as a set of directions.

## Material balances: the coordinating instrument

The technical heart of the method was the material balance. For each commodity group within its remit, the planning staff constructed a table with two sides. On one side stood the sources of the commodity for the coming period: production from domestic enterprises, imports, and stocks carried forward. On the other side stood the uses: deliveries to each consuming branch, exports, additions to reserves, and losses. The task of the planner was to adjust the two sides until they matched, and the adjustment was the planning. If uses exceeded sources, something had to give: production targets for the supplying branch were raised, allocations to a consuming branch were trimmed, imports were increased, or a reserve was drawn down. If sources exceeded uses, targets could be relaxed or allocations enlarged. Each balance was connected to others, since steel was a source in its own balance and a use in the balances for machinery, construction, and transport, so that changing one figure propagated changes through a web of tables, and the staff worked through successive approximations, round after round, until the set of balances cohered.

Several features of this instrument deserve emphasis, because they determined what the system could and could not do. The balances were drawn in physical units: tonnes, metres, cubic metres, pieces. Money entered the process through enterprise accounting and the financial plan, but the coordinating arithmetic of the material balance was an arithmetic of quantities, and it contained no variable representing the relative scarcity or value of the things being balanced. A tonne of steel allocated to one use rather than another appeared in the tables as a tonne either way. The balance could therefore establish feasibility, the consistency of a set of physical flows, but it could not establish desirability, the proposition that this pattern of flows was better than another feasible pattern. The second feature is aggregation. A balance for rolled steel treated as one commodity what the consuming factories experienced as hundreds of profiles and dimensions. The balance could be exactly satisfied while a machine works starved for the one profile it needed and drowned in profiles it could not use, and this gap between the balanced aggregate and the unbalanced specification was a permanent feature of the system's operation, not an occasional mishap. The third feature is time. Constructing and iterating the balances for an annual plan consumed most of a year, which meant that the plan for a given year was substantially built from information about earlier years, and that by the time targets reached enterprises the conditions they described had already moved.

### What is a material balance, in plain terms?

A material balance is a two sided table for one group of goods, listing where the quantity will come from and where it will go, in physical units. Planners adjusted production targets and allocations until the two sides matched. A satisfied balance proved a flow of goods was physically consistent, never that it was the best available pattern.

The method had genuine strengths, and the description should record them before turning to its costs. A system of physical balances is a powerful instrument for mobilisation. When the leadership decided that certain outputs mattered above all others, the balance method could identify what those outputs required, trace the requirements backward through the supplying branches, and concentrate authorisations accordingly. The construction of heavy industry in the nineteen thirties, the evacuation and reassembly of industry during the war, and the postwar reconstruction were all conducted through this machinery, and the machinery delivered the prioritised goods, at whatever cost to the unprioritised ones. The method is also transparent in a way market coordination is not: every allocation exists as a document, signed by an identifiable official, and the historian can reconstruct who authorised what. What the method could not do was weigh. It could not say whether the steel in a bridge would have served the society better as rails, or the rails as pipes, except by the political judgement of the officials adjusting the tables, exercised without any common measure of the alternatives. That absence, of a common measure, is the point at which the description connects with the theoretical objection, and the connection is closer than either side of the old debate usually allows.

### Why did the balances never quite balance?

Because every figure entering them was supplied by an organisation with an interest in its size. Producers understated capacity, consumers overstated need, ministries defended their branches, and the staff adjusted claims against one another using past performance as the only firm datum. The iteration converged on a negotiated consistency, not on the true quantities.

There is a further, quieter point about the balances that the literature stresses and popular accounts miss. The plan that emerged from the iteration was not, in the end, a command that the economy obeyed in the manner of a soldier obeying an order. It was a settlement. Ministries that lost an argument in one round reopened it in the next; enterprises that received impossible targets set about renegotiating them through the year, and plan corrections, adjustments, and supplementary allocations were a continuous feature of operation rather than an exception. The annual plan was revised so routinely that participants distinguished between the plan as approved and the plan as it stood after the year's corrections, and success was judged against a target that had itself been a moving object. None of this means the plan was unreal. Enterprises that ignored it entirely faced sanctions, and its priorities genuinely governed the flow of investment and of scarce producer goods. It means that the plan's authority was the authority of a framework within which bargaining continued, not the authority of a blueprint that ended discussion. Planning, in the Soviet practice, was a verb that never quite became a noun.

## The plan cycle: how a year of planning unfolded

The annual plan was produced by a cycle with a stable shape, and the cycle is the article's findable artifact, because laying its stages side by side shows where the distortions entered. The sequence began well before the year it governed. Gosplan, working from the directives of the political leadership and from the framework of the five year plan within which the annual plan sat, drafted control figures: provisional targets for the main branches and commodity groups, together with limits on investment, employment, and the wage fund. These figures descended to the ministries, which disaggregated them among their enterprises, adjusting for the particularities of each plant as the ministry understood them. The enterprises then did the most consequential work in the whole cycle. Each director, with his chief engineer and his planning department, examined the control figures against his machinery, his labour force, his expected supplies, and his judgement of what could be squeezed or concealed, and composed the enterprise's draft plan, accompanied by requests for the inputs, investment, and supply allocations the draft required. Those drafts ascended to the ministry, which consolidated them, defended the branch's aggregate against rival branches, and bargained with Gosplan over the branch totals. Gosplan revised the balances in light of the consolidated claims, the political leadership settled the remaining disputes and approved the plan, and the approved figures descended again, now as binding targets, accompanied by the supply allocations through Gossnab that entitled enterprises to receive their inputs. Production then proceeded under a plan that the year itself kept amending.

The table below sets out the plan cycle stage by stage. Its columns follow the brief of this article: who acted, what information they held, what interest they had in bending that information, and what distortion the bending produced. Read down the final column and the article's argument assembles itself, because the distortions are not separate accidents. They compound, each stage handing the next a set of figures already shaped by the interests of the stage before.

Two properties of the cycle deserve to be drawn out. The first is that the enterprise draft was the only document in the chain composed by people who knew the productive reality at first hand, and it was composed by people whose careers depended on the gap between that reality and what they reported. Every later stage worked from the enterprise's numbers. The picture of the economy held at the centre was thus built, at its foundation, from testimony given by witnesses with a professional interest in the verdict. The second property is the direction of the argument at each stage. At no stage did any participant ask what pattern of production would be best for the economy as a whole, because no participant was positioned or rewarded to ask it. Gosplan asked whether the balances could be made to close. Ministries asked whether their branch had received its due. Enterprises asked whether the target could be met with the inputs obtainable and a margin to spare. The plan that emerged was the residue of those three questions, and it is a category error to judge it as an answer to a fourth question nobody had posed.

### What happened when an enterprise received its targets?

The director compared the output target with the supply allocations and his real capacity, judged whether the combination was survivable, and opened negotiations for adjustments while production began. He hoarded materials against late deliveries, arranged informal supply contacts, and shaped the assortment toward whatever indicator his success would be measured by.

The tempo of the cycle imposed its own signature on production, and the signature was visible in any month of any year. Because fulfilment was assessed over the plan period and the assessment concentrated at its close, and because supplies arrived unevenly while penalties attached to the final accounting, enterprises worked in a rhythm the literature calls storming: a slack opening to the month or quarter, while inputs were gathered and machinery prepared, followed by a frantic closing stretch in which overtime, deferred maintenance, and every available hand were thrown at the target. Storming wasted resources in ways no balance recorded. Machinery ran without the pauses its upkeep required, quality sagged in the rush, and the output of the closing days arrived in bursts that overwhelmed transport and storage. The rhythm was not a failure of discipline. It was the rational response of managers whose inputs arrived on one schedule and whose judgement day fell on another, and it persisted for decades because the conditions producing it were structural. Any account of the system that omits the tempo has missed something its participants experienced as the texture of ordinary life: the plan was not only a set of quantities but a calendar, and the calendar had a pulse.

## Planning from the achieved level: the namable claim

At the centre of the method stood a rule of thumb so pervasive that the literature gave it a name, and the name is this article's namable claim: planning from the achieved level. When a target had to be set for a product, a branch, or an enterprise, the starting point was the level of output already achieved in the preceding period, and the planning consisted of determining the increment to be added to it. The increment reflected the leadership's ambitions, the branch's bargaining strength, the investment the enterprise had received, and the planner's judgement of what could be extracted, but it did not reflect, because it could not, a computation of what output of that product the economy most needed. The achieved level was the anchor because it was the only magnitude in the system that was both known and verifiable. Needs were contestable, capacities were concealed, and projections were negotiable, but last year's tonnage had been weighed and recorded, and a target built upon it could be defended in any committee room in the country.

The consequences of the rule ramify through everything else in this article, so they are worth tracing slowly. Because the target grew from the achieved level, the structure of production reproduced itself. Branches that had been large received large increments, new products without an achieved level behind them entered the plan awkwardly and late, and the pattern of output in any year resembled the pattern of the year before, adjusted at the margins by political priority. Because the target grew from achievement, achievement itself became dangerous to the achiever, a paradox examined in the section on the ratchet effect below. Because increments were argued rather than derived, the skills the system selected for, at every level, were the skills of argument: the well prepared claim, the documented bottleneck, the timely appeal, the cultivation of the official who would settle the figure. And because the whole method was incremental, the question that the theory of planning had placed at the centre, how a society should choose among alternative allocations of its resources, was never operationally posed. The system had an answer to a different question, how to make last year's economy somewhat larger and somewhat more weighted toward the leadership's priorities, and it mistook that answer, when it thought about the matter at all, for an answer to the first.

### Why is planning from the achieved level the right name for the method?

Because it identifies the actual operation the planners performed. Targets began from recorded past output and grew by a negotiated percentage, so the economy's existing structure supplied the plan's content and bargaining supplied its changes. Naming the operation correctly dissolves the picture of an optimising centre that neither defenders nor critics should have been discussing.

It is worth pausing on how completely this single rule reorganises the familiar controversy. The socialist defence of planning, in its classic form, answered the calculation objection by describing how an allocation could in principle be computed or simulated, whether through trial and error adjustment of accounting prices, as Oskar Lange proposed, or through the solving of vast equation systems. The objection's proponents answered that such computation was impossible in practice or empty in principle. Both sides conducted the argument as if the Soviet planning agency were attempting, however clumsily, the computation under dispute. The record shows an agency doing something else entirely, something that required no solution to the calculation problem because it never posed the problem. An increment on an achieved level can be set without knowing the value of anything. It requires only the achieved level, a growth ambition, and a negotiation. The Soviet system's true relationship to the calculation debate is therefore oblique, and the article returns to the point when the objection is stated in full below: the objection predicted that a planning agency, lacking prices, would have no rational criterion for allocation, and the agency's actual method, rolling forward the inherited pattern, is close to a confirmation of that prediction wearing the clothes of a refutation. What the method plainly was not is what its defenders described when they pointed to the Soviet Union as proof that comprehensive rational planning had been achieved in practice.

## Aggregation and the world below the plan

Return now to the distance between the aggregate that balanced and the specification that did not, because it governed daily economic life more than any other feature of the system. The balance for a commodity group certified that the economy would produce enough of the group and that the group's uses were covered. It said nothing about whether the foundry that needed a particular casting alloy would receive it, whether the shoe factory's allocation of leather included the grades its lasts required, or whether the chemical works would get its catalyst in the month its reactors were scheduled to run. Those questions were settled in the space below the plan: by ministries subdividing their branch totals, by supply organisations matching orders to producers, by contracts between enterprises that specified assortment within the framework of the allocations, and, when all of that failed, by the informal mechanisms examined later in this article. The volume of economic life conducted in that space was enormous. For most enterprises, most of the decisions that determined whether production succeeded were taken there, in negotiation with suppliers, customers, ministry officials, and supply agents, under the constraint of the plan but not under its direction in any detailed sense.

This has a consequence for how the system's performance should be described, and the article flags it here because it recurs. When observers praised Soviet planning for coordinating a complex economy, the coordination they praised was only partly the centre's work. A substantial share of the matching of supply to requirement was performed by the horizontal dealings of enterprises themselves, operating through official contracts, administrative appeals, and unofficial arrangements in proportions that varied by branch and period. The plan set the quantities in aggregate and distributed the entitlements; the economy's participants then spent a large part of their working lives converting entitlements into deliveries. The energy consumed in that conversion was a real cost of the method, visible in the size of supply departments, in the stocks enterprises carried, and in the careers of the expediters, and it is a cost that neither the plan's documents nor the national accounts recorded. A system should be charged with the labour it requires to operate, including the labour of circumventing it, and by that accounting the Soviet method was expensive in a currency its own statistics did not denominate.

The aggregation gap also explains a feature of the system that puzzled foreign observers: the coexistence of taut planning with pervasive slack. Targets were described as taut, set deliberately beyond comfortable reach to extract effort, and at the same time enterprises sat on hidden reserves of capacity, materials, and labour that the centre suspected and could not locate. Both were true, and the gap between aggregate and specification is where they met. A target could be taut in aggregate while the enterprise held slack in the particulars, because the centre could verify the aggregate and not the particulars. Slack was the enterprise's insurance against the specification failures the system guaranteed: the late delivery, the wrong grade, the broken machine for which no spare had been allocated. An enterprise without reserves was an enterprise at the mercy of every gap in the plan, and directors learned, usually from one early catastrophe, that survival required buffers the plan did not authorise. The centre, knowing buffers existed, set targets tauter to smoke them out, which raised the value of concealment, which deepened the buffers. The spiral is the system's information problem wearing its everyday clothes, and no exhortation ever broke it, because each participant's move was the correct reply to the move the others were making.

## The incentive structure: the rational manager

Every account of Soviet planning that treats its participants as executors of instructions fails at the same point: the participants were not executors but bargainers, and their behaviour follows from the bargains available to them. The classic study remains Joseph Berliner's Factory and Manager in the USSR, published in 1957 and built on extended interviews with former Soviet managers who had left the country during and after the war. Berliner's managers describe a world of consistent, intelligible self interest operating inside the plan's framework, and his findings were confirmed so repeatedly by later scholarship, by archival research after the archives opened, and by the testimony of participants across the system's life, that his book stands as the standard account of managerial behaviour under planning. The director's situation, as Berliner reconstructs it, was defined by an asymmetry. His rewards, his bonuses, his standing with the ministry, and in the system's harsher decades his personal safety, depended on fulfilling the plan indicators assigned to his enterprise. His information about his enterprise's true capacity was better than anyone else's. And the plan he would be judged against was negotiable in advance, through the draft he submitted and the arguments he marshalled behind it. A man in that situation does not need to be cynical to behave as Berliner's managers behaved. He needs only to understand his position.

The behaviours followed in a chain. The director sought a plan with a comfortable margin: an output target set below what his plant could produce in a decent year, and input allocations set above what production strictly required. To obtain it, he presented his capacity conservatively, emphasising worn machinery, difficult supply conditions, and the peculiar obstacles of his branch, while presenting his requirements generously, since an allocation unused could be traded or held against emergency, while an allocation short could stop his line. He concealed reserves: stocks of materials beyond the norms, machinery kept off the books of available capacity, workers whose labour could be intensified in the storming weeks. He cultivated his ministry, because the ministry was both his judge and his advocate, and a ministry that believed in its director fought for his figures in the centre. And he attended, above all, to the indicators by which fulfilment would be measured, because the plan was not one obligation but a bundle of them, and the bundle's composition determined what his factory would actually make. None of these behaviours was a secret. The centre knew, discounted the claims it received by the margins it estimated, and built the discount into its own demands, so that the figures in the final plan embodied two layers of strategic distortion: the enterprise's understatement and the centre's correction for understatement, each side calibrating against its model of the other.

### Why did a rational manager ask for a plan below his capacity?

Because his rewards hung on fulfilment, not on the ambition of the target. A modest target met in full paid bonuses, protected his standing, and left reserves for emergencies, while an ambitious target missed by a fraction paid nothing and invited investigation. Understating capacity in the draft was the cheapest insurance available to him.

The asymmetry had a corollary that outsiders often miss. The director's interest in an easy plan was not an interest in idleness. Berliner's managers were, by their own accounts, intensely hard working, and the enterprises they ran were hives of exertion, negotiation, repair, and improvisation. The ease they sought was ease relative to the judgement, a margin between the obligation and the achievable, and they spent enormous energy manufacturing and defending that margin. The system's characteristic waste was therefore not sloth. It was the diversion of managerial talent, in one of the most manager hungry economies in the world, from the improvement of production to the management of the plan relationship. The ablest director was not the one who organised his factory best but the one who kept his factory merely adequate while organising his ministry, his suppliers, and his indicators superbly. A selection pressure of that kind, applied over decades across an entire industrial economy, shapes the economy's capabilities in ways that aggregate statistics register only dimly, as a persistent gap between the resources the system commanded and the results it obtained from them.

## The ratchet effect: success punished by arithmetic

The incremental method contained a mechanism that turned its own logic against effort, and the mechanism is known in the literature as the ratchet effect. Because the coming year's target was built upon the output achieved in the year ending, an enterprise that exerted itself and substantially overfulfilled its plan did not bank the achievement. It raised the base from which its next target would be calculated, and it raised it permanently, since the achieved level, once recorded, became the floor of every subsequent negotiation. The ministry, observing the overfulfilment, drew the reasonable inference that capacity had been understated, and set the following target to capture the revealed capacity plus the usual increment. The director who had strained every nerve to exceed his plan thus purchased, with his success, a harder plan, a thinner margin, and a greater risk of failure in the year to come. The director who fulfilled his plan exactly, or by a hair, kept his base low, his margin intact, and his bonuses flowing. The system thereby taxed precisely the behaviour it existed to elicit, and taxed it at a confiscatory rate, in the only currency that mattered to the taxed.

### What does the ratchet effect do to a successful enterprise?

It converts success into a harder future. Since targets grew from achieved output, a large overfulfilment revealed concealed capacity and raised every later target built on the new base. Managers learned to exceed the plan modestly, to hold back a reserve of achievement, and to treat a spectacular year as a liability rather than a triumph.

The ratchet's effects were pervasive and subtle. Enterprises rationed their own improvement. A technical innovation that raised productivity was adopted cautiously, or its gains were absorbed invisibly, because a visible jump in output reset the base. Production in a good year was sometimes deliberately restrained in its closing weeks, output held for the next period's account, materials conserved, orders paced, so that the year's figure would show a respectable fulfilment and nothing more. Managers spoke the logic openly in Berliner's interviews and in the memoir literature: the plan had to be fulfilled, but not by too much. The phenomenon deserves its place in any general theory of target based administration, because it does not depend on Soviet conditions in particular. Wherever a principal sets a subordinate's future obligations from the subordinate's recorded performance, and the subordinate knows it, performance will be managed rather than maximised. The Soviet economy was the largest field in which this logic ever operated, and its scale made the logic's costs macroeconomic: an entire industrial system's growth was throttled by the prudence of its plant managers, each acting correctly on the information the system gave him about how achievement would be used.

There is a matching effect on the centre's side of the relationship, and the two together produced the system's characteristic epistemology, its way of knowing. The centre could never learn the economy's true capacities, because every report it received was filtered through the ratchet. What it learned instead was the economy's revealed bargaining positions: the levels enterprises could be pushed to when pressed, which is a different magnitude from capacity, and usually a lower one. Planning from the achieved level thus degraded its own foundation over time. The achieved level was supposed to be the one hard datum in the system, and the ratchet ensured that even it was a negotiated artefact, an output chosen by managers with an eye to its consequences as a base. The planner who believed he was incrementing from fact was incrementing from strategy, and the whole majestic apparatus of balances rested, at the last remove, on numbers that the people supplying them had selected for their effects.

## Success indicators: the measure becomes the product

An enterprise's plan was expressed through indicators, and the choice of indicator was a choice about what the economy would actually produce. The principal measure for much of the system's life was gross output, the value of everything the enterprise produced, counted at fixed prices, known in Russian by the abbreviation val. Gross output had an administrative convenience: it aggregated heterogeneous production into a single number that ministries could sum and compare. It also had a property that no amount of administrative vigilance could remove. Any single measure, used as the basis of reward, teaches the rewarded to produce the measure rather than the thing the measure was meant to represent. The literature on the Soviet economy is a catalogue of this teaching, and its examples have the quality of parables, except that they are documented.

Where output was measured by weight, products grew heavier. The standard illustration in Berliner's study and the literature following it concerns nails: assessed by weight, a nail works had every reason to concentrate its steel into fewer, larger nails, and the literature's emblematic case is the plant whose fulfilment took the form of a single enormous nail, useless for every purpose except the indicator that called it into being. Where output was measured by value at fixed prices, enterprises drifted toward expensive inputs and costly assortments, since dearer materials raised the value of output without raising its usefulness, and a furniture maker rewarded on value chose the costly veneer over the sound cheap one. Where output was measured in units, products shrank and thinned: more pieces, each of less substance, because the count rewarded number and not durability. Where the indicator was assortment, the fulfilment of the listed lines in the listed proportions, enterprises met the letter by producing the easiest variants within each line. Quality, which no indicator captured well, was the universal casualty: goods were made to the minimum of the standard that inspection would pass, and inspection itself became another relationship to be managed. The assortment problem compounded all of these, because consumers of producer goods needed particular specifications, and an enterprise fulfilling its indicator had no reason to privilege a difficult specification over an easy one of equal weight or value. The result was an economy simultaneously fulfilling its plans and failing its users, a combination that baffled observers who assumed the two must coincide.

### How did success indicators bend the assortment of goods?

Each indicator rewarded one dimension of production and taxed the rest. Weight rewarded mass, value rewarded expensive inputs, unit counts rewarded smallness, and assortment lists rewarded the easiest variant within every line. Enterprises responded exactly, so the goods that emerged were the indicator's portrait: heavy, costly, flimsy, or standard, regardless of what users had asked for.

The centre was not naive about these distortions, and its responses form a subhistory of the system's administration. Indicators were multiplied, so that an enterprise answered for output, assortment, cost, labour productivity, and delivery discipline together, in the hope that the distortions of each would be checked by the others. The multiplication created its own pathology, since a bundle of indicators always contains some that conflict, and the enterprise then chose which to sacrifice, usually the one whose guardian was weakest that year. Inspectors and standard setting bodies policed quality; campaigns against particular abuses flared and subsided; the press exposed emblematic outrages, the giant nail and its cousins, to general amusement and no structural effect. The deeper difficulty was that the distortion was not a malfunction of the indicator system but its normal operation. A measure is a simplification, adopted because the centre cannot attend to everything, and the simplification's blind spots are where production migrates. The Soviet experience generalises into a principle that the article states plainly because it reaches far beyond its case: any administration that rewards a proxy will receive the proxy, in abundance, and will receive the underlying good only to the extent that the two happen to coincide. The planners knew the principle in practice. They lacked, within their method, any instrument that could repeal it.

## The unofficial supply agents: the men who made the plan work

Every Soviet enterprise of any size employed, formally or informally, a figure for whom the plan documents made no provision and without whom the plan could not have been fulfilled: the unofficial supply agent, known in Russian as the tolkach, a word derived from the verb to push. The tolkach was an expediter. His workplace was the railway station, the ministry corridor, the warehouse of a supplier in another republic, and the telephone. His capital was a network of personal relationships, built over years, with counterparts in supplying enterprises, with officials in the supply organisations, with transport clerks and warehouse keepers, and his stock in trade was the favour: the delivery advanced, the allocation interpreted generously, the wagon found, the document signed at once rather than next month. He obtained for his enterprise the inputs the plan had promised and not delivered, or delivered in the wrong specification, or delivered to the wrong place, and he obtained them by negotiation, by reciprocal obligation, by barter between enterprises of materials each held in surplus, and by the whole repertoire of semi legal improvisation that the system's gaps made necessary. Berliner's managers describe the tolkach as indispensable, and later research, including the work of scholars who interviewed participants after the system's end, confirms the description across branches and decades. An enterprise's real supply department had two halves: the official one, which processed the allocations, and the tolkach, who made the allocations good.

The legal status of this activity was deliberately ambiguous, and the ambiguity was functional. Barter between enterprises, the diversion of materials from their allocated purposes, and the cultivation of officials all stood in varying relations to the law, from tolerated practice to criminal offence, and enforcement oscillated with campaigns and with the political weather. The tolkach operated in the penumbra, and his enterprise protected him because his results protected the enterprise. What matters for the description is not the legality but the structural position. The formal system allocated aggregated quantities through documents. The actual economy required specific goods at specific places at specific times, and the distance between the document and the requirement had to be travelled by somebody. The tolkach travelled it. He was, in effect, the system's unacknowledged market mechanism: a decentralised network for matching surpluses to shortages, exchanging information about who held what and who needed what, and clearing transactions through reciprocity where prices were forbidden to clear them. The resemblance should not be pressed too far, since the tolkach's exchanges carried no prices and generated no signals legible to the centre, but the function he performed, the matching function, is the function whose absence the calculation objection had identified as planning's fatal gap. The Soviet economy filled the gap with a man carrying a briefcase of favours, and it filled it unofficially because its official doctrine had no room for the function to exist.

### What work did the supply agents perform that the plan could not?

They converted paper entitlements into physical deliveries. The plan allocated a quantity of a commodity group; the agent located the actual grade, dimension, and timing his factory required, expedited its release and transport, and reciprocated with favours or surplus materials. He performed the matching of particular supply to particular need that aggregation left undone.

The scale of the informal economy extended well beyond the tolkachi. Enterprises maintained networks of mutual assistance, the system of blat in its industrial form, through which materials, spare parts, and services circulated against future obligations. Workshops repaired one another's equipment off the books. Directors lent workers, vehicles, and machinery across enterprise boundaries in transactions no plan recorded. At the consumer level, a second economy of private trade, moonlighting craftsmen, and favoured access supplied what the shops did not. Scholars differ on how to characterise this whole penumbra, whether as corruption parasitic on the plan or as the economy's true circulatory system, and the description here follows the structural reading: whatever its moral character in particular cases, the informal economy was load bearing. Remove it in imagination, enforce every allocation exactly as written and prohibit every unofficial exchange, and the formal system seizes within weeks, because the aggregated plan cannot specify the economy it governs and something must perform the specification. The system's participants understood this, which is why campaigns against the informal economy were always campaigns against its excesses rather than its existence, and why the tolkach survived every administration that denounced him.

### Why was the informal network necessary rather than merely corrupt?

Because the gap it filled was created by the method itself. Aggregation guaranteed that entitlements and requirements would mismatch in specification, place, and time, and no official channel existed to reconcile them at the level of the individual factory. The network reconciled them. Its necessity is proved by its universality: it appeared in every branch and every decade.

There is a lesson in the tolkach for readers of the planning debate, and the article states it before moving on. Both the defence and the critique of planning tended to treat the plan as the economy: the defence credited the plan with the economy's achievements, the critique charged the plan with its failures. The tolkach stands as evidence that the economy was never only the plan. It was a dual system, formal and informal, in which the formal part set the framework of entitlements and priorities and the informal part performed a large share of the actual coordination. Judgements about what planning achieved or failed to achieve that ignore the second part are judgements about a system that did not exist. The Soviet economy's real constitution was written in two hands, one official and one not, and the historian who reads only the official hand will misunderstand both the economy's resilience, which owed much to the unofficial hand's flexibility, and its inefficiency, which owed much to the fact that the unofficial hand's signals were private, local, and invisible to anyone trying to improve the whole.

## The shortage economy: Kornai's analysis

The most powerful explanation of how the system behaved as a whole came from inside it. János Kornai, a Hungarian economist who had worked within a planned economy's institutions, published Economics of Shortage in 1980, and the book reorganised the study of the system around a single claim: shortage in such an economy is not an accident, a planning error, or a transitional difficulty, but the system's normal, reproduced condition, its equilibrium. The claim inverted the usual analysis. Economists had treated each shortage as a disequilibrium to be explained, a queue here, an empty shelf there, a missing spare part elsewhere, each with its local cause. Kornai proposed that the local causes were surface phenomena of a single deep mechanism, and that the mechanism would reproduce shortage under competent and incompetent planners alike, because it was built into the relationship between the enterprise and the state that owned it.

The mechanism runs through the soft budget constraint, Kornai's name for the enterprise's financial situation. In a market economy, an enterprise that persistently spends more than it earns faces bankruptcy, and the expectation of that fate disciplines its demands: it buys inputs carefully, because waste threatens its existence. The Soviet type enterprise faced no such fate. It was owned by the state, its losses were covered by subsidy, credit, tax adjustment, or price revision, its closure was a political act almost never performed, and its managers knew all of this from experience. The budget constraint was soft: the enterprise's spending was not effectively limited by its earnings. From that single softness, Kornai derived the system's characteristic behaviours. An enterprise whose survival does not depend on economising has no reason to restrain its demand for inputs. It orders more than it needs, because the cost of ordering is trivial and the cost of running short is a missed plan. It hoards materials and labour against the uncertainties of supply. It demands investment continuously, because investment enlarges the enterprise, raises its standing, and costs its managers nothing they will personally repay. Demand, aggregated across an economy of such enterprises, presses permanently against supply, and the economy settles into chronic shortage: sellers' markets in producer goods, queues and empty shelves in consumer goods, forced substitution everywhere, as buyers accept the available grade, size, or model because the specified one cannot be had.

### What does it mean to call shortage an equilibrium?

It means shortage is the condition the system returns to after every disturbance, because the mechanism generating it operates continuously. Planners could relieve a particular shortage by priority allocation, but the relief displaced shortage elsewhere, since the demands causing it were undiminished. Kornai's point is that shortage was reproduced by the system's structure, not caused by its mistakes.

The elegance of the analysis is that it explains the system's texture, the details that aggregate accounts leave as local colour. Hoarding is rational for the enterprise and catastrophic in aggregate, since every enterprise's insurance stock is another enterprise's missing input, and the shortage thereby manufactures more of itself. Forced substitution degrades the assortment further, as producers use the materials they can get rather than the materials the design specifies, and quality falls in ways the indicators again fail to record. Investment hunger produces the characteristic landscape of the planned economy: a vast stock of unfinished construction, projects begun because starting was rewarded and finished slowly because the resources to complete them were spread across too many sites, each ministry defending its own unfinished portfolio against completion discipline. The consumer economy mirrors the producer economy: households, facing chronic unavailability, buy what appears when it appears, hold stocks of goods they may need, and spend their time in the search and queueing that the system's statistics counted as leisure. Kornai's framework also explains why shortage intensified rather than relaxed when plans grew more ambitious: tauter targets raised every enterprise's incentive to over order and hoard, tightening the very scarcity the tautness was meant to overcome. The system, on this account, did not merely tolerate shortage. It manufactured shortage as reliably as it manufactured steel, and by a parallel process: in both cases, as the byproduct of its participants pursuing exactly what the system rewarded.

### How does a soft budget constraint change what an enterprise wants?

It removes the penalty for wanting too much. An enterprise that cannot be allowed to fail treats inputs as free insurance and investment as pure gain, so its requests express its ambitions and its fears rather than its requirements. Multiplied across the economy, unrestrained wanting becomes the shortage that every participant then experiences as an external fact.

Kornai's analysis carries a methodological consequence that the article notes because it disciplines the rest of the argument. If shortage is systemic, then the familiar procedure of explaining the system's failures by the errors of its planners, this target misjudged, that balance miscalculated, is a category mistake, like explaining the wetness of the ocean by the errors of its waves. The planners' errors were real, but they were not the cause of the pattern; the pattern would have emerged from correct arithmetic too, because the pattern's source was the incentive structure within which arithmetic was performed. This is why the article's namable claim matters beyond its immediate subject. Planning from the achieved level describes the method; the soft budget constraint describes the environment in which the method operated; and the two together explain why better planners, better balances, or better computers, the remedies proposed across the system's life, left the pattern intact. The remedies addressed the arithmetic. The pattern lived in the incentives.

## Investment hunger and the unfinished landscape

The soft budget constraint expressed itself most visibly in investment. For the enterprise and its ministry, a new plant or an expanded line meant capacity, employment, standing, and a claim on the centre's attention, while the cost of the investment was borne by the state budget and repaid, if the question arose at all, by the economy in general. The rational branch therefore demanded investment without internal limit, and the centre, which could not compare the returns of competing projects on any common measure, rationed the flood of applications by bargaining, by priority ranking, and by the achieved level logic applied to construction: branches received increments on their existing investment programmes. The predictable result was a construction sector permanently overloaded, completion times stretching year by year, and a landscape dotted with projects in their second decade of building, their equipment ageing before it was installed. Capital that a harder budgeted economy would have concentrated on a few completions was dispersed across a multitude of starts, because starting a project created a fact the centre would later feel obliged to finish, and every ministry understood that the way to secure the following year's allocation was to have a project already sunk. The phenomenon is known in the literature as the investment cycle, as ministries' demands surged and were cut back in rhythm with the plan periods, and it illustrates the article's recurring point in its purest form. Nobody in the chain behaved foolishly. The waste was the sum of correct decisions, and a system whose waste is the sum of correct decisions cannot be repaired by exhorting its participants to decide better.

The consumer economy, which occupied a residual place in the planners' priorities, displayed the same logic from the other side. Consumer goods branches received the allocations left after the priority sectors were served, their enterprises faced the same indicators and the same supply uncertainties, and their output reached a distribution network that had no mechanism for registering what households actually wanted beyond the coarse signal of what sold out fastest. The household adapted as the enterprise adapted: by hoarding against unavailability, by cultivating access through personal connections, by accepting substitutes, and by treating the appearance of a desired good as an event to be seized rather than a stock to be drawn on. The queue, the era's emblem, was in Kornai's terms the visible price of a system that refused to let money prices clear its markets: where price does not ration, time rations, and the hours spent standing in line were the economy's unrecorded tax on its consumers, paid disproportionately by those whose time the system valued least in its accounts and most in their lives. The description should be exact about the distribution of this burden. Shortage did not fall evenly. Access through position, connection, and closed distribution networks softened it for the well placed, while the unconnected paid in full, so that the shortage economy was also a quiet system of privilege, all the more durable for never appearing in any table of incomes.

## The calculation objection, stated at full strength

The article now states the strongest objection to the whole enterprise of planning, at the strength its authors gave it, because the description assembled so far earns its significance only against that objection. Ludwig von Mises opened the argument in 1920, in his essay on economic calculation in a socialist commonwealth, and Friedrich Hayek deepened it across the following two decades. The objection is not that planning is administratively difficult, nor that planners are corrupt or incompetent. It is that rational allocation is impossible without market prices for the means of production. Where land, machinery, and materials are not bought and sold, they have no prices; where they have no prices, there is no common unit in which the cost of using a resource in one way can be compared with the value of using it in another; and where no such comparison is possible, a planning agency choosing among uses is choosing blind. It can count quantities, as the material balances counted them. It cannot weigh alternatives, because weighing requires the commensuration that only exchange ratios supply. The objection concludes that a planned economy will therefore allocate by rule of thumb, by precedent, by political weight, by anything except a criterion of efficiency, and that its apparent rationality is an inheritance: it begins from a structure of production built under prices, or copied from economies that have them, and consumes that inheritance without being able to renew it.

### What exactly did Mises claim a planner could not do?

He claimed a planner could not compare. Without prices for producer goods, the agency could know that a bridge needed so many tonnes of steel and so many hours of labour, yet could not know whether the bridge was worth its steel, because worth requires a common measure between the steel's alternative uses, which only exchange supplies.

Hayek's development of the argument shifted its centre from arithmetic to knowledge. The information relevant to allocation, the state of a particular machine, the skill of a particular workforce, the temporary surplus in a particular warehouse, is dispersed among millions of participants, much of it tacit, local, and perishable, and it cannot be transmitted to a centre in usable form because the people holding it often cannot articulate it and have no reason to report it truthfully when they can. Prices, on Hayek's account, are the economy's solution to this problem: a compression of dispersed knowledge into signals that each participant can act on without understanding the whole. A planning agency, deprived of the signals, must substitute reports, and reports are exactly what the Soviet system's participants supplied, shaped by every interest this article has described. Read against the description, Hayek's version has a striking property: the Soviet record reads like its illustration. The centre's picture of the economy was built from strategic testimony; the knowledge that made production actually work, the tolkach's knowledge of who held what, was precisely the local, tacit, untransmittable kind, and it circulated in a network the centre could neither see nor use. The objection and the description are not two accounts competing for the same territory. The description is what the objection looks like when it is true.

## What the system achieved: the defence stated fairly

Neutrality in this article is not a posture but a discipline, and it requires the achievements to be stated with the same specificity as the failures, attributed to those who made the case. The defence of Soviet planning, as its serious proponents stated it, Maurice Dobb prominent among them in his studies of Soviet economic development, rested on mobilisation. A backward economy, on this account, faces a coordination problem that markets solve slowly or not at all: investment must be concentrated in the heavy industries whose products are the prerequisites of all other growth, the surplus to fund that investment must be extracted from a poor countryside, and the whole transformation must proceed faster than private calculation, which discounts the future heavily and shuns the longest projects, would ever carry it. Planning, the defence held, is the instrument for exactly this task. It can set the investment share of national income at levels no electorate or capital market would sustain, direct the increment to the bottleneck sectors, and hold the course for decades. The record the defence cited was the industrialisation itself: the creation, within a generation, of a complete heavy industrial base in a country that had entered the century predominantly agrarian, the defeat of an invasion sustained by that base, and the reconstruction that followed the war's devastation. Whatever is said of the costs, and the costs were immense and are part of the record this series treats elsewhere, the mobilisation happened, and any theory of the system must explain it as well as the queues.

The comparison basis discipline of this article requires the terms of that claim to be fixed before it is weighed. The industrialisation claim compares the Soviet economy of the late nineteen twenties with the Soviet economy of the nineteen fifties: the same territory, transformed in its structure, measured by the physical output of the basic industries and by the share of the workforce outside agriculture. Comparisons with Western economies in the same period must be handled with a warning the literature insists on, associated with the work of Abram Bergson and the scholars who followed him: Soviet statistics were compiled on a different national accounting concept, the net material product, which excluded most services and treated depreciation differently from Western gross national product, so that growth comparisons require adjusted recalculations and remain, even after adjustment, comparisons between economies at very different levels of development, where high growth from a low base is a different achievement from growth at the frontier. Stated with those qualifications, the defence's core survives: the system was formidably effective at the tasks its method was built for. Material balances and priority allocation are mobilisation instruments. They identify the decisive goods, trace their requirements, and concentrate entitlements upon them, and for as long as the leadership's objectives were few, physical, and overriding, the method's inability to weigh alternatives mattered less than its ability to move masses. The system's tragedy, on the description given here, is that the economy outgrew the method. As the structure diversified, as the objectives multiplied from a handful of tonnages to the whole range of a modern consumer and technological economy, the incremental bargaining that had sufficed for mobilisation became the binding constraint, and the method that had built the industrial base could not operate the economy the base had made possible.

### What did the system achieve that its critics must explain?

It industrialised a predominantly agrarian economy within a generation, sustained a war effort against invasion from its own industrial base, and reconstructed after devastation, all through the machinery described here. A critique that cannot accommodate mobilisation on that scale has explained the queues and missed the phenomenon, exactly as a defence that cannot accommodate the queues has done the reverse.

The answer to the calculation objection, on the evidence of the description, is therefore double, and the article gives both halves without flinching. What the system achieved in mobilising resources toward prioritised objectives was real, and it demonstrates that an economy can be moved, massively and deliberately, without market prices for its producer goods; the objection, read as a prediction that nothing coherent can happen without them, is refuted by the steel and the tanks. But the objection's deeper prediction, that the agency would possess no rational criterion for choosing among allocations and would fall back on precedent and increment, is borne out by the method actually used, in the planners' own practice, documented in their own procedures. Planning from the achieved level is the improvisation Mises predicted, institutionalised and given a timetable. The Soviet system did not solve the calculation problem and did not refute the objection. It declined the problem, built an economy by declining it, and paid the price of the declension in the currency of assortment, quality, and shortage, over decades, at continental scale.

## The reforms: three attempts to change the method

The system's participants were not blind to the description this article has given, and the history of its later decades is in part a history of attempts to reform the method from inside. Three attempts matter most, because each attacked a different element of the mechanism, and the fate of each shows where the mechanism's defences lay. The first attacked the indicators. The second attacked the arithmetic, proposing to replace bargaining with computation. The third, in Hungary, attacked the framework itself, and went furthest precisely because it was willing to let markets back into the coordination the plan had monopolised.

The reform associated with Premier Alexei Kosygin, launched in the mid nineteen sixties, began from the success indicator problem. Its diagnosis, argued in the Soviet press by the economist Evsei Liberman and others, was that gross output had taught enterprises to produce the measure, and that the remedy was to judge enterprises by sales and profit instead: by whether their output was actually taken by its users and by whether its value exceeded its cost. The reform reduced the number of compulsory indicators, gave enterprises somewhat greater discretion over their funds, and introduced charges on capital to discourage the hoarding of equipment. Its logic was sound within its terms, and its terms were its limit. Prices remained fixed administratively, so profit measured the enterprise's success at navigating the price structure as much as its efficiency; ministries retained the power to recentralise what the reform decentralised, and exercised it as the indicators crept back, plan by plan, because the ministry's own position depended on controlling its enterprises; and the ratchet survived intact, since a profitable enterprise merely presented the centre with a new achieved level from which to plan. Within a few years the reform had been absorbed by the system it was meant to change, leaving behind a vocabulary of profitability that enterprises learned to satisfy as they had satisfied val before it. The episode is the indicator problem's revenge: a reform expressed through indicators will be gamed through indicators, and the gaming is not a betrayal of the reform but its predictable administrative fate.

### What did the Kosygin reform change, and what did it leave untouched?

It changed the scoreboard, substituting sales and profit for gross output among the judged indicators and trimming the list of compulsory targets. It left untouched the fixed prices that made profit an unreliable signal, the ministries that recentralised discretion, the soft budget constraint, and the ratchet that converted every success into a harder future plan.

The second attempt attacked the arithmetic. The Soviet Union possessed, in Leonid Kantorovich, the economist who had invented linear programming, the mathematical technique for computing optimal allocations under constraints, and Kantorovich and his school argued for decades that planning should use such methods: that the balances should be solved rather than bargained, with shadow prices emerging from the computation to guide allocation. Fragments of the programme were applied, in transport scheduling, in cutting patterns, in particular branches where the problem was bounded and the data honest. The comprehensive version was the proposal, developed from the early nineteen sixties by the cybernetician Viktor Glushkov, for a nationwide computer network, known by its Russian initials as OGAS, that would gather enterprise data continuously and manage the economy's planning and supply in something approaching real time. The proposal was serious, technically literate, and repeatedly advanced at high levels. It was not adopted. The reasons given in the literature are instructive, because none of them is that the computers were beyond Soviet capability. A network that reported enterprise data truthfully and continuously would have abolished the information asymmetry on which ministries and enterprises depended; a computation that allocated by algorithm would have abolished the bargaining through which the whole apparatus exercised its power; and a system of shadow prices would have created a criterion of efficiency outside the leadership's political control. The ministries understood, without needing the point spelled out, that OGAS was not a tool for the existing system but a replacement for it, and they resisted it as such. The episode deserves emphasis in any account that attributes the system's failures to technological backwardness. The technology for a different method existed, was proposed by the system's own scientists, and was refused by the system's institutions, because the method and the institutions were incompatible. The bargaining was not a placeholder awaiting a computer. The bargaining was the constitution.

### What was the proposed computer network supposed to replace?

It was to replace the annual bargaining cycle with continuous data collection and algorithmic allocation: enterprise reports flowing automatically to computing centres, balances solved rather than negotiated, and supply managed through the network. Its defeat showed that the obstacle to computed planning was institutional, since the network threatened every interest the bargaining sustained.

The third attempt took place outside the Soviet Union, in the Hungary whose economist had written the shortage analysis, and its difference from the Soviet reforms is the measure of their limits. The New Economic Mechanism, introduced in 1968, abolished compulsory plan targets for most enterprises, freed many prices to respond to supply and demand within regulated bounds, and left the plan as an instrument of macroeconomic guidance rather than microeconomic command. Enterprises under the Hungarian reform bought and sold, kept a meaningful share of their earnings, and faced something closer to a market test, while the state retained ownership, the commanding heights, and the framework of regulation. The reform's results were correspondingly different: the consumer economy visibly eased, assortment and availability improved by the standards of the bloc, and Hungarian shops became the envy of visitors from stricter systems. Its limits were also instructive. The budget constraint of the large enterprise remained soft in Kornai's analysis, investment hunger persisted, and the party's political control set a boundary the mechanism could not cross, since a genuine market test would eventually have bankrupted enterprises whose survival was politically guaranteed. Hungary demonstrated that the method could be changed, that the bargaining plan was a choice and not a necessity of socialist ownership. It also demonstrated the price of the choice, which was the steady reintroduction of the market logic the system had been built to replace, and the Soviet leadership, watching the Hungarian experiment across the decades, drew the conclusion that the price was the point: to reform the method thoroughly was to cease, in the relevant sense, to plan.

### Why did the Hungarian reform go further than the Soviet one?

Because it changed the framework rather than the indicators or the arithmetic. By abolishing compulsory targets for most enterprises and freeing many prices, it let market coordination perform work the plan had reserved to itself. The Soviet reforms had tried to improve the bargaining; the Hungarian reform reduced the territory over which bargaining ruled.

## Why every reform stopped at the same boundary

Lay the three attempts side by side and a pattern emerges that is worth more than any of them separately. Each reform identified a genuine defect of the method, the indicators, the arithmetic, the absence of market signals, and each was defeated or absorbed at the point where its remedy touched the system's distribution of power. The indicator reform was absorbed by the ministries, whose authority was the power to set and judge indicators. The computing reform was refused by the apparatus as a whole, whose authority was the bargaining the computer would have replaced. The Hungarian reform succeeded in proportion as a political leadership accepted a reduction of its own directive power, and stopped where that acceptance stopped. The lesson generalises beyond the case, and the article states it as one of its conclusions. A planning system of the Soviet type is not a technique that a government uses; it is a structure of authority that a government is. The plan's documents were the medium through which ministries commanded enterprises, the centre commanded ministries, and the political leadership commanded the whole, and a reform of the documents was therefore a reform of the command structure, resisted by everyone whose command it would have diminished. This is why the system's reform history reads as a sequence of intelligent diagnoses followed by administrative digestion. The diagnoses were not refuted. They were outvoted by the constitution they proposed to amend.

### Why did every reform stop at the same boundary?

Because each remedy, followed far enough, abolished a power that some part of the apparatus existed to exercise. Indicators were the ministries' levers, bargaining was the apparatus's medium, and directive planning was the leadership's constitution. Reforms were adopted exactly as far as they left those powers intact and absorbed or refused beyond that line.

There remains the question of whether the method could have been reformed gradually into something workable, the question the Hungarian case poses most sharply, and the article leaves it where the evidence leaves it: open in principle, unanswered in fact. No Soviet leadership attempted the Hungarian road at Soviet scale, and the counterfactual has no data. What the record does show is the direction of the pressures. Every element of the method described here, the achieved level, the ratchet, the indicators, the soft budget, the tolkach, formed a connected structure in which each part compensated for the others' defects, and connected structures resist piecemeal repair because the repair of one part exposes the unpatched strain on the rest. Free the indicators and the fixed prices corrupt the signal; free the prices and the soft budget makes demand limitless; harden the budget and enterprises fail whose failure the polity cannot accept; compute the plan and the data arrive poisoned by the ratchet. The reformers of three decades were not lacking in ingenuity. They were working on a knot, and the knot's property is that pulling any single strand tightens the others.

## The supply system: entitlements, funds, and orders

Between the plan's approval and the factory's production stood the supply system, the machinery that converted targets into deliveries, and its operation explains why the tolkach had a career. Producer goods in the Soviet economy were not, in general, bought. They were allocated. The plan and the balances established, for each funded commodity, a fund: the total quantity available for distribution in the period. The supply organisations, headed by Gossnab and descending through republican and regional supply administrations and the supply departments of the ministries, divided the funds among claimant organisations according to the priorities of the plan and the norms of consumption attached to production programmes. A claimant's share was embodied in documents: an allocation, which entitled the holder to receive a stated quantity of the commodity, and then a more specific instrument, the order or outfit authorisation, which named the supplying enterprise and the delivery terms. Only with those papers in hand could the receiving enterprise conclude the supply contract with its designated supplier that specified assortment, quality, and schedule within the allocation's frame. The supplier, for its part, produced against its own plan, in which the delivery figured as an obligation to the state before it figured as a service to the customer named in the contract.

Each step of this machinery generated its characteristic friction. The fund for a commodity was fixed by the balance before the year's claims were fully known, so the division of the fund was a zero sum contest among claimants, prosecuted with the same arts as the plan bargaining itself: overstated programmes, inflated consumption norms, appeals to priority. The allocation specified quantity within a commodity group, so the contest continued into the contract stage over grade and dimension, where the supplier's indicator interests, described earlier, pulled toward the easiest assortment. Delivery schedules set in contracts met the reality of storming at the supplying end, so that inputs arrived bunched at period ends, overwhelming the receiver's storage and starving its opening weeks. And the whole apparatus operated without a residual claimant for its failures: when a delivery failed, the receiving enterprise's remedy was complaint upward, arbitration between organisations of the same state, and the tolkach. The literature's verdict on the supply system is consistent across otherwise divided scholars. It was the point at which the plan's aggregate coherence dissolved most completely into particular incoherence, and the volume of managerial effort it consumed, the supply departments, the expediters, the stocks held against its failures, was the method's largest single overhead, a tax on every act of production that the national accounts attributed to no one.

The system's defenders sometimes answered that allocation by entitlement had a virtue markets lack: it guaranteed the priority user his supply in a scarcity, where a price system would have let a richer but less essential claimant outbid him. The description supports the virtue, within limits. In war and in the great construction drives, the fund system did deliver the decisive goods to the decisive users with a reliability that a poor country's thin markets could not have matched, and the priority ranking was a real instrument of policy, not a fiction. The limit is equally visible in the description. Priority is a ranking, and a ranking can order a queue but cannot lengthen the goods, so every exercise of priority displaced a shortage onto the unprioritised, whose production then failed in ways that fed back, through the web of balances, into the priority sectors themselves. The machine tool works starved of bearings delivers late to the armaments plant; the priority is preserved on paper and eroded in fact. Participants called the unprioritised residue the economy's tail, and the tail's condition was the system's confession: what the method could not prioritise, it could not reliably supply at all.

## Money under the plan: the passive rouble

Money existed in the Soviet economy, in quantity, and its role is one of the description's most instructive elements, because money was present everywhere and decisive almost nowhere in the producer economy. Enterprises held accounts at the state bank, paid one another by transfer, paid wages in cash, and accounted for their output in roubles. But the money of the producer sector was passive, in the literature's term: it recorded and verified transactions whose substance had been decided in physical terms. An enterprise could not use its balance to buy what it had not been allocated, since the supplier required the allocation documents, not merely the funds; nor could it convert its transfer balances into cash, the two circuits of money, the non cash circuit between organisations and the cash circuit of wages and retail, being kept deliberately separate, with the bank policing the frontier between them. The state bank's celebrated control by the rouble was therefore a control of conformity: it checked that payments matched plans, contracts, and wage funds, and refused those that did not. It was an audit instrument of great reach, and it caught discrepancies, unauthorised diversions, and accounting fraud. What it could not do was allocate, because allocation had already happened upstream, in the balances and the funds, in physical terms, before money moved at all.

Prices, in this architecture, were accounting instruments rather than signals. Wholesale prices for producer goods were set administratively, held for long periods, and constructed from cost calculations plus a margin, branch by branch, so that the price of a good embodied the average recorded cost of producing it in its branch, including that branch's inefficiencies, rather than any relationship between supply and demand. A turnover tax, levied mainly on consumer goods, drove a wedge between the price the producer received and the price the household paid, and served as one of the state budget's principal revenue sources. Retail prices were fixed with an eye to stability and to the political sensitivity of the basic goods, bread and rent and transport held low for decades as a matter of social contract. The consequence for the planning method was the one the calculation objection predicted, operating quietly inside the system's own accounts. Since prices did not measure scarcity, no calculation performed in prices could guide allocation, and the planners, to their credit as practical men, did not pretend otherwise: they planned in tonnes and used the roubles to check the paperwork. The system's economists debated price reform for decades, and the school of optimal planning argued that computed shadow prices could give the accounts meaning, but the prices that enterprises actually faced remained, to the end, the prices of the accountants. An economy can run for a long time on such prices. What it cannot do is learn from them, and the Soviet economy's accounts, immaculate in form, told its managers almost nothing about what anything was worth.

## Labour under the plan: the wage fund and the hoarded worker

Labour, like materials, was planned, and the planning of labour shows the system's logic operating on people. The enterprise's plan included a labour section: a limit on its workforce, a wage fund it might not exceed, and targets for labour productivity from which the first two were derived. Workers were not allocated to enterprises as materials were; they were hired, in a labour market that officially did not exist and practically did, since workers could leave and seek other employment, and did so at turnover rates that planners deplored and could not suppress. The enterprise's problem was therefore the mirror of its materials problem. Labour was cheap to hold and expensive to lack: the wage fund was a limit, but a soft one in Kornai's sense, since an enterprise that exceeded it in pursuit of the plan could usually regularise the excess, while an enterprise short of hands in the storming weeks missed its target outright. Directors hoarded labour as they hoarded materials, keeping workers against peak needs, concealing vacancies, resisting the release of redundant hands to other employers, and inflating the labour norms on which staffing requests were based. The result was the system's celebrated paradox of simultaneous labour shortage and labour surplus: enterprises complained chronically of being understaffed while the economy employed, in aggregate, more workers per unit of output than comparable economies, the surplus sitting inside the factory gates as insurance, invisible to the statistics that recorded only the complaints.

The hoarding of labour had a human texture the aggregate description should not flatten. Inside the enterprise, the surplus hands were not idle in any simple sense; they were the repair crews, the fetchers and carriers, the substitutes who made an unreliable supply system survivable, and their presence was one reason Soviet factories could absorb shocks that would have stopped a leaner operation. The cost was paid in the economy's structure rather than in any single factory's accounts: labour that might have staffed the chronically starved service sector, the shops, clinics, repair trades, and restaurants whose thinness every visitor noticed, sat instead within industrial enterprises as a reserve against the plan's emergencies. The planners knew the pattern and fought it with productivity campaigns and staffing norms, and the pattern survived the campaigns, because its cause was not indiscipline but the same asymmetry that governed materials: the penalty for holding too much labour was diffuse and negotiable, the penalty for holding too little was a failed plan and a ruined career. Where the penalties point, the behaviour follows, for workers in the stockroom as for steel in the yard.

## Agriculture: the sector the method fitted worst

No account of the planning method is complete without the sector where its fit was worst and its consequences longest. Agriculture entered the plan through a different door from industry. The collective and state farms were not state enterprises in the industrial sense; the collective farm was formally a cooperative, holding its land in perpetual use, electing its chairman in form and receiving him in fact, and delivering its produce to the state under a system of compulsory procurements at administratively fixed prices that for long periods stood below the cost of production. The plan reached the farm as delivery obligations, as sown area directives for the principal crops, and as the allocations of machinery, fuel, and fertiliser through which the state supplied the inputs it chose to supply. What the method could not reach was the thing agriculture most depends on: the daily, local, weather governed judgement of the people working the land, exercised on plots whose condition no aggregated balance could describe. The farm's incentive structure compounded the difficulty. The collective's members were remunerated from the residual after the state's claims, so the state's procurement stood first in the queue and the cultivator last, and the private plot, the small holding each household worked for itself, demonstrated the consequence in the most cited contrast in the whole literature of the system: a small fraction of the land, worked with the cultivator's own interest engaged, produced a share of the country's food, especially of livestock products, vegetables, and potatoes, out of all proportion to its area. The article states the contrast without figures, in accordance with its protocol, because the proportion is contested in its exact size and uncontested in its direction, and the direction is the argument.

Agriculture also exposes, in its purest form, the limits of planning from the achieved level. A factory's achieved level is a fact about its machinery and organisation, stable enough to increment. A farm's corresponding figure is a fact about last year's weather as much as about its husbandry, and a target incremented from a good harvest punishes the farm for the rain, while a target incremented from a drought rewards it for the drought's passing. Procurement targets built on achieved deliveries therefore oscillated with the climate while pretending to measure effort, and the farm's rational response mirrored the factory's: conceal the good year's surplus, plead the bad year's losses, and treat the state's claim as a tax to be minimised rather than a plan to be fulfilled. The state's countermeasures, inspection, campaign, and the periodic reorganisation of the whole agricultural administration, attacked the symptoms in rotation for sixty years. The underlying condition, a procurement system that made the producer the residual claimant on his own product, was the one element never reformed, because the cheap food it delivered to the cities was a pillar of the social contract on which the regime's urban peace depended. Here as everywhere in this description, the method's irrationality at the level of the unit was the regime's rationality at the level of its survival, and any reform that threatened the second was sacrificed to it, whatever it cost the first.

## The ministry: an advocate dressed as a judge

Between the centre and the enterprise stood the branch ministry, and no description of the method is adequate that treats the ministry as a relay. The ministry was an interested party. Its standing in the state's hierarchy, its claim on investment, its officials' careers, and its institutional survival all depended on the size, growth, and visible success of its branch, so that in every negotiation the ministry appeared in two costumes at once: as the centre's instrument for disciplining its enterprises, and as its enterprises' advocate for extracting resources from the centre. The second costume usually fitted better. A ministry that failed to win increments for its branch lost standing to ministries that succeeded, and its enterprises, knowing where their protection lay, supplied it with the ammunition of overstated needs and understated capacities that the bargaining required. The literature's term for the resulting pathology is departmentalism: the pursuit of branch interest against the economy's general interest, expressed in each ministry's drive toward autarky, its own supply bases, its own construction organisations, its own repair facilities, duplicating across branches what a single organisation might have supplied to all, because dependence on another ministry's deliveries was dependence on a rival's priorities. The Soviet economy's map of production was thus drawn twice over: once by the technology of the products, and once by the boundaries of the ministries, and the second map explains duplications and gaps that the first cannot.

Departmentalism also governed the fate of innovation, and its verdict belongs in this description because it shaped what the economy could become. A new product or process typically required cooperation across ministry boundaries, a new material from one branch, new equipment from another, a new customer in a third, and each boundary crossing was a negotiation between organisations whose indicators did not reward the crossing. Within a ministry, innovation threatened the achieved level arithmetic of every enterprise it touched, since new products reset assortment obligations and new processes disrupted the fulfilment of the current plan. The system's bias was therefore structural and double: against novelty that crossed boundaries, and against novelty that disturbed the base from which targets grew. The bias did not prevent innovation where the state's priority overrode it, in armaments and in the prestige programmes, where resources and attention were concentrated by political command. It taxed innovation everywhere else, in the civilian economy's vast ordinary territory, and the tax compounded over decades into the technological lag that the leadership's own reports lamented with increasing alarm. A method that plans from the achieved level will always prefer the achieved product to the unachieved one; the preference is written into its arithmetic, and no campaign for innovation ever repealed an arithmetic.

## A year in the life of an enterprise: the method at ground level

The description so far has proceeded by components, and it may help to reassemble them into the experience of a single enterprise across a single year, in the composite form the literature supports. Consider a medium sized machine building plant in a provincial city, subordinate to a branch ministry in Moscow, employing some thousands of workers, producing equipment for other industries to its ministry's assortment plan. Its year begins before the calendar's, in the autumn, when the ministry's control figures arrive: output to grow by a stated percentage over the level being achieved, the wage fund and workforce set accordingly, investment confined to the completion of a shop begun two years earlier. The director and his chief engineer spend the autumn in the year's most consequential labour, the composition of the counter plan. They know the plant can do more than the figures demand in some lines and less in others; they know which machines are worn, which suppliers had failed them in the year now ending, which assortment items lose money on every unit and which carry the value indicator. Their draft concedes the aggregate growth while pleading for its redistribution toward the lines they can fulfil, requests investment the ministry has not offered, and states input requirements with the margins that experience has taught them. The draft goes up; the ministry, consolidating forty such drafts, trims the requests, defends the branch total at Gosplan, and returns in the new year with the approved plan: the growth figure intact, the redistribution partly granted, the investment refused, the inputs allocated at levels below the request in two critical materials.

The year's production then unfolds as a negotiation with reality conducted through the plan's framework. The first quarter runs short of a casting alloy; the supply department works the official channels while the plant's tolkach travels to the supplying works, where a counterpart obligation from last year is called in and a surplus stock is released against a promise. A customer's ministry presses for a specification variant that the assortment plan did not include; the plant agrees, against a supplementary allocation and a revision of its assortment obligation, after the ministry brokers the exchange. In the second quarter a machine fails whose spare part exists in no allocation; the plant's own repair shop fabricates an approximation, as it fabricates a substantial share of the small equipment the supply system never delivers. Storming closes each quarter, overtime and deferred maintenance buying the fulfilment figures, and the half year accounting shows the value indicator met, the assortment indicator missed in two lines, and the cost indicator rescued by a fortunate allocation of materials. In the autumn the cycle of the next year begins while the current year's corrections are still being argued, and the director, who has fulfilled his plan and kept his plant intact, closes the year having spent, by any honest reckoning of his diary, more hours on the plan's relationships than on the plant's production. Multiply the composite by the economy's enterprises and the description's abstractions acquire their weight: the method was not something that happened in Moscow. It was the daily occupation of the industrial population of a continent, and its costs were paid in the currency of their working lives.

## Did the planners try to compute an optimum?

The question is natural, because the system's own rhetoric spoke the language of optimal proportions and scientific planning, and the honest answer has two parts. As a description of the operating method, no: the balances were iterated by bargaining until they closed, targets were incremented from achieved levels, and no Soviet plan was ever produced by solving an optimisation problem for the economy. As a description of a persistent aspiration inside the system, yes: a school of mathematical economists, Kantorovich foremost, with allies such as Vasily Nemchinov and Viktor Novozhilov, argued across four decades that planning should become computation, developed the techniques, built input output tables, compiled for the economy from the late nineteen fifties onward, and won every intellectual honour the system could bestow, including, for Kantorovich, the Nobel Prize it shared with a Western economist for the theory of optimal allocation. The school's fate is the sharpest evidence in this article for the distinction between the system's rhetoric and its constitution. Its methods were adopted exactly where they did not disturb the bargaining: in bounded technical problems, in transport and cutting and scheduling, where a computation could serve a single organisation's plan without creating a criterion that judged the plans of others. Where the methods implied a general criterion, a set of shadow prices against which ministries' claims and enterprises' performance could be measured without negotiation, they were studied, praised, and shelved. An optimum is not merely a calculation. It is a standard that demotes every bargainer to a witness, and the system that employed the bargainers declined the demotion.

### Did the planners ever attempt a computed optimum?

Individual economists built the mathematics and a few bounded applications ran in transport and materials cutting, but the economy's plans were never computed. The balances were bargained to consistency and the targets incremented from achievement. Optimisation survived as a research programme and a rhetoric, admired by the system and excluded from its operating method.

The distinction matters for the wider argument, and the article marks it as one of the points on which the description corrects both camps. Critics of planning sometimes write as if the Soviet failure were the failure of an attempted computation, as if the planners had set up the equations and the equations had beaten them. Nothing of the kind occurred, and the correction strengthens rather than weakens the critics' deeper case, since the reason no computation was attempted at scale is the reason Mises and Hayek gave: the data for a genuine computation, truthful capacities and commensurable values, were exactly what the system could not supply. Defenders of planning, for their part, sometimes write as if Kantorovich's mathematics vindicates the Soviet method, as if the existence of an optimal planning school inside the system showed the system groping toward rationality. The record shows the school and the method proceeding in parallel for forty years, the one honoured and the other untouched, and a vindication that never reaches the operating level is a vindication of the school's mathematics, which nobody disputed, not of the method, which is the subject here.

## Two kinds of shortage: price and scarcity

One distinction, required by precision and often blurred in the literature's popular versions, separates two conditions that share a symptom. A shortage can arise because a price is held below the level at which supply and demand would meet: the good is producible, the resources exist, but at the fixed price the quantity demanded exceeds the quantity supplied, and the excess demand appears as queue and empty shelf. Such a shortage is an artefact of the price, and it would vanish if the price rose, at whatever cost in distribution and discontent. A shortage can also arise from scarcity itself, from the genuine absence of the resources to produce more of the good at any price the society could pay: the queue then expresses a real constraint, and raising the price would change who goes without, not whether someone does. The Soviet economy exhibited both, and the analysis of any particular empty shelf must ask which condition produced it. Kornai's contribution was to show that the system's characteristic shortage was of neither simple kind. The soft budget constraint generated excess demand as a permanent condition across the whole range of goods, whatever their prices, because the demanders were enterprises whose spending was not limited by their earnings; and the shortage reproduced itself through hoarding at every level, so that goods physically present in the economy were absent from the points of need. This systemic shortage could coexist with genuine scarcity, in a poor country often did, and with price artefacts, in the consumer sector usually did, and the three layers compounded into the lived experience of an economy where almost everything was, in the participants' phrase, deficit: a deficit good being simply a good one could not reliably obtain.

### What test distinguishes a shortage made by prices from a shortage made by scarcity?

The test is what a price rise would do. Where price alone binds, raising it clears the shelf by reducing demand to the producible quantity. Where scarcity binds, the shelf stays empty and only the identity of the disappointed changes. Soviet shortage usually mixed both with a third cause, systemic excess demand, which no single price could cure.

The distinction has a practical consequence for reading the system's history. Episodes of acute consumer shortage, the empty food shops of particular years and cities, were typically the price and scarcity layers biting together under a shock: a poor harvest, a procurement failure, a monetary overhang of wages paid against goods that did not exist. The chronic industrial shortage, the missing bearing and the undelivered casting, was the systemic layer, present in good years and bad, in plenty and in dearth, because its cause was the demand behaviour of soft budgeted enterprises and not the harvest. Reformers who treated the chronic shortage as a pricing problem prescribed price adjustments that the systemic layer absorbed without effect; reformers who treated it as scarcity prescribed production drives that the hoarding mechanism converted into larger stocks in the wrong places. Kornai's analysis earned its standing by explaining why both prescriptions failed in the same way, and any student of the system who cannot reproduce the three layer distinction will misread most of the episodes the system presents.

## The five year plan and the annual plan: strategy and instrument

The phrase five year plan dominates the system's public memory, and the description should fix its relation to the annual plans that did the operating work. The five year plan was a strategic document: it set the period's growth ambitions, its investment programme, the great construction projects, and the intended shifts in the economy's structure, and it was approved with ceremony as the state's covenant with the future. The annual plan was the legal instrument: the document whose indicators bound the enterprise, whose balances governed the funds, and against whose fulfilment careers were judged. The relationship between the two was looser than the ceremony implied. The annual plans were supposed to disaggregate the five year trajectory, each year delivering its fifth of the period's increments, but the annual bargaining proceeded from what the year just ending had achieved, not from the trajectory's requirement, and the two arithmetic paths diverged whenever achievement fell short or the leadership's priorities moved. Five year plans were therefore routinely unfulfilled in their particulars while being celebrated in their rhetoric, and their real function settled into something the documents did not announce: they were the reservoir of priorities and projects from which annual bargaining drew, the statement of direction that told ministries which claims would find favour, rather than a schedule the years were bound to keep. The system's historians treat the five year plans accordingly, as evidence of the leadership's intentions and of the economy's structure of ambition, while treating the annual plans, the corrections, and the fulfilment reports as the evidence of what the economy actually did. A reader who confuses the two documents will mistake the regime's hopes for its history, an error the regime's own publicity was designed to encourage.

The divergence had a rhythm worth noting. The opening years of a five year period typically inherited the previous period's unfinished construction and its accumulated strains, and the plans of those years were set taut in the rhetoric of a fresh start. The middle years ground through the bargaining described in this article, with fulfilment uneven across branches. The closing years faced the accounting: the period's promises audited against achievement, the shortfalls explained, the next period's document drafted with the explanations absorbed as lowered bases or redefined indicators. Participants understood the rhythm and discounted the documents accordingly, and the discounting is itself a datum about the method: a plan that its executors expect to be renegotiated is a different instrument from a plan they expect to be enforced, and the Soviet plan, for all its legal force, lived in the first category in the minds of the men who worked under it. The law said the plan was binding. Experience said the plan was the opening position of a year long negotiation whose other parties were circumstance and the centre itself, and experience, as usual in this system, was the better guide to behaviour.

## Priority: how the ranking actually bit

Priority has been invoked throughout this description as the system's answer to scarcity, and its mechanics deserve a section, because priority was not a sentiment but a set of administrative devices. A branch, a project, or a category of supply enjoyed priority when its claims were satisfied first from the funds, when its allocations were protected against the cuts that fell on others, when its construction received materials and labour ahead of competing sites, and when its failures summoned help from the centre rather than penalties. The devices included the designation of funded commodities and of listed enterprises whose supply was guaranteed by the centre itself, the maintenance of state reserves that priority users could draw upon, and the intervention of the party apparatus, whose regional and central officials acted as expediters of last resort for the projects their careers were bound to. The armaments sector stood at the ranking's head for most of the system's life, with the heavy industries that fed it, and the great construction projects of each period, a dam, a steel complex, a railway, enjoyed a temporary elevation into the protected class. The system's ability to concentrate was real, and concentration is what priority means: not that the priority sector escaped the method, but that the method's scarcities were deliberately exported from it to the rest of the economy.

The export had a price that the priority sectors themselves eventually paid, and the price closes the argument of this section. An armaments plant of the highest priority still drew its components, its materials, and its workers' consumer goods from the unprioritised economy, and as that economy's assortment narrowed and its quality sagged under the exported shortage, the priority sector's own inputs degraded with it. The system's last decades show the pattern plainly in the leadership's own internal complaints: the protected core complaining of the unprotected periphery on which it depended, and no administrative device available to protect the whole, since protection for everyone is protection for no one. Priority, in short, was a method for deciding who would suffer the shortage first and who last, and the method worked exactly as designed. Abolishing the shortage it distributed lay beyond it, and the belief that it could be abolished, that one more elevation of one more priority would finally exempt the decisive sector from the economy it inhabited, was the leadership's persistent version of the error this article has traced at every level: the belief that the pattern was a problem of arrangement rather than of structure.

## Foreign trade: the plan crosses the border

The planning method extended across the state's borders through the monopoly of foreign trade, one of the system's founding institutions, and its operation completes the picture of how allocation by entitlement handled the outside world. Foreign trade was conducted not by enterprises but by specialised foreign trade organisations subordinate to the ministry responsible for external commerce, each handling a category of goods. The producing enterprise delivered export goods to the trade organisation at domestic wholesale prices and received its payment in roubles, insulated from the foreign price the goods actually fetched; the importing organisation likewise bought abroad at world prices and sold to domestic users at domestic prices, with the state budget absorbing the differences through a mechanism of price equalisation. The insulation was deliberate and double edged. It protected the plan from the disturbances of world prices, which could not blow through the domestic price structure and unmake the balances, and it protected domestic producers from any knowledge of their standing in world markets, since an enterprise never learned whether its product had sold abroad dear or cheap, or been saleable at all. The trade plan was built like every other plan, from balances and achieved levels: export obligations set as quantities of the goods the country traditionally sold, raw materials and fuels prominent among them, and import plans constrained by the foreign exchange those exports were expected to earn, with machinery and grain at the head of the import queue in the decades when the country bought both.

Trade among the socialist states of Eastern Europe, organised through the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance, added a second planning layer with its own instructive properties. Deliveries between member states were negotiated bilaterally, balanced in accounting units that were not convertible into Western currency or freely usable outside the pair of countries that had generated them, and priced by formulas derived from world prices averaged over past years, which insulated the bloc's internal trade from price movements at the cost of insulating it from price information as well. The bilateral balancing reproduced, at international scale, the domestic system's characteristic pattern: quantities negotiated between interested bureaucracies, surpluses in the accounting unit that could not be spent where they were needed, and a permanent incentive to export what the partner was least eager to receive and to import whatever the partner was least willing to supply, since hard goods, those saleable in the West, were hoarded for the Western trade and soft goods circulated within the bloc. Observers called it the hard goods problem, and it is the tolkach's world raised to the level of states: entitlement and negotiation where prices were not permitted to clear, with the same ingenuity and the same waste. The description of the domestic method thus travels without modification across the border, which is evidence for the article's central claim that the method was a constitution rather than a technique. Constitutions govern everything they touch in the same way, and this one governed a trade bloc as it governed a shoe factory, by entitlement, increment, and bargain.

## The regional experiment: planning by territory

One major reorganisation interrupted the branch principle, and its fate confirms the description's account of where power lay. In the late nineteen fifties, under Nikita Khrushchev, the branch ministries in Moscow were largely abolished and their enterprises transferred to regional economic councils, the sovnarkhozy, each administering the industry of a territory regardless of branch. The reform's logic attacked departmentalism directly: a regional council, owning all the local industries together, would coordinate across branch lines, exploit local resources, and end the duplication by which each ministry had built its own supply empire in every region. For several years the experiment ran, and its results reproduced the system's logic in a new geometry. The regional councils developed the territorial version of the old disease, localism: each defended its region's interests against the centre and its neighbours, hoarded its region's products for its region's enterprises, and resisted the specialisation between regions on which a continental economy depends. Coordination across regions, which the branch system had supplied badly through ministries and Gosplan had supplied at the aggregate level, now lacked an owner, and the great interregional flows of a continental economy frayed. Within a decade the reform was reversed, the branch ministries restored, and the episode entered the literature as the demonstration that the system's bargaining structure survives the rearrangement of its boxes. Whether the negotiators sat in branch ministries or in regional councils, they negotiated, with the same information problems, the same ratchet, and the same soft budgets, because those features belonged not to the organisational chart but to the relationships the chart merely housed. The centre drew the lesson that reorganisation was safe, since nothing essential changed, and Soviet administration settled into its late pattern of periodic structural reshuffles whose effects the participants learned to wait out.

## New products and the price of novelty

The indicator system's most refined pathology appeared where old products met new ones, and it deserves a place in the description because it shows the gaming of the method at its most inventive. An enterprise rewarded on the value of its output faced fixed prices for its existing assortment, which capped the value it could record without producing more. A new product, however, required a new price, and new prices were set from the producing enterprise's stated costs, with temporary prices for novel items set generously to encourage their adoption. The incentive wrote itself. An enterprise could raise its recorded output without raising its production by modifying a product cosmetically, registering the variant as new, and obtaining a price that embodied the improvement's claimed cost rather than its value. Pseudo novelty became an industry within industry: the same coat with a different button, the same radio in a restyled cabinet, each carrying a fresh price and a fresh contribution to the value indicator, while the assortment available to buyers stagnated beneath a froth of registered innovation. The price committees fought the practice with rules about genuine novelty and with audits of claimed costs, and the practice adapted faster than the rules, because the enterprise's knowledge of its own product exceeded any committee's, and the information asymmetry that governed the plan governed the price list too.

The pathology had a mirror image in genuinely new products, which the system handled badly for the opposite reason. A real innovation required investment, disrupted current fulfilment while lines were retooled, entered the plan without an achieved level to increment from, and faced a price setting process that might leave it unprofitable in the enterprise's accounts even when the economy needed it urgently. The rational director therefore preferred the cosmetic variant to the real one with a consistency that no exhortation disturbed, and the economy's product range evolved toward a distinctive profile: enormous breadth of registered items, narrow depth of genuine novelty, and a persistent lag in exactly the consumer facing industries where novelty is the substance of progress. Historians of technology under planning treat this as one of the method's deepest costs, deeper than the queues, because it was cumulative and self concealing. An economy can see its empty shelves. It cannot easily see the products that were never developed, the assortment that never diversified, the improvements that died in a director's office as a threat to the year's indicators. The counterfactual shelves, stocked with the goods the system discouraged, are invisible in every archive, and the description of the method is the only instrument that can make them visible, by showing the incentive that emptied them before they were built.

## Bonuses and the enterprise funds: the human margin of the indicators

The indicators governed not only the enterprise's standing but the incomes of the people inside it, and the transmission mechanism is part of the method's description. A share of the enterprise's results was formed into incentive funds, from which bonuses were paid to managers, engineers, and workers, scaled to the fulfilment of the judged indicators. The sums were significant in the lives of their recipients: managerial bonuses could rival salary, and the workforce's quarterly premium was a fixture of the household budget. The consequence was that the indicator system did not merely direct the enterprise as an organisation; it aligned the personal interests of everyone in the plant with the behaviours this article has described. The worker who connived at storming, the engineer who signed for the cosmetic variant, the storekeeper who concealed the reserve stock, were not sabotaging a system imposed on them from outside. They were participants in a distribution of income that rewarded exactly those acts, and their participation gave the method its social depth. Reformers who proposed to change the indicators discovered that they were proposing to change the incomes of millions of households, defended by everyone whose premium depended on the familiar distortions, and the discovery explains part of the reform history's pattern of bold announcement and quiet absorption. A constitution, again, is not a technique. It is a settlement among interests, and the Soviet plan's settlement reached into the pay packet of every industrial family in the country, which is why the settlement outlived every minister who promised to revise it.

## The enterprise plan: anatomy of a document

The method's unit of account was the enterprise plan, and its anatomy shows the whole system in miniature. The document, known by the abbreviation of its full title as the techpromfinplan, the technical industrial financial plan, ran to hundreds of pages for a large enterprise and bound together every aspect of its existence in a single negotiated settlement. Its sections covered the production programme in assortment and value; the technical development plan, with its measures for new equipment and improved processes; the plan for material and technical supply, listing requirements against allocations; the labour and wages plan; the cost plan, from which the enterprise's financial result would be derived; the financial plan proper, relating the enterprise to the budget and the bank; and the plan for capital construction where investment was under way. Each section was a bargain struck in the cycle described earlier, and the sections constrained one another, so that a concession won in the supply section could be spent in the production section, and the document's final form recorded the enterprise's whole negotiating position as the year began. The plan's authority over the enterprise was real but peculiar. It was simultaneously the enterprise's orders, its budget, its defence brief, and its opening position for the year's renegotiations, and a director used it in all four characters, citing it to the ministry as a shield when demands exceeded it, exceeding it where the year's opportunities invited, and pleading its inadequacies when its targets came due unmet.

The document's fate through the year is as instructive as its composition. Plan corrections arrived continuously: supplementary allocations, revised assortment obligations, adjusted indicators, investment rescheduled, each correction a small treaty between the enterprise and its superiors, and by the year's end the operative plan, the one against which fulfilment was finally judged, could differ substantially from the document approved in January. The literature distinguishes accordingly between the plan as decreed and the plan as adjusted, and treats the distance between them as a measure of the system's true operating mode. A command economy in the strict sense would show a small distance. The Soviet record shows a large and systematic one, varying by branch and period but never approaching zero, and the distance is the empirical signature of everything this article has described: the bargaining did not precede the plan and then stop. The bargaining was the plan's mode of existence, from the first control figures to the final fulfilment report, and the document was its stationery.

## The party in the plan: directives and the parallel hierarchy

One institution stood behind the planning apparatus and intervened in it at every level, and no description is complete without it: the Communist Party. The plan's strategic directives originated in the party's leading bodies, which set the period's priorities, approved the five year framework, and settled the disputes that the administrative hierarchy could not settle among itself. Below that summit, the party's apparatus duplicated the state's at every level, and its officials treated the plan's fulfilment in their territories and branches as their own responsibility, which made them participants in the method rather than spectators of it. The regional party secretary was an economic manager in all but title, pressing enterprises for fulfilment, expediting supplies for the projects his region was judged by, and intervening in the bargaining as a power behind both parties. This parallel hierarchy performed functions the formal system could not. It supplied an enforcement and arbitration channel outside the ministerial chain, able to cut across departmental boundaries when a priority demanded it, and it supplied the political discipline that held the apparatus's bargainers within the settlement's outer limits. Its costs were the mirror of its functions. Intervention by authority short circuited the already weak signals of the formal system, taught enterprises that the decisive relationship was political rather than economic, and made the plan's outcomes partly a record of the party's attention, which is a scarce and arbitrarily distributed resource. The archives opened to scholars after the system's end have deepened this part of the picture considerably, and the work of historians such as Paul Gregory, reconstructing the decision process from the documents of the nineteen thirties, shows a leadership receiving information through the same filtered channels this article has described, aware of the filtering, and governing by intervention in individual cases because the general picture could not be trusted. The dictator's information problem, in the literature's phrase, is Hayek's problem viewed from the top of the pyramid: the higher the report ascended, the more completely it had been shaped by the interests of everyone it had passed.

## Was the plan taut? The scholarly argument about pressure

One scholarly dispute about the method should be recorded, because it refines the description at a point where outsiders commonly go wrong. Soviet plans were conventionally described, by the system's own economists and many Western ones, as taut: set deliberately at the edge of the possible, to mobilise effort and smoke out reserves. The description is true of the rhetoric and of particular branches at particular moments, and the storming, hoarding, and bargaining of this article are all consistent with enterprises living under pressure. But a revisionist line in the scholarship, associated with the emigré economist Igor Birman and developed by others, argued that the operating plans, after the bargaining and corrections had done their work, were often not taut at all for the enterprises that had bargained skilfully: that the system's insiders arranged, through the mechanisms described here, plans they could fulfil with the margins their bonuses required, and that the appearance of universal tautness was part of the theatre both sides maintained, the centre to claim mobilisation, the enterprise to claim heroism. The truth the description supports is distributional. Tautness fell unevenly, on the weak bargainers, the unprioritised, the new directors without networks, and the branches out of favour, while the skilled and the connected lived under gentler arithmetic. The system's pressure, like its shortage, was real in aggregate and negotiable in particular, and the skill of operating within it consisted largely of moving oneself from the category of those to whom the rules were applied to the category of those with whom they were discussed. That movement, repeated across millions of careers, is as good a one sentence sociology of the late Soviet order as the literature offers.

## Quality and the inspectorate: the standard nobody could enforce

Quality was the indicator that resisted indication, and the system's long war to enforce it is a study in the limits of administration. The formal defences were formidable on paper. Products were governed by state standards, the GOSTs, specifying composition, dimensions, tolerances, and performance, elaborated by a standards apparatus whose documents ran to tens of thousands of titles. Enterprises maintained technical control departments charged with rejecting substandard output before it left the gate. Customers could refuse deliveries that failed the standard and claim penalties through state arbitration. In the system's last reforming years, a state acceptance inspectorate was imposed on enterprises from outside, its inspectors independent of the director and empowered to stop shipments, in a deliberate attempt to break the collusion between producer and inspector that the internal arrangements invited. Each defence failed in the same instructive way. The standards specified what could be measured, and production migrated to the unmeasured margins, the finish, the durability, the fit, exactly as the indicator logic predicts. The internal inspectors were the director's subordinates, paid from the enterprise's funds, judged in the end by the enterprise's fulfilment, and their rejections bent accordingly. The customer's refusal was a weapon few dared use in a shortage economy, where the supplier whose delivery had been rejected controlled the following allocation, and where the customer's own plan depended on incorporating whatever arrived. The external inspectorate bit hardest and was resisted hardest, its first years producing genuine disruption in branches unaccustomed to rejection, and its history thereafter recapitulating the system's general law: an enforcement mechanism inserted into the incentive structure is absorbed by the incentive structure, its inspectors cultivated, its standards negotiated, its statistics reconciled, until the new institution performs the old behaviours in new uniforms.

The consumer experienced the war's outcome as the texture of material life: goods that met their standards and failed their purposes, shoes that fitted the last and not the foot, appliances whose rated performance survived exactly as long as the inspection. The description should resist the easy conclusion that the system's producers could not make quality. The same economy produced, in its priority sectors, instruments and weapons of formidable refinement, where the customer was powerful, the inspection genuine, and the resources unstinted. Quality followed the distribution of power to reject. Where the buyer could refuse, quality rose; where the buyer was captive, quality fell to the standard's floor and through it. The market economies' unglamorous mechanism, the customer's exit, was the missing institution, and no inspectorate, however designed, could simulate the discipline of a buyer who can walk away. This is perhaps the description's most portable finding, since it requires nothing specifically socialist to operate: any system that allocates by entitlement and forbids refusal will manufacture the Soviet quality profile in whatever it produces, and any system that wishes to avoid the profile must protect the refusal, which is to say, must reintroduce the one element of the market that planning existed to abolish.

## Enterprise computing: the ASU systems and the local network

The computerisation that the centre refused at national scale proceeded, in a contained form, inside the system's constituent parts, and the contrast is instructive. From the nineteen sixties onward, large enterprises and ministries built automated management systems, known by the Russian initials ASU, computerising their accounting, their supply records, their production scheduling, and the preparation of their plan documents. By the system's last decades, thousands of such systems operated, and their aggregate computing power was considerable. Their function within the method, however, was the opposite of the one Glushkov had proposed. The national network would have made enterprise data transparent to the centre and substituted computation for bargaining. The enterprise systems served the enterprise: they accelerated its paperwork, strengthened its hand in negotiation by marshalling its figures faster than the ministry could check them, and automated the production of the reports through which the centre saw the economy. Computing power flowed to the points in the system where it reinforced the existing distribution of information, and stopped at the boundary where it would have redistributed that information. The episode refines the article's earlier conclusion about OGAS. The obstacle was never the machines, and it was not exactly a refusal of computing. It was a selection, operating through thousands of adoption decisions, in favour of every application that left the bargaining intact and against the one application that would have ended it. Systems absorb the technologies that flatter their constitutions and reject the ones that amend them, and the Soviet planning apparatus, offered the computer, took the half that made it a faster version of itself.

## Extensive growth: the method and the frontier of resources

The method's performance changed over the system's life, and the change has a pattern the description should register, because it explains why the same machinery that delivered mobilisation in one era delivered stagnation in another. The Soviet economy grew, for most of its history, extensively: by drawing ever greater quantities of labour, capital, land, and raw materials into production, rather than by extracting steadily more output from given quantities. Extensive growth is the natural register of a mobilisation system. The plan's instruments, balances of physical quantities and increments on achieved levels, are quantity instruments; they command more tonnes, more hectares, more hands, and they are silent on the ratio between input and output, which no balance records and no indicator long survived rewarding. While the economy commanded vast unused reserves, the peasantry's underemployed labour, the unexploited resources of a continental territory, the backlog of known technology to be copied, extensive growth could proceed at impressive rates, and the method's blindness to efficiency cost little that showed. As the reserves thinned, the labour surplus absorbed, the easy resources committed, and the technology frontier reached in the priority sectors, growth by addition required ever larger increments of input for each increment of output, and the method had no instrument for the transition to growth by improvement. The leadership's economists named the required transition intensification and demanded it in document after document across the system's last decades. The demand failed for the reasons this article has assembled: intensification is efficiency, efficiency requires a criterion, the criterion requires prices or their computed equivalent, and the system's constitution excluded both. The stagnation of the late period was therefore not the method ceasing to work. It was the method continuing to work, in an environment that had stopped rewarding what the method did. Few judgements in the literature command wider assent, and the article adopts it as the frame for its assessment: judge the machinery by its fit to its tasks, and the history divides into the era when the task was mobilisation, which the machinery performed, and the later era, when the task had become efficiency, which the machinery could not so much as describe.

## The retail network: planning the shop shelf

The consumer's point of contact with the method was the state retail network, and its planning completes the circuit of the description. Trade was itself a planned sector, with its own ministry, its own indicators, and its own place at the end of every queue for resources. Shops received their goods through wholesale bases that allocated the output of the consumer industries among regions and stores, in assortments determined by the same aggregate logic as everything else: quantities within commodity groups, settled by increment and negotiation, with the shop's own knowledge of its customers' wants entering the process weakly if at all, since the shop's indicators rewarded turnover in value, which the allocation of scarce popular goods served and the accumulation of unwanted goods did not prevent. The result, familiar to every citizen, was the simultaneous presence of glut and dearth on adjoining shelves: the unpopular variant in heaped abundance, its popular counterpart absent for months, the pattern reproducing the producer economy's assortment failure at the final point of distribution. The shop assistant, in this arrangement, occupied a position of quiet power that the system's formal equality never acknowledged, since access to the deficit goods that arrived in small quantities flowed through the counter's discretion, and the economy of favours that the tolkach represented at industrial scale reproduced itself at retail scale in the relationships between households and the people who stood between them and the shelves. The description of planning that ends at the factory gate has stopped one stage early. The method's final product was not the good but the queue, the favour, and the empty shelf beside the full one, and its full cost accounting must include the hours and the humiliations of the distribution it organised.

## The vocabulary of the method: words that were institutions

A description of the system should pause over its vocabulary, because the Soviet economy's key words named institutions that have no equivalents in market economies, and a reader who masters the words has mastered half the method. Val, the gross output indicator, has appeared already: a single word for the measure that bent production for decades. Fondy, funds, named the allocated quantities of producer goods, and the adjective fondiruemye, funded, marked the commodities important enough to be distributed by entitlement rather than by contract alone. Limity, limits, named the ceilings set on investment, employment, and construction starts, the negative space of the plan within which bargaining moved. The naryad, the outfit or work order, was the supply document that turned an allocation into a named delivery from a named supplier. Shturmovshchina, storming, named the end of period rush with a contempt the participants shared and a resignation they also shared. Blat named the economy of personal pull, the use of connections to obtain what entitlement and queue could not, practised by households and enterprises alike as a normal instrument of life. Tolkach, the pusher, has been met. Defitsit, deficit, was the citizen's everyday word for the shortage condition, applied to goods as an adjective of their nature rather than their momentary state: a deficit good was not temporarily out of stock but constitutionally unavailable, and the word's grammar, which made scarcity a property of the object rather than of the situation, records the population's accurate understanding that the condition was systemic. A vocabulary is a theory in disguise. The Soviet economy's words assume a world of entitlements, ceilings, allocations, and pushes, in which nothing is simply bought and everything is obtained, and the citizen who spoke the language had already absorbed the description this article labours to give.

## Research and development: planning the unknown

The method faced its hardest test in the sector whose output is by nature unpredictable, and its performance there completes the picture of its limits. Scientific research was planned like production: institutes received thematic plans, staffing tables, and budgets, reported fulfilment in completed themes and delivered reports, and answered to indicators as surely as any factory. The branch research institutes, subordinate to the industrial ministries, were charged with the development work their branches required, while the Academy of Sciences governed fundamental research through its own hierarchy. The arrangement reproduced the system's general pattern in an aggravated form. An institute's achieved level was its past themes, its ratchet the expectation that each new programme would exceed its predecessor, its indicator the completed report rather than the adopted innovation, since adoption happened in enterprises whose own indicators punished disruption. The celebrated gap of Soviet technology, between research results that existed on paper and production practice that ignored them, was the institutional distance between the institute that was rewarded for the report and the enterprise that was punished for the retooling, with no organisation in the structure rewarded for carrying a result across the gap. Priority programmes crossed it by command, as they crossed everything, and the civilian economy accumulated its backlog of unadopted research, a library of solutions to problems the producing system had every incentive to leave unsolved. The description's moral is by now familiar, but its application here is the saddest in the article, because the wasted asset was not steel or labour but knowledge, produced at great expense by a system that educated scientists superbly and then arranged its incentives so that their work could not reach the factory floor.

## The last reform: state orders and the unravelling of the cycle

The system's final attempt at reform, in the late nineteen eighties, is worth describing because it attacked the cycle itself and thereby revealed, in its failure, how completely the cycle had become the economy. The Law on the State Enterprise of 1987 proposed to replace the compulsory plan with the state order: the centre would commission a portion of the enterprise's output, declining over time, and the enterprise would sell the remainder freely, keep its earnings, elect its director, and govern itself through its workforce's council. The design was the most radical of the Soviet reform era, since it proposed to shrink the territory of entitlement year by year until the market occupied the field. What happened instead instructs the reader in the method's deepest property. The ministries, charged with issuing the state orders, set them at levels that absorbed nearly the whole of their enterprises' capacity, since a ministry that released its enterprises released its own reason to exist; the state order became the plan under a new name, at a higher level of tautness than the old plan had usually reached, because it now claimed the legitimacy of a contract freely accepted. Enterprises, for their part, used their new freedoms where freedoms helped, raising prices on their free output, shedding unprofitable assortment, and bargaining their state orders as they had bargained their plans, while the supply system, still allocation based, could not serve the market the law had conjured. The reform thus produced, in its first years, the plan's discipline without the plan's coherence and the market's freedoms without the market's supplies, and the economy's performance deteriorated in ways that discredited both vocabularies at once. The episode is not, for this article's purposes, a story about the wisdom or folly of the reformers. It is the final demonstration of the description's central structural claim: the cycle was not a policy laid over the economy, which a law could lift. It was the economy's constitution, embodied in the ministries' survival, the enterprises' relationships, and the supply system's documents, and a constitution is not amended by renaming its articles.

## The second economy: the scholarly dispute about scale and meaning

Scholars of the informal economy disagreed, productively, about what they were looking at, and the dispute belongs in the description because it governs how the whole system should be judged. Gregory Grossman, who named the field with his studies of the second economy, treated it as a vast penumbra of private and semi legal activity, production and trade conducted for personal gain outside the plan's framework, and argued that its scale made any account of the Soviet economy that ignored it a fiction. Others, studying the same phenomena from the enterprise's side, stressed the functional reading this article has adopted: much of the informal activity was not an alternative economy competing with the plan but a component of the planned economy itself, the lubricant its own operation required, undertaken by state organisations in pursuit of state targets. The two readings are not as opposed as their proponents sometimes made them, and the description supports their synthesis. The informal economy had two faces that shared personnel and techniques: a plan serving face, the tolkach's expeditions and the directors' mutual assistance, which existed because the formal system could not function without it; and a private face, the moonlighting craftsman, the traded favour, the diverted goods, which existed because the formal system left the population it served unsatisfied. Both faces drew on the same capital of connections and the same skills of negotiation, and individuals moved between them without noticing a frontier. The judgement that follows for the system's assessment is double, like so much in this article. The informal economy was the planned economy's confession of incompleteness, written in its participants' behaviour; and it was also the planned economy's rescue, the reason a method that could not specify its own requirements nevertheless fed, clothed, and equipped a superpower for decades. A theory of planning that cannot hold both halves of that sentence has not yet described its object.

## Wages, savings, and the money that could not be spent

The household side of the money system displayed the shortage economy in monetary form, and the description should include it, because it explains a pressure that built silently beneath the system's last decades. Wages were paid in cash, from wage funds the plan controlled, and they grew across the postwar decades as the leadership bought social peace with rising incomes. The goods against which those wages could be spent grew more slowly, and at fixed retail prices the gap appeared not as open inflation, which the price system forbade, but as its repressed form: money accumulating in savings accounts and mattresses because the shops held nothing the holder wished to buy at the moment of holding it. Economists call the accumulated excess a monetary overhang, and its existence was an open secret, debated in the Soviet press in the system's final years with increasing frankness. The overhang was the household's version of the enterprise's hoard: a stock held against an unreliable supply system, rational for the holder, corrosive in aggregate, since every rouble of it represented demand that would descend on any good that appeared and strip the shelf in an hour. It also represented a quiet falsification of the social contract's arithmetic. The state could report rising incomes and stable prices simultaneously for decades, and both reports could be accurate, while the citizen's experience was of money that depreciated in the coin that counted, the time and connection required to convert it into goods. When prices were finally freed after the system's end, the overhang converted itself into open inflation in a matter of months, and the savings of a generation dissolved. The episode belongs to the prehistory of that event rather than to this article's subject, but its roots are here, in a planning method that could fix the wage fund and the price list separately and never had to reconcile them, because reconciliation is what markets, for all their cruelties, perform every day without being asked.

## Transport: the plan's circulatory system under strain

The economy's physical coherence depended on transport, and the railway network that carried the bulk of its freight was planned as a single state system, under a ministry of its own, judged by indicators of its own, and afflicted by a version of every pathology in this description. Freight plans assigned tonnage and ton kilometres; wagon supply was allocated among loading enterprises as steel was allocated among users; and the chronic mismatch between the freight the plan generated and the wagons the system could place produced the transport version of storming, as enterprises loaded frantically when wagons appeared and demurrage charges accumulated when they did not. The railways' own priority was high, their investment protected, their performance by the standards of freight railways considerable, and still the network ran as a permanent bottleneck, because the economy it served generated transport demand wastefully by design. Departmental autarky duplicated hauls; the assortment failures of industry shipped the wrong goods to the wrong places and shipped them back as returns or replacements; the location of new capacity, decided by ministries for whom transport cost was an accounting figure at fixed tariffs rather than a real constraint, spread production across distances that a price sensitive system would have refused to span. The transport case generalises the article's argument one final time. A system that does not price a resource will overconsume it, whether the resource is steel, labour, or distance, and the overconsumption will present itself to the system's managers as an inexplicable shortage of the resource, to be met by the method's eternal prescription: a larger increment on the achieved level, more wagons, more track, more tonnes, while the demand that devoured them grew unexamined in the plan's own arithmetic.

## Pripiski: the falsified report and the corruption of the record

Every mechanism in this description depends on reports, and the system's participants, at the end of the chain of incentives, sometimes falsified the reports themselves. The practice had a name, pripiski, literally additions: the inflation of reported output, the claiming of production not performed, the counting of unfinished goods as complete, the harvesting on paper of crops that stood unharvested in the field. Its scale varied by branch and period, and the literature treats particular epidemics of it as diagnostic events, moments when the pressure of the indicators overwhelmed the remaining checks. The checks were real: the bank's control by the rouble cross verified payments against deliveries, the statistical administration collected returns through channels formally independent of the ministries, and inspectors audited stocks against documents. The falsification survived because the auditors faced the same asymmetry as the planners. The enterprise knew its stocks; the inspector sampled them; and the ministry, whose own standing rode on its branch's reported fulfilment, had reasons of its own to accept reports it suspected, since exposing a subordinate's pripiski exposed the ministry's supervision and reduced the branch's recorded achievement in the only ledger the centre kept. Falsification thus enjoyed a measure of protection at every level that might have policed it, and the centre's statistics, the foundation of the achieved level from which all planning proceeded, absorbed an unknown and unknowable margin of invention. The point completes the article's account of the system's epistemology. The planner's data were strategic at the draft stage, negotiated at the approval stage, and occasionally fictitious at the reporting stage, and the great apparatus of balances, for all its tonnes and tables, rested on a record that its own incentive structure had compromised at the source. When the archives opened and historians compared the reported series with the documents behind them, the discrepancies they found were not the largest of the system's problems, but they were the most emblematic: a method that could not learn the truth about the economy it governed, because it had arranged that telling the truth was nobody's interest.

## The statistical administration: measuring an economy that hid itself

The state's statistical apparatus, the central administration and its regional offices, occupied an impossible position in the method, and its history deserves a paragraph in any honest description. It was charged with measuring an economy whose participants were rewarded for deceiving it, and with publishing the measurements of a state whose legitimacy rested on the growth the measurements recorded. Its professional staff included statisticians of genuine distinction, and its internal working series were often better than the published ones, a distinction the archives have documented: the figures the leadership saw in confidence and the figures the public saw in print diverged, and both diverged from the recalculations that Western scholars, Bergson and his successors, constructed by rebuilding the accounts on consistent definitions. For the planning method itself, the consequence was practical rather than propagandistic. The balances were built from the statistical record; the achieved level was the record's creature; and a record shaped by pripiski, by the exclusion of services from the material product concept, and by prices that valued output at accounting conventions, transmitted all of those distortions into every subsequent plan. The system's defenders abroad often cited its published growth as the method's vindication, and its critics cited the recalculations as the method's exposure, and the description here suggests that both citations miss the deeper point. The method did not merely produce statistics that outsiders disputed. It produced statistics that its own operators could not fully trust, and it planned from them anyway, because they were the only statistics there were. An economy that cannot measure itself cannot correct itself, and the Soviet economy's celebrated stability of method across six decades owes something to that blindness: the feedback that might have forced the method's revision arrived filtered, flattered, and late.

## Sanctions: what happened when the plan was missed

A description of incentives should state the penalties as precisely as the rewards, since the balance between them set the director's behaviour. Formal failure to fulfil the plan exposed the enterprise and its management to a graduated armoury: loss of bonuses, which fell on the whole collective and made the workforce itself an enforcer of the director's strategy; administrative penalties and party discipline for the responsible officials; reduction of the enterprise's funds and allocations in the next cycle; and, in the system's severe periods, criminal liability for the disruption of state discipline, a charge whose memory long outlived its routine application. The armoury's interesting property is how rarely its heavier weapons were used against the behaviour this article has described. Understated capacity, hoarded stocks, bent assortment, and the whole repertoire of plan management were not, in the ordinary course, punished at all, because they were the means by which fulfilment, the punished category's opposite, was achieved. The sanctions attached to the outcome, the missed indicator, and not to the methods, however irregular, that delivered the indicator met. A director who falsified his assortment and fulfilled his value plan stood safer than a director who produced honestly what was asked and missed his figure through a supplier's failure. The system's jurisprudence thereby taught the same lesson as its bonuses, from the other side: the indicator is the reality, and everything else is commentary. Participants who internalised the lesson survived; participants who mistook the formal rules for the operative ones were educated by the first missed plan of their careers. The education was universal, which is why the behaviours of this description were universal, and why appeals to the participants' morality, a staple of the system's own press, changed nothing. The morality on offer was the morality of the indicator, and it was observed with a fidelity that the system's preachers, who had built the indicator, were in no position to deplore.

## The fuel and energy balance: the master constraint

Among the thousands of balances, one family stood above the rest in the planners' own estimation, and a section on it shows the method at its most nearly successful. The fuel and energy balance, which set the production of coal, oil, gas, and electricity against the economy's requirements for them, was the master constraint of the whole system, because energy entered every other balance as a condition of its fulfilment. The Soviet state disposed of continental energy resources, and the planning of their extraction and distribution was the balance method's home ground: a small number of aggregated commodities, produced by a manageable number of large organisations, consumed in quantities that engineering norms predicted tolerably well, and prioritised by a leadership that understood energy as the foundation of its industrial and military power. The record here was correspondingly strong. Electrification, the founding programme from which Gosplan itself descended, was substantially delivered; the great shifts of the energy base, from coal toward oil and then toward gas, were planned and executed across decades; and the energy sector's exports earned the foreign exchange on which the late system's import plans depended. The contrast with the assortment economy is the point. Where the product was homogeneous, the producers few, the norms stable, and the priority absolute, the method worked as its designers had hoped, and the description that omits this success cannot explain the system's longevity or its leaders' confidence. The method was not a universal failure. It was a specialised instrument, superb for a narrow class of problems, applied by its constitution to every problem, and the history of its performance is the history of that mismatch widening as the economy's centre of gravity moved from the problems the method could solve to the problems it could not.

## The republics and the centre: planning a federation

The Soviet state was a federation in form, and the planning method had to govern the tension between the union centre and the republics and regions that composed it, a tension the description should record because it added a second axis of bargaining to the first. Republican planning commissions drew up the plans for their territories, consolidating the enterprises of local subordination and representing the republic's claims in the union bargaining. Enterprises of union subordination, the great plants and the priority branches, stood outside the republic's authority while sitting inside its borders, drawing their supplies from union funds and their orders from Moscow ministries, so that a republic's government presided over a territory whose commanding economy it did not command. The bargaining between centre and republic reproduced the bargaining between centre and ministry with a national inflection: republics pressed for investment in their territories, for the processing of their raw materials at home rather than their export to other republics' factories, and for the location of new capacity whose employment and prestige would be theirs. The centre's allocation of investment among territories was thus one of the plan's most political products, audited by every republic for its share, and the resulting geography of industry, the cotton of one republic ginned in another, the components of a single machine scattered across four time zones by the autarky of ministries and the claims of republics together, embodied compromises that no efficiency criterion had been invited to judge. The system's students treat the territorial question as a subplot, and for the method's description it is one; but the subplot carried the main plot's moral in a second register. Wherever two axes of entitlement cross, the bargaining doubles and the criterion recedes, and a method that allocates by negotiation will always produce the map that negotiation draws, which is the map of the negotiators' power.

## Input output and the SOFE programme: the road not taken, surveyed

The mathematical school's most ambitious construction deserves a fuller account than the reform narrative gave it, because it represents the system's one genuine attempt to imagine computed planning whole. From the nineteen sixties, economists around the Central Economic Mathematical Institute developed the programme the literature calls SOFE, from its Russian initials, the system of optimal functioning of the economy: a hierarchy of models in which the centre would compute the plan's main proportions with the aid of input output tables, the interindustry accounts that Wassily Leontief had pioneered and that Soviet statisticians compiled for the economy from the late nineteen fifties. In the programme's full form, the computation would generate a consistent set of valuations, objectively determined estimates in Kantorovich's phrase, against which decentralised decisions could be guided without detailed commands: the plan as a solved model rather than a bargained document, with prices doing inside socialism the commensurating work that Mises had declared impossible without markets. The programme attracted the system's best economic minds and the hostility of its planning establishment in equal measure, and the establishment's objection, rarely stated as plainly as the archives now allow, was the one this article has generalised: the model's valuations would have judged the ministries' claims by a criterion the ministries did not control. Input output tables were compiled and used, as technical aids to the balances; the valuations were debated in journals for twenty years; and the operating method continued to increment achieved levels while the optimal economy circulated in the literature as a standing reproach. The episode's significance for the wider argument is precise. The socialist calculation debate, conducted in the West as a question about whether computed planning was possible in principle, was conducted inside the Soviet Union as a question about whether it would be permitted in practice, and the answer the institutions gave, across four decades and several changes of leadership, was no. An account of the method is incomplete without that answer, because it converts the debate's terms: the obstacle to rational planning in the Soviet case was not the absence of a technique but the presence of a constitution that the technique would have dissolved.

## Construction: the sector where the plan built its own delays

Capital construction was the plan's most troubled province, and its troubles condense the method's logic into a single sector. Construction stood at the intersection of every other plan: the investment plan authorised the projects, the material balances supplied the cement and steel, the labour plan supplied the workers, and the equipment producing branches supplied the machinery to be installed. The sector's organisation divided responsibility in a way that guaranteed friction. The customer, the ministry or enterprise for whom the project was being built, held the investment funds and the design; the contractor, a construction organisation subordinate to its own ministry, performed the work and answered for its own indicators, which rewarded the value of construction work performed rather than the completion of usable objects. The distinction between those two measures is the sector's whole story. A contractor paid on work performed had every reason to begin many projects, to perform the expensive early work on each, the foundations and frames that carried high value, and to starve the finishing stages whose value was low and whose completion alone made the project an asset. The customer, whose standing depended on the object's commissioning, pressed for completion with the weapons available: appeals, expediters, the party's intervention. Between them, the economy accumulated its celebrated stock of unfinished construction, projects frozen at every stage, their installed equipment rusting, their designs obsolescing, their costs mounting in the accounts as investment that produced nothing. Design organisations, a third party, worked to their own plans and norms, and bore no responsibility for the operating costs of what they designed, so that projects emerged over specified and under adapted to their sites. Every participant's behaviour was, once again, a correct response to the indicator in front of him, and the sector's aggregate result, a construction industry permanently busy and permanently behind, was the sum the indicators dictated. The reformers attacked the sector oftener than any other, with completion based payment, with turnkey contracts, with the consolidation of responsibility, and the sector absorbed the attacks in the usual way, because the division of interests that produced the delays was the division of the administrative structure itself.

## The enterprise as a town: social assets and the hidden wage

The Soviet enterprise was more than a production unit, and its social functions belong in the method's description because they entered its bargaining and its costs. Enterprises built and held housing for their workers, maintained kindergartens, clinics, canteens, holiday facilities, and cultural institutions, and distributed access to them as part of the real wage. The arrangement originated in the scarcities of the industrialisation era, when the state built factories faster than it built cities, and it hardened into a permanent feature: the enterprise as the citizen's landlord, nurse, and travel agent, as well as employer. For the planning method, the social assets mattered in three ways. They enlarged the enterprise's plan and its claims, since housing construction and social maintenance figured in its negotiations as obligations requiring funds. They bound the worker to the enterprise with ties stronger than wages, since leaving a job could mean leaving a queue for a flat, which softened the labour market's discipline in ways the planners alternately deplored and relied upon. And they gave the director a second currency of patronage, the allocation of flats and places and vouchers, which sustained his authority inside the plant as the tolkach sustained his plan outside it. The social wage also falsified every comparison of living standards made in money terms, in both directions at once: the citizen's low money wage understated the provision he received through his enterprise, while the provision's quality and its dependence on his employer's favour overstated its value to him. The description's point is structural. An economy that allocates through organisations rather than markets will load those organisations with every function the market and the municipality perform elsewhere, and the enterprise's inefficiency as a producer cannot be separated from its burden as a welfare state in miniature, a burden the plan imposed and the plan's accounts never priced.

## Operative planning: the year beneath the year

Beneath the annual plan's stately cycle ran a faster rhythm of operative planning, and the description should descend to it, because it was the level at which production was actually steered. The annual indicators were broken into quarterly and monthly assignments, and within the enterprise into decade and shift programmes, each level reperforming the year's negotiations in miniature: the ministry pressing for the quarter's catch up, the enterprise pleading its supply position, the figures adjusted in the light of the months already lost. Operative planning was where the system's information problem bit hardest, because the corrections it existed to make depended on reports of the position as it stood, and those reports were compiled by the same interested parties, at the same strategic discount, as the annual drafts. The centre's picture of the year in progress was therefore a picture of the year as its participants wished it to appear, and the leadership's celebrated interventions, the telephone calls, the dispatched plenipotentiaries, the sudden audits, were attempts to pierce the picture with direct observation, one enterprise at a time, in an economy of tens of thousands. The rhythm also explains the system's strange combination of rigidity and improvisation that struck every observer. The documents were rigid: approved, signed, legally binding, amended only by authority. The practice was improvisation unbroken, as each level adjusted the documents to the reality the documents had failed to foresee. The two were not in contradiction. The rigidity was the framework that made the improvisation a negotiation rather than a free for all, since every departure from the plan had to be transacted with the plan's owners, and the improvisation was the tribute that reality exacted from the framework, paid daily, in every enterprise, by the men with the briefcases.

## What a manager anywhere would recognise

The description has insisted on the Soviet system's specificity, its vocabulary, its constitution, its history, and it should close its analytical part by insisting equally on the description's portability, because the mechanisms it catalogues are not Soviet inventions but general properties of administration that the Soviet case displays at continental scale. The ratchet effect operates wherever a superior sets future targets from recorded performance: in the sales organisation whose best territory earns the hardest quota, in the public service whose efficient office is rewarded with a cut budget, in the university department whose success in attracting students becomes the baseline for its funding. Success indicators bend output wherever a proxy is rewarded: the hospital that meets its waiting list target by reclassifying the wait, the school that teaches to the examination it is judged by, the police force whose recorded crime falls as recording discourages. Soft budget constraints soften wherever an organisation's failure is unthinkable, and produce the same unrestrained demand and the same shortage of the thing the organisation exists to supply. Even the tolkach has his cognates, in the fixer of every large bureaucracy, the person whose real job is to move the organisation's own obstacles out of its way. The Soviet system's distinction is not that it invented these pathologies but that it abolished the counterweights. In mixed economies, the proxy gaming of one organisation is checked by the exit of its customers, the entry of its competitors, the hardness of its budget, and the prices that contradict its reports. The planned economy removed the checks in the name of the plan, and thereby conducted, unintentionally, the largest controlled experiment in the history of administration: a demonstration, across seventy years and a continent, of what the familiar pathologies become when nothing limits them but the ingenuity of the people they govern. That is the description's final use. It is a mirror held up to every target driven administration, and the face in the mirror is recognisable because the mechanisms are universal. Only the scale, and the absence of appeal, were Soviet.

## Inventories: the economy's hidden stockpile

The shortage economy's balance sheet had an asset side, and its size astonished the economists who measured it. Enterprises held stocks of materials, components, and unfinished production far beyond the norms the plan prescribed, and the economy's aggregate inventories grew across the decades until they represented, by the recalculations of the scholars who studied them, a share of national resources that market economies, holding leaner stocks against reliable supply, never approached. The stockpile was the system's insurance made visible. Each enterprise's hoard was a rational premium against the supply failures the method guaranteed; the sum of the hoards was a mountain of frozen capital, materials oxidising in yards while other enterprises stopped for want of them, components for machines already scrapped, steel in profiles nobody had ordered held by works that could not obtain the profiles they needed. The plan prescribed stock norms and the bank policed credit for above norm stocks, and the hoards grew through every campaign against them, because the penalty for an above norm stock was negotiable and the penalty for a stopped line was not. The stockpile also falsified the system's own measures of itself, since output counted in gross terms included the growth of the inventories, production for the warehouse indistinguishable in the accounts from production for use. When the system's critics abroad charged that its growth figures counted goods nobody wanted, the inventories were the charge's material evidence: an economy can be busy, growing, and fulfilling its plans while an increasing share of its product accumulates in yards, and the Soviet economy, in its last decades, was doing exactly that. The hoard is the shortage economy's self portrait. Shortage and surplus, which a market treats as opposites, were in this system the same phenomenon seen from two ends of the same warehouse: too much of everything in the wrong places, too little of anything in the right one, and no price mechanism to move the stock from the first condition to the second.

## The planner himself: Gosplan from the inside

The description has followed the enterprise's incentives in detail, and fairness requires a section on the incentives of the planner, the official in Gosplan or the ministry whose signature moved the figures. He was not the omniscient calculator of the debate's imagination, nor a cipher. He was a specialist, usually an engineer or economist by training, responsible for a branch or a commodity group across a career, possessed of a deep, narrow knowledge of his territory: its enterprises, their directors, their histories of promise and performance. His information came from their reports, discounted by his experience of their habits, and his craft consisted largely of calibrated disbelief: he knew the claims were padded and the capacities concealed, and his skill lay in estimating the padding, branch by branch and man by man. His own incentives pointed, as everyone's did, toward the settlement. He was judged on the production of an approved plan, coherent on paper, delivered on schedule, and on the subsequent fulfilment of the branches in his charge, which gave him an interest in targets his enterprises could meet that warred with his instructions to demand the maximum. The tautness of the final plan was thus negotiated inside the planner's own conscience before it was negotiated with the ministry. His career's dangers were asymmetric in the familiar pattern: a plan that collapsed brought enquiries, while a plan that coasted brought none, and the system's vaunted pressure for growth was filtered, at the desk where the figures were actually set, through a prudent man's desire to survive his own plan. The literature's portrait of the planner, assembled from memoirs and interviews, is of a knowledgeable, overworked, and sceptical official, cynical about the figures he processed and serious about the work of processing them, who understood the system's games because he played his position in them. The description of planning that personifies the centre as a single calculating mind fails here as everywhere: the centre was thousands of such men, each defending his branch's figures against his colleagues' branches, and Gosplan's internal bargaining mirrored the economy's so exactly that the plan can be described, without much exaggeration, as the residue of two nested negotiations, the one inside the building and the one outside it.

## Price revisions: the accountants' occasional revolution

The fixed prices of the producer economy were not literally eternal, and their periodic revision shows the price system's true place in the method. Wholesale prices were comprehensively revised at long intervals, most substantially in the late nineteen sixties, when the price reform accompanying the Kosygin changes recalculated branch prices from costs, raised the prices of undervalued raw materials, and introduced a capital charge into the cost base. The revision's purpose was accounting hygiene: to make the value indicator and the profit measure mean something closer to what they claimed, by aligning prices with the recorded costs of the branches that produced the goods. Its limits define the method's relationship to prices exactly. The new prices were computed from costs, not discovered from exchange, so they embodied the existing structure of production, including its inefficiencies, at the moment of revision; they were then frozen again, while costs, technologies, and scarcities moved on, so that the price list began its drift away from any economic meaning on the day it was published; and no enterprise's allocation depended on them, since the balances and funds continued to govern who received what. A price revision in this system was a recalibration of the measuring instrument by the measurements it had already taken, and its effects were confined to the accounts: profits redistributed among branches on paper, indicators made temporarily more comparable, and the underlying allocation proceeding in tonnes as before. The episode matters because it refutes, from inside the record, the suggestion that the planners simply forgot about prices. They attended to prices repeatedly, intelligently, and at length. What they could not do, within the constitution they served, was let prices allocate, because allocation by price would have dissolved the entitlements through which the whole structure of authority flowed. Prices in the Soviet economy were the plan's bookkeepers. The one duty they were never permitted was the duty the calculation objection identified as indispensable: telling the planner what anything was worth.

## Campaigns and seasons: the plan against the calendar

Superimposed on the plan's annual rhythm was an older rhythm it never mastered, the rhythm of seasons and campaigns. Agriculture imposed it absolutely: sowing and harvest arrived on the weather's schedule, and the state's response, across six decades, was the campaign, the mobilisation of students, soldiers, factory workers, and officials into the fields at the peaks, the whole apparatus of priority and exhortation thrown at a problem whose essence was that the work of a season could not be incremented from an achieved level or stormed in a month without loss. The harvest campaign was the plan's confession that its methods stopped at the field's edge: where production is biological and dispersed, administration gives way to mobilisation, and mobilisation, repeated annually for generations, is an admission that the routine system has failed to routinise. Industry had its campaigns too, for the fulfilment of the plan in its closing weeks, for the commissioning of the period's prestige objects by their ceremonial dates, for quality, for discipline, for the saving of fuel, each campaign a surge of attention from the apparatus onto a single indicator, with the predictable consequence that the surged indicator improved while its neighbours, unattended, decayed. Participants learned the campaign's rhythm as they learned the storming rhythm, and discounted both: the campaign would pass, the attention would move, and the structure that had produced the deficiency would remain, awaiting the next surge. The description's judgement on the campaign is the judgement on the method in miniature. A system that must periodically mobilise against its own routine has announced the routine's inadequacy, and a system that responds to the announcement with another mobilisation has chosen the surge over the repair, because the surge is within its constitution and the repair is not.

## The defence economy: the priority of priorities

Armaments stood first in the priority ranking for the whole of the system's mature life, and the sector's internal arrangements refine the description's account of what priority could and could not do. Defence production was governed by a dedicated apparatus, a commission for military industrial questions at the summit, a set of defence branch ministries, and a system of military representatives stationed inside the producing plants, officers empowered to accept or reject output on the armed forces' behalf. That last institution is the sector's distinction, and its consequences vindicate this article's analysis of quality. The military representative was a customer with the power of refusal, backed by a purchaser for whom performance outweighed price and whose rejection carried consequences no director could negotiate away. Under that discipline, the defence plants produced to standards the civilian economy never approached, and the contrast between the two economies sharing one industrial base became a commonplace of the literature: the same ministries, the same labour force, the same supply system, and two quality regimes, separated by nothing but the presence or absence of a buyer who could say no. The sector's costs to the rest of the economy were correspondingly structural. It drew the best engineers, the finest materials, the first claim on new equipment, and the protection of the highest priority in every fund, and it returned its products to no one's assortment. The civilian economy's lag, which the leadership lamented, was in part the defence economy's shadow, the price of maintaining an island of excellence in a sea the method had made shallow. Even the island felt the sea's level: defence production depended on civilian suppliers for components and materials, and its programmes were delayed, in case after documented case, by the failures of the unprioritised economy on which the prioritised one stood. The system's final lesson about priority is written in that dependence. Priority can protect a sector's claims. It cannot protect a sector from its economy.

## The director's appointment: the nomenklatura and the cadre question

The men who operated the method were selected by a mechanism that belongs in its description, because selection determines behaviour as surely as reward. Enterprise directors and ministry officials were appointed through the nomenklatura system, the party's lists of posts whose occupants required approval by the corresponding party committee, so that a director held his position by a double tenure: formally from his ministry, effectively from the party organs that had vetted his appointment and could procure his removal. The system's criteria for advancement were the criteria this article would predict. Fulfilment of the plan in one's charge was the first; the maintenance of workable relations with the ministry, the party organs, and the network of counterparts was the second; and a reputation for delivering without scandal, for managing the indicators and the inspections and the campaigns into a presentable record, was the compound of the two. Technical brilliance unaccompanied by those arts was not a career; administrative reliability accompanied by modest technical gifts was. The selection pressure operated across decades and produced a managerial stratum of a distinctive type, superbly adapted to the method it operated: negotiators of genius, hoarders by instinct, masters of the document and the relationship, and, by the same adaptation, poorly prepared for any economy in which the customer, the price, and the balance sheet outrank the ministry. When the system ended and its enterprises passed into a market order, the stratum's adaptation became its tragedy, and the speed with which the old skills converted into the new economy's rents, through the same networks and the same arts of entitlement, is a story the literature of the transition tells at length. For the description of the method, the point is simpler. A planning system does not merely allocate goods. It allocates careers, and the careers it allocates shape the people who will operate it in the next generation, so that the method reproduces not only its output but its operators. The ratchet, the indicator, and the tolkach were not policies that a different directorate might have repealed. They were the environment in which the directorate itself was bred.

## The assessment: what the description changes

The article has followed its brief, which was to describe before arguing, and it ends its analytical work by stating what the description does to the argument it postponed. The famous controversy over socialist planning was conducted, on both sides, as a controversy about a computation: whether a central agency could, in principle or in practice, determine the allocation of an economy's resources by solving the problem that markets solve by price. The description shows that the one historical case on which the controversy was fought did not instantiate the computation at all. The Soviet agency incremented, bargained, and balanced a few thousand aggregates while an unofficial network performed the matching the aggregates could not. From that single fact, consequences flow in both directions, and the article has tried to give each its due. Against the planning defence in its classic form: the Soviet record cannot be cited as proof that comprehensive rational allocation was achieved, because it was not attempted, and the achievements the record does prove, mobilisation, priority, industrialisation, are achievements of a different and lesser claim, that a state can move an economy's masses in chosen directions by command of quantities. Against the calculation objection in its crude form: nor does the record prove that an economy without producer goods markets must collapse into incoherence, because the Soviet economy cohered, after its fashion, for decades, feeding and arming a superpower. But against the defence and for the objection at the deeper level where Mises and Hayek actually pitched it: the agency's method is itself the evidence. Confronted with the task of allocation, an apparatus staffed by intelligent men and armed with absolute authority declined, year after year, to allocate in the objection's sense at all, because it possessed no criterion by which allocation could be rationally performed, and it substituted the one procedure that requires no criterion: the repetition of the inherited pattern at a negotiated rate of growth. The objection predicted that without prices the planner would have nothing to go on but precedent and power. The planner, in the event, went on precedent and power. The description does not settle the theoretical question, which has its own articles in this series, of whether some other planning, differently constituted, could do what the Soviet method did not attempt. It settles the historical question of what Soviet planning was, and it leaves the theoretical question permanently altered, because after the description, both sides must argue about a bargaining system that incremented the past, and not about a computer that never existed.

### Which picture of planning should a reader discard first?

The picture of the centre as a calculating mind, weighing the economy's needs against its resources and issuing a coherent optimum. Nothing in the record corresponds to it. Discard next the picture of pure chaos, since the bargaining was orderly, skilled, and effective within its limits. What remains is the method itself: increments, balances, and negotiation.

A word, in closing the assessment, about the human register of the description, because the article's neutrality has been a discipline of analysis and not a claim that the analysed system was neutral in its effects. The method described here was operated, in its first decades, through terror as well as bargaining, and the targets of the industrialisation era were enforced on a population whose sacrifices were not negotiated. The bargaining world of Berliner's managers belongs largely to the mature system, in which the sanctions had softened into careers and bonuses; the same machinery, in its youth, ran on fear, and the achieved levels from which the later increments were calculated had been achieved, in part, by methods this article's procedural vocabulary does not capture. The series treats the human record of those decades in its own articles, and this one has confined itself to the mechanism, as its brief required. But the confinement should be acknowledged at the end, lest the elegance of the mechanism, the ratchet's logic, the indicator's ironies, the tolkach's ingenuity, anaesthetise the reader to the fact that the mechanism's first product was a famine scarred countryside and its permanent product included the queue, the privilege, and the lie in the report. Description is not absolution. It is the precondition of a judgement that knows what it is judging, and the judgement, when the reader forms it, will be the stronger for having the mechanism plain.

## Misreadings corrected

The description permits a compact correction of the errors that recur in popular coverage, and the article gathers them in one place. The first misreading holds that the planners attempted to compute an optimal allocation and failed at the computation. They did not attempt it; the method was incremental bargaining, and the failure, where failure is the word, belongs to a different operation. The second holds that the plan covered the economy's products. It covered a few thousand aggregated groups at the centre, on the order of magnitude the scholarship documents, and the millions of specifications beneath the aggregates were settled by ministries, contracts, and unofficial agents. The third holds that the informal economy was a marginal corruption of an otherwise functioning formality. It was a structural component, without which the formal system could not have produced at all, and its scale was that of a second circulatory system. The fourth holds that shortage was a failure of execution, a matter of plans badly drawn or officials incompetent. On Kornai's analysis, which the description supports, shortage was the system's equilibrium, generated by the soft budget constraint and reproduced by the rational behaviour of every participant. The fifth holds that the system never reformed itself, that its method was a monolith from first plan to last. The record shows three generations of intelligent reform, indicator, computational, and marketising, defeated not by stupidity but by the constitution they attempted to amend. A reader who carries the five corrections into the wider literature will find that they dissolve most of what passes for argument about Soviet planning, on every side, and leave standing the smaller, harder set of questions that the description cannot answer and the theory must.

## A note on sources and their languages

The literature on which this description rests is unusually well supplied in English, and the article's protocol requires the exceptions to be stated. The principal analytical works cited are available in English: Berliner's study of the factory and manager, Kornai's economics of shortage, translated from the Hungarian, Nove's account of the economic system, and the recalculations of the national accounts by Bergson and his successors. The archival scholarship of the decades since the system's end, by Gregory, Harrison, and others, is likewise published in English. What remains substantially untranslated is the mass of the primary administrative record in Russian, the internal documentation of Gosplan and the ministries on which the archival scholars have drawn, much of the Russian language memoir literature of the planning apparatus, and parts of the Hungarian reform literature surrounding Kornai's work. The reader confined to English is therefore well served on the analysis and partially served on the evidence, dependent on the archival scholars for the documents they have mined. That dependence is the normal condition of the subject and no bar to confidence in the description's main lines, which rest on the convergence of the interview literature, the memoir literature, and the archives, three sources with different biases agreeing on the method's essential character. Where they agree, this article has spoken plainly. Where the scholarship divides, on the tautness of plans and the scale of the second economy, the article has reported the division rather than settling it.

## Khozraschet: the enterprise's formal economics

The enterprise's formal economic constitution rested on a principle with a name of its own, khozraschet, usually translated as economic accounting or cost accounting, and the description owes it a section, because it was the point where the system's theory of the enterprise met its practice. Under khozraschet, the enterprise was supposed to operate as an accounting entity in earnest: to cover its costs from its revenues, to hold its own funds, to borrow from the bank against its plan and repay with interest, to form its incentive funds from its profits, and to bear material responsibility for its failures, including penalties payable to its customers and its bank. The principle was proclaimed from the system's early decades and reaffirmed by every reform, and its purpose was to simulate, inside the plan, the discipline that a balance sheet imposes in a market: the enterprise that wasted resources would see the waste in its own accounts and feel it in its own funds. The simulation failed at the point Kornai later formalised. The enterprise's prices were fixed, its customers captive, its plan negotiable, and its losses, when they came, absorbed by the budget through subsidies, price adjustments, and the renegotiation of its obligations, because the state that owned the enterprise was also the state that judged it, and the state's interest in the plan's fulfilment outranked its interest in the enterprise's solvency. Khozraschet therefore operated as an accounting form without its economic substance: the books were kept, the profits computed, the funds formed, and the discipline, which depends entirely on the credibility of failure, absent. The literature treats the principle's long career, proclaimed in every decade and subverted in every decade, as the enterprise level version of the reform story told earlier. A form that threatens the constitution will be preserved as a form and emptied as a practice, and khozraschet's immaculate ledgers, recording the profitability of enterprises that could not fail at prices that meant nothing, are among the method's most eloquent artefacts.

## The seller's market: the producer's sovereignty

Kornai gave the shortage economy's market form a name that completes the description's picture of daily economic relations: the seller's market. In a buyer's market, the seller courts the buyer, adapts to the buyer's specifications, competes on quality and delivery, and absorbs the risk of refusal. In the Soviet producer economy the relation was inverted. The seller, holding goods in chronic excess demand, allocated his favours among suitors; the buyer courted, expedited, bribed with reciprocity, and accepted what was offered, in the grade offered, at the time offered, because refusal meant the line stopped. The inversion explains phenomena that the aggregate description leaves as colour: the rudeness of the supply clerk and the shop assistant, functionaries of a system in which the customer's gratitude was the expected tribute; the enterprise buyer's permanent posture of petition, documented in the correspondence archives as a literature of supplication; the irrelevance of the customer's specifications, which the seller could disregard because a queue of other buyers stood behind the dissatisfied one. It also explains the economy's resistance to quality and novelty from the demand side, since a seller who cannot fail to sell has no use for either. The seller's market was not a policy. Nobody decreed it. It precipitated out of soft budgets and fixed prices wherever the two met a buyer, which was everywhere, and it persisted through every administration because its causes were the constitution itself. A reader who understands the seller's market understands why the Soviet consumer and the Soviet purchasing agent, at opposite ends of the economy's scale, lived the same day: both spent it obtaining, by favour and persistence, what a price would have obtained by being paid.

## Planning exported: the method abroad

The method's influence extended beyond the state that built it, and a brief account of its export belongs in the description, because the export tested the method's portability and returned a verdict. After the war, the states of Eastern Europe adopted the apparatus entire: planning commissions on the Gosplan model, material balances, branch ministries, the indicator system, and the bargaining cycle, staffed by officials trained in Moscow and advised by Soviet specialists. The transplantation took, in the sense that the machinery operated everywhere it was installed, and it reproduced the method's behaviours with a fidelity that amounts to a controlled replication of this article's argument: the ratchet, the storming, the tolkachi under their local names, the shortage, and the reforms, arriving in each country on a schedule offset by its date of adoption. Beyond the bloc, the method's prestige in the era of its apparent success drew the attention of the newly independent states of Asia and Africa, many of which established planning commissions and five year plans of their own. The export version usually lacked the apparatus of compulsion, the supply funds, the ministerial hierarchy, the party's parallel discipline, that made the original a constitution rather than a document, and the resulting plans were indicative frameworks over market economies, a different species that borrowed the name. The contrast is instructive for the article's central distinction. What travelled successfully was never the technique of balances and increments, which any ministry can imitate in a season. What made the Soviet method itself was the structure of entitlements and sanctions in which the technique was embedded, and that structure could be installed only by a revolution or an occupation. Planning, in short, exported as an idea everywhere and as a system only where the constitution travelled with it, which is the final confirmation that the system, and not the paperwork, is the unit of analysis.

## The plan and the city: housing and the municipal economy

Beneath the union and republican plans lay the municipal economy, the housing, heating, water, transport, and services of the cities, and in its planning the method appears at its most threadbare, because the municipal sector stood last in every queue the system formed. Housing was allocated, like producer goods, by entitlement: waiting lists administered by enterprises and local soviets, norms of space per person that defined the entitlement's size, and a stock that grew by increments planned at the margin of the industrial programme, since housing construction competed for the same materials and labour as the factories and lost the competition in every plan that had to choose. The result was the communal flat and the decades long queue, the municipal face of the shortage economy, and a distribution of life chances governed, once again, by position in the entitlement structure: the priority enterprise's workers housed from its stock, the unconnected waiting on the soviet's list, the well placed advancing through the familiar currencies of favour. Municipal services ran on the same arithmetic, their budgets residual, their tariffs fixed at levels that made their accounts fictitious, their maintenance deferred in the storming pattern of the whole economy, in which the urgent always consumed the important. The description includes the city because the citizen met the plan there, not in the ministry's documents, and the meeting's character, a life arranged around queues for space, for goods, for services, for permission, was the method's most widely distributed product. Historians of the system's legitimacy note that the social contract's other side, the security of employment, the cheapness of the basics, the predictability of the entitlement's slow advance, was also delivered through the same municipal machinery, and the citizen's judgement on the method, insofar as the record lets us hear it, weighed both sides with a precision the system's critics abroad sometimes lacked.

## Perspective plans: the future as a document

Beyond the five year framework stood the perspective plan, the long range document in which the system addressed the future at twenty years' distance, and its character completes the description's account of the plan's genres. Perspective planning produced the comprehensive programmes for the development of science and technology, the general schemes for the location of productive forces, the territorial development strategies for Siberia and the Far East, in which the method's imagination ran at its freest, unconstrained by the annual bargaining that governed everything nearer. The documents are fascinating and were almost entirely without operational force, since no enterprise's indicators, no ministry's funds, and no supply allocation ever depended on them. Their function was different: they were the regime's literature of intention, the place where the leadership's vision of the future was elaborated by its best specialists, and they served the annual process as a reservoir of sanctioned projects and phrases from which bargainers could draw authority for their claims. The description notes the genre because it corrects a misunderstanding the system's publicity encouraged abroad, where the perspective documents were read as evidence of a planning that operated at the frontier of foresight. The operational system lived in the annual cycle and thought, by the logic of the achieved level, exactly one year ahead of the recorded past. Its long documents were its dreams, filed and quoted and unbinding, and the distance between the dream and the cycle is the last measure this article offers of the distance between the planning of the imagination and the planning of the record.

## The Gosplan calendar: a year in the centre

The enterprise's year has been described from below; the centre's year, seen from inside the apparatus, closes the description's circuit. Gosplan's calendar opened with the preparation of the draft plan's control figures, drawing on the statistical record of the year in progress, the five year framework, and the leadership's directives, and it proceeded through the descent of the figures, the ascent of the drafts, the consolidation battles in which the apparatus's own departments defended their branches against one another, and the final settlement approved at the summit. The approved plan then had to be converted, in the year's first weeks, into the thousands of specific assignments, funds, and limits through which it would reach the economy, a clerical labour of enormous volume performed against the calendar's pressure, since production could not wait for its paperwork. Through the year, the apparatus monitored fulfilment through the statistical returns, convened the laggards, brokered the corrections, and managed the emergencies, the failed harvest, the broken dam, the priority project in trouble, that the year's reality supplied. And from the summer, while the current plan was still being corrected, the next cycle's preparation began, so that the apparatus lived permanently in two years at once, closing one settlement while opening the next. The portrait that emerges, from the memoirs and the archives alike, is of an institution neither omnipotent nor idle but saturated: drowning in paper, starved of the truthful information its own system denied it, and sustained by the craft knowledge of officials who knew their branches' real capacities better than any document recorded and spent their careers in the gap between the knowledge and the record. The plan was their product, and its character, prudent, incremental, negotiated, and defensive, is theirs.

## Secrecy and the plan: the classified economy

A portion of the planned economy was secret, and secrecy interacted with the method in ways the description should record. Defence production, the output of the closed cities, the reserves, and large parts of the statistical record were classified, which meant that the balances governing them circulated in restricted versions, that the published statistics omitted or disguised them, and that the planners themselves worked, outside their own compartments, from a picture of the economy with holes cut in it. The compartmentalisation compounded the information problem at the centre's summit: the leadership's general staff of planning saw the whole only through summaries prepared by the compartments' keepers, and the civilian economy's balances silently absorbed the priority sector's claims as unexplained deductions from the funds. For the historian, the secrecy has a methodological consequence that the literature has absorbed: the published record of the system is a record with its most prioritised sector redacted, so that every series, every balance, and every growth calculation built on the published figures carries a concealed margin, and the recalculations of the Western scholars were, among other things, attempts to estimate the size of the hole. The description's judgements have been framed to survive that margin: the mechanisms it describes operated identically inside and outside the classified perimeter, since the same ministries, indicators, and bargains governed both, and the military representatives' discipline, described earlier, was the perimeter's one institutional distinction.

## Scrap, waste, and the plan for secondary resources

The system's waste was so visible that the plan attempted to plan its recovery, and the attempt's fate is a small perfect parable of the method. Secondary resources, scrap metal, waste paper, used oil, industrial residues, were assigned collection plans, with targets for enterprises and for the population, collection points, and procurement prices. The ferrous scrap plan mattered genuinely to the steel balance, and the collection apparatus, extending to schoolchildren gathering waste paper under plan discipline, mobilised real quantities. Yet the recovery economy reproduced the parent economy's pathologies in miniature: collection targets met by weight with contaminated loads, scrap delivered to fulfil the plan of the collector rather than the specification of the smelter, valuable residues hoarded by enterprises against their own needs while the collection plan went unmet in the aggregate. The parable's point is the one the whole description makes. Waste in this system was not a residual oversight that better planning could sweep up. It was generated by the same incentives that generated the output, at every stage, in every material, and a plan for the waste inherited the generation mechanism along with the waste. The economy's mountains of scrap awaiting collection, in a country chronically short of metal, stand as the shortage economy's most photographable monument: abundance and dearth in the same yard, separated by nothing but the absence of a reason, legible to any participant, to move the one to the other.

## The environment: the plan's unpriced victim

The description's account of unpriced resources finds its largest instance in the environment, and a section belongs to it because the environmental record is often treated as a separate indictment when it is in fact the method's predictable output. Enterprises discharged waste into air and water whose use carried no price and whose condition entered no indicator; ministries judged on output treated protection equipment as a cost against their plans and its installation as a competitor for their investment funds; the state's own inspectorates, subordinate to the same structure that owned the polluters, enforced standards with the flexibility this article has described in quality control. The great hydraulic projects, the river diversions and the drained sea that became the era's emblems, were planned as balances of water and cotton and power in which the ecological consequences appeared, if at all, as lines in documents whose authors answered for the cotton. Nothing in this record required malice or unusual ignorance; the same decades saw environmental damage in market economies whose prices also excluded it. The difference the method made was one of recourse. Where a price system at least registers the scarcity of what it wastes, and a political system with independent courts and press gives the wasted a voice, the planned economy's victim had neither a price to rise nor a forum to appeal to, since the polluter, the planner, the inspector, and the judge were one state. The environment was the ultimate unprioritised sector, the tail of the tail, and its condition at the system's end is the description's final exhibit on what a method does to everything it cannot measure.

## The fulfilment report: December in the enterprise

The year's closing ritual deserves its place in the description, because the report was the moment when all the method's fictions and all its realities were reconciled into the figures from which the next year would be incremented. December in the enterprise was storming raised to its annual pitch: the last plan period's shortfalls hunted through the shops, output rushed across the accounting line, goods shipped to customers who had not asked for December delivery so that the dispatch could be recorded, unfinished production argued into the completed column, and the final indicators assembled for the ministry's consolidation. The report that ascended was a negotiated document like every other in the cycle, its figures defended in the ministry's corridors through January, its pripiski, where they existed, compounded into the branch totals that Gosplan would receive as the year's achieved level. On those totals, in the spring, the next year's increments would be calculated, so that the December fiction, where fiction there was, became the following year's fact, and the system's record accumulated its own momentum, each year's account resting on the audited, corrected, and partly invented account of the year before. Participants understood the ritual's double character completely. The report was simultaneously the enterprise's judgement day, on which bonuses and careers depended, and a move in the continuing negotiation, in which a skilful director reported the year he needed to have had. The method's historians treat the fulfilment statistics accordingly, as the record of a negotiation's outcome rather than a measurement of an economy, and the description closes its account of the cycle where the cycle closes: with the pen, in December, writing the numbers from which the whole apparatus would plan again.

## How this description was assembled: a note on method

The article's brief required description before argument, and its method of assembly is worth stating, since it is the guarantee of the description's neutrality. The account rests on the convergence of four literatures that rarely agree on anything else. The interview literature, founded by Berliner's work with the managers who left, supplies the behaviour from inside the enterprise. The analytical literature, Kornai above all, supplies the mechanism that explains the behaviour's aggregate pattern. The archival literature, opened by the system's end, supplies the documents against which both can be checked, and has confirmed the interview record's essentials to a degree that surprised the archivists themselves. The statistical literature, Bergson and his successors, supplies the discipline on figures, the insistence that the system's numbers are constructions requiring recalculation before use. Where the four converge, as they converge on the incremental method, the ratchet, the indicators, and the shortage mechanism, the description speaks without qualification. Where they divide, on tautness and on the second economy's scale, the description reports the division. The reader who wishes to test the account should test it at the convergences, since a description independently reached by the participants, the analysts, the archives, and the accountants is as close to a settled record as the study of a vanished economy can come.

## Norms and piece rates: the plan inside the workshop

Within the enterprise, the plan descended to the level of the individual worker through the system of norms, and the workshop's microcosm reproduced every mechanism of the macrocosm. Output norms, the quantity a worker was expected to produce in a shift, were set by norm setters from time studies and technical handbooks, and earnings above the norm were paid at piece rates, so that the worker's income, like the director's bonus, hung on a measured performance against a negotiated standard. The negotiation was continuous and bilateral. Workers learned to pace themselves during time studies, to conceal the shortcuts and fixtures that raised their true productivity, and to resist the revision of norms that followed any visible improvement, because the ratchet operated at the bench exactly as it operated at the ministry: a norm once exceeded was a norm about to be raised. Norm setters, for their part, worked under pressure from both directions, from management demanding norms that would discipline the wage fund and from the shop floor whose cooperation the whole system required. The literature on the Soviet workshop, built on the same interview tradition as Berliner's, describes an equilibrium of mutual accommodation in which norms settled at levels the experienced worker could exceed comfortably in the storming weeks and meet without heroism in the ordinary ones, and in which the formal apparatus of scientific norming, the handbooks, the coefficients, the time study engineers, clothed a bargain as thoroughly negotiated as the plan itself. The Stakhanovite movement of the nineteen thirties, the celebrated campaign of record breaking workers, is best understood in this frame as the state's attempt to break the equilibrium by political means, using the records of the few to justify the revision of the norms of the many. The attempt succeeded episodically and was absorbed structurally: the records were achieved with prepared conditions and support crews whose existence the publicity omitted, the norm revisions provoked the shop floor's defensive ingenuity, and the movement subsided into the calendar of campaigns. The bench, like the ministry, had learned the system's first law, that achievement is evidence, and evidence is used against the achiever.

## The collective farm market: the legal island of price

One genuine price mechanism operated legally inside the system throughout its life, and its behaviour is a natural experiment the description should not omit. The collective farm market, the urban bazaar where collective farms and individual households sold the produce of their plots and their permitted surpluses, traded at prices formed by supply and demand, unregulated in their level though regulated in their place and their participants. The market's prices stood persistently above the state shops' fixed prices for the same goods, and the gap between the two price levels, for food of comparable quality, was the system's own published measure of its consumer shortage, quoted in the literature for decades as the repressed inflation's thermometer. The state tolerated the island because it performed functions the plan could not: it fed the cities a significant share of their fresh produce, it gave the peasantry a money income that reconciled it, partially, to the procurement system, and it absorbed demand that would otherwise have pressed on the state network's empty shelves. Its existence also disciplined the description's generalisations in a useful way. Where a price was permitted to form, goods appeared, assortment responded to the buyers, and sellers courted purchasers, in the same country, in the same years, under the same state that operated the shortage economy a street away. The contrast was not lost on the population, whose judgements about the two systems were formed at the bazaar and the state shop counter with an empiricism no propaganda could reach. For the analyst, the island's lesson is precise: the shortage was not a property of the country's resources or its people's capacities. It was a property of the mechanism, and it stopped, visibly, at the mechanism's border.

## Mass housing: the method's consumer triumph and its terms

The description has recorded the method's failures in the consumer economy at length, and fairness requires its largest consumer success to be stated with its terms. In the decades after the war, and above all under Khrushchev's housing programme, the state built urban housing on a scale that transformed the population's living conditions, moving tens of millions from communal flats and barracks into separate family apartments. The programme was the mobilisation method applied to a consumer good: a small number of standardised designs, prefabricated components produced by a dedicated industry, quantity indicators in square metres commissioned, and the whole driven by priority and campaign. Its achievements were real and are remembered by the generations it housed as the system's most tangible gift. Its terms were the method's signature. The standardisation that made the quantity possible made the assortment minimal; the indicators rewarded floor space delivered, and the buildings' finish, sound insulation, and fittings settled at the level the indicators ignored; the maintenance of the stock, unprioritised and unmeasured, decayed from the day of commissioning. The housing programme is therefore the consumer economy's clearest verdict on the article's central question. The method could deliver a simple, standardised, desperately needed good in enormous quantity when the leadership made it a priority, and it delivered that good in exactly the quality and variety that its indicators specified, which is to say, in the quality and variety of a mobilisation, not of a housing market. Judged as mobilisation, it was a triumph. Judged as the permanent organisation of a society's shelter, it bequeathed a stock whose uniformity and decay became the next generation's complaint, and both judgements are true of the same buildings.

## The tempo question: growth as a political number

The plan's growth rates, the famous tempos, occupied a place in the system's politics that the description should make explicit, because they explain the pressure under which the whole bargaining structure laboured. The tempo was never merely an economic magnitude. It was the regime's claim to historical legitimacy stated as a number: the rate at which the socialist economy would overtake the capitalist world, printed in the plan documents, audited in the fulfilment reports, and defended by the statistical apparatus whose constructions this article has described. A number with that burden attracts the system's characteristic treatment. Targets for the tempo were set by the leadership's political requirements and defended against the apparatus's doubts; fulfilment was reported through the channels whose interest in the figure has been detailed; and the recalculating scholars abroad, rebuilding the accounts on consistent prices and definitions, persistently found lower rates than the official ones, not because the growth was invented, growth on a large scale plainly occurred, but because the official constructions, the material product concept, the fixed price weights of a young industrial structure, the concealed margins, systematically flattered it. The description's discipline on this point has been stated and is repeated here because the tempo is where readers most often demand the figures the protocol forbids: the article gives no growth rates, official or recalculated, because the honest statement is about the constructions, not the digits. What can be said without digits is the pattern the constructions agree on: very rapid growth in the mobilisation decades from a low base, decelerating growth as the extensive reserves thinned, and a late period in which the argument among scholars concerns the distance between slow growth and stagnation. The tempo's political career ended when the number could no longer be defended in any construction, and the leadership that inherited that moment inherited also the reform problem this article has described, with the additional burden that the system's legitimacy had been mortgaged to the figure that had failed.

## The Ministry of Finance: the veto that audited

The financial organs have appeared in this description as bookkeepers, and their real power deserves a corrective paragraph, because the Ministry of Finance and the state bank together constituted the system's one systematic check on the bargaining's exuberance. Every plan had its financial shadow: the state budget, which collected the turnover tax and the enterprises' profit deductions and disbursed the investment funds and subsidies; the credit plan, which governed the bank's lending to enterprises against their plans; and the cash plan, which regulated the emission of currency against the wage funds and the retail network's expected takings. The financial organs audited the physical plan's consistency in money terms, and their objections, that a ministry's programme exceeded its financing or an enterprise's wage fund its labour plan, carried a veto's weight in the settlement, since a plan adopted without its financing was a plan adopted in fiction. The check was real and its limits were the system's. The financial organs could enforce consistency; they could not enforce value, because the prices in which consistency was expressed were the accounting prices this article has described, and a programme could be perfectly financed and perfectly worthless in the same ledger. The bank's inspectors, verifying payments against contracts and deliveries across the whole economy, constituted the nearest thing the system possessed to an independent information network, and the leadership drew on their reports as a cross check on the ministries' returns. Even that network reported transactions, not truths: it could establish that a delivery had been paid for, not that the goods were as specified, wanted by their recipient, or worth their price. The financial veto thus perfected the system's form while leaving its substance untouched, and the immaculate budgets of the late Soviet state, balanced in the accounts while the economy stagnated beneath them, are the fitting emblem of a control that controlled everything except the thing that mattered.

## Culture under indicators: the plan reaches the arts

The method's reach extended to cultural production, and a short section on it completes the survey of the plan's empire, because the arts display the indicator logic in its most exposed form. Publishing was planned: publishing houses received thematic plans and paper allocations, paper being a funded commodity whose distribution among publishers, journals, and the press was a balance like any other, so that the material possibility of a book began as a line in a paper fund. Print runs, titles, and sheets were indicators; the house that fulfilled them with safe, standard, reprinted material stood better than the house that gambled its paper on the new. Theatres, film studios, and concert organisations worked to repertoire plans and attendance indicators, with the predictable migration toward the measurable: the proven repertoire, the spectacle that filled the hall, the production whose fulfilment could be reported. None of this required the censor's office, though the censor existed and the series treats the ideological apparatus elsewhere; the planning of culture would have produced its characteristic profile, cautious, standardised, weighted to the classics and the approved, through the paper fund and the indicator alone. The description includes the arts for the sake of the general law they illustrate with unusual clarity. A method that allocates by entitlement and judges by proxy will govern everything it touches in the same way, and the distance between a nail and a novel, in the plan's arithmetic, was only the distance between two lines in two balances, each with its indicator, each with its ratchet, each with its quiet migration of effort from the thing to the measure of the thing.

## The plan's geography of supply: the northern delivery

One specialised mechanism deserves mention as the supply system's extreme case, the northern delivery, the annual sealift and riverlift that provisioned the Arctic and Far Eastern settlements whose existence the state's strategy required and whose climate permitted resupply only in a short season. The operation concentrated every feature of the method into a single logistical drama: the balances for a territory's whole year of fuel, food, and equipment assembled in a few months; the priorities absolute, since a missed cargo was a winter without the good; the storming imposed by ice rather than by the calendar; and the consequences of the assortment failure, the wrong specification delivered to a settlement a thousand miles from the nearest alternative, measured not in a stopped line but in a community's survival margin. The northern delivery usually succeeded, and its success is part of the record: where the task was bounded, the goods standard, the deadline absolute, and the priority unquestioned, the mobilisation method performed at its best, as it had in war. The description sets the case at the end of its survey deliberately. The method's admirers and its critics both reach for the extreme instances, the triumphs of mobilisation and the catastrophes of assortment, and the truth the survey supports is that the extremes are the same method at different distances from its home ground. Bounded, physical, prioritised, and temporary: those are the conditions under which planning from the achieved level, balances, and bargaining delivered what it promised. The tragedy of the system's ambitions is that it applied the method where none of the four conditions held, to the whole of a modern economy, permanently, and called the application socialism.

## The invisible sector: services outside the material product

The national accounting concept on which the system planned, the net material product, counted only material production and the services that directly served it, freight and trade margins among them, and excluded the rest of the service economy from the product altogether: health, education, passenger transport, housing services, personal services, and the state's own administration produced, in the accounts, nothing. The exclusion was doctrinal in origin, descending from a reading of Marx in which only material labour is productive, and its consequences were practical and severe, because what the accounts exclude the plan undervalues. The service sector planned as a cost rather than a product received the residual of every allocation, its investment deferred, its wages set at the bottom of the scales, its staffing treated as a burden on the productive economy rather than a part of it. The citizen experienced the doctrine as the thinness of the service world: the understaffed clinic, the absent repair trade, the restaurant deficit, the queue for the hairdresser in the economy that could build a steel mill. The description notes the mechanism because it is the accounting version of the article's general law. The plan could not value what its categories did not count, and it could not count what its theory did not recognise, so that a doctrine about the nature of productive labour, debated in the language of political economy, arrived in the citizen's life as the impossibility of getting a tap repaired. Ideas have logistics. In a planned economy, the logistics are literal, and a category error in the national accounts becomes, by the ordinary operation of the balances, a shortage in the street.

## The trade unions: administrators, not bargainers

The trade unions of the system occupied a position that puzzles readers formed by market economies, and the description should place them, since they formed part of the enterprise's governing structure. The unions did not bargain over wages in the adversarial sense; wages were set by the plan's tariff scales and the enterprise's wage fund, and the union's role in the enterprise was administrative and welfarist: it managed the social insurance funds, distributed the holiday vouchers and the sanatorium places, supervised the canteens and the safety rules, and sat in the collective agreement that each enterprise concluded annually with its management, an agreement whose terms largely restated the plan's provisions and the labour law's guarantees. The union chairman was a figure of the enterprise's establishment, a channel for grievances that stopped short of the plan itself, and a distributor of the social wage's benefits, which gave the office its real influence over workers' lives. For the planning method, the unions' significance is that they closed the last gap in the structure of consent. The worker's relationship to the plan was mediated not by a representative who might contest it but by an administrator who implemented it, and the discontents the system generated, over norms, over bonuses, over the distribution of flats and vouchers, were processed inside the enterprise's own institutions, by the people who operated them. The description does not need to judge the arrangement to complete its account of the mechanism; it needs only to note that a system which allocates everything will eventually allocate representation itself, and that the plan's empire, surveyed from the steel balance to the holiday voucher, left no institution outside its arithmetic from which the arithmetic could be appealed.

## The memoir record: what the participants said when they could speak

A final source deserves its own paragraph, because it has grown since the system's end into a literature of its own: the memoirs and oral histories of the participants, the directors, planners, supply agents, and officials who described, in retirement and often abroad, the method they had operated. The memoir record's value is its texture. The interviews Berliner conducted in the nineteen fifties gave the behaviour in outline; the memoirs give it in grain, the particular negotiation remembered across forty years, the ministerial corridor, the telephone call that released the wagons, the December night when the report was made to balance. Their testimony converges with the earlier record on every structural point, and adds one emphasis the documents understate: the pride. The men who operated the method, whatever their verdict on the system it served, remembered their own practice with the craftsman's satisfaction, the plan fulfilled against the supply system's chaos, the plant kept running by ingenuity and connection, and the memoir literature is full of a nostalgia that is not for the ideology but for the competence, for a world in which their difficult, useless, indispensable skills were the difference between production and collapse. The description ends its survey with their voice because it corrects the last possible misreading of this article: that the system's participants were its fools. They were its masters, in the only sense the system allowed mastery, and the method's final cost accounting must include what it did to their abilities, training a continent's worth of organisational genius on the arts of obtaining, hoarding, and reporting, and leaving it, when the system ended, with skills the new world priced at nothing and habits it priced, in places, at a great deal.

## The argument restated: the chain in one view

The article's sections have followed the method piece by piece, and a restatement of the whole chain, in the order of its causation, is the proper close to the analytical survey. A state that owns the means of production and forbids their exchange deprives itself of prices for them, and therefore of any criterion by which allocations can be compared. Deprived of a criterion, its planning agency does the one thing that requires none: it repeats the inherited pattern of production at a negotiated rate of growth, planning from the achieved level, coordinating a few thousand aggregates by physical balances. The repetition makes the report the system's foundation, and the report is supplied by men whose rewards depend on its content, so the foundation is strategic: capacities concealed, requirements inflated, achievements rationed against the ratchet. The concealment guarantees that entitlements and needs will mismatch in specification, and the mismatch summons the unofficial network that performs the matching the plan cannot, at the price of making the real economy invisible to its governors. The state's guarantee of its enterprises' survival softens every budget, and the softness makes demand limitless, and limitless demand at fixed prices is chronic shortage, which teaches every participant to hoard, and the hoarding deepens the shortage the hoarding insures against. Each link in the chain is a rational response to the link before it, and the chain has no link at which exhortation, competence, or computing power can break it, because the break would have to come at the first link, the criterion, and the criterion is the one thing the constitution excludes. That is how Soviet planning actually worked, and why it worked that way, and the rest of the argument about planning, in this series and elsewhere, is commentary on the chain.

## The plan and the harvest of information: why the centre kept asking

One habit of the apparatus, visible in every archive, deserves a final analytical note: the centre's insatiable demand for reports. Enterprises filed returns on fulfilment by the decade, the month, the quarter, in hundreds of forms, to the statistical administration, the ministry, the bank, the party organs, and the supply organisations, and the volume of paper the economy generated about itself exceeded, by any estimate the literature offers, the reporting burden of comparable market economies many times over. The paradox is the description's last illustration of the information problem. A market economy coordinates through prices, which are information compressed to the point of invisibility; the planned economy, having abolished the compression, had to substitute volume, and the volume defeated the purpose, since no staff could read what the economy filed, and the summaries that ascended were prepared by the interested at every stage. The centre's response to its blindness was always more reporting, new forms, new indicators, new inspections, each addition increasing the burden on the enterprises and the gaming surface available to them, since every new return was a new artefact to be managed. The archives preserve the result in physical form: the planning of a continent documented in a paper trail that no historian will ever read entire, the most completely recorded economy in history and among the least known to its own governors. Information, the case demonstrates, is not a quantity that accumulates with collection. It is a relation between a signal and a reason to send it truly, and where the reason is absent, the collection is theatre, performed in triplicate.

## The endurance of the method: why nothing replaced it

The description has explained the method's defects so thoroughly that its endurance requires an explanation of its own, and the explanation completes the article. The method lasted, with its essentials unchanged, for six decades, through the deaths of its founders, the discrediting of its terror, the exposure of its statistics, and the repeated demonstration of its failures, because it served, with great effectiveness, the purposes of the people positioned to replace it. It gave the political leadership a legible instrument of command over a continent's resources, and the experience, renewed annually, of directing an economy by signature. It gave the ministerial apparatus a medium in which its authority was indispensable and its failures attributable to the bargaining's other parties. It gave the enterprise's managers a game they had mastered, with rewards they understood and risks they could manage, preferable in every particular to the unknown disciplines of any alternative. And it gave the population, in its mature form, a settlement of low pressure and predictability, employment guaranteed, the basics cheap, the entitlement's queue advancing slowly but visibly, which the majority, whatever its private verdict on the system's claims, experienced as a life that could be planned around, an irony the record supports. A method survives not by working in the abstract but by working for its operators, and the Soviet method worked for everyone who had the power to end it, at a price paid by everyone who did not: the consumer in the queue, the innovator at the ministry's door, the future in the unreformed accounts. When it ended, it ended not because a better method outcompeted it in argument but because the leadership that inherited it lost faith in the settlement and reached, clumsily and too late, for another. The description's neutrality has been maintained throughout at some effort, and it permits itself, at the very end, the observation the evidence supports: the system's most remarkable product was its own stability, and the stability was the problem, since everything else the economy needed required the method to change, and the method's one perfected function was preventing exactly that.

## The plan's shadow: what the description means for the word planning itself

A closing reflection on vocabulary is warranted, because the description has implications for the word at its centre. Planning, in ordinary language, names a family of activities that every complex organisation performs: the firm planning its production, the city planning its transport, the household planning its budget. The Soviet case took the word for something categorically different, the replacement of the price mechanism across an entire economy by administrative allocation, and the controversy's confusion owes much to the slippage between the two senses. Defenders of the Soviet method pointed to the ubiquity of planning inside capitalist firms, some of them larger than small economies, as proof that the administrative coordination of production was the modern world's direction. The description supplies the distinction the argument needs. The firm plans inside a price system that surrounds it: it buys and sells at prices it does not set, faces customers who can refuse it, and dies if its internal allocations persistently waste what the prices value, so that its planning is disciplined at every boundary by the mechanism it has internally suspended. The Soviet method suspended the mechanism at every boundary at once, and the description of what followed, the bargaining, the ratchet, the shortage, is the record of an economy trying to replace the discipline rather than merely relocate it. The word planning therefore covers two experiments that history has run separately, and the article's subject is only the second. A reader who keeps the distinction will find that much of the twentieth century's loudest economic argument dissolves into the two sides using one word for two things, and that the residue, the question of how far the boundary of administrative allocation can be pushed before the criterion fails, is the genuine question the series takes up in its articles on the calculation debate and on the comparison between planning and the market.

## The reader's test: applying the description

The article ends its survey with a test the reader can apply to any account of a planned economy encountered elsewhere, since the description's value lies in its use. Ask of the account three questions. Does it say what operation the planners performed, incrementing or computing, and does it know the difference? Does it follow the information, asking who reported each figure and what the reporter gained by its shape? Does it treat shortage, hoarding, and the unofficial network as the system's products or as accidents that befell it? An account that answers the three questions as this one has will be found, on inspection, to be describing the same economy, whatever its author's politics. An account that cannot answer them is describing the debate about planning, which is a different and much smaller subject. The series proceeds from here to the debate itself, in the articles on the calculation controversy and on the comparison with markets, and it proceeds with the description in hand, which is where the brief for this article always intended the reader to arrive: knowing what the thing was before being asked, once more, whether it was a good idea.

One further discipline follows from the test, and it applies to the reader's own judgements as much as to other writers' accounts. Whenever a claim about the system is encountered, in a textbook, a speech, or an argument about the present, the claim should be traced to the level of the mechanism: which stage of the cycle produced the phenomenon, which indicator rewarded it, which budget absorbed its cost, which agent's network carried it. Claims that survive the tracing are knowledge. Claims that dissolve under it were never about the Soviet economy at all, but about the hopes and fears its name still carries, and the reader who can tell the two apart has received from this article the only equipment it ever promised to give.

The ratchet effect rewards a full session on its own, since students grasp it instantly and then find it everywhere, and the success indicator catalogue, the nail, the veneer, the shrinking unit, makes the general law of proxy measurement unforgettable. Pair the description with the behavioural analysis in [the article on the incentive problem in socialism](/2019/11/01/incentive-problem-socialism/), and close by asking the seminar to design the indicator set that would have defeated the gaming, a task whose failure, honestly attempted, teaches more about administration than a term of theory.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this subject lies in the archives, and the description indicates where to dig: the distance between the plan as approved and the plan as it finally stood, measurable in the correction files; the pripiski margin between reported and audited output; and the supply system's records, where the economy's real matching was transacted. Frame questions against the theoretical literature rather than inside it: the objection in its original form is treated in [the socialist calculation debate](/2019/08/15/socialist-calculation-debate/), the general question in [whether planning can beat the market](/2019/10/15/can-planning-beat-the-market/), the outcomes in [the economic record of the socialist states](/2019/06/15/socialist-states-economic-record/), and the endpoint in [Marxist explanations of the Soviet collapse](/2019/05/01/soviet-collapse-marxist-explanations/). Journalists should note the discipline this article observes on figures: output, growth, and shortage numbers from the Soviet statistical record are constructions on a foreign accounting concept, and any figure carried into print needs its recalculation named. The untranslated Russian administrative record remains the field's largest unworked seam.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How did Soviet planning actually work?

It worked as an annual bargaining cycle over physical quantities. Gosplan drafted control figures from the five year framework, ministries disaggregated them, enterprises replied with draft plans that understated capacity and overstated needs, and the consolidated figures were revised, approved, and issued as binding targets with supply allocations attached. The coordinating instrument was the material balance, and the targets themselves were set by incrementing the output already achieved. Through the year the plan was corrected continuously, while expediters working outside the plan's own channels fetched what the allocations had failed to deliver. The system genuinely coordinated the economy's commanding heights, and it did so without ever computing an allocation or consulting a price.

### Q: What was Gosplan?

Gosplan was the State Planning Commission, established in 1921, the central agency charged with drawing up the plans for the national economy. It produced the five year frameworks and the annual plans, constructed the material balances for the commodity groups within its remit, and settled, with the political leadership, the disputes the bargaining generated. It was never the whole apparatus: supply allocation belonged to Gossnab, money and prices to the finance and price organs, and the branch ministries stood between Gosplan and the enterprises. Gosplan's own staff worked from aggregated quantities and reported figures, which is why its picture of the economy was always a picture of claims about the economy, filtered through the interests of everyone who had filed them.

### Q: What are material balances?

Material balances were the planning method's coordinating instrument: two sided tables for a single commodity group, listing the sources of the quantity, production, imports, and stocks, against its uses, deliveries to consuming branches, exports, and reserves, all in physical units. Planners adjusted targets and allocations through successive rounds until the two sides matched, and since each commodity appeared in many balances at once, every adjustment propagated through the web. A closed balance proved that a pattern of flows was physically consistent. It could not prove the pattern was desirable, because the tables contained no measure of value or scarcity, and their aggregation concealed the specification mismatches that dominated the economy's daily life below the level the balances reached.

### Q: Did planners try to compute an optimum?

The operating method never did. Plans were produced by iterating the balances until they closed and by incrementing achieved output, not by computing an optimum for the whole economy. A circle of mathematical economists, Kantorovich at their head, built the techniques for computed planning, won the system's highest honours, and saw its methods applied in bounded problems such as transport scheduling and materials cutting. Its comprehensive programme, including the proposal for a national computer network to manage planning, was studied for decades and never adopted, because a computed criterion of efficiency would have displaced the bargaining through which the apparatus exercised its authority. Optimisation was the system's honoured research programme and its excluded practice.

### Q: What is the ratchet effect?

The ratchet effect is the mechanism by which success under incremental planning punishes the successful. Because each year's target was built on what the enterprise had achieved the year before, an enterprise that overfulfilled its plan by a wide margin raised the base from which all its future targets would be calculated, and revealed to its ministry a capacity the ministry then planned against. Managers learned to fulfil the plan by a modest margin, to restrain achievement in good years, and to absorb improvements invisibly rather than let them reset the base. The effect throttled the whole economy's growth to the pace its managers judged safe, and it appears wherever future obligations are set from recorded performance.

### Q: How did success indicators distort output?

Every measure paid for one property of output and penalised the others, and production migrated to the paid property. A weight target made goods heavier; the literature's emblem is a nail plant that met its tonnage with one enormous nail. A value target, reckoned at administrative prices, pulled assortment toward costly materials. A count of pieces made articles smaller and flimsier. Assortment obligations were met by the easiest variant in every line, and quality, which no indicator captured, fell to the floor of the standard everywhere the buyer lacked the power to refuse. Multiplying the indicators only changed which one would be sacrificed. The bending was the measurement system working exactly as built rather than breaking down, repeated across every branch for decades.

### Q: Who were the unofficial supply agents?

They were the expediters known in Russian as tolkachi, the pushers, employed by enterprises to obtain the inputs the plan had allocated on paper and failed to deliver in fact. Working through personal networks among suppliers, supply officials, transport clerks, and warehouse keepers, an agent advanced deliveries, interpreted allocations generously, arranged barter of surplus materials between enterprises, and called in the reciprocal favours that were the network's currency. The activity was semi legal, periodically denounced, and structurally indispensable, since the aggregated plan could not match particular supply to particular need and someone had to perform the matching. Berliner's managers described the agent as essential to the enterprise, and later research confirmed the description across the system's life.

### Q: What is a soft budget constraint?

A soft budget constraint, in János Kornai's term, is the financial condition of an enterprise whose survival does not depend on its earnings. The Soviet type enterprise was state owned, its losses covered by subsidy, credit, or price adjustment, its shutting a step the state essentially never took, and its managers knew from experience that overspending would be regularised rather than punished by extinction. The softness transformed the enterprise's behaviour: inputs could be over ordered as free insurance, investment demanded without internal limit, and hoarding practised without financial risk, because none of these carried the penalty that a hard budget imposes. Kornai built his analysis of the whole system on this single property, deriving from it the unrestrained demand that made shortage the economy's normal condition.

### Q: Why was there chronic shortage?

Because the system's demand was unrestrained by design. Enterprises facing soft budget constraints ordered inputs beyond their needs, hoarded materials and labour against unreliable supply, and demanded investment continuously, so that aggregate demand pressed permanently against supply at the fixed prices the state maintained. Every relief of a particular shortage displaced it elsewhere, since the behaviour generating it was untouched, and hoarding converted each shortage into the cause of the next, as one enterprise's insurance stock became another's missing input. Kornai called shortage the system's equilibrium, the condition it returned to after every disturbance, and the description bears him out: shortage persisted through good harvests and bad, through taut plans and slack ones, because its source was the incentive structure and not the circumstances.

### Q: What reforms to planning were attempted?

Three generations of reform attacked three different elements. The Kosygin reform of the mid nineteen sixties attacked the indicators, replacing gross output with sales and profit as the judged measures, and was absorbed as ministries recentralised and fixed prices made profit meaningless. The cybernetic programme, Kantorovich's optimal planning school and Glushkov's proposed national computer network, attacked the arithmetic, and was refused because computation would have displaced the bargaining that constituted the apparatus's power. The Hungarian New Economic Mechanism of 1968 attacked the framework, scrapping compulsory targets for most enterprises and letting many prices move, and went furthest because it alone reduced the plan's territory. A final Soviet attempt in the late nineteen eighties replaced the plan with state orders, which the ministries promptly set at the whole of capacity.

### Q: How does planning from the achieved level grade the calculation debate?

It grades it obliquely, against both camps. The defence had described planning as rational allocation, and the objection had answered that such allocation was impossible without prices; the record shows an agency that performed neither the allocation nor the impossibility, but a third operation neither side had described, the incrementing of inherited output through bargaining. The objection's prediction that the planner would lack a rational criterion is vindicated by the method actually used, while its cruder corollary, that nothing coherent could be organised without prices, is refuted by the mobilisation the method achieved. A student should therefore cite the Soviet case as evidence about what planners do when they cannot calculate, not as a verdict on calculation itself.

### Q: What should a student separate between computing an allocation and bargaining over increments?

The student should separate the question each operation answers. Computing an allocation answers the question of what the economy ought to produce, and it requires a criterion, prices or their computed equivalent, by which patterns can be compared. Bargaining over increments answers the question of how much more of the existing pattern each producer can be induced to deliver, and it requires only last year's recorded output, a decision about how much faster to go, and a negotiation. The Soviet system performed the second operation while its rhetoric claimed the first, and most confused writing on the subject comes from grading the second operation by the first operation's standards, praising it as allocation or condemning it as failed computation.

### Q: Why did managers have an interest in understating their capacity?

Because the plan they would be judged against was built substantially from their own testimony. A director who reported his plant's capacity honestly received a target at the edge of that capacity, with no margin for the supply failures the system guaranteed, while a director who understated received an achievable target, bonuses for fulfilling it, and reserves for emergencies. The understatement was compounded by the ratchet effect, since any capacity revealed through overfulfilment became the base of future targets. Honesty in the draft plan was therefore a donation of the enterprise's safety margin to the ministry, and Berliner's managers, who understood their position exactly, declined to make the donation.

### Q: How does the plan cycle table order the distortions?

The table orders them in the sequence in which they compound. At the control figure stage, targets are tied to what was achieved before rather than to any assessment of need. At disaggregation, ministries convert branch totals into entitlements. At the enterprise draft, capacity is understated and requirements overstated, poisoning the information on which every later stage depends. At consolidation, weak claims are bundled with strong ones as bargaining stock. At approval, settlements certify feasibility at the aggregate level only. At allocation, entitlements mismatch specifications, and in production the assortment bends toward the judged indicator while the plan is corrected around the damage. Read down the column, each distortion is the raw material of the next, which is why no reform that addressed a single stage ever held.

### Q: What separates a shortage caused by prices fixed too low from a shortage caused by scarcity itself?

The test is the counterfactual of the price. Where a price fixed below the clearing level causes the shortage, raising the price would empty the queue by reducing demand to the quantity that can be produced, and the goods exist to be rationed by money instead of by time. Where scarcity itself causes the shortage, the resources to produce more do not exist whatever price is named, and raising the price changes only who goes without. The Soviet economy exhibited both, layered with a third condition the distinction exists to reveal: the systemic excess demand of soft budgeted enterprises, which no individual price could cure because the demanders' spending was limited by nothing.
