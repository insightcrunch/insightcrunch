A candidate who has read four passages of Capital properly will outscore a candidate who has read the whole book and cites it vaguely. That is not a consolation for the unprepared; it is a statement about what assessments in this area actually reward, which is located evidence deployed against a specific question, not coverage. Markers cannot give credit for reading. They give credit for a definition stated with its qualifying clause intact, a mechanism run correctly, a passage named precisely enough that they can find it, and an evaluation that identifies what a critic was targeting.

This guide sets out the four passages, the mechanism each one unlocks, the answer structures that work across the assessment formats where this material appears, the evaluative moves that lift a script into the top band, and the two errors that cap a script no matter how much else is right. It also solves a problem no revision resource addresses: chapter numbers in Capital differ between the German and the English editions, so a candidate who cites a chapter number may be pointing a marker at a different passage from the one they mean.

The content itself is not the business of this article. What the book argues, how the argument is built, and how to work through the text belong to [the complete guide to Capital Volume One](/2010/06/01/capital-volume-one-complete-guide/) and to [how to read Capital Volume One](/2010/06/15/how-to-read-capital-volume-one/), which own those questions. What follows is the assessment layer: how the material is examined, what an answer must contain, and how to build one under time pressure.

## How this material is actually examined

Capital and Marx's economic thought appear across a wider range of assessments than most candidates realise, and the form of the question changes what a good answer looks like.

Civil service optional papers in political science and international relations, and in sociology, examine Marx's economic thought as part of the theory component, usually through questions that ask a candidate to explain a concept and then evaluate it, with the marks split between the two. Answers are written under severe time pressure, the expected length is short relative to the conceptual load, and precision is worth more than range.

National eligibility and qualifying examinations for research and lectureship, across political science, sociology, philosophy, and economics, test the same material in shorter formats, including objective and short-answer questions where a single precise definition is the whole answer. Here the surplus value formula, the definition of socially necessary labour time, and the labour and labour power distinction are the highest-yield items, because they are exactly the kind of content that a short question can test unambiguously.

Undergraduate courses in political economy, sociological theory, and the history of economic thought examine through essays and seen or unseen papers, with more room for textual engagement and a correspondingly higher expectation that a candidate can locate an argument rather than summarise a position.

School-level sociology and economics specifications include the labour theory of value and Marx's account of class as named theories to be explained and evaluated, usually with an explicit assessment objective for evaluation that carries a fixed share of the marks. Candidates at this level lose more marks to missing evaluation than to weak explanation.

General studies and general knowledge papers ask for a short definitional answer, where the entire task is to state the concept accurately in two or three sentences without wandering into biography.

The one constant across all of these is that describing the book earns nothing. The question is always about a concept, a mechanism, or a judgement, and the book is evidence rather than subject matter.

### What do examiners reward in answers on Capital?

A definition with its qualifying clause intact, a mechanism run in the correct order, a located textual anchor, and an evaluation that names a critic and specifies what that critic was targeting. Narrating the book's contents earns nothing, however accurate, because it answers no question.

## The four-passage economy

The claim this guide advances is the four-passage economy: a candidate who can use four passages precisely will outscore one who has read the book and cites it vaguely, because assessments reward located evidence rather than coverage.

The four are the first section of Chapter One, the fourth section of Chapter One, the chapter on the sale and purchase of labour power, and the chapter on the general law of capitalist accumulation. Between them they unlock value, fetishism, exploitation, and the dynamics of accumulation, which is the whole of what any assessment at any level asks about this book. Everything else in the eight hundred pages is either elaboration, historical illustration, or polemic against writers no marker will ask about.

The economy in the name is deliberate. Reading four passages carefully takes an evening. Reading the book takes a term. The candidate who has done the evening and can deploy what they read will produce a better script than the candidate who has done the term and produces a summary, because the marks are attached to precision and location rather than to duration of exposure.

Two warnings attach to this claim and both matter. The first is that four passages read carelessly are worse than nothing, because a misremembered definition is a positive error rather than an absence. The second is that the four-passage strategy is a floor rather than a ceiling: a candidate aiming at the top band in an undergraduate or postgraduate context should read more, and the strategy exists to make sure that a candidate who cannot read more still has located evidence rather than vague gestures.

## Passage one: value and socially necessary labour time

The first section of the first chapter establishes the commodity as the unit of analysis, distinguishes use value from exchange value, and arrives at value as the common element that makes commodities commensurable, identified as human labour in the abstract and measured by labour time.

The critical sentence for examination purposes is the qualification. Labour time does not mean the time an individual producer happened to take; it means the labour time socially necessary under the prevailing conditions of production and with the average degree of skill and intensity. A lazy or badly equipped producer does not create more value by taking longer.

This qualification is where most of the available marks sit, because it is the clause that separates a candidate who understands the theory from one who has memorised a slogan. An answer that says value is determined by the labour that went into a commodity has stated the version every critic refutes. An answer that says value is determined by socially necessary labour time and then explains what socially necessary excludes has stated the theory Marx actually holds, and has simultaneously disposed of the most common objection before it is raised.

The passage also gives the candidate the use value and exchange value distinction, which is required for any question about the paradox of value or about why usefulness does not determine price. The precise terminology, including the third term that the pair is defined against, is set out in [use value, exchange value, and value](/2010/07/15/use-value-exchange-value-and-value/), and candidates who confuse exchange value with value are making an error that a well-prepared marker will see immediately.

## Passage two: the fetishism of the commodity

The fourth section of the first chapter is the passage most frequently misused in examination answers and the one that most reliably distinguishes a strong script when it is used correctly.

The argument is that in a society where production is private and dispersed, the social relations between producers appear to them as relations between things. The value of a commodity looks like a property the object possesses, in the way that weight is a property, when it is in fact a relation between the producer's labour and the total labour of society. The producers relate to one another through the movement of prices, which means their social relation appears in the form of a relation between products.

The misuse is treating fetishism as a claim about consumerism, brand worship, or attachment to possessions. It is not. Marx's argument is about how a social relation is perceived, not about how much people love their objects, and a candidate who writes about advertising and designer labels has answered a different question and will be marked accordingly. The concept and its correct boundaries are set out in [commodity fetishism explained](/2010/08/01/commodity-fetishism-explained/).

The reason this passage is worth an evening is that it is where the sociology and philosophy questions live. Questions about ideology, about false appearances, about the relation between Marx's economics and his social theory, and about the connection between alienation and the mature work all run through this section, and a candidate who can state the argument in three sentences has an entry point to all of them.

## Passage three: labour power and the origin of surplus

The chapter on the sale and purchase of labour power is the pivot of the entire book and the single most valuable passage for examination purposes, because it contains the distinction that carries the exploitation argument.

The problem Marx sets himself is this: if commodities exchange at their values, and every exchange is an exchange of equivalents, where does profit come from? It cannot come from selling above value, because what one seller gains another buyer loses and the aggregate is unchanged. The answer is that the capitalist finds on the market a commodity whose consumption creates value, and that commodity is labour power.

The distinction is between labour power, which is the capacity to work, described in the Moore and Aveling rendering as "the aggregate of those mental and physical capabilities existing in a human being", and labour, which is the exercise of that capacity. What the worker sells is labour power, and its value, like that of any commodity, is determined by what it costs to reproduce, which is the value of the means of subsistence required to maintain the worker. What the capitalist obtains is the use of that capacity for an agreed period, and during that period the worker's labour creates value. If the value of labour power corresponds to four hours of labour and the working day is eight, the four-hour difference is surplus value.

Nothing has been cheated. The worker received the full value of what they sold. The surplus arises from the difference between the value of the capacity and the value the exercise of the capacity creates, which is why exploitation in Marx's sense is a structural feature of exchange between equals rather than a matter of unfair dealing.

This is the argument, and a candidate who can run it in five sentences in the correct order has the core of any question on exploitation, surplus value, or class. The rate of surplus value, the ratio of surplus value to variable capital, follows directly and is worth stating where a question invites quantification. The developed treatment sits in [the Marxist theory of exploitation](/2010/12/01/marxist-theory-of-exploitation/).

## Passage four: accumulation and the reserve army

The chapter on the general law of capitalist accumulation supplies the dynamic material that most candidates lack, and it is the difference between an answer that describes a static system and one that describes a process.

The argument is that as capital accumulates, the proportion of investment going into means of production rises relative to the proportion going into wages, so that the demand for labour grows more slowly than capital itself. This produces a relative surplus population, an industrial reserve army of the unemployed and underemployed, which is not an accident or a failure of the system but a product of its normal operation. The reserve regulates wages: when accumulation is rapid it is drawn upon and wages rise, and when accumulation slows it swells and wages are pushed down.

For examination purposes this passage does three jobs. It answers any question about unemployment, about whether capitalism tends toward crisis, or about the position of workers under accumulation. It supplies the mechanism connecting Marx's economics to his account of class, since the reserve army explains the structural weakness of labour without appeal to anyone's intentions. And it gives candidates a source for the immiseration question, which arises constantly and which most answers handle badly by asserting either that Marx predicted absolute impoverishment or that he did not, without reference to what the text says. The safe formulation is that the argument concerns the position of workers relative to accumulated capital and the size of the reserve, and that the interpretive question about absolute and relative immiseration is genuinely disputed among scholars.

## The Capital answer matrix

The table below is this guide's findable artifact. It maps the common question types onto the passage that anchors them, the mechanism the answer must run, the evaluative move that lifts the band, and the trap that costs marks.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Define value or explain the labour theory of value | First chapter, opening section on the commodity | Commodities are commensurable through abstract labour; magnitude is socially necessary labour time under prevailing conditions | Note that the theory is restricted to reproducible commodities, which disposes of the land and artwork objection before it is raised | Writing that price equals labour time, which the third volume denies |
| Explain exploitation or surplus value | The chapter on the sale and purchase of labour power | Labour power is sold at its value; its exercise creates more value than it costs to reproduce; the difference is surplus value | State that no cheating occurs and that exploitation is compatible with exchange of equivalents, which is the point most answers miss | Confusing labour with labour power, which collapses the entire argument |
| Discuss commodity fetishism | First chapter, fourth section | Social relations between producers appear as relations between things because production is private and validated only through exchange | Connect the argument to ideology or to the early writings on alienation, with the continuity or break argued rather than asserted | Treating fetishism as consumerism, brand attachment, or materialism |

## The content every answer must contain

Whatever the question, an answer on this material is assessed against a small number of content requirements, and a script missing any of them is capped regardless of its fluency.

The definition of value must carry the socially necessary clause. Without it the candidate has stated a theory Marx explicitly rejects, and a marker who knows the material will read the omission as a signal about the depth of preparation.

Labour power must be distinguished from labour. This is the single most costly omission on the topic and it is discussed at length below.

Surplus value must be presented as a difference between two magnitudes, the value of labour power and the value created by its exercise, rather than as a synonym for profit or as the amount the capitalist takes. Surplus value and profit are related but not identical, and a candidate who treats them as interchangeable has removed the analytical content from the concept.

There must be one located textual anchor. Not four, not a parade of references, but one place in the book where the argument being used actually sits, named in a form the marker can identify.

And there must be an evaluation that names a critic and specifies what the critic targets. An evaluation paragraph that says the theory has been criticised, or that some scholars disagree, is the weakest thing a candidate can write and it is extremely common. The material for this belongs to [criticisms of the labour theory of value](/2010/09/01/labour-theory-of-value-criticisms/), and a candidate who reads that article's target-check rule will be able to write an evaluation sentence that most examiners rarely see.

## The distinction that carries everything

Labour and labour power is the distinction that separates a top-band script from a middling one, and the reason is that it is doing all the analytical work in the exploitation argument.

If the worker sells labour, then the wage is the price of labour, and if the wage is less than the value the labour creates, the worker is being underpaid and the transaction is unfair. That is a moral complaint about cheating, it is not what Marx argues, and it is refutable in a sentence by anyone who points out that the wage was freely agreed. If the worker sells labour power, then the wage is the price of that capacity, determined like the price of anything else by its cost of reproduction, and the worker receives the full value of what they sold. The surplus arises from the gap between what the capacity costs and what its exercise produces, without any departure from the norms of exchange.

The classical economists lacked this distinction and it generated a circularity they could not escape: if labour is a commodity whose value is determined by the labour required to produce it, the definition eats itself. Marx's move dissolves the circularity and simultaneously turns a theory of relative prices into a theory of exploitation.

For examination purposes, the practical consequence is a one-sentence test a candidate can apply to their own script. Read back the sentence that introduces the wage. If it says the worker sells their labour, the answer is wrong and the correction takes four words. If it says the worker sells their labour power, and the next sentence says that the value of labour power is determined by what it costs to reproduce, the candidate is in the territory where the marks are.

### What is the difference between labour and labour power in an exam answer?

Labour power is the capacity to work, which the worker sells at its value, determined by the cost of reproducing the worker. Labour is the exercise of that capacity, which creates value. Surplus value is the difference. Confusing the two collapses the exploitation argument into a complaint about underpayment.

## Answer structures, narrated

Different formats demand different shapes, and the shapes are learnable. What follows are skeletons in prose, to be adapted rather than reproduced.

For a short definitional answer of two or three sentences, the structure is definition, qualification, consequence. State what the concept is, state the clause that most definitions omit, and state what follows from it. For surplus value: it is the difference between the value that labour creates and the value of the labour power the capitalist purchased; the worker sells the capacity rather than the labour itself, and receives its full value; the surplus therefore arises without any violation of exchange. Three sentences, complete, no wasted words, and every clause doing work.

For a medium-length answer where explanation and evaluation are both marked, the structure is definition, mechanism, textual anchor, evaluation, judgement. Open with the concept stated precisely. Run the mechanism in the correct order, which for exploitation means value of labour power first, working day second, surplus as the difference third. Locate the argument in the text by naming the chapter's subject rather than its number. Give the evaluation with a named critic and a specified target. Close with a judgement that says what survives, not a summary of what was said. The proportion of the answer given to evaluation should match the proportion of marks allocated to it, and candidates routinely under-allocate.

For a full essay, the structure is the same with an added layer: an argument that the essay is making, stated early and returned to. An essay on whether Marx's value theory holds should say in its opening paragraph what it will conclude and on what basis, then build the case, then meet the strongest counter-argument at full strength before answering it. A body of accurate paragraphs with no thesis is a summary, and summaries do not reach the top band at any level.

For a comparison question, the structure is both positions, then the genuine point of difference, then the criterion, then the judgement. The step candidates skip is the criterion: stating explicitly what the two positions are being judged on before judging them. General essay method that applies across this whole subject rather than to this book specifically belongs to [how to write a Marxism essay](/2021/10/15/how-to-write-a-marxism-essay/), which owns that guidance and which this guide does not duplicate.

## Three worked skeletons

Structures described in the abstract are hard to use. What follows are three skeletons written out as narration, showing the shape an answer takes rather than supplying prose to be reproduced. Reproducing them would be obvious to any marker and would also fail, since a skeleton fitted to a different question does not answer the one in front of you.

The first is a short definitional answer on surplus value, of the kind a general studies paper or an objective examination requires. It opens by naming what the capitalist buys, which is labour power rather than labour, and stating that this commodity is purchased at its value, determined like any other by what it costs to reproduce. It then states that the exercise of that capacity during the working day creates value, and that the value created exceeds the value of the capacity purchased. It names the difference as surplus value. It closes by noting that this occurs without any departure from exchange of equivalents, since the worker received the full value of what they sold. That is five sentences, it contains the mechanism in the correct order, it pre-empts the objection that the worker is simply being cheated, and it can be written in under four minutes.

The second is a medium-length answer to a question asking a candidate to explain and evaluate the labour theory of value, of the kind that dominates civil service optional papers and undergraduate examinations. It opens by stating the theory with the socially necessary clause intact and by naming the restriction to reproducible commodities, which does two jobs at once. It establishes the distinction between use value and value, and explains why usefulness cannot serve as a measure, since use values are qualitatively incommensurable. It states how magnitude is determined and locates the argument by naming the opening section of the first chapter. It then introduces the level-of-abstraction point in a single sentence: the first volume assumes exchange at values in order to analyse the origin of surplus, and the third volume introduces prices of production, which diverge systematically because competition equalises profit rates across capitals of unequal composition. It moves into evaluation by naming one objection and specifying its target, then giving the reply, then naming a second objection with a different target and its reply. It closes with a judgement that says which claim survives and which does not, rather than restating the essay. The evaluation occupies roughly the share of the answer that the marks allocate to it, which in most such papers is between a third and a half.

The third is a full essay responding to a question about whether Marx's account of exploitation is convincing. It opens with the position the essay will argue, stated plainly, and with the criterion on which the judgement will be made. It reconstructs the argument in its own terms, running the labour power mechanism carefully and noting that the argument's whole force comes from its compatibility with exchange of equivalents, since an account that relied on cheating would be trivial. It supports this with a located reference to the chapter on the sale and purchase of labour power. It then states the strongest objection at full strength, in the terms its own proponents would use, which for this question means either the argument that exploitation can be established without the value theory at all or the argument that the surplus is a return to abstention and risk rather than an appropriation. It answers the objection on its merits, conceding what the objection gets right. It notes where the exchange leaves each side. And it closes by returning to the criterion stated at the start and delivering the judgement against it, naming what would change the verdict.

The feature all three share, and the one that most distinguishes them from typical scripts, is that no sentence exists to announce what the next sentence will do. Every sentence advances the definition, the mechanism, the evidence, or the judgement. A script written this way will be shorter than a script that narrates itself and will score higher.

## Beyond the four passages

The four-passage economy covers the material that is actually asked about, and a candidate aiming higher, or facing a paper with a broader specification, should know a further layer. Each item below is worth a sentence or two in an answer and none justifies extensive preparation on its own.

Constant and variable capital is the distinction between the part of capital laid out on means of production, which transfers its existing value to the product and creates no new value, and the part laid out on labour power, which is the only source of new value. It is the answer to any question about whether machines create value and it can be stated in one sentence. It also underpins the composition of capital, which is the ratio between the two and which the third volume uses to explain why prices of production diverge from values.

Absolute and relative surplus value is the distinction between increasing surplus by lengthening the working day and increasing it by reducing the labour time necessary to reproduce labour power, which is achieved by raising productivity in the industries producing wage goods. The second is the mechanism that connects technical change to the rate of exploitation and it is the more analytically interesting of the pair. This distinction is worth knowing because questions about technology, productivity, and the position of workers are answered badly without it. The developed treatment sits in [absolute and relative surplus value](/2010/11/15/absolute-and-relative-surplus-value/).

The working day material, which occupies a long and historically detailed part of the English editions, supplies the argument that the length of the working day is not determined by economics but by struggle, since both parties have an equal right under the rules of exchange and force therefore decides. That formulation is useful in any question connecting the economic analysis to class conflict, and the historical detail is not worth memorising.

The machinery material supplies the argument that technology under these conditions is deployed to cheapen labour power and to intensify labour rather than to reduce toil, which is the source of most contemporary applications of Marx to automation. One sentence of it converts a static answer into one that engages a live question.

So-called primitive accumulation supplies the historical argument that the initial separation of producers from the means of production was accomplished by force rather than by thrift, which directly attacks the political economy account of original saving. It is examined in its own right in some sociology and history contexts and is treated in [primitive accumulation explained](/2011/01/01/primitive-accumulation-explained/).

A candidate who adds one sentence from this layer to an answer built on the four passages will read as better prepared than one who adds three paragraphs, because the marks come from precision and relevance rather than from volume.

## The evaluative moves that lift a band

Evaluation is where scripts separate, and a small number of moves reliably do the work.

The strongest is the target-specification move: naming an objection and stating precisely what part of the theory it attacks. Writing that the transformation problem attacks the internal consistency of the derivation from values to prices of production, while the redundancy argument concedes consistency and attacks necessity, demonstrates a level of control that most scripts do not approach and it takes two sentences.

The second is the scope move: noting that the theory is restricted to freely reproducible commodities, which handles the land, antiques, and artwork objection cleanly. Many candidates raise this objection themselves in an evaluation paragraph without knowing the theory has an answer, which loses marks twice.

The third is the empirical move: noting that the theory's price claim has been tested using national input-output data, that the reported correlations are high, and that they are contested because both quantities scale with industry size. This is available material, almost no candidate uses it, and it converts an evaluation from a recitation of theoretical objections into something that looks like scholarship. The full account is in [the evidence on the labour theory of value](/2010/10/01/labour-theory-of-value-evidence/), and a candidate needs one sentence of it.

The fourth is the internal-critic move: noting that the most serious challenges to the value theory have come from writers sympathetic to Marx's political conclusions rather than from hostile economists, and that at least one argument holds that the exploitation claim does not require the value theory at all. This signals wider reading and it is true.

The fifth is the level-of-abstraction move, which is the highest-value item in the whole list and the rarest. Noting that the first volume assumes exchange at values for the purposes of analysing the origin of surplus, and that the third volume introduces prices of production which diverge systematically from values, tells a marker that the candidate has understood the architecture of the work rather than a chapter of it. It also inoculates the script against the most common objection a marker might otherwise expect the candidate to have missed.

## The moves that lose marks

The mirror image is worth stating explicitly, because these appear constantly.

Asserting that Marx said price equals labour time. This is wrong, the third volume denies it, and a marker who knows the material will discount the rest of the evaluation.

Writing about the Communist Manifesto in an answer about Capital. The two are different works with different purposes, thirty years apart in Marx's development, and a candidate who reaches for the Manifesto's rhetoric in an economic answer has signalled that they do not have the economic material.

Spending the word budget on biography. No marks are attached to where Marx lived, how he was funded, or his relationship with Engels, in any assessment of this material, and a paragraph of it is a paragraph not spent on content.

Narrating the book. A chronological walk through the parts of Capital answers no question that has ever been set on it.

Offering an evaluation that consists of naming schools. Writing that Weberians, feminists, and postmodernists have criticised Marx, without saying what any of them said or what it targeted, is a list rather than an evaluation and is marked as one.

Quoting at length. Long quotations consume the word budget and demonstrate nothing except transcription. One short quotation, accurately attributed, is worth more than a paragraph of extract, and no quotation at all is better than a misremembered one.

## The traps in detail

The list of errors given earlier is the summary. Each deserves a fuller treatment, because knowing that an error exists is not the same as knowing why it costs and how to avoid it under pressure.

The price-equals-labour-time trap costs because it is not a simplification but a misstatement. Marx's mature system holds that commodities exchange at prices of production, which diverge systematically from values wherever a branch's ratio of constant to variable capital departs from the social average, and the third volume constructs a procedure to derive one from the other. A candidate asserting proportionality has stated the position that every critic since the eighteen nineties has attacked, and has done so in a context where the marker may well have set the question precisely to see whether candidates know better. The fix is a habit: whenever the word price appears in a draft answer, check whether the sentence claims equality with value, and if it does, replace equality with regulation or with governs.

The labour and labour power trap costs because it silently converts the argument into a different and weaker one. A candidate who writes that the worker sells labour has produced an answer in which the employer pays less than the labour is worth, which is a claim about unfairness that any reader can reject by pointing to the freely agreed contract. The whole force of the original argument is that it survives the free contract. The fix is to check the sentence that introduces the wage, which takes five seconds.

The fetishism-as-consumerism trap costs because it answers a different question entirely. Fetishism concerns how a social relation appears, not how much people want objects, and the two have almost nothing to do with each other. Candidates arrive at this error honestly, because the word fetish carries a colloquial sense and because a great deal of popular writing uses the concept loosely. The fix is a definition memorised in the correct form: relations between people appearing as relations between things.

The chapter-number trap costs because it produces an unverifiable reference, and an unverifiable reference is worth less than a description. It has the further disadvantage of looking like precision, which makes the failure more conspicuous when a marker checks. The fix is the convention already given: cite the chapter by its subject.

The narration trap costs because it consumes the entire word budget producing content that answers no question. Candidates fall into it when they are underprepared on the specific concept and compensate with what they do know, and markers recognise the pattern immediately. The fix is structural: never write a sentence beginning with a statement about what the book does, and if a paragraph could stand unchanged in an answer to a different question, delete it.

The biography and Manifesto trap costs for the same reason and has the same cause. Neither carries marks in an assessment of the economic content, and a paragraph on either is a paragraph of the answer given away. The fix is a rule applied while planning rather than while writing: nothing about Marx's life, and nothing from the Manifesto, unless the question names them.

## Checking your own script

The most useful five minutes in any examination on this topic are the ones spent checking, and checking is far more effective when it looks for specific things rather than reading for general quality.

Look first for the phrase socially necessary. If the answer discusses value and the phrase is absent, insert it, which normally takes one clause. This is the single highest-return check available.

Look second at the sentence introducing the wage or the employment relation. If it says the worker sells their labour, correct it to labour power. If the following sentence does not say what determines the value of labour power, add it.

Look third for the evaluation and count its sentences against the marks allocated to evaluation. If the proportion is wrong, and time remains, add rather than rewrite: one more sentence naming a second objection and its target is the cheapest available improvement.

Look fourth for any chapter number and replace it with a subject description.

Look fifth for any sentence that announces what the next sentence will say, and delete it. There is usually at least one, it costs nothing to remove, and its removal makes room for content.

Look sixth for the judgement. If the final paragraph summarises the answer rather than delivering a verdict, rewrite the last two sentences. Summaries at the end of an answer are the commonest waste of the highest-value position in the script.

A candidate who runs those six checks will recover marks on almost every script, and the checks work under pressure precisely because none of them requires rereading the whole answer or exercising judgement about quality.

## A revision timetable

Preparation on this topic compresses well, which is fortunate because it usually has to.

With a month available, spend the first week on the four passages themselves, reading them slowly and producing the one-page note. Spend the second week on the evaluation layer, acquiring three objections with their targets and one reply each. Spend the third week writing answers to past questions under time, one every other day, and marking them against the six checks. Spend the fourth week on the adjacent material, the second layer of concepts, and on further timed practice.

With a week available, produce the one-page note on day one, acquire the three objections on day two, and spend the remaining days entirely on timed writing, because at that point the marginal return on additional content is far below the marginal return on fluency and structure.

With a day available, learn the exploitation mechanism until it can be written from memory in five sentences in the correct order, learn the socially necessary clause, learn one objection and what it targets, and write one full answer under time. That is a viable minimum and it will produce a competent script.

The night before is for the mechanism and nothing else. Reading new material the night before an assessment has a poor record and a well-understood failure mode: it displaces the material that was secure with material that is not.

Across all of these, the practice that repays most is writing under time rather than reading. Candidates consistently overinvest in reading because it feels like progress and underinvest in writing because it exposes gaps. The gaps are the point. A script written badly in practice is information; a chapter read passively is not.

## The citation problem nobody warns candidates about

Chapter numbers in Capital Volume One are not stable across editions, and this is a genuine hazard rather than a pedantic point.

The German editions divide the work into seven sections and twenty-five chapters. The standard English editions divide it into eight parts and thirty-three chapters, because material that appears as a single chapter in German is split into several in English. The consequence is that a chapter number cited from an English edition points somewhere else entirely in a German one, and vice versa. The passage on the general law of capitalist accumulation carries different numbers in the two traditions. So does the material on so-called primitive accumulation, which is a single chapter in German and a whole part in English.

A candidate citing a chapter number is therefore making a claim that may be false for the edition their marker uses, and the marker has no way of knowing whether the candidate is wrong about the content or right about the content and using a different edition.

The convention that solves this costs nothing. Cite by subject rather than by number: the chapter on the sale and purchase of labour power, the section on the fetishism of the commodity, the opening section on the two factors of the commodity, the chapter on the general law of capitalist accumulation. These titles are stable across editions and translations, they are more informative than a number, and they demonstrate that the candidate knows what is in the passage rather than where it sits in a particular printing. Where an edition is being used directly and a number is genuinely helpful, name the translation as well, and be aware that the two principal English renderings differ in wording as well as in structure.

### How do you cite Capital when editions differ?

Cite by subject rather than by number: the chapter on the sale and purchase of labour power, the section on commodity fetishism. Chapter numbers differ between the German and English editions, so a number may point a marker to a different passage from the one intended.

## The strategic question: can you write about a book you have not read?

Candidates ask this and deserve an honest answer rather than either reassurance or a lecture.

The answer is that located, accurate use of four passages is better scholarship than vague gestures at the whole book, and it is also better than the alternative most candidates actually face, which is not reading the book carefully but reading a summary of it and reproducing the summary. A candidate who has read the first chapter and the chapter on labour power, understood them, and can deploy them precisely knows more about Capital that is usable in an assessment than a candidate who has read all eight hundred pages once and retained an impression.

The risk is not the reading gap. The risk is the temptation to claim more than the four passages support. A candidate who has read four sections and writes as though they have surveyed the work will make errors of scope: attributing to the first volume claims that belong to the third, describing the book's structure inaccurately, or asserting what Marx concluded about matters the four passages do not address. The discipline that avoids this is simple: write about what you have read, cite what you have read, and do not characterise the whole.

The counter-view deserves stating. Some markers, particularly at postgraduate level and in courses where the text is set reading, do reward evidence of wider engagement, and they can tell. The tell is not the number of citations but the ability to relate one part of the argument to another, to note where the exposition changes its level of abstraction, and to handle a passage the summaries do not cover. A candidate who wants the top band in that context has to read more than four passages, and the reading route that gets there efficiently is in the reading guide for this book rather than here.

The honest summary is that the four-passage strategy takes a candidate from weak to solid reliably, and from solid to outstanding only in assessments where breadth of reading is not itself being assessed.

## Timing and the word budget

Most scripts on this topic fail on allocation rather than on knowledge, and allocation is trainable in a way that knowledge is not, at least not in the final weeks.

The governing principle is that the word budget should mirror the mark allocation. If a question carries an explicit split between explanation and evaluation, and the evaluation share is substantial, then roughly that share of the answer belongs to evaluation. Candidates systematically violate this, writing four fluent paragraphs of exposition and two defensive sentences of evaluation, because exposition is easier and feels productive. The correction is mechanical: before writing, decide how many sentences the evaluation gets, and stop the exposition when that budget requires it.

The second principle is that the opening should not warm up. An answer that begins by establishing that Marx was an influential thinker who wrote about capitalism has spent a sentence on nothing. The first sentence should state the concept or the position, and under time pressure the first sentence is often the only one a rushed marker reads carefully. Candidates who practise writing first sentences in isolation, twenty of them across different questions, gain more than from any equivalent amount of content revision.

The third principle concerns what to cut when time runs out, and it is worth deciding in advance rather than in the moment. Cut examples before cutting mechanism. Cut the second objection before cutting the first. Cut the historical illustration before cutting the definition. Never cut the evaluation entirely, since an answer with no evaluation is capped in most schemes regardless of the quality of what remains, and two sentences of evaluation are worth far more than the fourth paragraph of exposition they replace.

The fourth principle is that a short complete answer beats a long truncated one. An answer that runs out of time mid-argument loses the judgement, which is where the highest marks sit. Candidates who habitually overrun should plan to a shorter target than they think they need, since the discipline of finishing is worth more than the additional content.

For a candidate writing several answers under a single time limit, the risk is that the first answer consumes disproportionate time because it is the one they feel most confident about. Confidence is a poor guide to marginal return. The last answer written badly costs more marks than the first answer written well gains.

## What each assessment format rewards

The same content is marked differently depending on the format, and adapting is worth a band.

Where the format is a short definitional response, the reward is precision and completeness in the fewest words. Nothing is gained by context, by examples, or by evaluation, and the commonest failure is over-answering: producing a paragraph where two sentences were required, which wastes time that later questions need. The test of a good short answer is whether every clause is load-bearing.

Where the format is a medium-length answer with an explicit evaluation requirement, the reward shifts to the balance between the two components and to the quality of the evaluative move. Explanation is necessary but it is not where scripts separate, because most prepared candidates can explain. The separation happens in whether the evaluation identifies what an objection targets or merely reports that criticism exists.

Where the format is an essay, the reward is an argument. An essay is not a longer answer; it is a different object, with a position, a structure that serves the position, and a conclusion that follows from the argument rather than summarising it. Candidates who write long answers rather than essays are the largest single group in the middle band at undergraduate level, and the fix is structural rather than about content: decide the conclusion before writing, and make each section serve it.

Where the format is comparative, the reward is the criterion. Two accurate accounts placed side by side are not a comparison, and the marks attach to the moment when the candidate says what the two positions are being judged on and then judges them on it.

Where the format is applied, presenting a scenario and asking the candidate to analyse it using Marxist categories, the reward is the fit between category and case, argued rather than asserted. Answers that name the categories and then describe the scenario separately do not score; answers that show what the category reveals about the case, and where it strains, do.

Across all five, the constant is that markers reward the operation the question asks for. A question that says evaluate is not asking for explanation with a concluding paragraph, and a question that says compare is not asking for two descriptions. Reading the instruction word and doing what it says is the least glamorous advice available and it recovers more marks than any content addition.

## The vocabulary that must be exact

A small number of terms on this topic cannot be paraphrased without loss, and using them loosely is read as imprecision even when the underlying understanding is sound.

Labour power, not labour, when referring to what is sold. This has been laboured already and it remains the single highest-cost slip.

Socially necessary labour time, not labour time, when stating what determines magnitude. The qualifier is the theory.

Value, exchange value, and use value are three different things, and exchange value is the form in which value appears rather than a synonym for it. Candidates who use the three interchangeably lose the analytical structure that the first chapter is built on.

Constant and variable capital, not fixed and circulating capital. These are different distinctions with different bases, the first turning on whether the capital creates new value and the second on how it turns over, and confusing them is a recognisable error.

Surplus value, not profit, when describing what is appropriated in production, with profit reserved for the form it takes after redistribution among capitals.

The rate of surplus value, which is measured against variable capital, is not the rate of profit, which is measured against total capital advanced. Candidates who confuse these two produce statements that are simply false.

Means of production, not capital, when referring to the physical inputs, since capital in this framework is a social relation rather than a stock of things, and a candidate who can make that point in one clause has demonstrated something worth marks.

Reproduction, in the sense of the reproduction of labour power, means the maintenance and renewal of the worker's capacity rather than anything to do with biological reproduction, although the two connect in the later literature on social reproduction.

None of this is pedantry for its own sake. Each of these pairs marks a distinction that the argument depends on, and a marker reading loose usage cannot tell whether the candidate knows the distinction and expressed it carelessly or does not know it.

## When the question is unfamiliar

Every candidate eventually meets a question on this material that does not match anything they prepared, and having a procedure for that moment is worth more than another topic.

The first step is to identify which of the four passages the question touches, which it almost always does. A question about the position of workers under automation touches the accumulation passage and the machinery material. A question about whether Marx's economics and his sociology are connected touches the fetishism section. A question about whether capitalism is exploitative by nature touches the labour power chapter. Almost nothing that can be asked about this book falls outside the four.

The second step is to identify the operation: explain, evaluate, compare, apply, or discuss. The operation determines the shape, and the shape is already prepared.

The third step is to write the definition of whatever concept the question names, precisely, as the first substantive sentence. This buys thinking time, it secures the marks attached to accurate definition, and it frequently reveals the answer's structure, because a concept stated precisely suggests what can be said about it.

The fourth step is to resist the temptation to write what you prepared. The most damaging failure on an unfamiliar question is answering the adjacent question you revised for, which markers recognise immediately and which scores worse than a partial attempt at the actual question. A candidate who addresses the real question with three quarters of the relevant content will outscore one who addresses a different question comprehensively.

The fifth step is to make sure the evaluation, however short, addresses the question rather than the topic. An evaluation of the labour theory of value pasted into an answer about alienation is visible as a paste.

## Questions candidates ask, answered plainly

Is it acceptable to disagree with Marx in an answer? Yes, and in most schemes a reasoned judgement in either direction scores better than neutrality, provided the position is argued from evidence rather than asserted. What is not acceptable is dismissal without engagement, in either direction.

Does political sympathy affect marking? A candidate cannot control what a marker believes, and worrying about it is wasted effort. What is controllable is the quality of the argument, and a script that states the opposing case at full strength before judging is defensible to any marker of any persuasion, which is the practical reason to write that way regardless of one's own view.

Should the answer mention that the second and third volumes were assembled by Engels? Only where the question turns on what Marx concluded, and then in one clause. It is a real point and it is not decoration.

Is it worth learning German terms? Almost never. A candidate who writes about Mehrwert rather than surplus value has added nothing a marker can credit and has risked a spelling error. The exception is where a translation dispute is directly relevant to the question, in which case naming both English renderings is more useful than naming the German.

How many critics should be named? Two, deployed precisely, is better than five mentioned. The marks attach to what the objection targets, not to the length of the list.

Is it a problem to have read secondary sources rather than the text? Not if the answer is accurate and does not claim more than it can support. It becomes a problem when a candidate reproduces a secondary source's characterisation of a passage without knowing what the passage says, since characterisations circulating in revision material frequently contain the errors this guide has spent its length correcting.

## For students: the revision sequence that works

This layer is expanded because this article is written for the student first.

Start with the four passages themselves, in this order: the opening section of the first chapter, then the chapter on labour power, then the fourth section of the first chapter, then the chapter on accumulation. That order is not the book's order and it is deliberate. Value first, because everything depends on it. Labour power second, because it is where the marks are and because reading it early means the rest of the revision has a destination. Fetishism third, because it is easier to understand once value is secure. Accumulation last, because it is the least conceptually demanding and the most self-contained.

For each passage, produce three things and nothing else: a definition in one sentence, the mechanism in four or five sentences, and one sentence locating the argument by subject. That is roughly a page for all four passages, and it is the entire content base. Candidates who produce twelve pages of notes on this topic have produced something they will not reread.

Then build the evaluation layer separately, because it is separately assessed. Take three objections, know what each one targets, and know one reply to each. Three is enough. A candidate with three objections deployed precisely will outscore one with seven mentioned vaguely, for the same reason that four passages beat eight hundred pages.

Then practise the structures under time. The single highest-return revision activity for this topic is writing the exploitation mechanism from memory in five sentences, repeatedly, until the order is automatic: value of labour power, working day, value created, difference, no violation of exchange. Under examination pressure the order is what collapses first, and a candidate who has automated it has removed the main failure mode.

Then practise with real questions rather than invented ones, because the phrasing of actual assessment questions is more constrained and more predictable than candidates expect, and pattern recognition across past questions is worth a great deal. Working through [previous-year question papers on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/upsc-previous-year-question-papers.html) is the efficient way to do this, since seeing how the same concept is asked in five different formulations teaches more about what is being tested than any amount of content revision. Keeping the four passage notes, the three objections, and the answer skeletons together in one place, with each claim attached to its source, is what [VaultBook's study and citation tools](https://vaultbook.org/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) are built for, and this topic rewards that organisation because the same four notes serve every question that will ever be set on it.

The wider revision sequence across the whole subject, of which this topic is one component, is set out in [the Marxism revision guide](/2022/01/15/marxism-revision-guide/), and candidates should slot this topic into that sequence rather than revising it in isolation.

## How this topic connects to the rest of a course

Capital and the value theory sit at the centre of a web, and knowing which questions elsewhere in a course this preparation also serves prevents wasted revision.

The labour power mechanism serves every question on class, because it explains the structural basis of the class relation without appeal to anyone's intentions, and it serves questions on the state and on ideology by supplying the economic account those arguments rest on. A candidate who has automated the mechanism has partly prepared for four or five other topics.

The socially necessary clause serves questions on competition, on technical change, and on why firms are compelled to innovate, since the clause is what makes an individual producer's excess labour worthless and therefore what makes technical change a matter of survival rather than choice.

The fetishism section serves questions on ideology, on alienation, and on the relation between the early and the mature writings. It is the passage that connects the economic argument to the social theory, and the continuity question between the young Marx and the mature Marx is frequently examined through it.

The accumulation material serves questions on crisis, on unemployment, on immiseration, and on globalisation, and it is the passage most useful for contemporary application questions, since the reserve army argument transfers directly to discussions of precarious work and labour market flexibility.

The practical consequence is that a candidate who prepares this topic thoroughly has done a disproportionate share of the work for a whole theory paper, which is worth knowing when allocating revision time across a syllabus. The reverse also holds: a candidate who skips this topic will find it reappearing inside answers on four other topics, in a form they cannot handle.

## Three facts most candidates do not know

Each of these is a single sentence in an answer and each signals preparation that most scripts do not display.

The first is that the value theory is restricted to freely reproducible commodities, and that Marx handles land through the theory of ground rent and unique goods through monopoly price, both in the third volume. Candidates who know this can dispose in two sentences of the objection about paintings and land, which many raise against themselves without realising there is an answer.

The second is that the first volume is the only one Marx saw through the press, and that the second and third were assembled by Engels from manuscripts left in varying states of completion. This matters wherever an answer turns on what Marx concluded about a difficulty he was still working on, and it is a fact that a marker with scholarly training will notice and credit.

The third is that the phrase class in itself and class for itself, which appears in an enormous number of answers attributed to Marx, is not his formulation in that form. The closest source is a passage in an early polemical work, and the crystallised version belongs to a later interpretive tradition. A candidate who uses the phrase should either attribute it carefully or use Marx's own vocabulary, and one who knows the difference has an easy way to demonstrate it.

None of these is difficult to learn and each converts a competent answer into one that reads as informed. They are the cheapest available upgrades in the whole of this preparation.

## Political balance in an answer

Candidates worry about this more than they need to and handle it worse than they should, so it is worth a short, direct treatment.

An assessment on this material is testing analytical control, not allegiance. A script that reads as advocacy, in either direction, is weaker than one that reads as analysis, and the reason is technical rather than diplomatic: advocacy tends to state the opposing case weakly, which forfeits the marks attached to evaluation, and it tends to substitute assertion for evidence, which forfeits the marks attached to argument.

The practical form of balance is a habit rather than a posture. State the strongest version of the position you are about to criticise, in the terms its own defenders would use, before criticising it. A candidate attacking the value theory should first state what it claims accurately, including the qualifiers that make it defensible. A candidate defending it should first state the objection at full force, including what it gets right. Doing this costs one sentence and it converts an evaluation from an opinion into an argument.

The second habit is keeping theory and historical record separate. Questions on this material sometimes invite a slide from what Marx argued to what states claiming his name did, in either direction, and the slide is always a mistake. The theory is not vindicated by any regime's achievements and is not refuted by its crimes; both require their own arguments. A candidate who states that separation explicitly, in one clause, has demonstrated exactly the discipline that a marker of any political persuasion will credit.

The third habit is avoiding the word obviously and its relatives. Anything obvious does not need arguing, and a script that leans on it is telling a marker that the argument was not available.

None of this requires a candidate to conceal a view. Reasoned judgement scores better than neutrality in most schemes. It requires the judgement to arrive after the evidence rather than in place of it.

## For teachers: what distinguishes a strong script

Marking answers on this material is easier than marking most theory topics, because the discriminating features are objective rather than impressionistic.

The first thing to look for is the socially necessary clause. Its presence or absence sorts scripts faster than any other single feature and it takes two seconds to check. A script that has it has almost always understood something; a script that lacks it has almost always memorised something.

The second is the labour and labour power distinction, and it is worth being explicit with a class that this is a hard discriminator. Scripts that confuse the two have not merely made a terminological slip; they have produced an argument that says the worker is underpaid, which is a different and weaker claim than the one they were asked about. This should be marked as a conceptual error rather than as an expression problem.

The third is whether the evaluation names a target. A script that says the theory has been criticised is doing something categorically different from a script that says a particular objection attacks the derivation of prices from values while leaving the account of surplus untouched. The second is analysis; the first is a gesture toward analysis.

The misconception that dominates a class here is that Marx is claiming that things should cost what the labour in them is worth. It arrives as a moral intuition and it is remarkably persistent. The question that surfaces it in one move is to ask what the theory predicts about a good produced by a firm with obsolete, slow equipment. Students holding the moral reading say it should be more expensive because more labour went into it; the theory says the excess labour is not socially necessary, does not count, and the firm loses. That single exchange teaches the qualifying clause more effectively than any amount of exposition, and it is worth running before any content is delivered.

For assessment design, questions that ask candidates to explain and then evaluate produce better discrimination than questions that ask for an account, because the evaluation component is where preparation shows. Questions that ask candidates to apply a concept to a described situation are better still, since they cannot be answered from memorised summary.

## For researchers: why the exam convention and the scholarly one diverge

A short note, because candidates who go on to postgraduate work need to know that the citation habit recommended here is a habit for assessments and not a scholarly standard.

In scholarly writing, a reference must be precise enough for a reader to locate the passage in a specific edition, which means naming the edition and translation and giving a page reference. Citing by subject alone is insufficient. The recommendation in this guide is a response to a particular constraint, which is that an examination candidate does not know which edition their marker uses and cannot append a bibliography that would resolve the ambiguity.

Two further points matter once a candidate moves into research. The English translations differ from one another in substantive ways, not merely in style, and the wording of key terms varies enough that an argument resting on a particular rendering should say which one it uses. And the second and third volumes of Capital are editorial constructions assembled by Engels from manuscripts Marx did not prepare for publication, which is a fact that must be stated whenever an argument turns on what Marx concluded, and which has no bearing at all on the first volume, the only one he saw through the press himself.

## Two paragraphs compared

Abstract advice about precision is hard to act on. The clearest way to convey what separates bands is to set two illustrative paragraphs on the same question side by side and say what the second does that the first does not. Both are constructed for this purpose rather than taken from any script.

The question is to explain how Marx accounts for the origin of profit.

The weaker paragraph runs roughly like this. Marx argued that capitalism is based on the exploitation of the working class. The capitalist owns the means of production while the worker owns nothing but their labour, and this unequal relationship allows the capitalist to pay the worker less than the value of what they produce. The difference is surplus value, which becomes the capitalist's profit. Marx believed this was fundamentally unjust and that it would eventually lead to the overthrow of the system.

Nothing in that paragraph is grossly wrong, and it will earn something. What it lacks is the mechanism. It says the capitalist pays less than the value of what the worker produces, which is the underpayment claim, and it therefore leaves the argument open to the obvious response that the worker agreed to the wage. It does not name labour power. It does not say what determines the wage. It offers no location in the text. And it closes on a claim about Marx's beliefs and predictions rather than on the analysis the question asked for.

The stronger paragraph runs like this. Marx's problem is that if all commodities exchange at their values, profit cannot arise from buying cheap and selling dear, since one party's gain is another's loss and the aggregate is unchanged. His solution is that the capitalist purchases a commodity whose consumption creates value: labour power, the capacity to work, which is distinct from labour, its exercise. Labour power is bought at its value, determined like that of any commodity by what it costs to reproduce, so the worker receives the full value of what they sell. During the working day the exercise of that capacity creates value exceeding the value of the capacity itself, and that difference is surplus value. The argument's force lies in requiring no cheating at any point, which is why it appears in the chapter on the sale and purchase of labour power, immediately after the analysis of exchange has established that equivalents trade for equivalents.

The second paragraph is barely longer. It contains the same conclusion. What it adds is the problem the argument solves, the distinction that solves it, the determination of the wage, the correct sequence, a located reference, and an explicit statement of why the argument matters. Those additions are what the marks are attached to, and none of them requires knowledge the first paragraph's author lacked; they require the mechanism to have been learned in order rather than as a summary.

## What a marker sees first

Scripts are read quickly, and the features that register in the opening moments shape how the rest is read. This is not ideal and it is how marking works at volume.

The opening sentence registers first. A sentence that states the concept or the position is read as competence; a sentence that establishes the importance of Marx is read as throat-clearing and lowers the expectation against which everything else is judged.

The technical vocabulary registers next, and it registers as a pattern rather than word by word. A script containing labour power, socially necessary, constant and variable capital, and prices of production reads as prepared before any of the arguments have been assessed. A script that uses labour, work, and profit throughout reads as general knowledge.

The presence of a located reference registers third. Not its accuracy, which will be checked later if at all, but its presence: a script that points at a passage is doing something a script that gestures at a book is not.

The shape registers fourth. A script with visible structure, in which the explanation and the evaluation are distinguishable, is easier to mark generously than one in which everything is a single undifferentiated flow, because the marker can find the components the scheme requires.

None of this means presentation substitutes for content. It means that a script with good content and none of these features will be marked less generously than one with the same content and all of them, and the four features cost nothing to acquire.

## If you remember nothing else

The irreducible core of this guide is four items.

The worker sells labour power, not labour, and its value is what it costs to reproduce. Value is determined by socially necessary labour time, and the qualifier is the theory rather than an embellishment. Cite by the chapter's subject and never by its number. And allocate words to evaluation in the proportion the marks allocate to it.

A candidate carrying only those four into an assessment will avoid both errors that cap a script, will state the theory in the form its author held rather than the form its critics attack, will produce references a marker can verify, and will not lose the evaluation marks that most scripts lose. That is a competent performance built on four sentences of preparation, and everything else in this guide is an improvement on a foundation that already works.

The wider point is worth stating for candidates who find this topic intimidating. Capital has a reputation for difficulty that its examinable content does not deserve. The book is long, the opening chapter is genuinely hard, and the secondary literature is vast, none of which changes the fact that assessments ask about a small and stable set of concepts, that those concepts have precise definitions, and that the definitions can be learned in an evening and deployed for the rest of a course. The gap between the book's reputation and the manageability of what is actually tested is the largest of any topic in this subject, and candidates who realise it early save themselves a great deal of unnecessary anxiety and a great deal of unfocused reading.

## Closing revision strategy

The whole of this guide reduces to a short sequence. Learn four passages properly. Automate one mechanism until it survives time pressure. Carry three objections with their targets specified. Cite by subject, never by number. Allocate words to evaluation in proportion to the marks attached to it. And check every script for the two errors that cap it: price equals labour time, and the worker sells labour.

A candidate who does that will produce answers that are accurate, located, and evaluated, which is what the assessment is measuring. Everything else in the preparation of this topic is either enrichment or displacement activity, and under time pressure the difference between them is the difference between a good result and a disappointing one.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How do you answer an exam question on Capital Volume One?

Identify what the question is actually asking about, which is always a concept, a mechanism, or a judgement rather than the book itself. State the concept precisely with its qualifying clause. Run the relevant mechanism in the correct order. Locate the argument in the text by naming the chapter's subject rather than its number. Evaluate by naming a critic and specifying what that objection targets. Close with a judgement rather than a summary. Allocate your words in the proportion the marks are allocated, which usually means more evaluation than candidates instinctively give. Never narrate the book's contents, which answers no question that has been set.

### Q: Which passages of Capital should you quote in an exam?

Four passages carry every question set on this book: the opening section of the first chapter for value and socially necessary labour time, the fourth section of the first chapter for commodity fetishism, the chapter on the sale and purchase of labour power for the origin of surplus, and the chapter on the general law of capitalist accumulation for the reserve army and the dynamics of accumulation. Quote sparingly if at all, since long extracts consume the word budget and demonstrate only transcription. A precise reference to the argument's location is worth more than a quotation, and a misremembered quotation is worse than none.

### Q: Can you write about Capital without reading the whole book?

Yes, and located use of four passages produces better answers than vague gestures at the whole work. Assessments reward precision and location rather than duration of exposure, and a marker cannot give credit for reading. The risk is not the reading gap but the temptation to claim more than the four passages support: attributing to the first volume claims that belong to the third, describing the book's structure inaccurately, or characterising conclusions the passages do not cover. Write about what you have read and do not characterise the whole. In postgraduate contexts where the text is set reading, markers can detect wider engagement, and more reading is required for the top band.

### Q: How do you cite Capital when editions differ?

Cite by subject rather than by number. The German editions have seven sections and twenty-five chapters; the standard English editions have eight parts and thirty-three chapters, because material appearing as one chapter in German is split into several in English. A chapter number from one tradition points elsewhere in the other, and a marker cannot tell whether a candidate is wrong about content or right about content using a different edition. Naming the chapter's subject, such as the chapter on the sale and purchase of labour power, is stable across editions, more informative than a number, and demonstrates knowledge of what the passage contains.

### Q: What do examiners mark down in Capital answers?

Six things recur. Asserting that Marx said price equals labour time, which the third volume denies. Confusing labour with labour power, which collapses the exploitation argument into a complaint about underpayment. Treating commodity fetishism as consumerism or brand attachment. Narrating the book's contents rather than answering the question. Spending the word budget on biography or on the Communist Manifesto, neither of which carries marks in an economic answer. And offering an evaluation that names schools of criticism without saying what any of them argued or what it targeted, which is a list rather than an evaluation.

### Q: How do you structure an essay on Marx's theory of value?

State the concept with the socially necessary clause in the opening paragraph, along with the position the essay will argue. Establish the use value and value distinction, then the determination of magnitude, then the restriction to reproducible commodities. Introduce the level-of-abstraction point, that the first volume assumes exchange at values while the third introduces prices of production which diverge systematically. Present the strongest objection at full strength before answering it. Close on what survives and what remains disputed rather than summarising. An essay that is a sequence of accurate paragraphs without an argument is a summary and will not reach the top band.

### Q: How do you evaluate Marx's value theory in an essay?

Name a specific objection and specify precisely what it attacks, since the theory does several jobs and most published refutations defeat one while assuming they have defeated all. State whether the objection targets the claim about relative prices, the claim about the origin of surplus, or the claim about the social form of labour allocation. Give the best reply rather than the most convenient one. The highest-value additions available are the restriction to reproducible commodities, the existence of empirical testing using input-output data, and the fact that the most serious challenges have come from writers sympathetic to Marx's conclusions.

### Q: Which topics from Capital come up most often?

Surplus value and exploitation appear more than anything else, followed by the labour theory of value as a named theory to be explained and evaluated, then commodity fetishism, then the accumulation and reserve army material. Comparative questions setting Marx against another theorist on class or on capitalism are common in sociology contexts. Questions on the structure of the book itself are rare, which is why narrating its contents is such a poor use of preparation. The four-passage strategy is built around this distribution: those four passages cover the material that is actually asked about.

### Q: How do you explain commodity fetishism in three sentences?

In a society where production is private and dispersed, producers discover whether their labour counted only when their products exchange. The social relation between their labours therefore appears to them as a relation between things, and value looks like a property the object possesses rather than a relation between the producer's labour and society's total labour. The appearance is not an illusion in the sense of a mistake, but the form the relation genuinely takes under these conditions of production, which is why the concept is about perception of social structure rather than about attachment to possessions.

### Q: How much of Capital do you need for an undergraduate course?

More than four passages if the text is set reading and the course assesses engagement with it, and the four passages plus a reading route through the rest if it does not. The distinguishing feature markers detect at this level is not citation count but the ability to relate one part of the argument to another and to handle passages the summaries do not cover. A practical target is the first chapter in full, the part on the transformation of money into capital, the chapter on the working day, and the accumulation material, with the historical chapters read selectively.

### Q: What is the fastest way to revise this topic?

Produce one page: a one-sentence definition, a four-sentence mechanism, and a one-sentence textual location for each of the four passages. Then automate the exploitation mechanism by writing it from memory in five sentences repeatedly until the order survives time pressure, since the order is what collapses under stress. Then carry three objections with their targets specified. Then practise on real past questions rather than invented ones, because the phrasing of actual questions is more constrained than candidates expect and pattern recognition across them is worth more than additional content.

### Q: Do you need to mention the third volume in an answer on Capital Volume One?

One sentence, and it is among the highest-value sentences available. Noting that the first volume proceeds on the assumption of exchange at values in order to analyse the origin of surplus, while the third volume introduces prices of production that diverge systematically from values, demonstrates that the candidate understands the architecture of the work rather than a chapter of it. It also pre-empts the most common objection a marker might expect a candidate to have missed. Do not go further than this, since the third volume's content is a different topic and a paragraph on it is a paragraph off-question.

### Q: How should a short-answer or objective question on surplus value be handled?

With a definition and nothing else. Surplus value is the difference between the value created by the exercise of labour power and the value of the labour power itself, which the capitalist purchased at its full value. If a formula is invited, the rate of surplus value is the ratio of surplus value to variable capital. Resist the pull toward context, examples, or evaluation, none of which is being asked for in this format. The commonest error in short-answer responses is over-answering, which wastes time that longer questions on the same paper will need.

### Q: What is the difference between surplus value and profit in an exam answer?

Surplus value is the aggregate unpaid labour appropriated in production, expressed in value terms. Profit is a form that surplus value takes once it has been redistributed among capitals through competition and measured against total capital advanced rather than against variable capital alone. They are not synonyms, and treating them as interchangeable removes the analytical content of the concept. In the first volume they can be treated as coinciding for the purposes of the argument; the divergence is introduced in the third volume with the equalisation of the profit rate. A candidate who states this distinction cleanly has shown control that most scripts lack.

### Q: How do you handle a question comparing Marx with Weber on capitalism?

State both positions in the terms their own defenders would use rather than as caricatures, identify the genuine point of difference rather than the differences that are only vocabulary, name the criterion on which you are judging, and then judge. The step almost every candidate skips is naming the criterion. Where the comparison concerns class, the difference worth drawing is between a relation to the means of production and a position in a market determined by marketable skills. Avoid the trap of narrating the two theories in sequence, which is the commonest structural failure in comparison answers at every level.

### Q: Should a candidate mention that Marx did not predict what happened in socialist states?

Only if the question invites it, and then carefully. In an answer about the value theory or about exploitation, the historical record of any state is off-topic and will read as padding. Where a question does ask about the relation between Marx's theory and its later application, the discipline that earns marks is keeping the two questions analytically separate: what the theory claims is one matter, what states claiming the tradition did is another, and neither settles the other. A candidate who states that separation explicitly demonstrates exactly the analytical control that questions of this kind are designed to test.

### Q: What should a teacher do about the fairness intuition students bring to this topic?

Surface it before delivering any content, because it is persistent and it distorts everything students hear afterwards. Ask what the theory predicts about a good produced by a firm using slow, obsolete equipment. Students holding the moral reading say it should be more expensive because more labour went into it; the theory says the excess labour is not socially necessary, does not count, and the firm loses money. That single exchange teaches the qualifying clause more effectively than exposition, and it also opens the distinction between a descriptive theory of how a system works and a normative claim about what people deserve.

### Q: Is it worth learning quotations from Capital for an exam?

One short, accurate, correctly attributed quotation is worth having, and more than that is a poor use of memory. Long extracts consume the word budget and demonstrate transcription rather than understanding, and a misremembered quotation is actively harmful because it suggests the candidate is working from an impression rather than a text. The better investment is precision about where an argument sits, since a marker can verify a location and cannot verify a half-remembered sentence. If one quotation is carried, make it a short definitional one from the passage on labour power, which is the passage most likely to be needed.
