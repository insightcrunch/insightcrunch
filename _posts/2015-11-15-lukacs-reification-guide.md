Reification is the word Georg Lukacs gave to the process by which a world human beings have made comes to stand over against them as a world of things, governed by laws of its own. Lukacs was a Hungarian philosopher writing in the nineteen twenties, and the concept he built in those years extends an argument of Marx's about commodities into an account of consciousness and social form: what begins, in Marx, as an analysis of how products confront producers in exchange becomes, in Lukacs, a claim about how the whole texture of modern life is organised and how the mind itself is shaped by that organisation. It is not a synonym for alienation, though the two are persistently confused, and the difference between them matters enough that this profile separates the concepts before it tells the life. The sources Lukacs worked from can be followed in this series: the commodity argument itself is treated in [commodity fetishism explained](/2010/08/01/commodity-fetishism-explained/), and the concept with which reification is so often merged is treated in the [guide to Marx on alienation](/2011/04/01/marx-on-alienation-complete-guide/). What neither of those articles can supply is the man, and the man is unusually difficult to hold steady in the mind, because the career that produced the concept also produced its author's repeated public disavowals of it.

Two facts organise everything that follows, and a reader who keeps them in view will understand both the achievement and the difficulty. The first is that Lukacs developed reification without having read the early manuscripts of Marx, in which the theory of alienation is set out, because those manuscripts lay unpublished in an archive and did not appear in print until nine years after his own book. Working from the opening chapter of Capital and from the sociology of rationalisation he had absorbed in Germany, he arrived independently at the problematic that the young Marx had stated in private notebooks eighty years before, and he later described that convergence as remarkable. The second fact is that he publicly repudiated the book that made his reputation, and did so more than once across five decades, under pressures that make the repudiations genuinely hard to interpret: each one can be read as the price of survival inside a movement that shot people for less, and each can also be read as an honest stage in a long intellectual life that really did move away from the positions of his youth. This profile states the record, including the record of his conduct as a political commissar in a revolutionary government, as plainly as the record allows, gives both readings of the recantations their full weight, and declines to decide between them, because the evidence does not decide between them and pretending otherwise would be a disservice to a reader who has come for the truth of the matter.

## The concept before the life: reification stated plainly

A thinker profile usually begins with a birth, and this one will reach the birth soon enough, but reification is a concept that punishes readers who meet it first as biography. Stated in its canonical form, reification is Lukacs's term for the process by which relations between people take on the character of relations between things, so that a socially produced world confronts its producers as an autonomous second nature governed by laws they can observe but not alter. Every element of that sentence carries weight. The relations are between people, first of all: the wage contract, the market price, the bureaucratic file, the legal obligation are all connections among human beings, established by human practice and sustained by it daily. In reified form they take on the character of things: the price appears as a property of the commodity, the file appears as a fact of the world rather than a decision someone made, the wage appears as the natural measure of a person's labour rather than as the outcome of a social arrangement. And the result confronts its producers as a second nature, a made environment that behaves with the independence of the first one, so that the beings who collectively sustain it experience it as weather: observable, calculable in its regularities, and not to be argued with.

### What does the German word Verdinglichung add to the English term?

Verdinglichung is built from Ding, the German word for a thing, and it names a making into a thing rather than the discovery of a thing already there. The English reification, drawn from the Latin res, keeps the same root but loses the verbal force: Lukacs's term describes a process that societies perform, not a property that objects possess.

The verbal force matters because it locates responsibility without assigning blame in the manner of a morality tale. A process has agents and mechanisms; it can be traced, described in its stages, and in principle interrupted. When Lukacs writes of Verdinglichung he is not saying that modern people suffer from an illusion, a false picture in the head that better information could dispel. He is saying that the social world really is organised in a form that makes human relations appear, and in appearing partly become, relations between objects. The appearance is objective. A trader watching prices move is not hallucinating when he treats the price as a property of the thing traded; the market is built so that the social labour embodied in the thing returns to its producers in no other shape than the price. The concept therefore refuses the comfort of the word illusion, and this refusal is one source of its continuing power: it explains why intelligent, informed people can know perfectly well that prices are social and still live their working days inside a world that behaves as if prices were natural facts.

Lukacs develops the concept in three dimensions, and keeping them distinct is the quickest defence against the summaries that flatten it. The first dimension is objective. A world of relations between things stands over against the people whose relations they are, and it acquires what he calls a phantom objectivity: an autonomy so complete that it seems to erase every trace of its human foundation. The market is the model case, but the model is meant to generalise, because the commodity structure, in his argument, does not remain a local feature of exchange. It becomes the universal category of society as a whole wherever production for exchange organises the labour process, and the objectivity it generates is phantom precisely because nothing supernatural is involved: the autonomy is real in its effects and manufactured in its origin, and holding both halves of that sentence at once is the discipline the concept demands.

The phrase second nature, which the definition leans on, carries a scholarly genealogy the profile should acknowledge, because Lukacs chose it from a tradition he knew intimately. The term descends from classical antiquity's account of habit, the acquired nature that education builds upon the given one, and it passes through Hegel's philosophy of right, where the institutions of ethical life are described as a second nature, a world of spirit produced by spirit itself and confronting each generation as the element it must inhabit. Lukacs's use retains the Hegelian skeleton and inverts its valuation. For Hegel the second nature of institutions is the home of freedom, the medium in which the rational will learns to be at home with itself. For Lukacs the second nature of the commodity world is freedom's counterfeit: a home built by human hands that its inhabitants experience as a wilderness of laws, at home nowhere in it precisely because its rationality is the rationality of things. The inversion is characteristic of the essay's method throughout, taking the categories of the classical tradition at full strength and reversing their sign by exhibiting the social form that fills them, and it explains the peculiar tone of the whole, at once deeply Hegelian and at war with Hegel's reconciliation: the system is honoured by being read as the truthful register of a world whose truth is its untruth. A reader who hears the genealogy will also hear why the concept could not be a psychology. A second nature is not believed in or doubted. It is lived in, as the first one is, and philosophies that mistake it for an opinion will be refuted by every working day.

The second dimension is subjective, and it is the one that carries the argument from economics into an account of consciousness. In a society ordered by the commodity form, a person's own activity, above all his labour power, becomes something he owns as an object and sells as one. The worker does not merely work; he watches his own capacity for work function as a possession whose performance he monitors, as a manager might monitor a machine's output, and the split this introduces into the subject is the subjective face of reification. The stance that corresponds to it Lukacs names contemplative. The reified subject confronts processes that run according to laws he can learn, predict, and exploit at the margins, but the processes themselves stand outside his reach, and his posture toward them is the posture of an observer before a mechanism: attentive, calculating, and structurally excluded from authorship of the whole.

### Why is the contemplative stance more than passivity?

The contemplative stance is not laziness or apathy. It is the posture of a subject who confronts a finished system of objects and laws, calculates within its rules, and never meets the system as something human beings made and could remake. The worker, the official, and the philosopher are each trained into it by the forms they inhabit.

The distinction matters because the usual objection to the concept misses its target. Critics picture the reified worker as a dupe, sunk in false beliefs about the market's fairness, and they reply triumphantly that actual workers are often cynical, informed, and rebellious. Lukacs's claim survives that reply untouched, because the claim was never about the content of belief. A worker may curse the market in the most lucid terms and still, at the bench, relate to his own labour power as an object with a measurable output, plan his life around the fluctuations of a labour market he experiences as climate, and treat the organisation of production as a technical given rather than a human decision. The contemplative stance is a form of practice before it is a form of opinion, and it is reproduced not by propaganda but by the daily structure of a world in which one's own capacities are commodities and the connections between people reach them as connections between things. When the concept is stated in this stronger form, the search for its evidence changes: one looks not for false beliefs but for the shape of activities, for the places where calculation replaces judgement and where the question of who made the arrangement, and whether it could be made otherwise, has quietly ceased to arise.

The third dimension is the extension, and it is the boldest part of the argument. The rationalisation that the factory imposes on the labour process, breaking skilled work into calculable partial operations governed by abstract rules, does not stay inside the factory. Lukacs traces the same form through the modern state, where administration handles citizens as cases under general rules; through law, where formal rationality prizes the predictability of the decision over the substance of the dispute; through journalism, where the reporter experiences events as material for a product manufactured against the clock; and finally into philosophy itself, where the great systems of German thought reproduce, in their antinomies, the structure of a world split between laws that can be known and a reality that cannot be reached by the knower. The antinomies of bourgeois thought, his name for the impasses of classical philosophy, are for Lukacs not mistakes made by careless thinkers but the signature of reification at the level of thought: freedom against necessity, form against content, subject against object, each pair frozen because the thinking that produced them was itself conducted from the contemplative standpoint.

The provinces deserve a slower walk, because the extension is where readers either convert or rebel. Take the law. A legal order organised on the reified pattern prizes, above every other virtue, the predictability of its decisions: the citizen, and above all the commercial citizen, must be able to calculate in advance how a court will treat a contract, a debt, a dismissal. To secure that calculability the law is rebuilt as a closed system of provisions, administered by a specialised caste of jurists whose craft consists in subsuming the unruly matter of disputes under general rules, and whose professional honour forbids them to ask whether the rules themselves are just. Lukacs, who had read his Weber on formal legal rationality, insists that this is not a corruption of law by commerce but the same form appearing in a second province: the case is to the judge what the workpiece is to the operative, a singular thing processed by a rationality that must not pause to contemplate the whole it serves. The justice that results is real, and its limits are the system's limits, visible whenever a dispute arrives whose substance the provisions cannot price, a wrong that fits no heading in the code.

### How does reification appear inside a courtroom?

It appears as the rule that decides before the case is heard. A body of law broken into calculable provisions, applied by specialists trained to exclude the matter the provisions cannot price, delivers decisions whose correctness is internal to the system. The judge contemplates the code as the worker contemplates the machine: both operate a rationality neither authored.

Administration generalises the pattern into the texture of ordinary life. The modern office does not encounter persons; it encounters files, and the file, not the person, is what the machinery can process. A biography becomes a sequence of entitlements and infractions, each attached to a paragraph of regulation, each decidable without knowledge of the whole life from which it was abstracted. The official's contemplative stance is the exact counterpart of the worker's: he operates a system whose laws he did not make, experiences its outputs as necessities, and prides himself, in the ethic Weber anatomised, on a dutiful impersonality that Lukacs reads as the subjective form of the file's objectivity. Journalism completes the triptych in the realm of consciousness itself. The reporter of the great metropolitan press, Lukacs observed from his own years in the newspaper world of two capitals, does not experience events and then report them; the production schedule precedes the event, the column's shape dictates what can count as news, and the journalist's own judgement is processed in advance by the form of the commodity his labour produces. That the analysis was written before the age of the continuous news cycle gives it, for later readers, the character of a prediction fulfilled by the industry's own development, though the profile confines itself to the claim as made: where experience itself is manufactured for sale, the contemplative stance ceases to be a posture toward the world and becomes the world's own method of appearing.

Two sources are fused in this construction, and naming them explains both its reach and its risks. From Marx's commodity chapter comes the founding insight that social relations assume the fantastic form of relations between things, an insight the series treats in its own right in the article on [commodity fetishism](/2010/08/01/commodity-fetishism-explained/). From the German sociology of the period, above all from the analysis of money in the work of Georg Simmel and the account of rationalisation in the work of Max Weber, comes the generalisation: the insight that calculability, abstraction, and formal rule are the characteristic products of a money economy and a rationalised administration, spreading outward from the economy into every province of life. Lukacs's originality lies in the weld. Where the sociologists described rationalisation as the fate of the modern world, to be recorded with whatever private regrets, Lukacs read the same process through the commodity form and found in it a historically specific structure with a historically specific author: the capitalist organisation of production. What sociology presented as destiny, the weld with Marx presented as a product, and products can be unmade. The essay's intellectual electricity comes from that reversal, and so does its vulnerability, since the weld is exactly where the strongest objection to the whole construction will later fasten.

### Is reification the same as commodity fetishism?

no. Commodity fetishism is Marx's account of how social relations appear as properties of things in exchange. Reification is Lukacs's extension of that argument into a claim about consciousness, social institutions, and thought itself, drawing also on the sociology of rationalisation.

The short answer deserves a slower one, because the conflation is the commonest error in the entire literature of summary, and the profile will return to it in the table that follows. Commodity fetishism, in Marx, names a specific appearance generated in exchange: because producers work independently and meet only through the market, the social character of their labour appears to them as a property of the products themselves, so that value seems to inhere in things as weight does. The analysis is localised, precise, and complete within its scope; it explains the form of appearance of one relation. Reification takes that localised analysis as its foundation and builds a general theory of social form upon it. It asks what happens to a whole society, its institutions, and the consciousness of its members when the commodity structure ceases to be one structure among others and becomes the organising principle of the whole. The concept therefore contains fetishism as its kernel and exceeds it in every direction: in scope, from exchange to the totality; in register, from the appearance of products to the constitution of subjects; and in consequence, from an explanation of economic illusion to an account of why philosophy itself develops the impasses it does. A reader who keeps the kernel distinct from the construction built on it is equipped to follow the rest of this profile, because the career of the concept, and the strange career of its author, both turn on the construction and not on the kernel alone.

## The worked example: the worker before the machine

Abstract statements of the concept persuade less than its central example, and the essay builds its case on a scene it returns to with the patience of a demonstration: the worker at the machine in the rationalised factory. The scene's elements are chosen with care. Under the handicraft order the worker's skill inhered in his person, the product bore the shape of his judgement, and time was measured by the task. Under the factory order that the essay anatomises, the labour process is first decomposed, in analysis, into its simplest repeatable motions; the motions are then recombined as a system that stands over against every individual worker as a finished mechanism; and the worker is inserted into the system at the point the decomposition assigned him, with his own qualities, his skill, his judgement, his fatigue, entering the calculation only as sources of error to be engineered away. What the worker now produces is not an object in any sense his activity can survey. He performs a partial operation on a product he never sees whole, at a tempo set by the mechanism, in a working day whose time has been rendered abstract, homogeneous, and exactly measurable, a quantity of pure duration into which his life is poured by the hour. The essay's phrase for the result is that the worker's labour becomes contemplative: he watches the system that employs his hands, and his own activity reaches him as the behaviour of the object he tends.

The demonstration's power lies in its refusal to stop at the factory gate, and the profile follows it one step further than summaries usually do, into the interior of the subject the scene produces. A man whose working hours are spent as the attendant of a mechanism he did not design, producing objects he does not recognise, at a tempo he does not set, does not leave that relation at the factory door like a coat. The stance generalises inward. His own capacities present themselves to him as assets to be managed, his time as a stock to be allocated, his biography as a sequence of positions in systems administered by others; the self becomes one more object in the world of objects, contemplated with the same calculating detachment the mechanism taught. This interior generalisation is the point at which the essay's concept separates itself decisively from a sociology of factory discipline, and it is also the point at which the subjective dimension of reification locks into the objective one: the second nature outside and the contemplative stance inside are one structure described from its two poles, and the identical subject object, when it arrives in the argument, is the proposal for how a being formed at both poles might know the structure as its own product. Readers who grasp the worked example possess the concept; readers who skip to the antinomies possess its shadow. The profile therefore records the scene at length, and commends it to students as the passage by which the whole essay can be reconstructed under examination pressure, since every other element, the phantom objectivity, the stance, the extension, the standpoint, is the scene unfolded.

The worked example has a temporal half that the profile must not omit, since its author regarded it as the demonstration's completion. In the rationalised process, time undergoes the same abstraction as the object. It ceases to be the variable, qualitative flow in which tasks ripen and skills mature, and becomes a homogeneous, exactly measurable continuum, divisible into units that can be priced, compared, and optimised; the working day is filled with time as a vessel is filled with water, and the worker's life inside the shift is the experience of a quantity passing. Lukacs draws the consequence for the subject with his usual relentlessness. A being whose activity is measured as the expenditure of an abstract quantity will come to experience its own life, outside the factory as inside it, under the form of that quantity: time as a stock that is spent, saved, wasted, and killed, in the idioms every modern language keeps ready. The contemplative stance has its calendar as well as its posture, and the analysis of time is the point at which the essay's readers most often report the shock of recognition, since the experience it describes is the one piece of the factory that every reader, of whatever class, carries home. The profile notes the shock without exploiting it. A description's familiarity is not its proof, and the proof, here as elsewhere in the essay, is the derivation: if labour power is a commodity, its measure must be an abstract quantum, and the consciousness of the seller will be formed by the measure. The chain is short, and every link is inspectable, which is why this portion of the argument has survived even the critics who reject the crown.

## The essay as literature: a rhetoric of totality

A word on the instrument itself is owed, because the reification essay's prose is part of its argument and part of its danger. The essay is written in the high Hegelian manner its author had mastered before he was a Marxist: long periodic sentences in which a phenomenon is first exhibited in its immediacy, then turned against itself by the mediation it conceals, then gathered into a wider whole that re-describes the starting point. The manner is not decoration. It performs the thesis. A reader who submits to the sentences experiences, in miniature, the passage from the contemplated fragment to the comprehended totality that the essay asserts as the movement of consciousness itself, and the persuasion the essay exercises has always been partly this performative one, the feeling of a mind watching its own categories dissolve and reform at a higher level of concreteness. The same manner exacts the price the strongest objection collects. Prose that performs totality can simulate the possession of it, and the cadence that carries a reader from the factory to the Critique can carry him across a gap in the argument with the same rhythm as across a demonstration, the join concealed by the music. Lukacs's critics have often located the book's power in exactly this rhetoric, and its defenders have often conceded the location while denying the inference, holding that a dialectical exposition is entitled to its form because the form is the content's own movement made audible. The profile takes no side in the quarrel over the prose, but it records the quarrel because a reader forewarned reads better: the essay should be read twice, once with the current and once against it, the second reading marking every place where the sentence's momentum, rather than a premise, did the work of transition. Few books survive that second reading with their stature increased. This one, in the judgement the profile has been building, is among the few, since the places where the music carries the argument are also the places its author marked, by returning to them across fifty years of self criticism, as the questions he had genuinely opened.

## The antinomies: the essay's philosophical crown

The boldest stretch of the reification essay, and the one on which the strongest objection fastens, is its final movement, in which the argument ascends from institutions to philosophy itself. Lukacs's claim is that the great systems of classical German philosophy, the tradition he knew most intimately, reproduce in the structure of their problems the structure of the reified world, and that their celebrated impasses are not accidents of individual genius failing at a solvable task but the necessary shape of thought conducted from the contemplative standpoint. He calls these impasses the antinomies of bourgeois thought, and the essay works through them as a prosecutor works through exhibits. There is the antinomy of subject and object: the knower confronts a world of objects whose laws are complete without him, and his knowledge, however exact, leaves him outside the reality he knows, a spectator of being. There is the antinomy of freedom and necessity: the same world that denies the subject any authorship of its processes is required, by the moral philosophy built upon it, to house a freedom that must be real if responsibility is to mean anything, and the system shuttles between a determinism that abolishes the moral agent and a moral agent exiled to a realm beyond experience. There is the antinomy of form and content: rational systems perfect their forms by emptying them, achieving universality at the price of the concrete matter that alone gives the forms anything to be about, so that the more rigorous the method becomes, the less of the world it can touch.

The reading of Kant that organises these exhibits deserves to be stated in its own terms, because it is the hinge between the essay's sociology and its philosophy. In Kant's critical system, the thing in itself stands behind the appearances as their unknowable ground: the mind legislates the forms under which objects can appear, and pays for that legislation with the confession that reality as it is in itself escapes it forever. Lukacs reads this confession as the philosophical registration of the reified standpoint. The contemplative subject of the commodity society, confronting a second nature whose laws he can calculate but whose genesis he cannot recover, finds his own predicament enthroned as the structure of reason itself: a world fully knowable in its appearances, fully alien in its essence, and a subject whose dignity must therefore be located in a freedom that the knowable world cannot accommodate. The antinomies are, on this reading, the points where the system honestly records its own impossibility, and the subsequent history of German philosophy, the attempts of Fichte and Hegel to overcome the Kantian dualism by making the subject the author of its world, becomes the record of thought straining against the walls of the contemplative standpoint from inside. Hegel's dialectic, in which subject and object are moments of a single self producing process, is for Lukacs the summit of that straining: the point at which philosophy formulated, in the medium of pure thought, the overcoming that only a practical transformation of the standpoint could accomplish in reality.

A reader does not have to accept the derivation to feel its force as an interpretation. The essay's Kant is recognisably the philosopher the nineteenth century wrestled with, and the mapping of the antinomies onto the structure of the commodity world gives the history of philosophy a sudden, unsettling coherence, as if a single pressure had bent a century of disparate systems into a family of shapes. That coherence is the essay's glory and its danger in one. It is the glory, because a theory of social form that can order the history of philosophy as one of its provinces has demonstrated a reach no rival account of the period attempts. It is the danger, because coherence of that kind is exactly what a powerful analogy produces in the absence of a mechanism, and the essay supplies the mechanism, the generalisation of the contemplative stance, at a level of generality that leaves the last steps of the derivation, from the factory's discipline to the Critique's architecture, to be taken on the reader's faith in the pattern. The profile's hearing of the strongest objection, in its place below, returns to this span and measures it. Here it is enough to fix what the crown of the essay claims: not that philosophers erred, but that they thought, with a fidelity that amounted to genius, the exact thoughts their world's form prescribed, and that the prescription can be read off their systems once the form itself has been named.

## Nature and science within the concept's reach

One province of the argument requires separate treatment because it generated the most consequential misunderstanding of the book, the charge that its author had dissolved nature into society. The reification essay contains a notorious aside to the effect that nature is a societal category: that what counts, in any epoch, as nature, what nature means for the knowledge and practice of the epoch, is mediated by the epoch's social forms. Read as the Deborin school read it, the sentence denies the material world's independence and convicts its author of idealism in a single line. Read in the essay's own economy, the sentence belongs to the theory of knowledge and not to cosmology: it concerns the category, the form under which nature is present to a society, the decision, itself historical, about which of the world's aspects count as nature and which as artifice, which as lawful necessity and which as human material. The distinction is not a quibble invented by apologists, since the essay elsewhere treats the material world with a realist's respect and founds its whole construction on the objectivity of the forms it analyses; but neither is the aside innocent, and the profile should register what it cost. A philosophy that had relocated the centre of Marxism from the economy of things to the forms of objectivity had made the status of nature genuinely difficult for itself, and the essay never resolves the difficulty, preferring to work at the altitude where the category, not the cosmos, is the object. The later Lukacs resolved it by capitulation in the authorised direction, affirming the dialectics of nature he had doubted; the resolution satisfied the institution and settled nothing philosophically, and the question his aside raised, how a theory of social form should house the sciences of a nature that precedes all form, remains on the agenda his book bequeathed. Readers meeting the charge of idealism in the summaries should know that it fastens on this province, and that the province is the concept's frontier rather than its foundation: the derivation from the commodity form neither needs nor supplies a philosophy of nature, and the essay's silence where one should be is a genuine incompleteness, of the kind that research programmes, unlike systems, are permitted to carry.

## The three concepts separated

The table below is the instrument this profile exists to provide, and it should be used without mercy against the summaries. Three concepts circulate in the discussion of the young Marx and his readers: commodity fetishism, which is Marx's; alienation, which is Marx's in a different text; and reification, which is Lukacs's construction on Marx's foundation. They are related, and the relations are the substance of the matter, but they are not interchangeable, and each has a distinct author, a distinct source text, a distinct claim, and a distinct level of society at which it operates. The commonest confusion in each case is named in the final column, because naming the confusion is the fastest way to retire it.

**The three concepts separated**

| Concept | Author | Source text | What is claimed | Level at which it operates | Commonest confusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Commodity fetishism | Karl Marx | The first chapter of Capital, volume one | Social relations between producers appear as relations between the things they produce and exchange | The sphere of exchange and the form taken by the product | Mistaken for a complete theory of consciousness under capitalism |
| Alienation | Karl Marx | The manuscripts of his Paris period, unpublished for nearly ninety years | The worker is estranged from the product of labour, from the activity of labouring, from his species life, and from other people | The labour process and the worker's relation to his own activity | Mistaken for a mood of dissatisfaction or a complaint about unpleasant work |
| Reification | Georg Lukacs | The central essay of History and Class Consciousness | The commodity structure penetrates the whole of society and remakes consciousness itself in its image | Society as a whole, including law, administration, journalism, and philosophy | Mistaken for alienation under another name, or for a state of individual psychology |

Read down the column of claims and the shape of the construction becomes visible. Fetishism explains an appearance: why the social character of labour shows up as a property of objects. Alienation describes a relation: the worker's separation from what he makes, from the making, from his own realised capacities, and from his fellows, all inside the labour process itself. Reification asserts a penetration: the form detected in exchange and in labour spreads until it supplies the model for the state, the courtroom, the newspaper office, and the philosophical system. The three claims can be held together consistently, and Lukacs's essay is best read as an attempt to hold the first and third together by deriving the third from the first. Whether the derivation succeeds is the question the strongest objection presses, and it is a question about the middle step, the penetration, not about the kernel.

Read across the row of levels and the scholarly stakes come into view. Because alienation operates at the level of the labour process, it can be investigated wherever labour is organised, and its presence in the early manuscripts raised the famous question of how the young Marx of the notebooks stands to the mature Marx of Capital. Because reification operates at the level of the social whole, its evidence is institutional and philosophical as much as economic, and its argument requires a category the other two do not: the totality, the whole within which the penetration can be followed from province to province. And because fetishism operates at the level of the product's form, it remains the most tightly specified of the three, the one whose mechanism can be stated in a page and tested against the daily experience of exchange. Students who arrive with the three fused into a single vague complaint about modern life leave the table with three instruments of different gauges, and the rest of this profile assumes the separation the table performs.

## Reification is not a state of mind

### Why is reification not a state of mind?

Because its location is not inside any head. The prices, files, ranks, and procedures that organise an order exist whether or not any individual believes in them, and a person may see through them and have to live by them. Psychology registers the order's effects; it is not the order, and curing attitudes would leave the machinery where it stood.

The correction is worth a section of its own because the psychological reading is the form in which the concept most often escapes into general use, and in escaping it loses the property that made it worth coining. In the diluted usage, to call something reified is to accuse somebody of a mental error: the manager who reifies his workforce is thinking of people as numbers, the economist who reifies the market is mistaking a model for the world, and the remedy implied is a change of mind, a recovery of human warmth or conceptual care. Lukacs's concept points in the opposite direction. The manager does not first think of his workforce as a set of numbers and then, in consequence, organise the plant by measurable outputs; the plant is organised by measurable outputs because the commodity form of labour power requires it, and the manager's numerical habits of mind are the deposit that organisation leaves in him. Reverse the causation and the politics dissolves into etiquette: instead of a structure to be changed, there is only a discourtesy to be regretted, and the most radical sounding word in the theory's vocabulary becomes a synonym for insensitivity.

The objective character of the appearance is the load bearing point, and Marx's kernel already contains it. When the social character of labour appears as a property of the product, the appearance is not a mistake that the producers make about an otherwise transparent process; it is the form the process itself takes, given that the producers meet only in exchange. A society cannot vote to perceive value as a relation while continuing to organise production so that value can only appear as a property of things. Lukacs generalises exactly this objectivity. The contemplative stance is subjectively real, but it is real as the interior of an exterior arrangement, and the test he implicitly proposes for any use of the concept is directional: does the analysis run from the form to the consciousness, or from the consciousness to the form. Readings that run the second way, however sympathetic, are describing alienation of the feelings, or false consciousness, or plain thoughtlessness, and each of those has its own name and its own article's worth of distinctions. The concept's severity is its identity, and a profile that let it soften into a psychology would have failed its subject at the point where he was most himself: a thinker for whom the world of things was a world people made, which is why it could be remade, and why merely thinking differently about it was the one remedy his theory explicitly refuses to promise.

## The comparative audit: what the concept explains that its rivals do not

A concept earns its keep against its neighbours, and reification has three neighbours whose territories it is persistently said to usurp. The first is false consciousness, the older movement term for the workers' failure to perceive their interests, which explains the failure as a defect of information or of honesty, implanted by the ruling ideas of the age. Reification explains the same failure without any implantation: a consciousness formed inside the contemplative stance will reproduce the form of that stance in its most lucid moments, and no quantity of correct information, absorbed from within the stance, dissolves the stance itself. The difference decides a practical question that divided the movement for a century: whether the remedy for the failure is propaganda, the delivery of true ideas to minds prepared by false ones, or a transformation of the practices that form the minds. The second neighbour is Weber's rationalisation, from which the concept borrowed so much that the debt is sometimes mistaken for the whole. Weber's process has no author and no exit: it is the fate of a civilisation that has chosen calculation, and its iron cage closes on bureaucrat and entrepreneur alike with the impartiality of a natural law. Lukacs's weld with the commodity form gives the same process an author, the historically specific organisation of production, and therefore a conceivable outside: what a determinate social form built, a determinate social transformation could unbuild. Whether the outside exists is the question the standpoint doctrine answers, too confidently, as the hearing below records; but the difference between a fate and a product is the difference between the two theories, and it is not a small one.

The third neighbour is the oldest, and the comparison is the most instructive because the two men never met in print on the point. Simmel's tragedy of culture describes the same landscape as the reification essay: an objective culture of things, institutions, and knowledges that grows by its own logic until it towers over the subjective culture that must assimilate it, so that the individual is crushed, in Simmel's melancholy formula, beneath the weight of the civilisation his kind produced. Lukacs had sat in Simmel's lectures and absorbed the diagnosis entire. What he added was the mechanism and the address. For Simmel the tragedy is a permanent structure of cultural life as such, the disproportion between the objective and the subjective spirit, and its consolation is the diagnostician's lucidity. For Lukacs the disproportion has a date and a form: it is the commodity structure's penetration of the whole, and the subjective spirit's crushing is the contemplative stance, which is a position inside a process and not the human condition. The audit can be summarised as a rule of thumb for readers sorting the period's theories. Where a diagnosis of the modern world's thinghood ends in melancholy, it belongs to the sociology of fate; where it ends in a standpoint and a summons, it belongs to Lukacs, and the price of the summons, the imputed consciousness and its party, is itemised elsewhere in this profile. The concept's distinctiveness is exactly this refusal to let the diagnosis be the end of the matter, and every rival that shares the diagnosis while declining the summons defines, by the declension, what reification was built to add.

## A Budapest formation: Lukacs before Marxism

### Where did the young Lukacs begin, if not in politics?

He began in the theatre seats and salons of Budapest, as a critic of drama and a philosopher of literary form. His first books treat the essay, the modern drama, and the novel, and his early reputation rested on them. Politics entered a life already formed by aesthetics, which is why his Marxism kept a literary intelligence at its centre.

Georg Lukacs was born in Budapest in 1885, into a family whose circumstances placed him at the summit of the assimilated Jewish bourgeoisie of the Austro Hungarian monarchy. His father, born Loewinger and ennobled as von Lukacs, directed the Budapest operations of a great banking house and accumulated the kind of fortune that buys a son unlimited education and no necessity of earning. The household was cultivated, multilingual, and oriented toward Vienna and the German culture of the empire rather than toward any Jewish communal life, and the son's precocity was of the kind such households are built to produce: gymnasium essays on drama while still a schoolboy, a book on the development of modern drama published when he was in his mid twenties, and a growing reputation in the literary life of two capitals before he was thirty. The misreading that casts him as a literary critic who strayed into philosophy inverts the sequence. The philosophy was there from the start, working through literary objects because those were the objects his formation supplied; the dramatic form, the essay form, and the novel form were his first laboratories for questions about how a culture's forms shape the souls that live inside them, and a reader of the reification essay who hears its literary intelligence is hearing a continuity, not a detour.

The early masterwork of this period is The Theory of the Novel, composed in the first winter of the Great War and published as a book after it. Its argument, that the novel is the epic form of a world from which meaning has withdrawn, the story of a problematic individual searching for significance in a reality that no longer answers, belongs to the philosophy of history wearing literary dress. Alongside it stands The Soul and Form, a collection of essays on writers that takes the essay itself as its subject: a form, Lukacs argued, in which criticism becomes a judgement on life conducted through the occasion of a book. These are works of a thinker steeped in Kant, in Kierkegaard, in the German classical tradition, and in the Budapest and Heidelberg circles where the crisis of that tradition was the standing topic. He moved in the orbit of the Sunday Circle in Budapest, where the young Karl Mannheim and Karl Polanyi also argued their way toward their futures, and in Heidelberg he entered the circle around Max Weber, whose household received him and whose categories would later supply, alongside Simmel's, the sociological half of the reification concept.

The Budapest circle deserves a sentence of its own in any honest roster of the formation, because it functioned as the first draft of the intellectual world he would spend his life trying to rebuild inside a party. The Sunday Circle, which met in the apartment of the film theorist Bela Balazs, gathered the generation that would scatter across the century's disciplines: Karl Mannheim toward the sociology of knowledge, Karl Polanyi toward economic anthropology, Arnold Hauser toward the social history of art, and a company of poets, critics, and philosophers around them, arguing through the war years about the soul, the artwork, and the collapse of the liberal world that had educated them all. The circle's premise, that the crisis of European culture was total and required a total answer, survived every member's subsequent politics, and in Lukacs's case it supplied the template of the totality argument before the argument had a Marxist content: the conviction, formed in discussion with friends he would argue with for fifty years, that the provinces of modern life stand or fall together. Several of the circle's members reappear in this profile at the stations where their paths crossed his again, in exile journals and in the expressionism debate, and the pattern is not accidental. The emigration of a whole intellectual generation, and its partial reassemblies in Vienna, Berlin, Moscow, and Paris, is the social form in which the ideas of this cluster travelled, and the reification essay's own itinerary, from a Budapest formation through a Vienna exile to a Moscow editorial desk, is one thread of that larger cloth.

What Heidelberg gave him deserves emphasis, because the weld described earlier was assembled there. From Simmel, whose lectures he had attended in Berlin and whose Philosophy of Money he knew intimately, came the analysis of how a money economy trains its participants in abstraction, calculation, and a peculiar blasé distance from the qualities of things. From Weber came rationalisation as a world historical process: the spread of formal, rule bound, calculable procedure through law, administration, and economic life, and the disenchantment that followed in its train. The young Lukacs received these analyses as descriptions of a fate. His pre Marxist work is saturated with the mood of them: forms triumph over life, systems over souls, and the novel's hero wanders a world organised by conventions he did not choose. What the formation lacked was any ground from which the fate could be judged a product rather than a destiny, and any agent by whom it might be resisted. The war supplied the crisis, the Russian Revolution supplied the suggestion of an agent, and the conversion, when it came at the end of 1918, came with the speed of a door opening in a wall he had been describing for a decade. He joined the Communist Party of Hungary within weeks of its foundation, an act he later compared, in a phrase the biographers repeat, to a leap; the literary philosopher of objective forms had decided that the forms had an author, and that the author could be fought.

## The pre Marxist masterworks: form against life

The books of the formation repay closer attention than a biographical summary usually grants them, because the reification essay's deepest habits were acquired in them. The Soul and Form, the essay collection of his twenty fifth year, takes writers as occasions for a single recurring drama: a soul, armed with a longing for a life shaped whole, confronts the forms that alone can give longing a durable shape, and discovers that form is both the fulfilment and the betrayal of the longing, since whatever is formed is fixed, and whatever lives refuses to stay fixed. The essay on Kierkegaard turns on the Danish philosopher's broken engagement, reading the rupture as a man sacrificing a possible happiness to the form of a gesture; the essay on the novelist Theodor Storm weighs a world of craftsmen's integrity against the dissolution of the world that sustained it. Already the signature is present: an intelligence that experiences the relation between life and form as the master problem of the age, and that treats literary works as the evidence on which the problem can be studied in its purest state. When the same intelligence later argued that a social form could penetrate consciousness itself, it was extending a method practised for a decade on poems and plays to the society that produced them.

The Theory of the Novel radicalises the drama into a philosophy of history. Its famous construction sets the epic, in which a world and its meaning are given together and the hero's path is a homecoming, against the novel, in which meaning has withdrawn from the world and the hero's path is a search conducted among things that no longer answer. The novel is therefore, in the book's phrase pattern, the form of a god forsaken age, and its irony, the novelist's godlike knowledge that his creatures seek a significance the world of the book cannot supply, is the ethical signature of the form. Lukacs composed the work in the first winter of the Great War, in a despair he later described as a rejection of the war and of the civilisation that had produced it, and the despair is legible in every chapter: the world of the novel is a second nature of conventions, institutions, and objects, estranged from every soul that moves through it. Readers of the reification essay will recognise the landscape. What the essay of 1923 adds to the literary diagnosis is precisely what the young critic lacked: a mechanism, the commodity form; a history, the expansion of that form; and an agent, the class whose position inside the mechanism might crack it. The continuity explains a feature of the mature work that puzzles readers who come to it from economics: its confidence that philosophy, literature, law, and the factory floor are chapters of one book. For their author they had always been one book, since the days when the book's title was the tragedy of form.

Two further labours of the formation complete the picture and correct the caricature of a critic amusing himself until history called. In Heidelberg, alongside the novel book, Lukacs laboured for years on a systematic aesthetics, a philosophy of art built from the ground up, whose vast manuscript he left unfinished and which appeared in print only long after his death; the project matters because it shows the systematic ambition, the will to a total account, already fully formed before Marxism gave it an object. And throughout the same years he projected a book on Dostoevsky, the Russian novelist standing in his notes for the possibility of a world beyond the novel's forsakenness, a new epic of communal life; the notes for that unwritten book fed The Theory of the Novel and then were set aside, the question they posed still open when its author found, in the Russian Revolution, what looked like the answer arriving by another route. A formation that contains a system of aesthetics in a drawer, a philosophy of the novel in print, and a messianic question addressed to Dostoevsky is not a dilettante's. It is the formation of a thinker who had already decided that the modern world was a totality in distress, and who lacked only the name of its disease and the address of its physician. December 1918 supplied both names at once, and the speed of the conversion, so often read as opportunism or as fashion, reads differently against this record: the questions were a decade old, and the party appeared to their author as the first institution of his life that claimed to be answering them in practice.

## The war: the formation's crucible

No account of the formation is honest that treats the Great War as scenery. Lukacs met the war of 1914 in a state he later described without euphemism, as a despair at the civilisation that had produced it so complete that the war's outbreak registered, in the first shock, almost as a relief: the world of the Budapest and Heidelberg salons, the world of forms triumphant over life, had pronounced its own sentence, and the slaughter was the sentence being carried out. He did not serve at the front. His war was spent in Budapest, in auxiliary service with the military censorship, reading the letters of soldiers for sedition in a back office of the empire whose collapse his desk was documenting, an assignment whose suitability for the future theorist of the contemplative stance has not escaped his biographers. The Theory of the Novel was written in that war's first winter, and its god forsaken world is the war's world transposed into the philosophy of a literary form: the age in which the hero's search for meaning is conducted among objects that have ceased to answer, because the civilisation that made the objects had, in the trenches, withdrawn its own meaning from them. His public interventions of the war years, essays on the German intellectuals and the war among them, circle the question that the revolution would shortly answer for him in the affirmative: whether the catastrophe was the old world's accident or its essence, and whether anything existed that could be built from the ruins by an act of collective will. When the empires fell in 1918 and the Budapest crowd filled the streets, the philosopher of the forsaken world was thirty three years old, armed with a decade of questions and a finished diagnosis of the age's sickness, and the party that presented itself as the age's physician had been founded in a hotel room a few streets from his father's bank. The conversion's speed, on this record, requires no explanation by opportunism. It requires only the war.

One document of the conversion must be entered before the commissariat itself, because it is the moral title deed of everything that follows and its author never disowned it. In December 1918, in the very weeks of the party's foundation, Lukacs published an essay on Bolshevism as a moral problem, and its argument was against the step he was about to take. The Bolshevik wager, he wrote, required its servants to enact evil means in the faith that good ends would issue from them, to answer the world's violence in kind and to trust history to launder the account; and this, the essay held, no one could do with a clear soul, since the means enter the ends and the soul that performs them is itself among the ends. Within weeks he had joined the party whose wager the essay refused, and he spent the rest of his life inside the refusal's consequences. The biographers have made the juxtaposition carry various loads, hypocrisy, tragedy, the Dostoevskian structure of a man enacting the temptation he had anatomised, and the profile confines itself to the fact and its plainest implication. The commissar who would shortly order deserters shot had published, the same winter, the most exact statement in the literature of why such an order cannot be cleansed by its purpose. He did not lack the knowledge. He lacked, or refused, the abstention the knowledge counselled, and the fifty years of recantations and renewed allegiances that followed are, in one of their aspects, the long reverberation of that December: a man paying instalments on a debt he had himself itemised, in print, before he incurred it.

## The commissar: the Hungarian record stated plainly

### How did a literary essayist come to hold a commissar's post within months?

The Hungarian party he joined in December of that revolutionary winter was weeks old and desperately short of educated cadres. A famous intellectual who had written on the war and the collapse of the old order was an asset a small party could not leave unused, and the republic that followed put its intellectuals where its needs were greatest.

The Hungarian Soviet Republic was proclaimed in March 1919, when the collapsing liberal government handed power to a coalition in which the communists, though the smaller partner, held the initiative. It lasted one hundred and thirty three days, besieged from its first week by the armies of the successor states and by the blockade of the victorious powers, and it governed by decree through people's commissariats staffed heavily from the party's thin layer of educated members. Lukacs served first as deputy people's commissar for education and culture, under a social democratic minister, and the educational record of those months is genuinely his: schools were secularised and thrown open, teachers' salaries raised, museums and theatres treated as public goods to be democratised rather than luxuries to be shuttered, and a programme of cultural provision for children and workers set in motion at a pace the republic's circumstances made frantic. The music settlement of those months survives as the emblem of the programme's ambition: the republic's directorate for music numbered among its members Bela Bartok and Zoltan Kodaly, the two composers who were remaking Hungarian music from its folk sources, summoned by a commissariat that regarded the nation's art as a trust to be administered for its citizens rather than as a decoration for its drawing rooms. That the summons was answered, by artists of that stature, in a besieged capital with the armies of the intervention closing in, says something about the moral credit the young republic briefly commanded among the country's intellectuals, and something about the deputy commissar whose office issued the summons: the philosopher of form had become, for one season, the administrator of a nation's culture, and he administered it as if the inheritance of European art were the revolution's to distribute. The profile records this because the later argument of his life, that culture is a battlefield and not an ornament, was first practised here, in decrees written by a thirty four year old philosopher who believed a revolution owed its citizens the inheritance of European art.

Then the record darkens, and this profile states what darkens it without softening or heightening. When the military situation collapsed in the early summer, with Romanian forces advancing toward the capital, Lukacs volunteered for the front and served as political commissar attached to a division of the Red Army on the Tisza line. In that capacity he bore responsibility for the shooting of deserters: soldiers who had abandoned their positions in the face of the enemy were executed on his authority as the division's political officer, an episode the biographical record documents and one that Lukacs himself, in the dictated recollections of his final years, acknowledged as his act and defended as a military necessity of a collapsing front. Accounts of the episode differ in their detail, as accounts of that summer differ in almost everything except its desperation, and this profile follows the series rule for contested episodes: it asserts the responsibility, which is documented and was admitted, and it asserts no tally. What must not be done with the episode is either of the two easy things. It must not be dramatised into an emblem that settles the philosophy, because the philosophy stands or falls on arguments any reader can inspect; and it must not be minimised into an administrative footnote, because men were shot on a philosopher's order, and a profile that aspires to be believed about the convergence of 1923 must be plain about the summer of 1919.

The republic fell in August, and the counter revolution that followed executed and imprisoned on a scale the republic never approached, though the same rule against tallies governs the sentence. Lukacs escaped to Vienna, where he lived the exile's decade of journalism, factional war inside a shattered party, and theoretical work conducted in the intervals of both. Lenin himself criticised the Hungarian party's conduct and Lukacs's contribution to its debates, and the exile years set the pattern of the life that followed: immense theoretical production, permanent institutional insecurity, and a party discipline he accepted as the price of belonging to the only movement he believed could change the world he had analysed. The reader should hold that bargain in mind, because the repudiations that puzzle the assessment of Lukacs are unintelligible without it. A man who had watched a revolution die, who had himself ordered shootings in its defence and hidden from the terror that avenged it, did not experience party discipline as an abstraction. He experienced it as the form survival took, and his recantations, whatever else they were, were made by someone with that knowledge in his body.

## The Vienna decade: the exile's apprenticeship in defeat

Between the fall of the republic and the writing of the essays stands the Vienna exile, and it belongs in the profile because the book's peculiar authority, its air of being written by a man arguing for the movement's soul from inside its defeat, was earned there. Vienna in the years after 1919 held the wreckage of several revolutions at once, and the Hungarian emigration conducted its post mortem in cafes and factional journals with a bitterness proportioned to the stakes it had lost. Lukacs edited the journal Kommunismus, the theoretical organ of the Austrian party's radical wing, and wrote for it the interventions that made his name inside the movement before the book did: essays on the tactics of the revolutionary party, on the Hungarian catastrophe, on the relation of spontaneity to organisation. The tone of those pieces is worth registering. They are not the work of a philosopher condescending to politics. They are the work of a factional combatant of real ferocity, and their author's later reputation for Olympian abstraction should not be allowed to airbrush the combatant away, because the essays' central claim, that consciousness is the decisive terrain, was a combatant's claim, forged in argument with comrades who blamed the defeat on betrayal, on encirclement, on anything except the movement's own understanding of itself.

The emigration's factional geography shaped the exile's options in ways the philosophy alone would not predict. The Hungarian party in exile was split between the following of its fallen leader, Bela Kun, who had carried the republic's government into the catastrophe and retained Moscow's ear, and the faction of Jeno Landler, the railwaymen's leader who had opposed the adventure's worst decisions from inside. Lukacs attached himself to the Landler opposition, and the attachment was more than a career calculation, though it was also that: the Landler circle's critique of the republic's failure, its insistence that the party had substituted its own will for the class's movement and paid the substitution's price, is recognisably the experiential draft of the essays' theory of consciousness, the doctrine of imputation being, among other things, an attempt to state the conditions under which a party may claim to know better than the class without ceasing to be the class's organ. When Landler died in exile in 1928, the faction's protection thinned, and the Blum Theses of the same year, the platform condemned as liquidationist, were drafted by a man whose Hungarian base was disappearing beneath him; the recantation that followed was made, among its other determinations, by an exile whose faction had lost its chief. The profile does not offer the geography as an excuse. It offers it as the kind of fact that the assessment of political intellectuals requires and that pure histories of philosophy omit: ideas in that world travelled in factions, and a man's faction was the material form of his audience, his protection, and his peril.

The exile also produced the first collision with the movement's highest authority, in an episode that prefigures the condemnations to come. Lukacs's pamphlet on tactics defended a boycott of parliamentary work by revolutionary parties, and Lenin, reading it in the course of composing his own pamphlet against the infantile disorders of the Western movement, singled out its reasoning for correction: the Hungarian's dialectical subtlety, Lenin judged, had produced a politics of purity that would leave the masses to their reformist leaders. The correction stung and instructed. It taught the young theorist the two lessons between which his whole career would oscillate: that the movement's centre would enforce its tactical judgements without regard for philosophical merit, and that the centre's judgement, however brutally delivered, sometimes saw the practical point his dialectic had refined away. He did not leave the movement over the correction, and he did not cease to think his own thoughts; he filed the experience in the place where the later recantations would also be filed, under the heading of the price of belonging to the only army he believed history had raised. The Vienna years thus supplied the book with its final ingredient. Budapest had given the questions, Heidelberg the sociology, the commissariat the knowledge of what revolution costs its servants; Vienna gave the knowledge of what defeat costs its analysts, and the determination, legible on every page of the essays, that the analysis this time would go deep enough to be of use.

The essays also carry a date that their first readers would have felt in their bones and that a later reader must be given. They were completed as the European revolutions of the postwar years approached their final defeat: the German October of 1923, the movement's last attempt at power in the West, collapsed in confusion within weeks of the book's appearance, and the stabilisation of the capitalist order that followed closed the revolutionary period the essays had been written to serve. The book is therefore the theoretical deposit of a defeated wave, composed at the moment of the defeat by one of its surviving officers, and its characteristic emphases bear the mark of that origin throughout. A theory formed in victory would have stressed the movement's power; this theory stresses the movement's consciousness, its failures of self knowledge, the forms in which a class misrecognises its own product and its own strength, because those were the failures the defeat had made undeniable. The tradition the book founded inherited the emphasis with the deposit: a Marxism of consciousness, of culture, of the forms of objectivity, elaborated in the long aftermath of a political defeat its founders declined to call final. When the profile speaks of the concept's career, that origin should be kept in view, since it explains both the tradition's depth on its chosen terrain and its celebrated weakness on the terrain of strategy, a weakness its critics in the movement never tired of itemising and its historians, Anderson pre eminently, built into the definition of the whole formation.

## Essays written in exile: the book as a thinker's workshop

The work on which everything else depends is a collection of essays written between 1919 and 1923, most of them first published as interventions in the debates of the young communist movement, gathered in 1923 under the title History and Class Consciousness. The text of the book, its essay by essay anatomy, and its place in the curriculum are the business of the companion article, the [history and class consciousness guide](/2015/12/01/history-and-class-consciousness-guide/), and this profile deliberately leaves that anatomy there. What belongs here is the book as an event in a thinker's life: the conditions under which it was written, the sources lying open on the desk, and the shape of the wager its author was making. The essays were not composed as a system in a study. They were written as journalism of a high order, in exile, amid the wreckage of the Hungarian revolution and the uncertainties of the German one, by an author who believed that the movement's practical failures were at root failures of consciousness, and that philosophy was therefore not a commentary on the struggle but one of its weapons.

That belief explains the book's two governing categories, and a profile of the concept must introduce both, since reification is unintelligible without them. The first is totality. The second is the identical subject object, the construction by which Lukacs proposed to heal the split that reification opens between the knower and the known. Between them they frame the reification essay: totality names the whole that the commodity form penetrates, and the identical subject object names the standpoint from which the penetration can be recognised as a human product. The treatment of class consciousness as a concept, including the doctrine of the consciousness that can be imputed to a class from its objective position, has its own article in this series, [class consciousness explained](/2011/09/15/class-consciousness-explained/), and is linked rather than expounded here, as the division of labour between profiles and concept articles requires.

### What work does the idea of totality do in the essays?

Totality is the essays' methodological warrant. It says that the facts of social life become intelligible only inside the whole that produced them, and that the commodity form matters because it is the form of the whole, not one department of it. Without totality, reification would describe a workplace problem; with it, reification describes a civilisation's form of objectivity.

The warrant is directed against two opponents at once. Against the positivism of the Marxism of the Second International, which had dissolved the social whole into a sum of economic facts governed by iron laws, totality insists that the facts are moments of a process and take their meaning from their place in it; the category of mediation, of the links between the immediate fact and the whole, is what orthodox method had lost. Against the specialised sciences of the bourgeois university, each cultivating its fenced province with its own formal laws, totality insists that the provinces are abstractions from a single social process, and that their separation is itself a product of the reified division of labour in thought. The reification essay is the place where the warrant is executed: chapter by chapter, the argument moves outward from the commodity to the enterprise, from the enterprise to the state and the law, from institutions to the structure of consciousness, and the movement is licensed at every step by the claim that the whole is present in each of its parts because one form organises them all. A reader may reject the licence, and the strongest objection does, but no reader should mistake the essay for a local study of factory discipline that wandered into philosophy by accident. The expansion is the argument.

### What is the identical subject-object meant to solve?

It is meant to solve the problem of how a society made by human beings can be known and changed by them. If the proletariat is the product of the commodity order and the class positioned to comprehend it, then knowledge of society and transformation of society become a single process, and theory ceases to contemplate its object from outside.

The construction completes the reification argument by supplying its exit. Reification splits the world into a subject who contemplates and an object that obeys its own laws; the classical philosophy that Lukacs had studied exhibited the split in its purest form, as the antinomy between a freedom that could not touch the world and a nature that could not justify freedom. His proposal is that one class occupies a position in which the split can be overcome in practice. The worker, whose own labour power is the paradigmatic commodity, experiences reification not as a spectacle but as his own substance sold and set to work; when that class comes to know the society that made it, the object of knowledge and the knowing subject are historically the same being, and self knowledge becomes the lever of transformation. The proposal is audacious, and its audacity should be registered alongside its difficulties, which the later Lukacs himself catalogued with some severity: it loads a philosophical reconciliation onto an empirical class, it risks substituting the party's imputed knowledge for the class's actual consciousness, and it borrows the architecture of Hegel's absolute while claiming to have replaced its bearer with history. Those difficulties belong to the assessment of the book, pursued in its own article; for the profile, what matters is that the construction shows the concept's ambition. Reification was never offered as a piece of social criticism to be filed beside others. It was offered as the diagnosis of a civilisational split, with a named agent of its healing, and it is that scale, more than any single argument, that made the essay collection the founding document of the tradition this cluster of the series surveys.

## The standpoint and the party: the political engine of the essays

A profile that stopped at the diagnosis would misrepresent its subject, because Lukacs built the concept as the premise of a politics, and the politics is where the book's influence and its troubles both begin. The steps run as follows. If reification fragments consciousness, delivering to each class and each individual a partial view naturalised by their position, then the fragmentation cannot be overcome by information, since the positions themselves generate the fragments. What is required is a standpoint: a position within the process from which the whole becomes visible because the process's secret is acted out there in its purest form. The essays assign that standpoint to the proletariat, on the ground developed in the identical subject object construction, and they then face the question that has dogged the book ever since: the empirical workers of Europe conspicuously did not occupy the standpoint the theory assigned them, a fact the Hungarian and German defeats had just finished demonstrating. The essays' answer is the doctrine of imputed consciousness, the consciousness that can be ascribed to a class as the rational response to its objective position, whether or not the class's members, in their reified actuality, entertain it. Between the imputed and the empirical stands the party, whose office, in the organisation essay that closes the collection's argument, is to embody the imputed consciousness in an institution capable of mediating between the class as it is and the class as its position would have it be.

A comparison the essays themselves invite, and which clarifies their originality, is with the doctrine of consciousness in the Russian tradition they inherited. Lenin's pamphlet of 1902 had argued that socialist consciousness must be brought to the workers' movement from outside its spontaneous development, by an organisation of revolutionaries armed with theory; the formulation had scandalised democrats at the time and hardened, in the movement's memory, into a warrant for tutelage. Lukacs's imputation differs from the importation in a way his partisans stress and his critics discount. What the party mediates, on his construction, is not an alien doctrine delivered to the class but the class's own position rendered conscious to itself: the imputed consciousness is ascribed to the workers from the structure of their situation, not from the reading list of their leaders, and the party's title to speak rests on the accuracy of the ascription rather than on the sanctity of the doctrine. The difference is real at the level of the theory's self understanding, and it explains why the essays could be received, by readers hostile to tutelage, as a philosophy of the class's self emancipation rather than of its guidance. Whether the difference survives the practice of parties is another question, and the profile's hearing of it is the same as its hearing of the substitution charge: the norm is stated in the work, the enforcement is not, and the distance between the two is the space in which the century's tragedies of the doctrine were enacted.

The construction should be given its due before its indictment. It solved, with genuine elegance, the problem that had broken the older Marxism: the gap between a theory that predicted revolutionary consciousness from economic position and a Europe in which economic position had just predicted nothing of the kind. By relocating the prediction from psychology to structure, from what workers think to what their position makes thinkable, the essays preserved the theory's core while explaining its apparent refutation, and by assigning the mediation to an organisation they converted a philosophy of history into a rationale for the institution their author served. The indictment is equally familiar and equally serious. An imputed consciousness, known to the party and not to the class, licenses the substitution that the book's critics, beginning with democrats inside the movement itself, have charged against it ever since: the vanguard speaks in the class's name against the class's expressed will, and the standpoint that was to heal the split between subject and object reopens it as a split between the institution that knows and the class that is known. Lukacs's organisation essay does not evade the danger so much as discipline it, insisting that the party's authority is a function of its mediation and forfeits itself when the mediation becomes command, but the insistence is a norm, and the century supplied few mechanisms to enforce it. The full anatomy of the concept of class consciousness, and of this doctrine's fortunes, belongs to the concept article linked earlier in this profile; the profile records the engine and its double legacy, an explanation of defeat that armed a tradition of theory and a warrant for substitution that the same tradition has spent a century trying, with mixed success, to hand back.

## The Luxemburg inheritance: spontaneity weighed in the essays

Among the essays gathered in 1923, the study of Rosa Luxemburg is the one that discloses the book's method in the act of judging a predecessor, and the profile pauses on it because the judgement organises the whole collection's politics. Luxemburg, murdered in Berlin in the January of the revolution's defeat, four years before the book appeared, had been the great theorist of spontaneity: of the mass strike as the school in which the class educates itself, of the party as an organ that must learn from the class's own initiative or become its gaoler. Lukacs's essay honours her as the one figure of the older movement who had grasped totality in practice, whose economics had treated the accumulation of capital as a world process and whose politics had trusted the process to generate its own consciousness. Then, with the honour recorded, the essay turns its instrument on her limit. Spontaneity, in the turning, is itself a reified category when it is absolutised: it treats the class's consciousness as a natural growth, arising from the process as a crop arises from a field, and thereby contemplates the very thing the theory exists to transform. The mass strike's lessons, Lukacs argues, do not accumulate by themselves; they are lost as fast as they are learned unless an organisation exists in which the learning is deposited, criticised, and carried into the next encounter, and that organisation is the party, not as the class's tutor but as the form of its memory. The essay is thus the hinge between the reification argument and the organisation doctrine, and it shows the profile's subject at his most characteristic: rescuing a murdered comrade's insight by correcting it, and building the correction into the foundation of the system that the International would condemn the following year, partly for the confidence with which such corrections were issued. Luxemburg's own ghost presides over the substitution debate to this day, since her warnings against the party that substitutes itself for the class are quoted wherever Lukacs's doctrine is indicted, and the fact that his essay on her is among the most respectful ever written by a critic who believed she was wrong is part of the record of the man.

## The independent derivation

### What exactly had Lukacs not read when the essays were composed?

He had not read the manuscripts of 1844, in which the young Marx set out estranged labour, because they survived only in notebooks that no publisher had printed. What he had read, closely and repeatedly, was the first chapter of Capital, together with the German sociology of money and rationalisation that his Heidelberg years had put in his hands.

The chronology is the argument, so the chronology must be exact. Marx wrote the notebooks now known as the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts in Paris in 1844, in his twenty sixth year, and did not publish them; they were exploratory drafts, quarried for later work and then left among his papers. When he died, the papers passed through hands that prized the finished system over the workshop, and the notebooks slept. Lukacs wrote the essays that became History and Class Consciousness between 1919 and 1923, published the collection in 1923, and built its centre, the reification essay, on the commodity analysis of the first chapter of the first volume of Capital, cross-bred with the sociology of rationalisation. The manuscripts were published in 1932, nine years after his book, in two editions that landed in the same season. Between the writing of the notebooks and their printing lie eighty eight years; between Lukacs's derivation and his first sight of the text it appeared to depend on lie seven years at the least. He encountered the manuscripts in Moscow, in 1930, while working at the institute charged with editing Marx's literary remains, where he read them in the course of the editorial labour that preceded their publication, one of the first readers the text ever had. He later described the convergence between what he had written and what he then read as remarkable, and treated the encounter as a turning point in his own understanding of Marx. The word remarkable, from that source, is an understatement deployed by a man not given to understatement about himself, and it points at the fact this profile exists to establish: reification was not paraphrased out of the young Marx. It was derived, independently, from the mature work.

The derivation deserves to be walked through slowly, because its steps are the strongest evidence the concept has. Begin where Lukacs began, with the fetishism section of the commodity chapter: Marx's demonstration that in a society of independent producers, the social relations of the producers take the form of relations between their products, so that the value of a thing appears as a natural property of the thing. Add the sociological generalisation Lukacs carried from Simmel and Weber: that the money economy and the rationalised institution extend the habits of abstraction and calculation across the whole of life, training every participant to meet the world as a field of quantifiable objects under formal rules. Now press the two together and ask what a society looks like when the form Marx isolated in exchange becomes the organising form of production, of administration, of law, and of the subject's relation to his own capacities. The answer that emerges, step by step and without any borrowing from the Paris notebooks, is the three dimensional concept stated at the head of this profile: the phantom objectivity of a second nature, the contemplative stance of the subject whose own powers confront him as objects, and the penetration of the form into thought itself. When the manuscripts surfaced, readers found there the theory of estranged labour, the worker alienated from product, activity, species being, and fellows, stated in the vocabulary of the young Marx's Hegelianism. Lukacs had reached a neighbouring structure by another road, and the neighbourhood is too close for coincidence and too independent for borrowing. That is the phenomenon. The remaining question is what it proves.

### What does the convergence license a reader to claim, and what does it withhold?

It licenses the claim that the problematic of alienation is no youthful episode external to the mature work, since the opening chapter of Capital proved sufficient to regenerate it. It withholds the claim that the two texts say the same thing, or that Lukacs knew the young Marx's intentions; a convergence is evidence about the problem, not about influence.

The licensed claim mattered enormously in the debates that followed the manuscripts' publication, because those debates were organised around a rupture. One party held that the young Marx of the notebooks, humanist and Hegelian, had been superseded and properly discarded by the scientific Marx of Capital, and that the alienation theory belonged to the discarded phase. Another held that the early work was the key and the late work its unfolding. Into that quarrel the Lukacs chronology drops a piece of evidence of an unusual kind. A reader of genius, working solely from the allegedly post humanist text, with the allegedly humanist text physically unavailable to him, regenerated the humanist problematic from the scientific text's own premises. If the problematic were truly external to the mature work, an excrescence of youth sloughed off in maturity, its regeneration from the mature text alone should not have occurred. The regeneration did occur, under conditions as close to a controlled experiment as intellectual history provides: the contaminated source was sealed, the derivation is documented, and the converger had every polemical reason of his own, as a militant of the movement that prized the mature Marx, to stay within the letter of the published canon. This profile's namable claim can now be stated in its full form: the independent derivation is the strongest available evidence that the alienation problematic is present in the mature work itself, latent in the commodity analysis, awaiting only a reader willing to generalise it.

The withheld claims matter just as much, and the profile states them so that the evidence is not overspent. The convergence does not show that reification and alienation are identical concepts; the table above separates them, and Lukacs himself, after reading the manuscripts, came to distinguish objectification, a neutral structure of all labour, from alienation, its historically specific deformation, in terms his 1923 essay had run together. Nor does the convergence show anything about Marx's intentions in 1844 or 1867, questions for the texts and their editors. And it does not settle the evaluation of Lukacs's construction, whose extension to the whole of thought remains exposed to the strongest objection. Convergence is evidence about the problem: it shows that two powerful minds, or one powerful mind at two moments separated by a revolution in its circumstances, found the same structure in the same social form. What structures recur in that way are rarely inventions. They are usually discoveries, and the history of thought knows a handful of such doubles, in mathematics and in the natural sciences, where independent arrival at the same result is taken as the signature of a real object at the end of both roads. The chronology argument this fact supports is developed, against the two Marxes literature, in the series article on the [young Marx and the mature Marx](/2011/06/01/young-marx-vs-mature-marx/), which the researcher persona below will commend to readers who want the quarrel in full.

The experiment, to press the profile's governing metaphor one step further, even has its control group, and the control sharpens what the derivation proves. The commodity chapter of Capital was the most read chapter of Marx in the Europe of 1923, the common property of a movement whose orthodox theorists had commented it for a generation, and none of them derived reification from it. The authorised reading found in the fetishism section a piece of economic doctrine, a veil to be noted on the way to the theory of value's real business, and passed on. What the control group lacked was not the text but the catalyst: the sociological formation that had taught one reader to see a form of objectivity where the others saw a doctrine, and to ask what a society must be like whose founding relation appears as a relation between things. The independence this profile celebrates is therefore a precise and limited thing. Lukacs was independent of the manuscripts, not of his century; the derivation ran from Capital through Heidelberg, and its route explains its result, since the question he brought to the chapter, how a social form constitutes the consciousness that inhabits it, was Simmel's and Weber's question before it was Marx's answer. Convergence arguments are often weakened by such qualifications; this one is strengthened, because the qualification identifies the derivation's mechanism. Two traditions, the critique of political economy and the sociology of rationalisation, were fused in one reader under the pressure of a revolution, and the fusion regenerated a third tradition's founding problematic from its mature text alone. That is the whole claim, and it is enough.

The editorial history of the manuscripts deserves its own paragraph, because the profile's central fact is an editorial fact. The complete edition of Marx and Engels undertaken in Moscow in the nineteen twenties, the most ambitious scholarly enterprise the movement ever mounted, was the instrument through which the sealed papers were to reach print, and its director, David Ryazanov, was the editor who first recognised the Paris notebooks for what they were and scheduled them for the edition's early volumes. A second, independent edition prepared by Siegfried Landshut and J. P. Mayer reached the bookshops in the same year, drawn from the copies held by the German party's archive, and between the two printings the young Marx entered public philosophy in 1932 with the force of a newly discovered continent. The first reviewers understood the scale of the event. Herbert Marcuse, then a young philosopher working at the edge of the Frankfurt Institute, greeted the manuscripts in an essay of that year as a discovery that would put the interpretation of Marx on a new foundation, and his judgement was representative: within a few seasons the notebooks had reorganised the field, splitting readers into the parties of continuity and rupture whose quarrel the convergence argument addresses. Ryazanov did not live to edit the harvest. Arrested in 1931 on fabricated charges, he was shot later in the decade, and the institute he had built was consumed by the terror whose other victims fill the footnotes of every history of these texts. The manuscripts that established the independence of Lukacs's derivation thus reached the world through an institution that devoured their editor, a circumstance the profile records without commentary, since the reader by this point has the materials to supply it.

This profile's series pattern should be named before the record darkens, since the derivation is an instance of it and the pattern is the series' standing instrument for such cases. Where a concept is reached independently of the text it appears to depend on, the independence is itself evidence about the underlying problem, and establishing the chronology is the contribution. The pattern has honourable precedents in every discipline that keeps its records honestly. The calculus was reached twice in one generation, by Newton and by Leibniz, from different problems and in different notations, and the double arrival is part of why mathematicians trust that the calculus describes something real rather than recording one man's notation. Natural selection was announced jointly because Wallace's manuscript arrived on Darwin's desk with Darwin's own theory in it, formed on the other side of the world from different specimens. In each case the independence of the derivations does the epistemic work: a single derivation might be an artefact of its author's premises, while two derivations sharing only the problem are evidence that the problem has the structure both found in it. The Lukacs case fits the pattern with one variation that sharpens it. The two derivations are by a single tradition's founder and its most gifted reader, separated by eighty years and by the accident of an archive, and the second derivation was performed in public, in a published book whose sources can be audited line by line. Intellectual history rarely gets its experiments so clean, and the profile has treated the case with the ceremony the pattern deserves. The ceremony ends here. What follows is what the institution did with the book, and with the man.

The debate the manuscripts ignited, once the Lukacs evidence was on the table, organised the interpretation of Marx for a generation, and the profile should trace its institutional shape before leaving the derivation. Through the nineteen fifties the humanist reading, fortified by the notebooks and by the convergence, built the young Marx into the foundation of a Marxism of alienation that flourished in the universities and in the dissident margins of the parties alike. The counter attack, when it came in the nineteen sixties from the circle of Louis Althusser, proposed an epistemological break between the ideological young Marx and the scientific author of Capital, and it had to deal with Lukacs as with a geological fault: the essays were either the break's refutation in advance or its most distinguished casualty, and the structuralist literature's handling of the book, respectful and dismissive in the same gesture, registers the difficulty. The division of the field into those two readings, and the terms on which they fought, is the subject of the series article on the [humanist and structuralist division](/2016/02/15/humanist-vs-structuralist-marxism/), and it is not re fought here. What the profile notes is the derivation's permanent alteration of the argument's burden. After 1923 and 1932, a reader who would sever the early problematic from the mature work must explain the regeneration, and a reader who would fuse them must respect the differences the table records. The convergence did not end the quarrel. It made the quarrel honest, by giving both parties a fact neither could have manufactured.

## Condemnation and the first recantations

### Who condemned the book first, and on what charge?

The first condemnation came from the summit of the Communist International at its congress the year after publication, where its chairman named the book as a specimen of professorial idealism. The charge was that Lukacs had dissolved the material world into consciousness, revised Marxism into Hegelian philosophy, and abandoned the objectivity that a materialist doctrine requires.

The book had been in print for a year when the Fifth Congress of the Communist International met in the summer of 1924, and its chairman, Grigory Zinoviev, used the congress platform to denounce the theoretical deviations of the movement's intellectuals, naming Lukacs and his German counterpart Karl Korsch as professors whose Hegelian Marxism threatened the doctrine's materialist foundations. The attack was not a book review. It was an exercise of institutional power at a moment when the International was consolidating its discipline after Lenin's death, and its effect was to mark the book, and the kind of Marxism it represented, as suspect in every party that took its line from Moscow. In the Soviet philosophical journals the case was argued at length by Abram Deborin and his school, who charged that Lukacs had replaced the materialist dialectic with an idealist one, had treated nature as a social category in defiance of the doctrine of dialectical materialism, and had made consciousness, in the figure of the identical subject object, the author of a reality that materialism requires to be independent. Lukacs replied as the institution required: with public self criticism, accepting the charge of idealism in terms his judges could register, while the manuscript he composed in the same years, a point by point defence of the book's positions against its critics, went into a drawer and stayed unpublished for seventy years. That drawer is the single most eloquent object in this profile, and the section on the repudiations below returns to it.

The philosophical case against the book, argued in the Soviet journals, had a texture the congress denunciation lacked, and the profile pauses on it because its terms governed the author's intellectual life for a decade. The Deborin school, then dominant in Soviet philosophy, charged the essays with three connected heresies. They denied, it was said, the dialectics of nature, treating nature itself as a historically mediated category and thereby dissolving the material world's independence into the history of society. They replaced the reflection of an objective reality with the productivity of a subject, the identical subject object standing accused as German idealism in party dress. And they subordinated the finished doctrine of the founders to a Hegelian method whose restless negativity could not be trusted to stop at any authorised conclusion. Each charge had its textual occasion in the essays, and the third in particular was not a misunderstanding: the book's author had indeed written that orthodoxy in Marxism refers exclusively to method, a sentence that demoted every particular doctrine, including the doctrines of its judges, to revisable status. The controversy was therefore a genuine philosophical dispute conducted inside an institution that settled disputes by other means, and its outcome was determined by the second of those facts. Lukacs's replies observed the forms of the first; his concessions observed the necessities of the second; and the defence he actually believed went, as recorded, into the drawer. Scholars who reconstruct the episode from the journals alone consistently underrate it, because the journals print the dispute and the drawer held the answer.

The second great recantation concerned not the book but the platform. In 1928 Lukacs drafted the political theses for the underground Hungarian party's approaching congress, writing under the name Blum, and the document proposed a democratic dictatorship of the proletariat and peasantry as the strategic horizon, a transitional demand pitched against both fascist consolidation and the fantasy of immediate soviet power. The party leadership and the International condemned the Blum Theses as liquidationist, a surrender of the revolutionary programme dressed as tactics, and at the end of the decade Lukacs recanted them too, in writing, withdrawing from the leadership politics of his own party and, in substance, from political intervention altogether. The withdrawal was permanent in its main line: the philosopher who had been a commissar and a factional strategist withdrew into scholarship, and with the exception of one Hungarian season thirty years later, he never again held a post or drafted a programme. Critics of the recantations see in the pattern a habit of surrender, formed early and repeated at every pressure. Defenders see a man who had decided that his contribution was theoretical, that the party's discipline was the condition of the movement's existence, and that a public figure in his position, an exile dependent on the movement's protection in a Europe moving toward fascism, had no honest alternative that did not begin with his own destruction. The profile gives both judgements their materials and defers its full hearing of them to the section devoted to the repudiations; here it is enough to fix the sequence: a condemned book defended in private and recanted in public, a condemned platform recanted and abandoned, and then Moscow.

## Korsch, the twin: the parallel founding and the parting

No profile of Lukacs in this cluster is complete without the figure who shares his founding moment and inverts his fate, because the comparison isolates the variable the repudiations hearing must weigh: the institution's price, and what paying it bought. Karl Korsch, a German jurist and former minister of justice in a short lived provincial coalition, published Marxism and Philosophy in the same year as History and Class Consciousness, arguing from his own side that the movement's theory had decayed into a dogma precisely because it had forgotten that Marxism was itself a historical product, to be applied to its own history. The two books were condemned together at the congress of 1924, named in the same breath by the same chairman, and the joint condemnation is as good a birth certificate as the tradition this series surveys will ever have: Western Marxism begins as the set of Marxisms the International anathematised for restoring philosophy to the doctrine. Thereafter the twins diverge, and the divergence is the data. Korsch refused the recantation's road. Expelled from the German party in 1926, he moved through the dwindling milieux of the anti authoritarian left, emigrated to America, and wrote his austere late study of Marx in a isolation so complete that his death in 1961 passed almost unremarked by the movement he had helped to found. Lukacs paid, stayed, and was published, translated, housed, and heard for fifty years, at the price the record shows. The profile draws no moral, because the record supports both of the morals on offer: that integrity without an institution is a whisper, and that an institution's hearing is purchased with the coin the drawer manuscript counts. Readers deciding what they think of the recantations should be required to say what they think of Korsch, since the two cases are the experiment and its control, run by history on matched subjects in the same decade.

## The Berlin interlude: reportage against the novel

Between Moscow appointments Lukacs spent two years in Berlin, attached to the cultural work of the German party in the republic's final crisis, and the episode produced a literary quarrel whose terms anticipate the Brecht debate and complete the profile's account of the realism doctrine's formation. The German party's writers' organisation had filled its journal with a literature of reportage: factory correspondents' accounts, documentary novels assembled from protocols and statistics, a prose that prided itself on refusing invention in the name of fact. Lukacs, reviewing a documentary novel of the Berlin courts, attacked the whole tendency with the reification argument in his hand. Reportage, he argued, photographs the surface of the process and thereby contemplates it; the reporter, however militant his sympathies, stands in the posture of the spectator before the mechanism, and his facts, unmediated by any shaping intelligence, reproduce the fragmentation they denounce. Against the fact he set the formed work, the novel that composes its materials into a totality in which the process becomes visible as a process. The quarrel's interest for the profile is the spectacle of the concept policing literature from inside the movement's own avant garde: the contemplative stance had been detected, by its theorist, in the art of his own side, wearing the costume of documentary virtue. Within two years the republic whose cultural front he was policing had ceased to exist, the journal was closed, its writers scattered or arrested, and Lukacs was in Moscow for the duration. The Berlin interlude is a small episode with a long shadow, since the doctrine forged there, formed totality against documentary immediacy, is the doctrine he carried into the expressionism debate and defended, with diminishing company, for the rest of his life.

## The Moscow decade: the manuscripts read at last

### What changed in his reading of Hegel during the Moscow years?

Access to the Marx manuscripts he was helping to edit turned Hegel from a philosophical ancestor into a historical worker in his own right. The study he wrote across that decade reads Hegel's logic as a response to the economics and politics of a specific age, which is why it remains in print when his polemics are not.

Lukacs arrived in Moscow in 1930 and joined the staff of the Marx Engels Institute, the scholarly establishment charged, under its director David Ryazanov, with preparing the complete edition of the works of Marx and Engels. The appointment placed him, by an irony no novelist would risk, in daily contact with the unpublished remains of the author whose sealed notebooks his own book had unknowingly answered. It was in the course of this editorial labour that he read the manuscripts of 1844, and the encounter reorganised his thought. The young Marx of the notebooks read Hegel as a labour philosopher manqué, for whom the self production of humanity through work was the buried truth of the system; Lukacs took the hint back to Hegel himself and began the long study, written across the Moscow years and the war, that appeared after the war as The Young Hegel. That book treats Hegel's formation inside the economic and political emergencies of his age, the factory reports and the French Revolution and the new science of political economy, and it remains the most durable monument of the Moscow period: a work of scholarship whose argument, that the dialectic was born as a thinking through of labour and history, could be engaged by readers who shared none of its author's politics.

The Moscow literary work had an official horizon it is worth naming, since the doctrine of great realism acquired its edges there. In 1934 the Soviet writers' congress codified socialist realism as the sanctioned method of the movement's literature, a formula whose custodians intended it as a licence for the affirmative epic of construction. Lukacs's realism was a different animal wearing a related coat: historically exacting where the formula was hortatory, committed to the critical Balzac against the formula's plaster heroes, and grounded in a theory of the typical that made most sanctioned production fail its test. The friction between the two realisms ran under the surface of the exile journals for a decade, visible whenever his essays praised a bourgeois master at the expense of a correct contemporary, and it explains the peculiar status his literary authority enjoyed in those years: indispensable to the movement's claim to a culture, and permanently suspect to the culture's appointed guardians, who could neither use the doctrine nor spare the prestige. He survived the friction as he survived the rest, by being, at the moments of decision, more valuable orthodox than heretical, and by writing nothing in the province that required a recantation to withdraw.

The institutional shelter was real and it was fragile. Ryazanov was arrested in 1931 and the institute purged and folded into a successor body; the terror that followed consumed editors, translators, and philosophers in numbers that made survival itself a statistical accident, a fact any honest account of Lukacs's Moscow conduct must set beside the accommodations survival required. He worked on, increasingly in literary scholarship, which offered a defensible province: essays on the great realists, on Goethe, on the historical novel, contributions to the exile journals through which German letters conducted their shadow life. In the summer of 1941, after the German invasion scattered the assumptions of a decade, he was arrested and held for some weeks before being released, an episode his biographers attribute to the indiscriminate suspicion that fell on foreign communists in the invasion's first panic; the institute evacuated him with others to Tashkent, where he waited out the war's worst years writing on German literature and the fate of reason. The evacuation years in Tashkent, often treated as a blank in the bibliography, were in fact a workshop. Cut off from the institute and its archives, Lukacs wrote the studies of German literature that gathered his wartime judgement of the national culture: essays on the great realists of the nineteenth century, on the novelists of the empire's decline, and on the line of German thought whose deformation the later irrationalism book would prosecute with a heavier and clumsier hand. The Tashkent essays show the critical intelligence at its most characteristic, patient with the works, exact about their worlds, and ruthless only toward the ideologies that the works, in his triumph of realism doctrine, could be shown to defeat. They also record, for the biographer, the uses of a internal exile: the philosopher whom the terror had arrested and the war had displaced spent the catastrophe's worst years doing, in a Central Asian city, the work of his youth, reading novels as the documents of a civilisation's soul. The profile notes the continuity because it bears on the assessment that dominates its second half. Through every change of regime, faction, and doctrine, one activity was never recanted, interrupted only by cells and evacuations, and resumed in every shelter the century afforded: the reading of the European novel as the register in which the modern world's form could be known. The Ontology's author and the commissar and the condemned heretic are one person in the record because that activity is one, and the concept of reification is its theoretical precipitate.

The Moscow decade thus holds the two extremes of the life in a single frame: the reader at the editor's desk, meeting at last the text his youth had answered blind, and the prisoner in the cell of the state his movement served, learning the price of the second nature that states, too, can build. The reification essay had argued that a human product can confront its producers as an alien law. Its author spent the nineteen thirties inside the largest demonstration of the argument his century provided, and what he wrote about it at the time observed the limits his survival dictated. The assessment of those limits belongs to the repudiations hearing below; the record itself is stated here because a profile that concealed it would forfeit its claim on the reader.

The literary labours of the Moscow years produced, almost as a by product, the work by which general readers beyond the movement have most often met him. The Historical Novel, composed in the later nineteen thirties and published after the war, studies the rise of the genre with Walter Scott and its decline into the decorative costume piece, arguing that the great historical fiction flourished exactly when history had become a mass experience, in the wars and revolutions that taught whole populations they lived inside a process, and decayed when that experience was replaced by the contemplation of a finished past. The argument is the reification thesis in literary dress and is none the worse for it: the costume novel is contemplative literature, the past as a wardrobe of things, while the Scott novel at its height renders a society in motion through the collisions of typical figures. Around this centre Lukacs built the campaign for what he called great realism, conducted through the exile journals and through an immense correspondence with the German writers of the emigration, Anna Seghers among them, whose own novels he pressed, in letters of formidable courtesy and inflexibility, toward the Balzacian standard. The campaign's institutional weight was real: for a decade, the most powerful critical intelligence in the communist cultural world used its authority to hold a line for the nineteenth century novel against the twentieth century's experiments. Whether that line preserved a heritage or embalmed it is the question the Brecht quarrel, to which the profile now turns, poses in its sharpest form.

## The triumph of realism: when the work defeats the author

One doctrine from the literary work must be stated separately, because it is the point at which Lukacs's criticism becomes a general theory of the relation between a mind and its products, and because it quietly governs how this profile has treated its subject throughout. The doctrine begins from an observation Engels had made in a private letter about Balzac: that the royalist legitimist, whose political sympathies belonged entirely to the doomed aristocracy, produced in the Human Comedy the most exact anatomy of the bourgeois society that was destroying his beloved class, learning, as Engels put it, more from him than from all the professed historians and economists of the period together. Lukacs generalised the observation into the triumph of realism: a great writer's work, when the writer's honesty of observation is stronger than his ideology, will render the truth of its world against the author's own intentions, the formed totality correcting the opinions of the former. The doctrine did heavy work in his system. It explained why the heritage of bourgeois literature could be claimed by a movement that rejected the bourgeoisie: the classics were not their authors' politics bound in cloth but their authors' vision liberated by craft from their politics. It armed his campaign against the documentary school, since reportage, which begins from correct opinions, has no formed totality to rescue it when the opinions prove thin. And it supplied his aesthetics with its criterion of greatness, the capacity of a work to let reality contradict the hand that made it.

The doctrine's application to its author is left as an exercise the profile commends to the reader, and the commendation is not a pleasantry. A thinker who taught that the work can defeat the author's opinions invites, and survives, the question of where his own work defeats his. The reification essay's diagnosis of the institution that publishes its servants' thoughts in the form of necessities is not diminished by its author's subsequent service to such an institution; if anything, the service reads, in the doctrine's light, as the work's truth continuing to operate inside the author's accommodations, the analysis of the second nature standing unretracted in the text while its author recanted around it. This is not offered as an acquittal. The doctrine of the triumph of realism was built for novelists, and a philosopher's recantations are acts, not chapters; they belong to the record of the life and are judged there, as this profile has judged them, without the shelter of the work. But the doctrine explains the otherwise inexplicable experience of reading the essays of 1923 against the record of the decades that followed: the sense that the book knew something its author spent fifty years alternately honouring and evading, and that the knowing, being in the work, did not require his permission to survive him.

## The Brecht quarrel: realism against modernism

### On what single point did Brecht's answer turn?

Brecht's answer turned on the audience. Where Lukacs trusted the achieved forms of the classic novel to school a reader in totality, Brecht held that new conditions demanded new forms, built to interrupt identification and make the spectator judge. The quarrel concerned what realism must do to a reader, not whether art should serve the cause both men served.

In the later nineteen thirties the German exile journals carried a debate on the literature of the anti fascist emigration that has outlasted nearly everything else its participants wrote for the occasion. Lukacs, installed as the leading theorist of the communist cultural apparatus, argued that the heritage of the great bourgeois realism, Balzac above all and Tolstoy and Thomas Mann in his train, supplied the forms in which a writer could still render the social totality: typical characters in typical circumstances, the individual fate made transparent to the historical process that shaped it. Against that canon he set the modernists, the expressionists and the experimenters with montage and interior monologue, whose fragmentation of form he read as the artistic registration of a decaying order, and whose work he charged with surrendering the totality for the immediate sensation. Bertolt Brecht, in exile in Denmark, read the essays and filled notebooks with rejoinders that he did not publish in his lifetime, and the debate as posterity knows it is therefore a strange object: a public doctrine and a private answer, assembled into a quarrel only when Brecht's papers were printed decades later. The wider argument, and the critical tradition it founded, is treated in the series article on [Marxist literary criticism](/2020/07/01/marxist-literary-criticism-explained/); the profile's business is what the episode shows about its subject.

The debate's preliminaries sharpen the picture. It opened with an attack, from a younger party publicist writing in the exile journal, on the legacy of expressionism, which the attack treated as the artistic seedbed of fascist irrationalism; Ernst Bloch, the philosopher of hope and an old friend of Lukacs's Budapest years, answered in expressionism's defence, arguing that its fractures registered a reality itself fractured and that the people, whose taste the doctrine claimed to serve, had never been consulted in the matter. Lukacs's intervention, the essay whose title announces that the issue is realism itself, raised the quarrel to the level of principle: beneath the disputed movement stood the disputed category, and the category, he argued, admitted no third position between the rendering of the whole and the registration of fragments. The exchange is the exile culture's most consequential argument about art, and its bitterness had a personal geology the profile should register: Bloch and Lukacs had been intimates of the Heidelberg and Budapest circles, divided since by their readings of the Russian experiment, and the debate reads in places like a correspondence between estranged brothers conducted in the pages of a journal with an army at its back. The canon Lukacs defended had a living centre, and the relation between the theorist and the novelist he placed there belongs to the record of the debate's human stakes. Thomas Mann received the communist critic's championship with a courtesy that masked genuine respect: the novelist who had anatomised the German bourgeoisie from inside recognised in Lukacs the one Marxist reader who treated the bourgeois novel as a world to be inherited rather than a symptom to be discarded, and their correspondence across the emigration years is among the civilised documents of a barbarous age. The championship had its ironies, which the profile notes because its subject would have insisted on them. Mann's own art, ironic, self conscious, saturated with the modernism of its century, stood closer to the experimental line than the doctrine of great realism could comfortably house, and the critic's praise was always partly a recruitment, an attempt to enrol the living master in a canon the master had joined only in the critic's construction of it. That the enrolment half succeeded, that Mann's name crowns the realist line in every subsequent account, is a measure of the doctrine's power to organise literary history, the same power, exercised on philosophy, that the strongest objection puts in question.

Posterity's distribution of honours has been even handed to the point of irony, awarding the theatre to Brecht, the heritage argument to Bloch's side of the expressionism question, and the novel's theory, in part, to Lukacs, whose standard of the achieved totality still disciplines the judgements of readers who have never heard his name.

It shows, first, the reach of the reification concept into his aesthetics. Lukacs's preference for the achieved novel was not a taste but a theorem: the work that renders the whole resists the fragmentation that reified consciousness takes for the nature of things, while the work that surrenders to immediacy, however radical its materials, reproduces the contemplative stance in aesthetic form, sensation without mediation. It shows, second, the limit that his critics inside the movement pressed then and press still: a doctrine of form derived from the canon of a previous century could not hear what the new forms were for. Brecht's counter argument, that a changed reality required changed instruments, that the spectator must be made a judge rather than a sympathiser, and that realism is a fighting word whose content each generation must rebuild, has worn at least as well as the doctrine it opposed, and the theatre of the later century is largely built on Brecht's side of the argument. And it shows, third, something about the man that the political record alone would not reveal: arguing about novels in exile journals while the terror proceeded and the war approached, Lukacs behaved as if literary form were a front line, because in his system it was one. The totality could be lost in a technique as surely as in a parliament. Adorno, no ally of either, later turned the same severity on Lukacs himself, reviewing his postwar defence of realism as an extorted reconciliation with the powers that published him; the phrase stuck, and it belongs to the record of the reception, a reminder that the quarrel's participants judged one another with the same unsparing instruments they had forged for the work.

## The existentialism polemic: the Paris front of the postwar years

The postwar sage fought on a second front that the profile should record, because it brought the reification argument into direct combat with the philosophy then conquering Paris, and the combat clarifies both. Existentialism, in the form Sartre had given it, proposed that existence precedes essence, that the human subject, condemned to be free, authors itself in a world without guarantees; and to the young intellectuals of the liberated capitals it offered what the parties could not, a philosophy of commitment that required no catechism. Lukacs's book length answer, published in French at the end of the nineteen forties, reads existentialism as the contemplative stance's philosophy of despair: a doctrine that discovers, with great literary power, the estrangement of the subject in the reified world, and then enthrones the discovery as the human condition, converting a historical structure into an eternal one and the nausea before the world of things into a metaphysics. Where the existentialist subject chooses itself in a void, the essay of 1923 had proposed that the void itself was manufactured, and that the choice that matters is the collective one by which the manufacturers take back the factory. The polemic was conducted with the author's full armament and the period's full discourtesy, and posterity has split its verdict in its usual manner: the existentialist century belonged to Sartre's themes, while the specific charge, that a philosophy of estrangement which eternalises estrangement becomes its accomplice, has been renewed by every subsequent critic who found the posture of heroic despair a little too comfortable in the world it despaired of. For the profile the episode's value is diagnostic. It shows the concept of 1923 still functioning, twenty five years on, as its author's instrument of first resort, applied to the newest philosophy in Europe with undiminished confidence in the derivation; whatever had been recanted in the interval, the instrument had not been among the recantations.

## The Destruction of Reason: the book the profile cannot defend

### What do the critics of the irrationalism book charge, in their strongest form?

The critics charge that the book builds a corridor from the critics of Enlightenment reason to the fascist regime, and treats every thinker stationed along it as an accomplice. Philosophy written as a prosecution brief, they argue, cannot explain why the same traditions also armed the regime's opponents, and it convicts ideas by the company they are made to keep.

The Destruction of Reason appeared in 1954, written in the last years of Stalin's life by a man who had watched fascism consume the Europe of his formation and who proposed to trace the catastrophe's intellectual genealogy. Its thesis is that a continuous tradition of irrationalism, beginning as a reaction against the revolutionary rationalism of the Enlightenment and running through the German nineteenth century to Nietzsche and beyond, progressively dismantled the defences of reason until the way stood open for the fascist demagogy that harvested the ruins. This profile does not defend the book, because the brief for the article records what the scholarship broadly holds: the criticism of it is wide, and it is reasonable. The method is the indictment's method. Thinkers are arranged along a single line of descent toward a destination none of them chose; the ambiguities of a Nietzsche, whose work armed resisters as readily as rulers, are flattened into the corridor's requirements; whole regions of the material, the empirical sciences, the liberal tradition, the rationalism inside the conservative camp, drop out of the picture because the picture has no compartments for them. A genealogy that cannot be surprised by its evidence is not a history but a verdict in search of its documents, and the book's considerable learning is spent in the service of a conclusion its first chapter has already reached.

The book had a long prehistory in its author's wartime and prewar polemics, and the prehistory clarifies the failure by showing it was a method before it was a book. Through the nineteen thirties and the war years Lukacs had written a series of studies of German intellectual life under the sign of the coming catastrophe: essays on Nietzsche as the philosopher of the imperialist epoch, on the fascist appropriation of the classical heritage, on the German novel's negotiation with a reactionary state. The best of those essays work by the standard of his own method, reconstructing a thinker's world from inside before judging its political yield, and the Nietzsche studies in particular retain readers who reject the later book. The deformation entered as the polemical frame hardened into a system: what had been a question, how the irrationalist motifs in German thought were preparing a politics, became a filing system, in which every station of the tradition was assigned its drawer in advance and the research consisted in filling them. The war's end, which might have relaxed the frame, instead confirmed it in its author's eyes, since the catastrophe the frame had predicted had arrived on schedule; and the settlement of accounts that followed in the Soviet cultural world rewarded exactly the prosecutorial manner, a history of philosophy written as an indictment of the class enemy's ideas. The Destruction of Reason is the monument of that manner, and its author's earlier essays are the standing evidence that the manner was a choice his circumstances pressed on him and not the limit of his gift.

Two things may be said that are true without constituting a defence. The question the book asked was not a foolish one: how a civilisation of unmatched intellectual achievement produced a politics of myth and race is a question that the century obliges every serious person to face, and Lukacs faced it earlier and more directly than most of his contemporaries, in print, while the phenomenon was still unfolding. And the book's failure is instructive about its author, in a way the profile is obliged to register. The man who had built his masterpiece on mediation, on the refusal to let a part be judged outside the whole that produced it, here judged a tradition by its terminus and its parts by their assigned places in a march. The category of totality, wielded as a prosecutor's rope rather than as a method, produces exactly this deformation, and the strongest internal criticism of The Destruction of Reason is that it violates the standards of the reification essay. A reader who wants the concept at its best should therefore read the irrationalism book, if at all, as the control case: the demonstration, by the author's own hand, of what his method becomes when the conclusion is fixed in advance by allegiance rather than reached through the material.

## The last Hungarian chapter

### What did he decline to disown after the deportation?

He declined to disown his part in the government the rising had raised to office, whose members the returning order arrested and deported with him. Statements were extracted from others in that company; from him the record holds no comparable recantation, and his silence was noted by the authorities who kept him at the margin of public life.

Lukacs returned to Hungary after the war to a professorship, a seat in parliament, and the cultural authority of the movement's most famous intellectual, and the early postwar years gave him a second career as a public sage: editions, lectures, a great Goethe study, the historical novel book, and the campaign for a democratic culture conducted in the brief season before the party state hardened. When it hardened, the reckoning found him. A staged debate on his work in 1949 and 1950, driven by party ideologues, charged his literary theory with bourgeois objectivism and his person with insufficient vigilance, and he answered with the practised instrument: a self criticism calibrated to concede the formulations and preserve the work, after which the university chair and the public platform were withdrawn by degrees and he withdrew into the study. The pattern of 1924 and 1929 seemed to be completing itself a third time when history intervened in a form nobody in Budapest had scheduled.

The sage's postwar authority had rested substantially on the Goethe labours, and they explain the particular shape of his cultural politics in those years. His great study of Goethe and his age, composed in Moscow and published after the return, reads the classical heritage as the Hungarian and German movements' common inheritance: a humanism achieved inside the bourgeois epoch at its highest point, which a socialist culture should appropriate critically rather than smash or embalm. The doctrine of the heritage, which he had defended in the exile debates against both the iconoclasts of the avant garde and the custodians of a purely proletarian culture, became in the Budapest of the late nineteen forties a semi official programme, and its author the programme's most distinguished expositor, lecturing, editing, and presiding over the republic of letters of a state that had not yet decided what to do with him. The decision, when it came in the staged debate of 1949, was therefore a fall from a real height: the ideologues who arraigned his literary theory were dismantling a cultural settlement of which he had been the architect, and the self criticism he delivered in that debate should be read with the fact in view. He was not a dissident surprised by power. He was a founder of the house being required to denounce its foundations, and he complied in the manner the record shows, conceding the formulations and keeping the Goethe.

The road to the ministry ran through the one public season of his Hungarian old age, and it should be traversed before the ministry itself. In the thaw that followed Stalin's death, the Petofi Circle, the discussion forum of the young party intellectuals of Budapest, became the arena in which the country's unspoken questions were first spoken aloud, and in the summer of 1956 its philosophical debate drew the seventy one year old Lukacs onto the platform before an audience that overflowed the hall. He spoke for a renewal of Marxism against its dogmatic custodians, for a return to the sources over the heads of the catechism, and the transcript of that evening circulated as evidence that the old man's allegiance to the questions had outlasted his allegiance to every institution that had punished him for them. The speech matters to the assessment this profile is conducting, because it was made in the open, at real risk, in a capital moving toward explosion, and it committed its speaker, before the whole country, to the side of renewal. When the explosion came in October and the revolution's government looked for elders whose names could certify its intentions, the certifier of the Petofi evening was the inevitable choice for the culture portfolio. The ministry was not an office an old man seized. It was the receipt, presented by history, for the one debt of courage the record had registered in his favour since the deportations and recantations began, and he paid it knowing, better than any man alive, what the presentation of such receipts had cost the last time.

In the autumn of 1956 the Hungarian revolution raised the government of Imre Nagy, and on its last reforming cabinet Lukacs accepted the ministry of culture, the post, at one remove, that he had held in 1919. He held it for days. When the Soviet intervention crushed the rising in November, he took refuge with the Nagy group in the Yugoslav embassy, was seized despite the safe conduct promised, and was deported to Romania, where the group was held at a resort town on a lake while its fate was decided in Moscow and Budapest. Nagy and his closest associates were tried and executed. Lukacs was permitted to return to Budapest in the spring of 1957, an outcome his age, his fame, and possibly his usefulness as evidence of the new order's clemency combined to purchase. He was not restored. He lived the remaining fourteen years of his life at the margin of the intellectual establishment whose elder he remained: a member of no ruling body, published and translated abroad, visited by the world's journalists and students, completing in those years the two vast late works, the Aesthetics and the Ontology of Social Being, the latter unfinished at his death in 1971 and published posthumously. He also dictated, in his final two years, the recollections in which the commissar's summer is acknowledged in his own words. The ministry of 1956 stands in the record as the one office he accepted after his withdrawal from politics, and the deportation that followed it as the one punishment he absorbed without the recantation that had twice been his price of readmission. Whether the difference marks a growth of courage, the protection of fame, or the arithmetic of an old man with less left to lose, the profile leaves where the evidence leaves it: visible, and undecided.

## The late system: aesthetics and ontology

The fourteen marginal years were not fallow, and a profile that ended the bibliography with the books of the storms would misstate the scale of the ambition that survived them. Two constructions dominate the late work, and they divide the inheritance between them. The first is the Aesthetics, published in 1963 after a decade of composition, an enormous systematic treatise that undertakes to ground art in the labour process itself: the aesthetic, on its argument, arises when human practice, which everywhere subordinates the object to a purpose, produces a sphere in which the object is contemplated as the self contained reflection of humanity's own essence, and the work of art becomes the self consciousness of the species in sensuous form. The treatise's erudition is oceanic and its manner forbidding, and it has been more honoured by citation than by reading; but it completes a trajectory visible from The Soul and Form onward, in which the young critic's question about how form can hold life receives, at last, a materialist answer built to systematic scale. The second construction is the Ontology of Social Being, the work of his final seven years, published posthumously and unfinished, which attempts the most fundamental labour of all: an ontology of the social world in which labour, understood as the teleological positing by which a consciousness sets a goal and subordinates nature to it, is the model and origin of every social practice, from language to law to the economy itself.

The Ontology matters to this profile for a reason beyond completeness. Its author intended it as the foundation his early work had lacked, and its pages return, with the freedom of a man settling accounts, to the master concept of his youth. Labour in the Ontology is precisely the opposite of the reified relation: it is the act in which the subject object split has not yet opened, in which a purpose formed in consciousness becomes a property of the world through work, and in which causality and teleology, nature's laws and humanity's intentions, are woven in a single process. Read against the essay of 1923, the late system is the diagnosis rewritten as a doctrine of origins: what reification tears apart, labour in its elementary form holds together, and the contemplative stance becomes intelligible as the inversion of the stance that labour itself teaches. Critics divide over the achievement, some finding in the Ontology the genuine renewal of a materialist philosophy and others a vast scholastic edifice raised after its hour; the profile notes the division and notes also the fact that bears on its subject's measure. The philosopher who was condemned in 1924 for dissolving the world into consciousness spent his ninth decade building, stone by stone, an account of how consciousness is itself built by work upon a world that precedes it. Whatever the recantations were, the trajectory of the thought across sixty years has a direction, and it runs from the standpoint toward the object, from the book that was recanted toward a foundation its author hoped no congress would need to judge.

## The Prague season: the last political act

One further political act belongs to the record, and it is the one on which the profile's account of the life should rest before the hearing of the recantations opens, because it shows the pattern of 1956 repeating itself in the author's tenth decade with the regularity of a signature. In 1968 the Czechoslovak reform movement undertook to build what its leaders called socialism with a human face, and Lukacs, at eighty three, recognised the attempt as the practical form of the renewal he had preached at the Petofi Circle and served, for a week, as a minister. He said so in interviews that the European press carried, and he set down the doctrine of the moment in a substantial political testament on democratisation, arguing that the alternative before the socialist world was neither the restored command system nor a simple importation of parliamentary forms, but a democratisation built upward from the workplaces and the communes, the social order's own second nature remade, this time, by its producers in the act of producing it. When the armies of the Warsaw Pact ended the experiment in August, he made his opposition to the invasion known, in the channels open to a man in his position, and accepted the consequences in his Hungarian standing without public recantation. The testament was published after his death. The profile draws from the episode the limited conclusion the evidence supports. Twice in his last fifteen years, at seventy one and at eighty three, the philosopher was offered the chance to certify a renewal of the world he had joined at thirty three, and twice he certified it, and twice the tanks of the order he served arrived to annul the certificate, and twice he declined to annul it himself. A life is not redeemed by its last decade, and the hearing that follows will not pretend otherwise. But the pattern is in the record, and any verdict on the recantations that cannot accommodate the pattern has been reached by reading only half the file.

## The repudiations weighed: coercion, sincerity, and the evidence

### What does the unpublished defence manuscript do to the two readings?

It discomforts both. Against the reading of coercion, the manuscript shows a man still arguing his book's case in private years after its condemnation, which is not the behaviour of an opportunist. Against the reading of sincerity, it shows that the public recantations and the private convictions ran on separate tracks, whatever mixture of fear and belief moved each.

No assessment of Lukacs can avoid the question his recantations pose, and the question deserves a hearing conducted like a trial in which this profile acts as clerk rather than judge. The facts to be explained are these. A book condemned in 1924 was recanted publicly by its author and defended, at length and with philosophical force, in a manuscript he did not publish. A platform condemned in 1929 was recanted and its author withdrew from politics on the strength of the recantation. A literary doctrine attacked in 1949 was met with a partial self criticism, apportioned with evident care. A ministry accepted in 1956 was followed, after deportation, by no recantation at all. And in 1967, in the preface to the reissue of the book that had started everything, the eighty two year old author criticised his own masterpiece with a severity no enemy had matched, calling its attempt to outdo its Hegelian master a fundamental flaw and its identical subject object a metaphysical construction, while insisting in the same pages that the book's questions had been real questions, posed earlier than the movement's authorised philosophy could pose them.

One document belongs to the evidence in a category of its own, and both readings claim it. In 1933, in Moscow, Lukacs composed a short autobiographical account of his road to Marxism, tracing the path from the Budapest aesthete through the commissariat to his present positions, and presenting the journey, including its reversals, as a continuous education: each stage, in the telling, corrects the last, and the Hegelian enthusiasm of 1923 takes its place as a youthful excess outgrown under the tuition of the classics and of practice. The sincerity reading exhibits the sketch as proof that its author understood his own development in exactly the terms his recantations used, years before the greatest pressures were applied, and that the genre of the account, a curriculum vitae for the movement's files, does not disqualify its content. The coercion reading replies that a curriculum vitae composed for those files, in that city, in that year, is the last genre on earth in which a man records that his conversions were performed at gunpoint, and that the sketch's smoothness is itself the symptom: a development with all its terror edited out is a document of the editing, not of the development. The profile records the standoff because it is the whole question in miniature. The archive of Lukacs's self interpretation was produced inside the institution the interpretation is about, and every future hearing of the case will begin from that contamination, which no ingenuity of scholarship can launder away.

The first reading holds that the recantations were coerced, and its evidence is the context, which requires no embroidery. Each condemnation issued from an institution that disposed of careers, of liberty, and in the Moscow years of lives; each recantation was the known price of remaining inside the institution's protection; and the drawer manuscript proves that in the central case the author's argued convictions survived the performance intact. On this reading the recantations are data about the institution, not about the man, and the biographical question dissolves into the political one: what kind of movement requires its philosophers to eat their books, and what did the eating cost the philosophy that survived it. The second reading holds that the recantations were, in significant part, sincere, and its evidence is the development, which is also real. Lukacs did change his mind about load bearing matters: reading the Paris manuscripts and the early Hegel genuinely displaced the identical subject object from the centre of his thought; the aesthetics of the later work is a different construction from the philosophy of history of 1923; and the 1967 preface, written when no institution on earth could have compelled its judgements, condemns the book's idealism in terms continuous with the earliest self criticisms, which suggests that some thread of the recantations was conviction all along. On this reading the man was not a ventriloquist's dummy mouthing survival, but a thinker whose evolution happened to run, in part, along tracks the institution also demanded, and the tragedy is the impossibility of separating the tracks from inside or out.

The profile adjudicates nothing between these readings, because the evidence available to it is the evidence available to everyone, and it points in both directions at once: the drawer holds a defence, and the late preface holds a condemnation no drawer was needed for. What the profile does assert is the standard by which the question should be approached. A recantation extracted under threat is not evidence about the recanter's beliefs, and a conviction maintained at cost is: by that standard the 1956 silence weighs more than the formulae of 1924 and 1929, and the 1967 preface, freely written, outweighs both sets of formulae as a guide to what the old man actually thought. Judged by that standard, the life presents neither the hero of resistance that one party of admirers requires nor the serial capitulator of the hostile summaries, but a more difficult object: a thinker of the first rank who spent fifty years inside an institution that demanded periodic self abasement, paid the demand often enough to survive, kept a private ledger in a drawer, changed his mind genuinely about the things the manuscripts taught him, and at the end, when the demand came for the one recantation that would have condemned his own revolution, did not pay. The difficulty of that object is the reason the profile exists. Easy lives do not need thirty thousand words, and easy verdicts do not need the record stated plainly.

## What the recantations cost the work: the books not written

An assessment confined to the books that exist will understate the life, because the recantations have a bibliography of their own, written in negative. The defence manuscript of the nineteen twenties is its best documented item: a full length philosophical answer to the book's critics, composed while its author was publicly accepting their verdict, and withheld from print for seventy years, so that the debate it might have altered was conducted, and concluded, without the strongest statement of one side. The cost there is measurable in the history of the doctrine, which codified the condemnation into the authorised accounts of Marxist philosophy for two generations, in textbooks from which the essays were absent or present only as a deviation with a name. A second item is the aesthetics, the systematic treatise of his Heidelberg youth, abandoned when its author concluded that the philosophical world in which it made sense had been superseded, and resumed only in old age on new foundations; between the abandonment and the resumption lie forty years in which the most gifted philosophical intelligence the movement possessed published its aesthetics as a campaign, in essays and polemics, rather than as the system it had twice attempted. A third item is quieter: the works of the Moscow and postwar decades were written inside a perimeter of the sayable that their author's own recantations had helped to police, and the reader who compares the speculative freedom of the essays of 1923 with the guarded constructions of the later books is reading, among other things, the price list of the survival those recantations purchased.

A fourth item in the ledger is prospective rather than retrospective, and it closes the catalogue with the right symmetry. The Ontology was designed as the foundation of a system whose upper storeys its author did not live to build: an ethics, in which the doctrine of labour and of the species was to issue in an account of how human beings ought to live inside the world their work creates, and a renewed treatment of the aesthetic on the foundation the Ontology had laid. Visitors to the Budapest apartment in the final years were shown the plans with the old man's undiminished architectonic confidence, and the death that interrupted the foundation left the system's intended crown unwritten, so that the oeuvre ends, as the formation began, with a vast design in a drawer. The symmetry is not a literary imposition. It is the shape the century imposed on every totalising intelligence it housed, and its appearance at both ends of this life, the Heidelberg aesthetics abandoned for the revolution, the Budapest ethics abandoned to mortality, frames the achievement that stands between them: not the system, which was never completed in any of its versions, but the concept, which was completed once, in 1923, in exile and in haste, and which no subsequent design succeeded in superseding.

The profile enters this ledger without drawing the moral that partisans of either reading would prefer. It does not follow that a Lukacs who had refused every recantation would have written more or better; the likelier counterfactual, on the evidence of his contemporaries' fates, is a shorter bibliography and a longer silence, imposed by exile from the movement's institutions or by the camps that consumed so many of the men who had shared his formation. Nor does it follow that the recantations were simply the tax on a career, since the same decades produced The Young Hegel and the historical novel study, works no tax collector ordered and no perimeter required. What follows is narrower and, for the assessment of the man, decisive: the oeuvre that exists is jointly authored by the thinker and by the institution that alternately sheltered and scourged him, and any reading of the work that ignores the second author will misunderstand the first. The concept at the centre of the oeuvre has a bearing on its author's fate that he was too intelligent not to have noticed. He had argued that a human product can acquire the autonomy of a second nature and confront its producers as an alien law. The movement he served was, among other things, such a product, and the recantations are the points in the record where its autonomy and his authorship met. A theory that can describe its author's predicament is not thereby vindicated. But a biography that finds the predicament everywhere in the record, and the theory nowhere embarrassed by it, is entitled to note the fit.

## Lesser objections, answered in passing

Before the strongest objection is heard, four lesser ones should be cleared from the record, since they circulate more widely and persuade more readers than they should. The first holds that the concept is a German mist, a metaphor of thinghood inflated into a theory. The worked example answers it: the factory analysis, the legal analysis, and the administrative analysis are stated in terms that can be checked against the institutions they describe, and a metaphor does not generate checkable claims about the training of judges. The second holds that the concept cannot see resistance, that its world is a closed cage in which workers never strike, officials never bend a rule, and readers never rebel. The concept's own logic answers it: reification is a tendency of a form, contested at every point by the producers whose product the form is, and the standpoint doctrine exists precisely because the cage's bars are the prisoners' own activity, which can be withdrawn or redirected; a theory whose centre is the possibility of the form's abolition is not well described as a theory of closure. The third holds that the argument romanticises the world before the commodity, the craftsman at his bench bathed in a prelapsarian glow. The texts answer it: the essay's author had no tenderness for the guild world, whose unfreedom he knew from the inside of its literature, and the contrast in the worked example is analytical, between two organisations of labour, not elegiac, between a paradise and a fall. The fourth objection is the philosopher's stone of the set, the charge of unfalsifiability: that a theory which explains both quiescence and revolt as products of its central form has insured itself against refutation. The charge has a target, and the profile concedes the target's existence in the concept's diluted descendants, where reification is indeed invoked as a universal solvent. Against the essay itself the charge must specify which of its claims could not have been wrong, and the claims are more exposed than the charge suggests: that rationalisation moulds the stance of those it organises, that the stance appears in province after province with a common structure, that a class's consciousness is a function of its position rather than its information. Each could have been false. That the century's evidence has been kinder to them than to their rivals is the finding the strongest objection must now attempt to disturb.

## The strongest objection: an analogy promoted to an argument

The objection must be stated at the strength its best proponents would insist on, and it is this. The reification essay moves from the commodity form, where its analysis is exact, to the whole of social life and then to the whole of modern thought, and at each stage of that ascent the connective tissue thins from argument into analogy. That production is organised by calculability is demonstrable. That law, administration, and journalism exhibit forms resembling the factory's is a suggestive parallel, open to other explanations: states require predictability for reasons internal to governing, professions develop routines for reasons internal to their crafts, and none of this needs the commodity form as its cause. That the antinomies of Kantian philosophy reproduce the structure of the commodity is, on the objection, not an explanation at all but a metaphor in the dress of one, since no mechanism is specified by which the organisation of production could dictate the architecture of the Critique of Pure Reason, and the essay's undoubted rhetorical power, its capacity to make a century of disparate phenomena suddenly rhyme, is precisely what should make a careful reader suspicious. Rhyming is what analogies do. Causes are harder, and the essay, in the objection's verdict, nowhere pays the harder price: it asserts the penetration it needed to prove, and its totality is less a finding than a licence, issued by the author to himself, to treat resemblance as descent.

The answer begins by identifying the mechanism the essay does in fact claim, because the objection, in its strong form, sometimes attacks a version of the argument the essay does not make. The claimed mechanism is not influence, not imitation, and not the direct dictation of philosophy by economics. It is the generalisation of a stance. A social order in which the commodity form organises production trains its participants, in the daily conduct of their activities, to meet the world as a field of calculable objects governed by laws, and to meet their own capacities as objects among them. That stance, practised at the bench, the desk, and the counter until it becomes the unexamined shape of experience itself, is then available as the form in which every other province is encountered: the administrator meets the case, the journalist meets the event, the philosopher meets the object of knowledge, each in the posture the economy has normalised. The claim is thus a claim about the formation of subjects, of the same logical type as the claim that a literate society forms a different consciousness from an oral one, and it stands or falls with the evidence that forms of practice mould forms of experience. So stated, the middle ranges of the essay are genuinely arguable and partly demonstrable: the rationalisation of law and administration in the period is a documented process with documented carriers, and the homology between the factory's subdivision of the worker and the office's subdivision of the case is a matter of record, not of rhyme.

Then the concession, which the brief for this profile requires and the evidence supports: the strongest version of the claim is not demonstrated. Between the moulding of experience by practice and the architecture of Kant's antinomies there lies a distance the essay crosses by assertion, and subsequent philosophy has not supplied the missing span on its behalf. The identical subject object, the construction meant to guarantee that the totality could be known from the standpoint that would also transform it, did not survive its author's own criticism, and without it the extension to thought itself loses its keystone and subsides into a research programme: a standing instruction to look for the commodity form's signature in province after province, fruitful wherever the mechanisms of formation can be specified, and merely incantatory wherever they cannot. That is a considerable residue. A research programme with several demonstrated provinces and one undemonstrated crown is not a refuted theory, and the concept's career, to which the profile now turns, is largely the story of other thinkers accepting the programme while quietly setting the crown aside.

One further defence, developed by the concept's most careful later readers, should be entered before the concession is allowed to close the account, because it changes what the concession concedes. The defence observes that the essay's central category is not the commodity as a thing but the form of objectivity: the specific shape in which objects, and the knowledge appropriate to them, are constituted for a society. On this reading, the extension to law, administration, and philosophy is not the claim that the economy causes the other provinces in the manner of a billiard ball, but the claim that a single form of objectivity, first isolated in the commodity because it appears there in its purest state, is at work wherever the society constitutes its objects as calculable, law governed, and external to the subject. The relation between the provinces is then one of shared form rather than of causal descent, and the demand for a mechanism connecting the factory to the Critique, so fatal to the causal reading, is answered by the demand's own misconception: forms are not transmitted like infections but instituted, reproduced in each province by the province's own practices, wherever those practices must render a human material calculable in advance. This reading does not rescue the identical subject object, and it does not demonstrate the Kantian crown; the essay's last movement remains a promissory note. What it rescues is the architecture: a unified account of why the sciences of society, the institutions of the state, and the systems of philosophy in one epoch exhibit one style of objectivity, stated as a thesis about form that subsequent research can confirm province by province. Judged in that form, the essay's standing among its critics is noticeably higher than its standing as a causal theory, and the difference between the two judgements is the most instructive measure of how much of its author's ambition the evidence of a century has sustained.

## The concept's career after its author

The tradition that gathered around the essays, and that this cluster of the series surveys under a name its members did not choose, is introduced in the [western marxism complete guide](/2015/11/01/western-marxism-complete-guide/); the reification essay stands near its source, and the channels of the concept's later career can be followed along four main lines. The first runs through the sociology of knowledge and the Paris of Lucien Goldmann, who took the young Lukacs's method of reading a world view out of a literary form and built from it a genetic structuralism, applying the categories to Pascal and Racine with a rigour that won the method a university audience the party schools never offered. The second runs through the Frankfurt of the Institute for Social Research, where a parallel generalisation of the commodity form into a theory of instrumental reason was developed by thinkers working, at first, in partial independence of the essay, a convergence within the convergence that students of the concept should note: the diagnosis of a world administered into thinghood was reached more than once in that generation, and the priority disputes matter less than the repetition of the finding. The third runs through Merleau Ponty's postwar reckoning, which took Lukacs as the conscience of a Marxism that had kept the dialectic alive and then quarrelled with the use the eastern parties had made of the inheritance, fixing the book's image for a generation of French readers. A third and a half line should be entered for the thinker who stood closest to the concept and quarrelled with it longest. Theodor Adorno had formed his own philosophy in the essays' aftermath, acknowledged the early Lukacs as one of the few Marxist thinkers of rank, and built the critique of the administered world in a neighbouring idiom, of exchange, identity, and the thinghood of the concept itself; yet his public verdict on the later Lukacs, the review that coined the phrase about an extorted reconciliation, remains the most quoted attack on the postwar work ever written by a peer. The doubleness is the Frankfurt relation to this profile's subject in epitome: the concept's most powerful inheritors were also its author's most unsparing judges, and the inheritance was transmitted through the quarrel rather than around it. The fourth runs into the lecture halls of the new century, where Axel Honneth returned to the concept and rebuilt it on a different foundation, proposing that reification be understood as a forgetfulness of the recognition that human relations presuppose, a revision that keeps the concept in circulation by detaching it from the commodity derivation its author had laboured to establish.

The cartography of this inheritance was fixed for the Anglophone world in the nineteen seventies by Perry Anderson's survey of the tradition, which placed the essays of 1923 at the source of the current it named Western Marxism and read the whole subsequent formation, the Frankfurt School and its successors, as the working out of possibilities and defeats that the founding moment contained. Anderson's map has been argued with ever since, and the cluster to which this profile belongs takes up the argument in its own guide to the tradition; for the profile the relevant point is narrower. The map's source is a book its author recanted, written by a man the tradition's official custodians expelled from the canon, and the persistence with which later cartographers return to that source, rather than to any of the authorised texts of the same years, is the reception's own verdict on where the living thought of the period resided. The tradition that the International condemned into existence in 1924 has outlived the International's philosophy in every university in the world, and it begins, by the consent of its historians including the hostile ones, in the essays of the Hungarian commissar.

The scholarly revival of the nineteen seventies added a final channel, and with it the concept acquired the academic infrastructure it retains. In America the journal Telos, founded at the end of the sixties, made the early Lukacs a house author, translating and annotating the essays of the revolutionary years and reading the whole subsequent tradition through the reification problematic; in Britain the translation of the essays joined the new paperback canon of the student left; in Germany the publication of the early writings, and then of the drawer manuscript's era documents, re-founded the scholarly industry on the texts themselves. The revival's significance for the assessment is that it was conducted, in the main, by scholars with no allegiance to the movement that had condemned the book, and their verdict, delivered in the currency of editions, conferences, and citations, is the nearest thing to a controlled judgement the case affords: readers with every institutional reason to treat the essays as a period piece instead treated them as a live research programme, and built on the derivation the profile has described. A concept's second life among its author's enemies' grandchildren is not an argument for its truth. It is an argument, of the sociological kind its author would have appreciated, about where the problems of the age continued to reside.

That last migration states the concept's fate in miniature. What survives of reification in the wider culture is often the word, carrying a charge of protest against a world of things, detached from the derivation that gave the charge its specific weight. Scholars of the concept police the detachment with varying success; the summaries against which this profile was written are evidence of the failure rate. But the survival of the derivation in the scholarly literature is equally a fact, and it rests on the chronology established above: a concept that was reached independently of the text it seemed to copy, and that regenerated a sealed problematic from public sources, has a claim on the attention of anyone who studies how social forms mould the minds that live in them. Lukacs died in Budapest in 1971, at work on the ontology that was to gather the whole into a final system and that his death left in the state in which his century had left so many systems, unfinished and enormous. The concept had already escaped the system, and the man, and the movement that had alternately published and condemned them both. It remains in the keeping of its readers, which is where this profile, its work of separation and record done, is content to leave it.

## The concept abroad: translation, dilution, and return

The Anglophone career of the book began late enough to constitute a second discovery. History and Class Consciousness circulated for decades in French and German while English readers met its arguments at second hand, and the standard English translation appeared only in 1971, the year of its author's death, nearly half a century after the essays were written. The delay shaped the reception. The book arrived in English in the aftermath of the student movements of the late nineteen sixties, whose participants had reached its themes by their own roads and who read it, consequently, as a contemporary's work rather than as a document of 1923: the critique of bureaucracy, the refusal of a Marxism of iron laws, and the insistence that consciousness was a battlefield all spoke to a generation arguing with its own communist parties and its own universities. University syllabuses absorbed the reification essay into the canon of social theory, where it keeps company with Weber and Durkheim in the curricula of departments that would not describe themselves as Marxist at all, an institutional fate its author, who wrote for a movement, might have found ambiguous. The absorption had the usual price of canonisation: the essay became a classic, assigned and excerpted, its standpoint doctrine quietly bracketed by editors and lecturers who wanted the diagnosis without the party that the diagnosis was built to arm.

Sociology performed the most consequential act of appropriation and dilution in a single gesture. In The Social Construction of Reality, the treatise of 1966 that founded a school of phenomenological sociology, Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann took over the word reification and generalised it into a universal moment of world building: the apprehension of human products as if they were facts of nature, a process their account locates in every society that objectifies its institutions in language and habit. The borrowing was acknowledged and the transformation was total. Where Lukacs's concept is historical, naming a structure specific to the society of the generalised commodity form and armed with a standpoint from which it can be broken, the sociologists' version is anthropological, a permanent possibility of consciousness as such, to be described rather than abolished. The migration illustrates, better than any polemic, the two fates available to a concept of this power: it can be kept at its original strength, bound to its derivation and its agent, at the price of remaining the property of a tradition; or it can be diluted into a general instrument of social description, at the price of the politics that made it dangerous. The word's presence in the vocabulary of journalists and managers, where it now signifies little more than treating people as things, marks the dilution's furthest reach, and the profile's table and derivations exist, in part, to give readers the means of returning the word to its strength when the occasion demands it. That the return remains possible, that the essay of 1923 still reads as a live option rather than a museum piece to the students who encounter it in those syllabuses, is the final evidence the profile offers about its subject: the concept was built, by a man working from a single chapter and a sealed archive, to survive the circumstances that were supposed to refute it, including its author's own signature on the refutations.

## The Budapest School: the disciples and the second exile of the ideas

The last institutional fact of the life is a school, and its fate recapitulates its founder's in a minor key, as if the pattern were hereditary. In the Budapest of the nineteen sixties, the elderly philosopher's seminar gathered a circle of younger thinkers, Agnes Heller, Ferenc Feher, Gyorgy Markus, and Mihaly Vajda among them, who took from the master the early work's questions and the late work's ontological ambition and set about rebuilding a Marxist philosophy of everyday life, of needs, and of the human substance that the official doctrine had mislaid. For a season the school flourished under his protection, publishing in the interstices of the permissible, its members' books appearing abroad in the languages of the West. Then the protection was withdrawn by the same logic that had withdrawn his own, two generations earlier and in a harder currency: the school's work was condemned by the party's cultural authorities in the early nineteen seventies as an alien deviation, its members were removed from their posts, and the road of emigration, the road of 1919, opened for the disciples as it had for the master. Several rebuilt their careers in the universities of the West, carrying the early Lukacs's questions into departments of sociology and philosophy on three continents. The profile records the episode because it completes the evidence about the institution whose price the recantations paid. In his ninth decade, the philosopher watched the movement he had served for fifty years, in the country whose revolution he had served as a commissar, expel his own students for the crime of taking his questions seriously. What he said about the episode in private has been reported variously and is not asserted here. What the record shows is the fact, and the fact is the last exhibit in the hearing this profile has conducted: the second nature he had theorised at thirty eight was still, at eighty five, confronting its producers as an alien law, and its producers now included the school that bore his name.

## The profile's evidence: sources and their limits

A profile that has made so much of a sealed archive and a drawer manuscript owes the reader a short account of its own evidence, and of the cautions that evidence imposes. The documentary base for the life falls into four deposits. There are the published works, in their successive editions, whose revisions across the decades are themselves evidence, since the author edited his past as energetically as he recanted it, and the textual history of the essays, from the journal versions through the collection to the reissue with its famous preface, is a primary source for the development this profile has traced. There is the correspondence, with publishers, with writers of the emigration, with the friends of the Budapest years, much of it printed, in which the public doctrines appear in their private registers and the man's courtesy, pedantry, courage, and calculation are all on display in proportions no memoir would have chosen. There are the institutional archives, of the parties and the institutes and the states that employed and imprisoned him, whose files record the condemnations and the recantations in the bureaucracy's own hand, invaluable for dates and worthless, without corroboration, for motives. And there are the dictated recollections of the final years, the old man's own narrative of the life, in which the commissar's summer is acknowledged and the recantations are arranged into a development, a source of the first importance and of a known bias, since its author had spent fifty years learning which arrangements of his past the record would bear. The profile has proceeded by weighing these deposits against one another, asserting the facts on which they converge, the chronology of the derivation, the sequence of the condemnations, the responsibility at the front, and flagging, at the points where they diverge, the divergence itself as the finding. On the two questions where the deposits conflict irreconcilably, what the recantations meant and what the 1956 silence meant, the profile has declined to purchase a verdict with someone else's certainty. That declension is not indecision. It is the form that accuracy takes when the evidence is the kind this life left behind.

The deposits have a postscript that belongs to the romance of archives and that a researcher should know before trusting any edition's completeness. Before leaving Heidelberg at the end of the war, the young philosopher deposited a suitcase of manuscripts and letters in a bank in that city, the working papers of the aesthetics project and the diary of his formative years among them, and the suitcase outlived the bank's century: through the inflation, the republic, the dictatorship, and the occupation, the luggage of a stateless revolutionary sat in a vault in the town where Weber had received him, unclaimed because its owner could not safely claim it and then because the claim had been forgotten in the press of survival. Recovered in the nineteen seventies, after its author's death, the Heidelberg papers gave scholarship the formation's missing interior: the diary in which the young critic records his despair and his ambitions in the years of The Soul and Form, and the drafts from which the systematic aesthetics of the drawer can be reconstructed. The episode is more than a curiosity for this profile's purposes. It is the archival form of the life's central pattern, the work preserved by being withheld, the manuscript in the vault answering the manuscript in the Moscow drawer across sixty years; and it is a standing warning to every user of the printed record, this profile included, that the deposits are not closed. Papers continue to surface for a figure whose century scattered them across four countries' archives, and the verdicts that depend on the drawer, on both sides of the recantations hearing, are provisional in the strict scholarly sense: true to the evidence that exists, and hostage to the evidence that may yet be carried out of a vault.

## For students and exam candidates

Begin with the separation, not the life. The single most reliable way to lose ground on this topic is to write about reification as if it were alienation under a German accent, and the single most reliable way to gain it is to state, early and exactly, what each of the three concepts in the table claims and at what level it operates. From the table, move to the chronology: the independent derivation is the highest value fact in the whole topic, because it converts a definition question into an argument about the unity of Marx's work, and arguments are what examiners reward. The text of the book itself, its essays and their order, is laid out in the [essay by essay guide to History and Class Consciousness](/2015/12/01/history-and-class-consciousness-guide/), which should be read alongside this profile rather than after it. Transfer the three concepts table into your own notes before the examination period begins; the study system at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) holds the table as a set of cards, one concept per card with its source text and its commonest confusion, and spaced review of three cards will fix the separation more durably than an evening of rereading. Practise the one sentence version aloud: reification is what the commodity form does to a whole society and to the consciousness that society forms, derived from Capital before the manuscripts on alienation could be read.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The three concepts table is the teaching instrument this profile was built to deliver, and it resolves the confusion that consumes more seminar time than any other in this cluster: students arrive with fetishism, alienation, and reification fused into one complaint, and they argue past each other for weeks because the fusion is never named. Put the table on the screen in the first session and require every subsequent contribution to declare its concept and its level before it proceeds; the discipline feels pedantic for ten minutes and then begins to pay compound interest. A productive seminar sequence runs from the table to the derivation, since the chronology gives students a genuine puzzle, how a sealed text's argument can be regenerated, rather than a doctrine to absorb, and from the derivation to the repudiations, where the two readings should be assigned to opposing halves of the room and defended from the evidence in the profile, the drawer manuscript doing duty for both sides. Close with the strongest objection, and insist that the answer's concession be stated as carefully as its mechanism; students who can hold the concession and the claim together have learned the transferable skill, which is the evaluation of a theory by the strength of what survives its refutation.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research problems on this topic are problems of editions and chronology, and they should be approached through the series bibliography rather than through the general literature. The [reading guide to the Western Marxist tradition](/2016/03/15/western-marxism-reading-guide/) surveys the editions of Lukacs's works, the publication history of the manuscripts whose absence this profile makes so much of, and the state of the archival record for the exile years, and it should be the first port of call for anyone citing the 1967 preface against the texts of the nineteen twenties. The chronology argument itself, and its bearing on the standing quarrel between the humanist and anti humanist readings of Marx, is developed in the article on the [young Marx against the mature Marx](/2011/06/01/young-marx-vs-mature-marx/), which assembles the dating evidence in full. Journalists covering the concept's periodic returns to public argument should note the two facts this profile leads with, since both are routinely got wrong in print: the derivation preceded any possible reading of the manuscripts, and the recantations were multiple, public, and are interpreted by serious scholarship in both of the ways set out above. A report that states either fact in its flattened form, the plagiarism that never happened or the sell out that cannot be documented, will be wrong in a way its subject's actual record does not excuse.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Who was Georg Lukacs?

Georg Lukacs (1885 to 1971) was a Hungarian philosopher and literary critic whose book of essays published in 1923 made him a founding figure of the Western Marxist tradition. Born into a wealthy Budapest banking family, he wrote celebrated works on the drama, the essay, and the novel before joining the Hungarian communist party at the close of 1918. He served the short lived Hungarian Soviet Republic as an education commissar and then as a political commissar at the front, spent the interwar years in exile in Vienna, Berlin, and Moscow, returned to Hungary after the war, served briefly as culture minister during the 1956 revolution, and was deported for his part in it. His standing rests on one concept, reification, and on the strange fact that he disowned the book containing it more than once.

### Q: What is reification?

Reification names the transformation that occurs when the commodity form stops being one feature of economic life and becomes the model for social life as a whole. Human relationships, which people create and could in principle arrange otherwise, harden into something resembling a landscape of objects: prices, offices, procedures, and credentials that seem to belong to the nature of things. Those caught inside such a landscape relate to it as spectators of a machinery, adapting and calculating rather than authoring. Lukacs built the idea by joining Marx's analysis of the commodity to the sociology of rationalisation, and he argued that the deformation reaches beyond markets into courts, ministries, newsrooms, and the categories of philosophy itself. It is a claim about the shape of a society and of the awareness that society produces in its members.

### Q: Is reification the same as commodity fetishism?

They are kin, not twins. Fetishism, as Marx develops it in the opening chapter of Capital, concerns a trick of appearance confined to exchange: the value of a product looks like a quality the product owns, although it records the labour and the social organisation behind the product. Reification starts from that kernel and asks a larger question, namely what becomes of institutions and of minds when the commodity pattern supplies the template for an entire civilisation. Its sources are also doubled, since alongside Marx it draws on the German sociology of money and rational administration. Where fetishism diagnoses how a thing can seem alive with social power, reification diagnoses how persons come to treat their own powers as things, and how law, bureaucracy, and philosophy absorb the same posture.

### Q: Had Lukacs read Marx's early manuscripts before writing about reification?

No, and the impossibility is documentary rather than a matter of testimony. The notebooks in which Marx explored estranged labour were composed in Paris in 1844 and then left unpublished among his papers; editors brought them to print only in 1932. Lukacs published his essays in 1923. When he finally encountered the manuscripts, it was in Moscow at the start of the nineteen thirties, in the course of work at the institute preparing Marx's literary estate for publication, which made him one of the earliest readers the notebooks ever received. He remarked in later years on how striking the overlap was between his own construction and the text he had never seen. The episode matters because it rules out derivation by copying and turns his essay into evidence about the source he did use, the commodity chapter of Capital.

### Q: What is the identical subject-object?

The phrase names Lukacs's proposed solution to the deadlock his diagnosis creates. If society confronts its members as an alien machinery, knowledge of that machinery threatens to remain another spectator's report, accurate and powerless. His answer assigns the proletariat a unique double position: it is the commodity order's product, sold daily in the labour market, and simultaneously the one standpoint from which the order can be grasped as a human product rather than a fate. A class that understands itself in those terms is, in his construction, both the subject doing the knowing and the object known, and its self knowledge is therefore already a practical act. Later criticism, including Lukacs's own in old age, judged the construction overreached, a Hegelian reconciliation transferred onto an empirical class.

### Q: Why did Lukacs repudiate his own book?

The honest answer is that the materials support two explanations and exclude neither. Condemnation by the Communist International in 1924 marked the book as idealist deviation, and within the movement of that period a public self criticism was the recognised price of remaining in good standing, a price whose refusal could end a career or, in the Moscow years, a life. Lukacs paid it, more than once, and also defended the book in a long manuscript he kept unpublished, which suggests a man separating his survival from his convictions. Yet his thought genuinely moved: encounters with the early Marx and with Hegel's development altered his positions, and the preface he wrote freely in old age criticises the book's idealism as sharply as any censor had. Readers must weigh coercion and conviction together, since the record will not separate them.

### Q: What was Lukacs's role in the Hungarian revolution?

He filled two roles in the Hungarian Soviet Republic of 1919, and an honest summary keeps both in view. As deputy commissar for education he drove a rapid democratisation of schools, theatres, and museums, treating cultural provision as a revolutionary obligation. When the front collapsed in the summer, he volunteered as political commissar with a division on the Tisza and, in that office, bore responsibility for the execution of deserters, an episode documented by biographers and acknowledged in his own late recollections. He defended the act as a necessity of a disintegrating front. Accounts of the brief republic differ sharply in detail and in judgement, and the tallies attached to its violence are contested, but the dual record itself, the school reforms and the shootings, is not in dispute.

### Q: What is the Brecht and Lukacs debate about?

The debate concerns the forms a socially truthful art requires. Lukacs, theorising for the anti fascist emigration in the nineteen thirties, defended the great realist novel, Balzac and Tolstoy and Thomas Mann, as the form able to render a society whole, and he treated modernist experiment, fragmentation, montage, interior monologue pursued for its own sake, as the aesthetic reflex of a decaying order. Brecht, answering in notebooks he left unpublished, argued that a transformed reality demanded transformed instruments: a theatre built to interrupt the spectator's identification and compel judgement, and a definition of realism wide enough to include any technique that lays bare the causal network of society. Underneath the quarrel about technique sits a quarrel about audiences, whether a work should school its public through achieved totality or provoke it through estrangement.

### Q: Is The Destruction of Reason a defensible book?

The prevailing scholarly judgement is that it is not, and this profile does not argue otherwise. Published in 1954, the book arranges a lineage of German thought as a single descent from the critique of Enlightenment reason into fascist irrationalism, and it judges each figure by his assigned station on that road. Critics object that the method convicts by genealogy: ambiguities are planed away, countervailing evidence is omitted, and thinkers whose work also armed the opponents of fascism are treated as its unwitting quartermasters. The question the book posed, how a highly cultivated civilisation bred a politics of myth, remains a serious one, and its author confronted that question earlier than most. But a serious question does not rescue a prosecutorial method, and the work is best read as evidence of what its author's own method became when its conclusion was settled by allegiance in advance.

### Q: Where should you start reading Lukacs?

Start with the central essay of History and Class Consciousness, the study of reification and the consciousness of the proletariat, in the standard English translation, and read it slowly with the commodity chapter of Capital open beside it, since its argument rebuilds that chapter stone by stone. The shorter programmatic essays in the same volume, on orthodoxy and on class consciousness, supply the frame. Readers drawn to the early work should then take The Theory of the Novel, which shows the philosophical imagination before its Marxist turn. The big late constructions, the Aesthetics and the Ontology, reward specialists and punish the curious, and the irrationalism volume is best approached, if at all, after the essay, as a measure of distance travelled. His own preface to the reissue of the essays, written in old age, is the sharpest short guide to where he came to think the book had gone wrong.

### Q: How does the independent derivation change the reading of the commodity chapter?

It converts the chapter from a foundation stone into a living centre. Commentators who treat the opening of Capital as a preliminary to the real economics, the theory of surplus value and accumulation, must now explain how a reader confined to that opening regenerated, unaided, the problematic of the early manuscripts. The derivation suggests that the fetishism section is not a detachable preface but the germ of the whole critique: a compressed account of how social form moulds consciousness, from which the theory of alienation can be unfolded and to which it remains answerable. It also disciplines interpretation in the other direction. Because the derivation is documented, claims about the unity of Marx's thought can appeal to it as evidence, a mind demonstrably extracting the early problematic from the late text, rather than resting on the interpreter's say so.

### Q: What should a student separate between reification and alienation?

Separate the site, the subject, and the remedy. Alienation is sited in the labour process: it names the worker's estrangement from the thing he produces, from the producing itself, from the human capacities the process might have realised, and from his fellows. Reification is sited in the social form as a whole: it names the hardening of human relations into a quasi natural order of things, and the contemplative posture that order breeds in everyone it touches, managers and philosophers included. The subjects differ accordingly, since alienation's sufferer is paradigmatically the producer, while reification also captures the official, the judge, and the trader. And the remedies implied differ: alienation points toward a transformation of labour, reification toward a transformation of the form that organises labour and everything beyond it. Examiners reward the separation because fusing the two forfeits exactly this analytical reach.

### Q: Why did the essay collection matter more than any single essay?

Because the collection performs an operation no single essay could: it shows one method at work across the whole range of the movement's problems, and thereby founds a way of doing Marxist theory rather than a set of opinions. The essays on orthodoxy, on Luxemburg, on class consciousness, on organisation, and on reification were written as interventions across four years, and gathered they reveal their author's wager, that the failures of the European revolutions were failures of consciousness before they were failures of force, and that philosophy was therefore a practical weapon. Movements and traditions form around methods more durably than around conclusions, and this book supplied the method, totality grasped through the commodity form, from which a generation of thinkers, the Frankfurt School among them, took their bearings, often in disagreement with every political position its author held.

### Q: How does class consciousness follow from the reification argument?

The connection runs through the worker's double experience. Reification analysis says the worker's own capacity reaches him as a commodity he owns and sells, an object whose performance he watches as a spectator. Class consciousness, in the essays' construction, is what happens when the spectator recognises the machinery as a human product and his spectatorship as a position assigned by it: the commodity that knows itself as a commodity has already begun to stop being one. That is why the essays distinguish the consciousness workers empirically hold, which reification accurately predicts to be fragmented, from the consciousness imputable to their objective position, which the theory supplies. Critics object that the imputation licenses a party to substitute its doctrine for the class's voice, and the objection has shadowed the book since its publication; the full treatment of the concept lives in the series article devoted to class consciousness.

### Q: What did the later self criticism concede and what did it keep?

The preface Lukacs wrote for the reissue of his essays, in his eighties, concedes the architecture and keeps the questions. Conceded: that the identical subject object was a metaphysical construction, an attempt to outdo Hegel on Hegel's own ground; that the book had blurred objectification, a universal feature of human practice, into alienation, its historically specific deformation; and that its picture of the proletariat owed more to philosophical need than to observation. Kept: that the essays had posed, earlier than the authorised philosophy of the movement could, the problem of how a reified world forms the consciousness that might transform it; that the commodity analysis of Capital contained that problem entire; and that the book belonged, whatever its errors, to the genuine history of Marxist thought rather than to its deviations. The balance sheet is the author's own, drawn up when no institution could still compel his signature.
