In one calendar year, American publishing produced two long looks at the same figure: a poor young man who decides that wealth and status are obtainable, pursues them past the point of safety, and dies for it. Gatsby vs An American Tragedy is therefore not a comparison a critic invented after the fact. It is a coincidence of publication that turns out not to be a coincidence at all, since two very different writers, working in incompatible styles and with opposite theories of human character, reached for the identical subject in 1925 and reached similar conclusions about what the country does to the man who tries to climb it.

![Gatsby vs An American Tragedy comparing Fitzgerald and Dreiser on class and ambition in 1925 - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

Fitzgerald's novel appeared in April 1925. Theodore Dreiser's appeared that December, running to something over eight hundred pages against Fitzgerald's slim two hundred. One is lyric, elliptical, and narrated by a participant who withholds. The other is documentary, exhaustive, and narrated by a voice that explains everything. Readers who love one often find the other unreadable. Yet the plots rhyme with a precision that should stop anyone who notices it: a young man from a religious, impoverished background changes his name or his story, attaches himself to a woman who represents a class he cannot otherwise enter, becomes entangled in a death on the water, and is destroyed while the people with money remain untouched.

## Gatsby vs An American Tragedy: What the 1925 Pairing Compares

The comparison has to begin with accurate accounts of both books, because most students meet Dreiser only through this pairing and vagueness is immediately visible to a marker.

The Great Gatsby follows Jay Gatsby, born James Gatz on a North Dakota farm, who acquires an enormous illegal fortune and installs himself in a West Egg mansion across the bay from Daisy Buchanan, the woman he loved five years earlier as a poor army officer. He wants her to leave her husband and to declare that she never loved him. She will not. Gatsby takes the blame for a death he did not cause and is shot in his own pool by a man deceived into believing he was the driver.

An American Tragedy follows Clyde Griffiths, the son of itinerant street missionaries in Kansas City, who escapes a childhood of public religious poverty into hotel work, flees the city after a fatal car accident involving his fellow bellhops, and eventually finds employment at his wealthy uncle's collar factory in the town of Lycurgus, New York. There he supervises a young factory worker, Roberta Alden, and begins a secret relationship with her while pursuing Sondra Finchley, a rich young woman whose circle represents everything Clyde has wanted since he first saw a hotel lobby. Roberta becomes pregnant. Clyde takes her out on a lake, she drowns, and the question of what exactly he did is left deliberately murky. He is tried, convicted, and executed. Dreiser drew the plot from a real 1906 case in upstate New York.

### What do The Great Gatsby and An American Tragedy have in common?

Both are 1925 novels about a poor young man who reinvents himself to reach a wealthy woman and is destroyed in the attempt. Both involve a death on the water, both leave the rich untouched by consequence, and both treat individual ambition as something the class system manufactures and then punishes.

Set out plainly, the structural rhyme is startling. Each novel begins with a boy whose origins embarrass him. Each gives that boy a moment of first contact with a world of money, and each treats that moment as formative rather than incidental. Each supplies a woman who is less a person than a certificate of admission, and each supplies a second woman or a second household that represents the life the man is trying to leave. Each turns on water: a lake in Dreiser, a swimming pool and a bay in Fitzgerald. Each ends with the poor man dead and the wealthy families intact.

What differs is everything about the telling, and that difference is the substance of the comparison. Dreiser explains causation at every step, tracing Clyde's decisions to environment, appetite, fear, and circumstance, in the manner of literary naturalism. Fitzgerald refuses explanation almost entirely, giving Gatsby's inner life in two or three concentrated passages and otherwise leaving him a rumor. The two novels therefore constitute a controlled experiment in method: same subject, opposite techniques, and a reader can watch what each technique can and cannot see. That is what makes this pairing more instructive than a pairing of two books that merely agree.

The comparison also corrects a common assumption about Fitzgerald's novel, which is that its concern with class is atmosphere rather than argument. Dreiser leaves no room for that reading. His book is unmistakably about the machinery of American class, and once a reader has seen the machinery described at length, the same machinery becomes visible in the compressed form Fitzgerald gives it. The [self-made man myth in The Great Gatsby](/2025/01/13/self-made-man-myth-gatsby/) is the shared subject, handled at two very different magnifications.

## The Genuine Points of Contact

Five contacts are real enough to carry an argument.

The first is the invented self. Fitzgerald gives the operation a famous sentence in Chapter 6: "James Gatz that was really, or at least legally, his name." The clause is precise about what invention can and cannot change, since the legal name persists underneath. Fitzgerald then names the machinery, that Jay Gatsby "sprang from his platonic conception of himself," that "he was a son of God," and that he must therefore serve "a vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty." Clyde Griffiths performs a quieter version of the same operation. He does not change his name; he changes what he lets people believe about his family, his money, and his prospects, and the entire Lycurgus phase of Dreiser's novel depends on the gap between what Clyde is and what he permits the Griffiths circle to assume. Both men are running a fiction, and both fictions are believed by the people who matter until they are not.

The second contact is the formative encounter with luxury. Fitzgerald locates Gatsby's conversion in Louisville, in Daisy's house, and states the effect exactly: Gatsby was "overwhelmingly aware of the youth and mystery that wealth imprisons and preserves." He adds a detail that makes the class content unmistakable, since "Her porch was bright with the bought luxury of star-shine." Dreiser locates Clyde's conversion in a hotel lobby, where a boy raised on street-corner hymns first sees what money looks like when it is spent. Neither man ever recovers from the first sight. Both novels treat that early exposure as the moment a life is set on a track, and both treat the desire produced as unappeasable rather than satisfiable.

### Is Clyde Griffiths a version of Jay Gatsby?

Not a version, a counterpart. Both are poor young men who reinvent themselves for a wealthy woman and die for the attempt. But Gatsby has enormous agency and enormous money before he fails, while Clyde has neither and fails on the way up. Fitzgerald tests arrival; Dreiser tests the climb itself.

The third contact is the woman as certificate. Fitzgerald is direct about this to a degree students often underestimate. In Chapter 7 Gatsby says of Daisy, "Her voice is full of money," and Nick's reaction confirms rather than corrects him. Chapter 6 adds that "It excited him, too, that many men had already loved Daisy," which locates her value in demand rather than in character. Sondra Finchley functions identically for Clyde. She is beautiful, but what makes her indispensable is that she is the visible property of a class, and Dreiser is explicit that Clyde's feeling for her is inseparable from what she would confirm about him. Both novels therefore stage romance as class transaction while allowing their protagonists to experience it as love.

The fourth contact is the death on the water and the question of guilt. Dreiser structures his entire second half around ambiguity: Clyde planned something, then did not act, then acted through inaction, and the law resolves the ambiguity in one direction while the novel refuses to. Fitzgerald arranges an inversion. Gatsby is entirely innocent of Myrtle Wilson's death, since Daisy was driving, and he is killed anyway, and the killer believes he has found the guilty party. Placed together, the two novels make a shared point about culpability under American class: the poor man's guilt is settled by the machinery whether or not he committed the act, and the rich party's guilt is not raised at all.

The fifth contact is the untouched elite. Tom and Daisy Buchanan leave. Nick's judgment is that "They were careless people, Tom and Daisy," people who "smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money." Dreiser's Griffiths family and the Finchley circle likewise absorb no consequence; Clyde's trial is partly a scandal to be managed, and the class closes around itself. Neither novel offers the reader the satisfaction of the wealthy being held to account, and in both cases the refusal is the argument. The [class anxiety in The Great Gatsby](/2025/02/03/class-anxiety-great-gatsby/) that drives Gatsby's every move is answered by the elite's total insulation from consequence.

## The Decisive Differences: Compression Against Accumulation

Four differences change the argument rather than decorate it.

The first is method of proof. Dreiser proves by exhaustion. Every stage of Clyde's descent is documented: the wages, the boarding house rules, the factory hierarchy, the cost of a dress, the mechanics of a small-town social season, the procedure of a county trial. The reader is not asked to infer. By the time Clyde is in the death house, the novel has established the causal chain so thoroughly that the verdict feels less like a judgment on a man than a report on a system. Fitzgerald proves by omission. He gives the reader almost nothing about how Gatsby made his money, almost nothing about the mechanics of bootlegging, and almost nothing about Gatsby's interior life outside three concentrated passages. The reader has to construct the chain, and the construction is the reading experience.

The second difference is theory of character. Dreiser is a naturalist. His people are shaped by heredity, environment, appetite, and chance, and their choices are constrained until the word choice becomes doubtful. That is a philosophical position, not merely a style, and it explains why the novel spends so long on conditions. Fitzgerald is a romantic writing about romanticism, and his protagonist is defined by a capacity that naturalism has no category for. Nick names it as "an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness" and locates it as the thing that separated Gatsby from everyone else. Dreiser could not have written that sentence. Clyde has appetites and fears; he does not have a gift.

### Why does Dreiser need eight hundred pages where Fitzgerald needs two hundred?

Because they prove differently. Dreiser demonstrates causation by documenting every condition that produced Clyde, so length is his evidence. Fitzgerald demonstrates by omission and image, leaving the reader to construct the chain. One method makes the system undeniable; the other makes the longing unforgettable.

The third difference is where the fatal moment sits on the ladder. Clyde dies on the way up, before arrival, still trying to convert a secret relationship and a wealthy flirtation into a permanent position. Gatsby dies after arrival, in possession of everything the climb was supposed to deliver. This is the single most productive difference in the comparison, because it means the two novels test different halves of the same proposition. Dreiser asks whether the climb is survivable. Fitzgerald asks whether the summit is worth reaching. Dreiser answers no; Fitzgerald answers no for an entirely different reason.

The fourth difference is the presence of an interpreting narrator. Fitzgerald installs Nick Carraway, a participant with his own class position, his own snobberies, and his own investment in the story he tells. Nick's early claim that he is "inclined to reserve" judgment is contradicted by the whole book, and the contradiction is deliberate. Dreiser has no equivalent. His narration is impersonal and encyclopedic, and no character stands between the reader and the events. The consequence is that Gatsby is a novel about how a story gets told and An American Tragedy is not. Any comparative essay that ignores this will miss the most obvious formal contrast on the table.

A fifth difference is worth a sentence. Fitzgerald is beautiful about the world he condemns, which creates a seduction Dreiser never risks. Readers routinely finish Gatsby remembering the shirts and the parties and forgetting the ash valley. Nobody finishes An American Tragedy charmed by Lycurgus. That asymmetry has consequences for how each book has been received and taught.

## What Each Novel Illuminates in the Other

Dreiser illuminates Fitzgerald's economics. Read alone, Gatsby can look like a novel about desire in which class supplies the furniture. Dreiser makes the furniture load-bearing. His long account of exactly how a poor young man in America is blocked from wealth, the wages that do not accumulate, the introductions that do not come, the family connection that is technically real and socially useless, retroactively explains why Gatsby's only available ladder ran through Meyer Wolfshiem. Fitzgerald does not show the blocked legitimate routes; he shows the illegitimate one already taken. Dreiser shows the blockage, and the two together produce a complete account.

Dreiser also illuminates what Fitzgerald leaves out about the woman who is not the prize. Roberta Alden gets hundreds of pages of interiority: her family, her wages, her hopes, her fear when she becomes pregnant, her letters. Myrtle Wilson gets a handful of scenes and dies in the road. This is not a flaw in Fitzgerald so much as a demonstration of his method, which keeps the reader inside a class that does not see such women. But reading Dreiser first makes Fitzgerald's omission audible. Myrtle's absence from the novel's moral center is itself the point, and it is much easier to notice with Roberta standing nearby.

Running the other way, Fitzgerald illuminates the interior of ambition in a way naturalism structurally cannot. Dreiser can show a man wanting; he cannot show wanting as a spiritual condition. Fitzgerald can, and Chapter 6 is where he does it. The passage about the young Gatz on the shore, whose "heart was in a constant, turbulent riot," and whose reveries offered "a promise that the rock of the world was founded securely on a fairy's wing," gives the reader the metaphysics of the striver rather than the sociology. It supplies what Dreiser's method has no instrument for, and a comparative essay that says so has made a real critical claim.

Fitzgerald also illuminates the cost of arrival, which Dreiser cannot test because Clyde never arrives. The reunion in Chapter 5 is the crucial data point: Gatsby gets exactly what he wanted, and Nick observes that "his count of enchanted objects had diminished by one." Later Nick concludes that "there must have been moments even that afternoon when Daisy tumbled short of his dreams," not because of any failure in her but "because of the colossal vitality of his illusion." That is the finding An American Tragedy is structurally unable to produce, and it completes the pairing's argument. The climb is lethal, says Dreiser. The summit is empty, says Fitzgerald. Together they close the exits.

Finally, Fitzgerald illuminates how memory works on ambition. Clyde wants a future. Gatsby wants a past restored, which is a stranger and more interesting proposition, and Fitzgerald builds the whole novel around it, from the reconstruction of Louisville in Chapter 6 to the closing insistence that effort carries a man backward. Dreiser's forward-only structure makes the peculiarity of Fitzgerald's temporal design newly visible.

## Naturalism and Romance: Two Theories of Why a Man Falls

The philosophical distance between the two books is the most interesting thing in the pairing, and it is where a strong essay can distinguish itself.

Literary naturalism, the tradition Dreiser worked in, treats human beings as organisms shaped by forces they did not choose: inheritance, environment, economic pressure, appetite, and accident. The naturalist novel is built to show those forces operating, which is why it favors length, documentation, and a narrator who explains. Under this theory, Clyde Griffiths does not fall because he is wicked. He falls because a boy raised in public religious poverty, exposed early to a hotel lobby full of money, given no education that could convert desire into a career, and placed by chance in proximity to a rich uncle, will predictably do what Clyde does. Dreiser's title says so. The tragedy is American, meaning national and systemic, not personal.

Fitzgerald is working from a different premise entirely, and the difference is not merely temperamental. Gatsby's defining quality is presented as excess rather than deficiency, a capacity that circumstance cannot fully explain. Nick says he possessed "an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person and which it is not likely I shall ever find again." That sentence is unavailable to a naturalist, because it locates the cause of the fall inside a faculty rather than inside a condition. Fitzgerald reinforces the point by giving Gatsby a religious vocabulary. He describes the young man as having "committed himself to the following of a grail," a phrase that recasts social climbing as quest.

The productive question is whether the two theories actually disagree about the outcome. They do not. Both novels kill the striver, and both leave the elite untouched. What they disagree about is the location of the tragedy. For Dreiser it sits in the conditions; remove the conditions and Clyde survives. For Fitzgerald it sits in the structure of desire itself; give Gatsby better conditions and he still wants a past that cannot be repeated. That is why Fitzgerald's ending has to widen out to the Dutch sailors seeing "a fresh, green breast of the new world" and to the observation that effort carries a man backward, while Dreiser's ending stays inside a prison.

A comparative essay can make this concrete by testing a counterfactual. Suppose Clyde had inherited his uncle's business. The naturalist logic says he would have been fine, because the destructive pressure was material scarcity plus proximity to unattainable wealth. Now suppose Gatsby had been born rich. Fitzgerald's logic says he would still have lost Daisy, because the object of his desire was a moment in 1917 and no amount of money buys back a moment. Running that pair of counterfactuals is a fast way to show a marker that the essay understands the philosophical distance rather than merely noting it.

There is one caution. Fitzgerald's romanticism should not be mistaken for endorsement. The novel is clear-eyed about what Gatsby's dream costs and about the criminality that funded it, and its most famous verdict on the object of that dream is that Gatsby served "a vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty." Fitzgerald admires the capacity and condemns the target, which is a more complicated position than either simple admiration or simple critique, and stating it precisely is worth marks.

## Women, Value, and the Ladder

Both novels place a woman at the top of the ladder and a woman at the bottom, and comparing the four positions produces the sharpest gender-and-class reading available from this pairing.

At the top, Daisy Buchanan and Sondra Finchley perform the same structural function. Each is the visible property of an established class, each is desired by many, and each confirms status by association. Fitzgerald compresses the whole analysis into Gatsby's line that Daisy's "voice is full of money," and then has Nick complete it rather than dispute it. Dreiser expands the same analysis across hundreds of pages of Lycurgus social maneuvering. Neither woman is a villain in her novel, and neither is fully a person to the man pursuing her, which is precisely the point both authors are making about how class converts human beings into credentials.

At the bottom, Myrtle Wilson and Roberta Alden reveal the difference in method most starkly. Roberta has a family, a wage, a boarding house, ambitions, fears, and letters written in her own voice. When she dies, the reader has spent enough time with her that the death registers as the loss of a person. Myrtle gets a party in Chapter 2, a scene of violence when Tom breaks her nose, and a death in the road described partly through what bystanders saw. Fitzgerald has been criticized for this and defended for it, and both positions have merit. The defense is that the novel is narrated from inside a class that genuinely does not see Myrtle, so her thinness is mimetic. The criticism is that mimesis is not immunity, and that the book's own moral accounting is limited by the same blindness it depicts.

What the pairing establishes is that the two novels agree about the economics. Both men treat the poorer woman as an obstacle once the richer one becomes available. Clyde's response to Roberta's pregnancy is to look for a way out that does not cost him Sondra. Gatsby's relationship to Myrtle is only structural rather than personal, but the pattern holds at the level of the novel's design, since Myrtle's death becomes the mechanism that removes the inconvenient and the rich drive away. The women at the bottom absorb the cost of the men's ambition in both books.

A further point rewards attention. Fitzgerald gives Daisy one line that undercuts any reading of her as merely shallow. Recalling her daughter's birth in Chapter 1, she says she hoped the girl would be "a beautiful little fool," which is a woman describing the survival strategy her class has assigned her. Dreiser has no equivalent moment for Sondra, whose interiority he is uninterested in. That asymmetry is worth naming, because it complicates the assumption that the compressed novel is necessarily the shallower one about women.

## Crime, Law, and Who the System Protects

The legal machinery in the two novels supplies a clean comparative axis that essays rarely use and markers reward.

Dreiser devotes an enormous stretch of his book to the trial. Prosecution, defense, jury, county politics, the manufacture of a narrative from ambiguous evidence: all of it is documented, and the cumulative effect is to show a legal process arriving at a verdict for reasons that have as much to do with class and publicity as with fact. Clyde may or may not have killed Roberta in the way the law says he did. Dreiser's refusal to resolve it is the whole point, because the machinery resolves it anyway.

Fitzgerald has no trial and needs none. His legal machinery operates entirely by absence. Nobody is charged with Myrtle's death. Tom Buchanan directs a grieving man toward Gatsby and faces no consequence for it. Gatsby's illegal fortune is gestured at through Wolfshiem and through a telephone call after the shooting, and no authority ever arrives. The novel's law is what does not happen, and the reader is left to notice the gap. Nick's closing verdict on the Buchanans, that they "smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money or their vast carelessness," is the nearest thing to a sentence handed down in the book, and it comes from a private citizen with no power to enforce it.

Placed together, the two novels make one claim about American law in the period: it is highly active against the poor climber and entirely inactive against the established rich. Dreiser proves it by showing the machinery in operation. Fitzgerald proves it by showing the machinery declining to start. Same finding, opposite demonstrations, which is the pattern this whole pairing keeps producing.

There is a documentary footnote worth knowing, since it strengthens the naturalist reading. Dreiser built his plot on a real 1906 case in upstate New York, in which a young factory worker drowned on a lake outing with a man who had been seeing a wealthier woman. The novel is therefore not a hypothesis about the machinery; it is a reconstruction of a case the machinery had already processed. Fitzgerald had no equivalent source and needed none, since his subject was the pattern rather than the instance.

## Ambition as an Interior Condition

The pairing's most useful single finding concerns what ambition feels like from inside, because that is where the two methods diverge furthest and where a comparative essay can say something a plot-based reading cannot.

Dreiser renders wanting as appetite. Clyde sees a hotel lobby and wants what is in it; he sees Sondra and wants what she confirms; he sees a way out of Roberta's pregnancy and wants it badly enough to take her onto a lake. The wanting is continuous, urgent, and entirely legible in material terms. Nothing in it exceeds its object. If Clyde got Sondra and the Griffiths money, the wanting would stop, and Dreiser's whole causal argument depends on that being true, since a desire that could be satisfied is a desire the economy can be blamed for withholding.

Fitzgerald renders wanting as a condition that has outgrown every available object. This is the reason Chapter 6 exists, and it is the passage that does the most work in any serious reading. The young man on the Lake Superior shore is described with his heart "in a constant, turbulent riot," visited by reveries that supplied "a promise that the rock of the world was founded securely on a fairy's wing." That is not appetite. It is a metaphysical claim about reality, held by a seventeen-year-old, and Fitzgerald is precise about its consequences: having invented himself, Gatsby "was faithful to the end" to that conception.

The decisive passage comes at the reunion. Fitzgerald refuses the satisfaction the plot has spent four chapters promising, and Nick states the reason directly: "there must have been moments even that afternoon when Daisy tumbled short of his dreams," a shortfall attributed not to her but to "the colossal vitality of his illusion." Dreiser has no instrument for that sentence. He can show a man denied and a man punished. He cannot show a man granted and disappointed by the granting, because his theory does not permit a desire that exceeds every object.

The essay-ready formulation is that the two novels disagree about whether ambition is a shortage or a surplus. For Dreiser it is a shortage: the man lacks money, position, and options, and the lack drives him. For Fitzgerald it is a surplus: the man has a capacity for belief that no arrangement of the world could absorb, and the surplus drives him. Both men die. But the two diagnoses point at different cures, and neither cure is available, which is why both books end without repair.

There is a corollary about the past. Clyde wants a future he has not had. Gatsby wants a past restored, and Fitzgerald builds the entire novel around the strangeness of that wish, from the twice-told reconstruction of Louisville to the closing insistence that the current runs backward. The most compressed statement of the peculiarity is Nick's observation that Gatsby "had come a long way to this blue lawn, and his dream must have seemed so close that he could hardly fail to grasp it," followed immediately by the news that it was already behind him. Reading Dreiser's strictly forward-driving structure alongside makes Fitzgerald's temporal design newly visible as a design rather than a mood.

One more distinction sharpens the point. Dreiser's Clyde is frightened almost continuously, and fear is the emotion that produces most of his decisions. Gatsby is not frightened at any point in the novel, not at the Plaza and not in the pool, and Fitzgerald grants him a composure that survives every reversal. A man driven by fear and a man driven by faith arrive at the same destination, which is the strongest thing the pairing establishes about the ladder itself: the quality of the climber does not alter the outcome.

## Using the Pairing in a Wider American Dream Argument

A comparative essay rarely stops at two books, and this pairing slots into a larger argument efficiently if the placement is handled with care.

The 1925 pair supplies the ascent and the summit. Dreiser covers the ascent in full documentary detail and proves it is engineered to fail. Fitzgerald covers the summit and proves it is empty on arrival. Between them the ladder is fully described, and any third text added to an essay should be chosen to supply something neither covers rather than to repeat one of them.

The most common third addition is Arthur Miller's Death of a Salesman, which adds the dimension of duration. Willy Loman neither climbs successfully nor dies young; he persists inside a belief for decades while the evidence accumulates against it. That is a temporal dimension the 1925 pair lacks, since both protagonists are killed before middle age. A second useful addition is Steinbeck, who supplies the case where the promise is withheld entirely rather than granted or half-granted, which closes the class range downward.

What a student should avoid is stacking three books that make the same move. Adding a novel that also follows a poor young man destroyed by ambition produces repetition dressed as breadth, and markers see through it immediately. The test for a third text is simple: name the thing it proves that neither Fitzgerald nor Dreiser can prove. If the answer is unclear, leave it out and go deeper on the two.

Placed in that wider frame, the 1925 pairing has a distinctive claim to make. Most books in the tradition examine one position on the ladder and therefore leave an escape open. Dreiser and Fitzgerald together close two escapes at once and do it inside a single publication year, from opposite ends of the literary spectrum, without either author knowing what the other was producing. That independence is the feature no other pairing in the tradition offers, and it is the reason this comparison is worth building an argument on rather than simply noting.

## Two Titles, Two Arguments

The titles are worth a paragraph because each states its book's thesis and the two theses do not match.

Dreiser's title makes a categorical claim. The tragedy is American, which asserts that the events are national in character rather than particular to one weak young man, and the indefinite article insists it is one instance of a recurring type. A reader who finishes the novel has been shown the type in operation across eight hundred pages, so the title functions as a verdict delivered in advance and then proved.

Fitzgerald's title works by irony that never quite resolves. The adjective is the word a promoter would use, and it attaches to a man who threw parties for strangers and staged himself for an audience of one. Yet Nick, who knows exactly how the fortune was made and how the persona was assembled, does not withdraw the word. He tells Gatsby directly in Chapter 8, "They're a rotten crowd," and adds, "You're worth the whole damn bunch put together." The title therefore holds admiration and advertisement in the same phrase without deciding between them, which is the novel's characteristic move.

Set side by side, the titles state the deciding factor in miniature. Dreiser names a system and files a case against it. Fitzgerald names a man and leaves the reader to work out whether the word is earned, borrowed, or sold. One book wants a verdict on America, the other wants a judgment about a person that turns out to be impossible to make cleanly. An essay that opens or closes on this contrast has a frame that carries the whole comparison, and it costs three sentences.

## Beginnings and Endings Read Side by Side

Openings and closings carry a novel's thesis in concentrated form, and comparing them here is efficient.

Fitzgerald opens with a father's advice about judgment and privilege, which Nick reports and then partially disowns. The remembered instruction is to "just remember that all the people in this world haven't had the advantages that you've had," and it establishes the novel's class subject in its second paragraph, before any character has appeared. Nick's response, that he became "inclined to reserve" judgment, sets up the unreliability the rest of the book exploits. Dreiser opens on a street corner in a city at dusk, with a family of missionaries singing hymns to indifferent passersby, one of whom is a boy who does not want to be there. No advice, no framing consciousness, and no privilege to check. The two openings announce their methods precisely: one gives a filter, the other gives a condition.

The closings diverge just as sharply. An American Tragedy ends after the execution, and its final movement returns to the missionary family, now with a grandson, the pattern beginning again. The circularity is the argument: the conditions have not changed, so the outcome will not change. Fitzgerald ends by leaving his characters altogether. Nick sprawls on a beach, imagines the island before the houses, and delivers a closing image in which effort itself carries a man backward: "So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past." Dreiser's ending is sociological and forward-looking in the bleakest sense, predicting recurrence. Fitzgerald's is metaphysical and backward-looking, denying that forward motion was ever available.

Both endings refuse repair, and neither offers a character who has learned enough to act differently. But the refusals differ in kind, and the difference tracks the deciding factor exactly. A book about the climb ends by showing the next climber starting out. A book about arrival ends by showing that the destination was never in the direction everyone was rowing.

## Reception and Afterlife: Two Very Different Careers

The two novels had opposite trajectories, and knowing them prevents a common anachronism.

An American Tragedy was a major commercial and critical event on publication. It confirmed Dreiser's standing as the most significant American realist of his generation, sold strongly for a book of its size, and generated argument about crime, class, and the death penalty. It was filmed twice, first in 1931 and again in 1951 as A Place in the Sun, which won multiple Academy Awards and introduced the story to an audience that never read the novel. Dreiser's reputation was secure in his lifetime.

The Great Gatsby had no such reception. It sold modestly in 1925, disappointed its author commercially, and was not the book Fitzgerald was primarily known for at his death in December 1940. Its ascent came afterward, driven by wartime distribution and postwar critical reassessment, and by the middle of the century it had begun the climb that made it the most taught American novel of its era. The book that now looks like the definitive statement on the 1920s was, in the 1920s, a commercial disappointment its author suspected had failed.

The reversal since then is nearly complete. Fitzgerald's novel is everywhere and Dreiser's is comparatively rarely assigned, largely because of length, prose, and the demands of a term. This matters for an essay in two ways. First, it prevents the assumption that the two books arrived with equal force in 1925, which they did not. Second, it complicates any claim that literary quality determines survival, since the book that survived was the one that failed commercially and the one that succeeded is now specialist reading.

There is a further point worth making about adaptation. A Place in the Sun kept Dreiser's plot and discarded much of his method, replacing documented causation with romantic close-ups, which is effectively a Fitzgeraldian treatment of Dreiserian material. The film's popularity relative to the novel suggests something about which of the two 1925 modes the wider culture has preferred, and it is a legitimate observation for a conclusion, provided it is offered as an observation rather than as a proven claim.

## Common Misreadings the Pairing Corrects

Three misreadings of Fitzgerald's novel are stubborn, and Dreiser dissolves each one.

The first treats Gatsby as a love story with a sad ending. On this account the tragedy is emotional and Daisy is simply unworthy. Dreiser makes that reading impossible to sustain by analogy, because nobody reads An American Tragedy as a romance, and the structural role Sondra plays is unmistakably that of a class credential. Once the role is named in Dreiser, it becomes visible in Fitzgerald, and the textual support is plentiful: the wealth that "imprisons and preserves," the porch bright with "the bought luxury of star-shine," the voice full of money. The romance is the delivery mechanism, not the subject.

The second treats Gatsby's ruin as a personal failing, whether dishonesty, criminality, or refusal to accept reality. That reading converts a structural problem into a character defect and quietly exonerates the system. Dreiser blocks the conversion, because Clyde is deliberately ordinary and his outcome is presented as predictable given the conditions. Applying the same test to Fitzgerald raises the right question: were legitimate routes upward available to a poor young officer from North Dakota in 1919? The novel's answer, delivered through Wolfshiem and through the total absence of any lawful path in the text, is no.

The third treats the two decades of Fitzgerald's and Dreiser's material as separate literary worlds, one modernist and lyrical, the other Victorian and plodding. The chronology refutes it. Both books appeared in 1925, in the same publishing season, addressed to the same readers. Treating Dreiser as a survival from an earlier era and Fitzgerald as the voice of the new one is a periodizing convenience that obscures the simultaneity on which this entire comparison depends. They were contemporaries writing about a contemporary problem, and their disagreement was about method, not about era.

A fourth error runs in the opposite direction and deserves a brief warning. Having noticed the class content, some readers conclude that Fitzgerald was a social novelist in Dreiser's sense. He was not. Fitzgerald names no policy, proposes no program, and shows no organized response to the conditions he depicts. His politics are diagnostic. Saying so accurately is worth more than overstating the case, and a marker will notice the difference.

## The Counter-Reading: Is the Shared Year Just Coincidence?

The objection is straightforward. Two novels published in the same calendar year is a fact about printing schedules, not about literature. American fiction in 1925 also produced work with nothing in common with either book. Dreiser had been writing about the same subject since Sister Carrie in 1900, long before Fitzgerald began Gatsby, and the resemblance between the plots is the resemblance any two rise-and-fall stories share. Treating the date as significant risks the numerological fallacy, in which a coincidence is dressed up as a pattern.

Half of that is fair, and an essay should concede it plainly. The date by itself proves nothing, and an argument that leans on 1925 as though the year were causal will collapse. Dreiser's preoccupations did predate Gatsby by a quarter century, and no evidence of influence in either direction is needed for this comparison to work. Claiming influence without evidence would be a fabrication, and it is not necessary.

The pairing survives for three reasons that have nothing to do with numerology.

First, simultaneity is evidence of a shared pressure. When two writers with incompatible aesthetics, incompatible politics, and incompatible theories of human character independently produce long treatments of the same figure within eight months, the most economical explanation is that the figure was pressing on the culture. The mid-1920s in America combined visible new wealth, a widened gap between inherited and acquired money, mass advertising that taught people to want specific things, and a popular myth of self-making that had never been more loudly promoted. Two novelists at opposite ends of the literary spectrum both reached for the poor young man destroyed by that myth. The year is not the argument; the convergence is.

Second, the resemblance is not generic. Rise-and-fall stories are common; this particular configuration is not. Both novels feature a protagonist from a religiously inflected poverty, a formative first encounter with luxury, a fiction about the self maintained in front of a wealthy circle, a woman who functions as a class credential, a second woman who becomes an obstacle, a death on the water, a resolution of guilt that does not match the facts, and an elite that faces nothing. That is eight shared elements, several of them unusual. A comparison built on eight specific correspondences is not built on a coincidence of dates.

Third, the divergence is what makes the convergence meaningful. If the two books were similar in method, their agreement would tell us only that two writers of one school agreed. Because they are opposite in method, their agreement tells us something about the subject rather than about the school. Naturalism and romantic lyricism have no common assumptions, and they arrived at the same verdict on the American climb. That is the strongest available evidence that the verdict is about the country rather than about a literary fashion.

An essay should state the concession and the response in that order. Concede that a shared publication year is not an argument. Then show that the year is a signal pointing toward a shared cultural pressure, that the correspondences are specific rather than generic, and that the methodological opposition strengthens rather than weakens the finding. Handled this way, the counter-reading becomes one of the strongest paragraphs in the piece.

## The Verdict and the Deciding Factor

The verdict: both novels convict American class of manufacturing an ambition it then punishes, and the deciding factor between them is where each places the fatal moment on the ladder.

Everything else follows from that placement. Dreiser kills Clyde during the ascent, which forces the novel to document the ascent in full and makes conditions the subject. Fitzgerald kills Gatsby after the ascent, which frees him to skip the documentation entirely and makes desire the subject. Dreiser therefore needs eight hundred pages and Fitzgerald needs two hundred, not because one writer is more disciplined but because a book about conditions requires evidence and a book about longing requires images.

The placement also determines what each book can prove. Dreiser can prove that the climb is designed to fail, because he shows every rung. He cannot prove anything about arrival, because his protagonist never arrives. Fitzgerald can prove that arrival is empty, because his protagonist gets everything, and Chapter 5 delivers the proof in a single clause about diminished enchantment. He cannot prove that the climb is blocked, because he skips it. Neither novel is complete on its own, and each supplies exactly what the other omits.

Naming the deciding factor also settles the question students most often ask, which is which novel is better on the shared subject. The question is malformed. Dreiser is unmatched on the machinery of American class and on how a legal system converts ambiguity into a verdict. Fitzgerald is unmatched on the interior architecture of longing and on what possession does to desire. An essay that ranks them has misread the assignment; an essay that names the deciding factor and shows what each book can therefore see has understood it.

One further consequence deserves mention. Because Gatsby arrives, his novel implicates readers who have money in a way Dreiser's does not. An American Tragedy can be read as a report on someone else's disaster. Gatsby cannot be held at that distance by anyone who has ever wanted something badly and then obtained it. The claim that the wanting was the substance and acquisition is a subtraction is uncomfortable precisely because it does not depend on being poor. That difference in reader implication is part of why the shorter novel has outlasted the longer one on syllabi, and it is a defensible point to make in a conclusion.

## How to Write the Comparative Essay From This Pairing

Organize by axis, never by book. The weakest structure summarizes Fitzgerald, then summarizes Dreiser, then observes that both criticize ambition. Markers cap that shape on sight. Take instead one row of the 1925 ladder table per body paragraph and handle both novels inside it, arriving at a conclusion neither text supports alone.

Build the thesis on the ladder position. A workable formulation: Fitzgerald and Dreiser published two verdicts on American ambition within eight months of each other, and because Dreiser kills his striver during the climb while Fitzgerald kills his after arrival, the two novels together demonstrate that the promise is unsurvivable whether or not it is fulfilled. That commits the essay to an argument and gives every paragraph a job.

Handle the publication year carefully. State it once as a fact, use it as a signal of shared cultural pressure, and never claim influence between the two writers. There is no need for an influence claim, and an unsupported one invites correction. The same caution applies to critical attribution: name a school such as naturalism, which is a real and documented category, rather than inventing a critic to agree with the essay.

Characterize Dreiser with specifics. Vague references to Clyde's misfortunes read as unfamiliarity. Name the collar factory, the uncle, Roberta Alden, Sondra Finchley, Big Bittern, the trial, the electric chair, and the 1906 case that supplied the plot. Three or four precise particulars establish credibility faster than a paragraph of general description.

Quote Fitzgerald densely, since Gatsby is almost always the set text. The lines that do comparative work here are the Chapter 6 self-invention material, the Chapter 6 Louisville passage on wealth that "imprisons and preserves," the Chapter 7 line about Daisy's voice, the Chapter 5 line about diminished enchantment, and the Chapter 9 verdict on the Buchanans. Famous lines that pull away from class, such as the green light or the closing image of rowing, should be used only if the paragraph genuinely needs them, since Dreiser offers no counterpart and the essay will drift.

Devote one paragraph to method rather than theme. The contrast between proof by documentation and proof by omission is the most sophisticated available point, and very few essays make it. Say what each method can see and what it is blind to, and support the claim with the length of each book treated as a formal choice rather than an accident.

Pre-empt the coincidence objection explicitly. Concede that the shared year proves nothing on its own, then supply the eight specific correspondences and the argument from methodological opposition. Essays that dismantle their own strongest objection score higher than essays that never raise one.

## Closing Verdict

Two American novels of 1925, written by men who agreed on almost nothing about how fiction should work, followed the same young man to the same end. One documented every rung and proved the ladder was built to break. The other skipped the ladder entirely, showed the view from the top, and proved the view was not what had been advertised. Neither writer needed the other, and neither knew what the other had produced when he began. The convergence is the finding.

For a reader of Gatsby, the pairing does one decisive thing. It removes the option of treating the novel as a story about a man who wanted the wrong woman. Dreiser's machinery, laid out at length, is visible in Fitzgerald's novel once you know to look: the legitimate routes that do not exist, the fortune that had to be illegal, the elite that closes, the law that never starts. Fitzgerald compressed all of it into gesture and image, and the compression is why the book is beautiful and why the class content is so easy to miss.

What Fitzgerald adds, and what Dreiser could not, is the report from the summit. Gatsby got there. He owned the house, threw the parties, and stood on the lawn reaching toward a light on a dock that he had already sailed past. Nick's account of the ruin is not that the dream was too large but that it had grown beyond any possible object, which is why Daisy could only tumble short of it. That is the sentence the pairing exists to isolate, and it belongs to the wider argument about [the corruption of the American Dream](/2024/04/29/gatsby-american-dream-corruption/) and to the tradition of American books about [the self-made man in literature](/2028/09/04/gatsby-self-made-man-literature/), where Fitzgerald and Dreiser sit as the two 1925 witnesses who never compared notes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How does The Great Gatsby compare to An American Tragedy?

Both are 1925 American novels about a poor young man who reinvents himself to reach a wealthy woman and is destroyed in the attempt. The correspondences are specific rather than generic: a religiously inflected childhood poverty, a formative first encounter with luxury, a maintained fiction about the self, a woman who functions as a class credential, a second woman who becomes an obstacle, a death on the water, a resolution of guilt that does not match the facts, and an elite that faces no consequence. The differences are equally sharp. Dreiser proves by documentation across eight hundred pages of conditions; Fitzgerald proves by omission and image across two hundred. Dreiser kills his striver during the climb, Fitzgerald after arrival. Read together they show the promise failing whether or not it is fulfilled.

### Q: What do the two 1925 novels share?

Beyond plot correspondence, they share a diagnosis. Each treats individual ambition as something the class system manufactures and then punishes, and each declines to hold the wealthy accountable for anything. Each stages romance as a class transaction while letting the protagonist experience it as love. Each turns on water and on a death whose legal resolution is dictated by social position rather than by fact: Clyde is executed for an act the novel refuses to clarify, and Gatsby is shot for an act he did not commit. Both books also refuse the consolation of institutional repair. Nobody is helped, no law is changed, and the families with money continue undisturbed. That is a shared conclusion reached by two writers with opposite theories of human character, which is what makes it worth noticing.

### Q: How do both follow a poor man's fatal climb?

Each novel gives its protagonist a starting point of embarrassing poverty, a moment of exposure to money that permanently sets his direction, and a route upward that requires deception. Gatsby leaves James Gatz behind, acquires an illegal fortune, and installs himself opposite the woman whose class he wants to enter. Clyde leaves street-corner missionary poverty, works his way into a hotel and then into a relative's factory, and lets a wealthy circle assume more about him than is true. Both climbs depend on the man being taken for something he is not. Both end with him dead and the deception exposed. What differs is timing: Clyde dies mid-ascent, still converting, while Gatsby dies in full possession of the wealth the ascent was supposed to deliver.

### Q: How do Fitzgerald's and Dreiser's styles differ?

Fitzgerald compresses and withholds. Nine chapters, one summer, a narrator who supplies impressions rather than explanations, and an inner life for Gatsby confined to two or three concentrated passages. The reader constructs the causal chain. Dreiser expands and explains. Wages, boarding house rules, factory hierarchy, social season procedure, and county trial mechanics are all documented, and the narrator supplies causation at every step. Fitzgerald's prose is lyrical to the point of seducing readers into admiring the world it criticizes; Dreiser's is flat, accumulative, and immune to that effect. Neither style is a decoration. Each follows from what the writer needs to prove, since a book about conditions requires evidence and a book about longing requires images.

### Q: Is the shared publication year mere coincidence?

The date alone proves nothing, and an essay that leans on it will not survive scrutiny. Dreiser had been writing about this material since Sister Carrie in 1900, and no influence between the two writers needs to be claimed or should be. What the simultaneity indicates is shared cultural pressure. When two novelists with incompatible aesthetics, politics, and theories of human character independently produce long treatments of the same figure within eight months, the figure was pressing on the culture. Mid-1920s America combined highly visible new wealth, a widening gap between inherited and acquired money, mass advertising that taught specific wants, and a loudly promoted myth of self-making. The convergence is the argument; the year is only the signal that points to it.

### Q: How does the class machinery destroy the climber?

It works by making the legitimate route unavailable and then punishing the illegitimate one. Dreiser documents the unavailability at length: wages that do not accumulate, introductions that do not come, a family connection technically real and socially useless. Fitzgerald skips the documentation and shows the result, a fortune that had to come through Wolfshiem because nothing else would have worked. The machinery then closes at the top, since neither Sondra's circle nor the Buchanans will absorb an outsider regardless of his money. Finally the law activates against the climber and stays dormant against the established. Clyde is tried and executed; nobody is charged with Myrtle Wilson's death, and Tom Buchanan directs a grieving man toward Gatsby without consequence.

### Q: Who was Theodore Dreiser?

Theodore Dreiser was an American novelist and journalist whose fiction, beginning with Sister Carrie in 1900, made the economics of American life its explicit subject. He is the central figure of American literary naturalism, the tradition that treats human beings as shaped by heredity, environment, appetite, and chance rather than by free moral choice. His prose has never been admired for its sentences, and his books are long, dense, and heavily documented, which is why they are read less often now than they were. An American Tragedy, published in December 1925, is generally considered his major achievement. Knowing where he sits in the tradition matters for the comparison, because his method is not clumsiness but a philosophical commitment about what causes a life to go the way it goes.

### Q: What is literary naturalism, and how does it shape Dreiser's book?

Naturalism treats character as the product of forces the person did not choose: inheritance, environment, economic pressure, appetite, and accident. A naturalist novel is built to make those forces visible, which is why it favors length, documentation, and a narrator who explains causation rather than implying it. The shaping effect on An American Tragedy is total. Clyde does not fall because he is wicked; he falls because a boy raised in public religious poverty, exposed early to hotel luxury, given no education that could convert desire into a career, and placed by chance near a rich uncle will predictably act as he does. The title says the tragedy is national rather than personal, and the eight hundred pages exist to prove the claim.

### Q: How do Clyde Griffiths and Jay Gatsby differ as strivers?

Gatsby has agency, imagination, and eventually enormous money. He builds a fortune, a persona, and a house, and he executes a five-year plan with patience. Clyde has almost no agency at any point; he drifts into opportunities, panics when they close, and never controls his circumstances. Fitzgerald frames his protagonist as possessing something extraordinary, calling it "an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness," a formulation naturalism has no category for. Dreiser frames Clyde as ordinary, which is essential to his argument, since the tragedy is national only if the man is representative. Gatsby is exceptional and dies anyway; Clyde is typical and dies accordingly. Together the two cases suggest the outcome does not depend on the quality of the climber.

### Q: Which of the two books is harsher toward American society?

Dreiser is harsher in explicit statement, Fitzgerald in implication. An American Tragedy names the indictment in its title and spends hundreds of pages documenting the case, including a trial that arrives at a verdict for reasons of class and publicity as much as fact. Nothing in it is left for the reader to infer. Fitzgerald's indictment is delivered in glimpses and then compressed into a handful of sentences, most devastatingly Nick's judgment that the Buchanans "smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money." The difference in loudness is not a difference in severity. Fitzgerald's method makes his critique easier to miss, which is why so many readers finish the novel remembering the parties.

### Q: Does Fitzgerald's lyricism soften his social critique?

It complicates it rather than softening it, and the complication is deliberate. The prose is beautiful about the world it condemns, so a reader experiences the seduction before understanding the emptiness, which is exactly Gatsby's own sequence. The risk is real: many readers finish remembering the shirts and the champagne and forget the ash valley entirely. But the novel supplies the corrective in its own vocabulary, describing the object of Gatsby's devotion as "a vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty." Fitzgerald admires the capacity for hope and condemns its target, which is a more difficult position than either straightforward admiration or straightforward critique. Dreiser takes no such risk and gains no such effect.

### Q: How does each book handle the death of its central man?

Dreiser makes the death procedural. The trial occupies an enormous stretch, and the execution arrives at the end of a documented legal process that has converted ambiguity into certainty for reasons the reader can see. The effect is cold and systematic, and it is meant to be. Fitzgerald makes the death almost casual. Gatsby is shot in his own pool, the sequence is reconstructed after the fact, and the killing of Wilson arrives in a subordinate clause noting that "the holocaust was complete." No inquiry follows. The contrast is exact: Dreiser shows the machinery grinding a man down in full view, and Fitzgerald shows the machinery declining to notice that anything happened.

### Q: What thesis works for a Dreiser and Fitzgerald comparative essay?

Build on the ladder position rather than on similarity. A workable formulation: Fitzgerald and Dreiser published two verdicts on American ambition within eight months of each other, and because Dreiser kills his striver during the climb while Fitzgerald kills his after arrival, the two novels together demonstrate that the promise is unsurvivable whether or not it is fulfilled. A second strong option builds on method rather than plot: the two books prove the same claim by opposite techniques, documentation against omission, and the fact that incompatible methods converge is stronger evidence about the subject than either book alone provides. Both formulations give every paragraph a job and avoid the empty thesis that both novels criticize ambition.

### Q: Why did Gatsby outlast Dreiser's book on reading lists?

Several factors, and an essay should present them as plausible rather than settled. Length matters in a term-limited syllabus, and two hundred pages is teachable in a way eight hundred is not. Sentence quality matters, since Fitzgerald's prose rewards close reading in a manner Dreiser's does not. Reader implication matters most. An American Tragedy can be received as a report on someone else's disaster, while Gatsby's central claim, that wanting is the substance and acquisition is a subtraction, does not depend on being poor and therefore reaches readers of any class. Fitzgerald's novel also rose late, having sold modestly in 1925 and gained its position through postwar reassessment, so its dominance is a mid-century development rather than an original verdict.

### Q: How do the two works treat the woman the climber wants?

Both treat her as a credential the man experiences as a beloved. Fitzgerald compresses the analysis into Gatsby's line that Daisy's "voice is full of money," and Nick completes the thought rather than disputing it. Chapter 6 adds that "It excited him, too, that many men had already loved Daisy," which locates value in demand rather than in character. Dreiser expands the same analysis across hundreds of pages of social maneuvering around Sondra Finchley, whose desirability is inseparable from her class visibility. Neither woman is a villain, and neither is fully a person to the man pursuing her. Fitzgerald does give Daisy one line of self-knowledge, her wish that her daughter be "a beautiful little fool," which Dreiser offers no equivalent for.

### Q: What does compression against exhaustiveness reveal about method?

It reveals that length is an argument, not a habit. Dreiser needs eight hundred pages because his claim is causal: remove any documented condition and the outcome changes, so every condition has to be shown. Fitzgerald needs two hundred because his claim is about the structure of desire, which is demonstrated by image rather than by evidence, and because omission forces the reader to build the causal chain personally. Each method has a blindness. Dreiser cannot render longing as a spiritual condition, only as appetite. Fitzgerald cannot demonstrate that legitimate routes upward are blocked, because he skips the routes entirely. Naming both the capacity and the blindness of each method is the most sophisticated move available in this comparison.

### Q: Which Gatsby passages best support the fatal-climb argument?

Five carry most of the weight. The Chapter 6 self-invention sequence, beginning "James Gatz that was really, or at least legally, his name," establishes the fiction the climb requires. The Louisville passage in the same chapter, where Gatsby is "overwhelmingly aware of the youth and mystery that wealth imprisons and preserves," establishes the formative exposure. The Chapter 7 line about Daisy's voice establishes the woman as credential. The Chapter 5 observation that "his count of enchanted objects had diminished by one" establishes what arrival costs. Nick's Chapter 9 verdict on the Buchanans establishes the elite's insulation. Those five, quoted precisely and analyzed rather than dropped in, will support a full comparative essay without drift.

### Q: How does crime function in each of the two works?

In Dreiser, crime is the subject of half the book. The act itself is deliberately ambiguous, the investigation and trial are documented at length, and the machinery converts uncertainty into a capital verdict through a process the reader watches in operation. In Fitzgerald, crime is everywhere and never prosecuted. Gatsby's fortune is illegal and gestured at rather than shown. Myrtle Wilson is killed by a driver nobody charges. Tom Buchanan sends a grieving man toward the wrong target and faces nothing. The novel's law is what fails to happen. Both books therefore reach one conclusion by opposite demonstrations: the legal system is highly active against the poor climber and effectively dormant against the established rich.

### Q: Do the two works blame the individual or the system?

Dreiser blames the system without qualification, which is the point of his title, and his protagonist is deliberately ordinary so that the outcome cannot be attributed to exceptional character. Fitzgerald divides the blame in a more difficult way. The system is clearly indicted, since the routes upward are closed and the elite absorbs no consequence. But Gatsby's ruin is also located inside the structure of his own desire, which had grown past any object it could reach, which is why Nick says Daisy tumbled short "because of the colossal vitality of his illusion." Remove the conditions and Clyde survives. Give Gatsby better conditions and he still cannot repeat a moment from 1917.

### Q: What errors do students make in a Dreiser pairing?

Four recur. Claiming influence between the two writers, for which there is no evidence and no need. Leaning on the shared publication year as though the date were itself an argument, rather than as a signal of shared cultural pressure. Describing Dreiser vaguely, referring to Clyde's misfortunes without naming the collar factory, Roberta Alden, Sondra Finchley, the lake, or the trial, which reads immediately as unfamiliarity. And ranking the two novels, usually in Fitzgerald's favor on the grounds of prose quality, when the useful move is to name what each method can see and what it cannot. A fifth, less common but costly, is treating Dreiser's length as a fault rather than as the form his argument requires.
