Most readers first meet this subject as an examination query, directive principles vs fundamental rights, and most textbooks answer it with a tidy comparison table. Part Three of the Constitution holds the Fundamental Rights, the table says; Part Four holds the Directive Principles of State Policy. The rights are justiciable, the principles are not. The rights bind, the principles guide. Every cell of that table is accurate, and the table as a whole is a trap, because it presents as settled doctrine a relationship that the founders of the Constitution deliberately left unsettled. The Directive Principles were not aspirational guidance subordinate to Fundamental Rights; they were the unresolved half of a deliberate constitutional ambiguity, and the proof is the seventy years of litigation that the ambiguity made necessary.

## Directive Principles Versus Fundamental Rights: The Comparison Table and What It Hides

The comparison-table framing has a grammar, and the grammar does quiet ideological work. It arranges two chapters of the Constitution in parallel columns, assigns each a fixed property, and implies that the properties were assigned once, at the founding, by people who knew exactly what they were doing. Justiciable here, non-justiciable there. Negative obligations on the state in Part Three, positive instructions to the state in Part Four. Enforceable rights for the citizen, unenforceable hopes for the republic. A student who masters the table can answer the examination question, and the examination question is precisely the wrong question, because the constitutional text the Assembly adopted on 26 November 1949 refuses the table's neatness at the exact point where the table is most confident. Article 37 declares the Directive Principles non-enforceable by any court, and in the same sentence declares them fundamental in the governance of the country, imposing on the state a duty to apply them in making laws. A provision cannot be both marginal and fundamental. The sentence holds the two words together and declines to say which governs when a law made under a Directive Principle collides with a right guaranteed under Part Three. That declension is the subject of this article.

The declension was not an oversight. The men and women who drafted the Constitution had before them models that resolved the question cleanly, and they considered draft language that resolved it in each direction, and the record of the Constituent Assembly Debates for the winter session of 1948 shows the resolution being considered and set aside more than once. K.T. Shah pressed the Assembly to give the principles enforceability within a stated period. B.N. Rau, the Constitutional Adviser, had drafted a clause that would have given laws implementing the principles primacy over conflicting rights. The Drafting Committee let both proposals fall. What survived was Article 37's double formula, which Granville Austin, writing in Working a Democratic Constitution in 1999, read as productive accommodation, and which Niraja Gopal Jayal, writing in Citizenship and Its Discontents in 2013, read as deferred adjudication whose bill later generations would have to pay. This article adjudicates between those readings on the primary evidence, and its verdict is a synthesis with a sharp edge: the deferral was deliberate, and it was costly, and the basic-structure doctrine that the Supreme Court announced in Kesavananda Bharati on 24 April 1973 was the institutional mechanism through which the cost was eventually paid and the ambiguity converted into doctrine.

To see why the founders chose ambiguity, a reader has to hold two fears in view at once, because the Assembly held both. The first fear was of the state: a generation that had watched the colonial government rule through preventive detention, press ordinances and sedition law wanted entitlements that a court could enforce against the new republic's own government, and Part Three supplied them. The second fear was of destitution: a country in which the great majority lived in poverty could not treat the social and economic programme as a mere hope, and members across the Assembly's spectrum insisted that the Constitution commit the state to education, livelihood, public health and a less unequal distribution of material resources. Part Four supplied that commitment. Either fear, pursued alone, would have produced a cleaner constitution. A pure rights charter would have frozen the social order of 1949 behind judicial vetoes. A pure programme charter would have left the citizen defenceless before the developmental state. The Assembly wanted both instruments at full strength, and the price of having both, without ranking them, was the ambiguity that Article 37 records. The comparison table hides the price by pretending there was none.

The working-out of the ambiguity followed a rhythm that this article traces decision by decision. The Supreme Court's first answer, in Champakam Dorairajan on 9 April 1951, was subordination: the Directive Principles must run subsidiary to the Fundamental Rights. Parliament answered within weeks with the First Amendment. Twenty years of property litigation produced Golak Nath in 1967, which withdrew Fundamental Rights from the amending power altogether, and then Kesavananda in 1973, which restored the amending power and fenced it with the basic structure. The Forty-Second Amendment of 1976 attempted to end the argument by giving the Directive Principles primacy over the two most litigated rights, and Minerva Mills on 31 July 1980 struck the attempt down and declared the balance between the two chapters to be itself part of the basic structure. Each of these holdings is treated in coaching material as a separate rule to be memorised. Read in sequence, they are one sentence being written across seven decades, and the sentence is the answer to the question the founders declined to answer in 1948.

One distinction disciplines everything that follows. The founders did make first-order textual choices, and those choices were real: Article 37's bar on enforcement is unambiguous, the placement of the two chapters is unambiguous, the wording of individual principles and rights is mostly unambiguous. What the founders deferred was the second-order doctrinal choice, the rule for resolving collisions between the chapters when a statute honestly implementing a Directive Principle trenches on an honestly held Fundamental Right. The strongest counter to the deferred-question reading points at Article 37 and says the founders plainly chose. The counter proves the first-order point and misses the second-order one, because Article 37 governs the enforceability of the principles themselves and says nothing about the hierarchy of values when the two chapters conflict inside a single statute. The Assembly's silence on that hierarchy, this article argues, was the most consequential silence in the founding text, and the Supreme Court has spent seventy years speaking into it.

## From Dublin and Delhi: The Road to Part Four

The Directive Principles arrived in Delhi with an Irish passport. Article 45 of the Constitution of Ireland, enacted in 1937, set out directive principles of social policy for the general guidance of the Oireachtas and specified that they were not to be cognisable by any court. Éamon de Valera's drafters had designed a device for a state that wanted a social programme inside a constitution without handing the programme to judges, and the device travelled. B.N. Rau, appointed Constitutional Adviser to the Constituent Assembly in July 1946, studied the Irish text during his preparatory work and carried its central mechanism into the Indian drafting process, adapting a provision written for a small, homogeneous island into an instrument for a subcontinent. The borrowing matters for interpretation. Because the Irish model supplied the form, the Indian debate was never about whether such principles belonged in a constitution at all. The debate, from the first committee rooms of 1947 onward, was about force: how much of it the principles should carry against the rights chapter that the same hands were writing in the adjacent Part.

The committee road began earlier than the famous debates. The Sapru Committee, appointed by the Non-Party Conference in 1944 and reporting in December 1945 under the chairmanship of Tej Bahadur Sapru, recommended that minority safeguards and rights be divided into justiciable and non-justiciable classes, an early Indian statement of the two-chapter architecture. Inside the Assembly, the Advisory Committee on Fundamental Rights, Minorities and Tribal and Excluded Areas, chaired by Vallabhbhai Patel, constituted a Sub-Committee on Fundamental Rights in February 1947 under J.B. Kripalani's chairmanship, with Ambedkar, K.M. Munshi, Alladi Krishnaswami Ayyar and others among its members. The Sub-Committee's report of April 1947 divided rights into two groups: those enforceable in court and those recognised as binding on policy without judicial enforcement. B.N. Rau's own draft of that spring drew the same line, placing the right to work and kindred social claims in the non-enforceable group because, as his memorandum reasoned, breaches of some obligations could form the subject of judicial pronouncement while breaches of others had to be remedied without recourse to courts. The two-chapter structure, in other words, was settled administrative practice inside the drafting process long before the Assembly argued about it in public session, which is one reason the public argument concentrated so entirely on the single question the committees had left open: what happens on collision.

The content of Part Four also carried a genealogy that the comparison table flattens. The Karachi Resolution of the Indian National Congress, adopted in March 1931, had promised fundamental rights alongside a programme of economic policy, including state ownership or control of key industries and a living wage, and the Congress tradition treated the two promises as halves of one commitment. Jawaharlal Nehru's Objectives Resolution, moved on 13 December 1946 and adopted on 22 January 1947, carried the programme commitment into the Assembly's founding declaration. When the Drafting Committee, constituted on 29 August 1947 with B.R. Ambedkar as chairman, published the Draft Constitution in February 1948, its Part Four ran from draft Article 28 to draft Article 40, and its accompanying notes presented the principles as instructions to whoever held power. Ambedkar, introducing the Draft Constitution on 4 November 1948, described the device by analogy to the Instruments of Instruction issued to Governors under the Government of India Act of 1935: directions binding in honour and in politics, addressed to legislatures and executives, whose sanction lay not in courts but in the electorate. The analogy deserves pause. Ambedkar did not describe the principles as decoration. He described them as instructions issued by a sovereign constituent authority to its successors, and he argued that a government which ignored them would answer for the neglect before the people at election. The political sanction was, in his design, a real sanction. Whether it was a sufficient one was exactly what K.T. Shah denied, and the Assembly's refusal to add Shah's judicial sanction to Ambedkar's political one is the hinge on which this whole article turns.

## December 1948: The Assembly Argues About Force

The Assembly took the Draft Constitution clause by clause from November 1948, and the sittings that considered the directive principles, recorded in Volume Seven of the Constituent Assembly Debates, show a chamber that understood precisely what it was leaving undecided. On 19 November 1948 Ambedkar defended the draft against the charge that a non-enforceable chapter was a fraud on the reader. His answer had two limbs. The first was the Instruments of Instruction analogy: the Government of India Act of 1935 had issued directions to Governors that no court enforced, yet no one called them meaningless, because the officeholder who flouted them risked dismissal and disgrace. The second limb was democratic. Whoever captures power, Ambedkar told the House, will have to answer to the electorate, and a party that sought a mandate while repudiating the principles would face the political consequence. The principles bound through politics. He added a further point that later commentary underplays: the draft did not fix economic policy in the text, because a constitution that froze one generation's economic programme would deny later generations their democratic choice, and the principles were framed broadly enough to instruct a socialist government and a liberal government alike. Breadth, in Ambedkar's defence, was the point of the form.

K.M. Munshi approached the two chapters from the other side, as a draftsman of the rights themselves. Munshi had prepared a personal draft of fundamental rights and directive principles as early as March 1947, one of the documents before the Sub-Committee on Fundamental Rights, and he sat in the Drafting Committee through the winter sittings of 1948, when the Assembly settled the final architecture of Parts Three and Four. His interventions defended the division of labour between an enforceable charter and an instructive one, and he resisted proposals that would have blurred the line in either direction.

### What did K.M. Munshi argue about the two chapters of rights in 1948?

K.M. Munshi, who had drafted a rights charter in March 1947 and sat in the Drafting Committee, defended the two-chapter design in the winter sittings of 1948. Rights fit for courts belonged in an enforceable chapter; social and economic commitments belonged in an instructive chapter binding governments politically, and mixing the two would weaken both chapters' distinct authority.

The sceptics in the chamber were neither few nor marginal, and their objections were recorded with a precision that later generations have mined. K.T. Shah, the Bombay economist who sat on the Sub-Committee on Fundamental Rights, warned that principles without enforceability would amount to pious wishes, and he compared them to a cheque on a bank, payable only when the resources of the bank permit. T.T. Krishnamachari, himself a member of the Drafting Committee, delivered the debate's most quoted insult on the floor of the House, calling the chapter a veritable dustbin of sentiments, sufficiently resilient, he said, to permit any member to ride a hobby horse into it. Nasiruddin Ahmad mocked the principles as resolutions of the kind made on the first day of a year and broken on the second. These were not enemies of the social programme; Shah wanted the programme enforced harder, and Krishnamachari wanted it written tighter. Their target was the gap between the chapter's mandatory tone and its remedial emptiness, and the gap is precisely the ambiguity this article audits. The Assembly heard the objection in full, on the record, and adopted the text anyway, which is the strongest evidence that the ambiguity was chosen rather than overlooked.

The winter debate also fixed the principles' content, and the content shows the two visions negotiating line by line. Draft Article 31, which became Article 39, directed the state to secure equitable ownership and distribution of material resources and to prevent concentration of wealth; its language, debated across the sittings of November 1948, carried the socialist reading of the freedom movement into the constitutional text. Other principles carried the liberal and Gandhian readings: village panchayats in draft Article 40, a uniform civil code in draft Article 35, prohibition, cottage industries, the protection of monuments, and, in what became Article 48, the organisation of agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines. A chapter assembled from such heterogeneous commitments could not be handed to courts as a code, because its clauses pointed in directions that only politics could reconcile in a given budget and a given session. The Assembly knew the chapter was a coalition document. The decision to leave its ranking against Part Three unstated was the coalition's price, paid knowingly.

## Article 37: The Choice That Was Made and the Choice That Was Not

Article 37 deserves to be read slowly, because both sides of the scholarly argument quote it and because its two clauses pull against each other by design. The article provides that the provisions contained in Part Four shall not be enforceable by any court, but that the principles therein laid down are nevertheless fundamental in the governance of the country, and that it shall be the duty of the state to apply these principles in making laws. The first clause is a jurisdictional bar. The second is a command addressed to the lawmaker, expressed in the language of duty, attached to principles described as fundamental. No other provision of the Constitution simultaneously withdraws a text from the courts and elevates it in the lawmaking process with this combination of words, and the combination was the Drafting Committee's formulation, carried through the Assembly's consideration of draft Article 28 in November 1948 without the hierarchy question being put to a decision. When Ambedkar defended the draft on 19 November 1948, he located the principles' bindingness in the political process; when the Assembly adopted the article, it adopted the location and left the collision rule for another day.

The distinction between first-order and second-order choice resolves the apparent paradox, and it is the analytical spine of this article. At the first order, the founders chose a great deal, and chose it clearly. They chose that the principles would not ground a writ. They chose that the rights would. They chose the contents of both lists, article by article, across three years of committee and chamber work between December 1946 and November 1949. At the second order, they did not choose the rule for the cases in which a statute enacted to apply a principle collides with a right, and those cases were not hypothetical even in 1948: any land reform law enacted under the principles' distribution mandate would meet the property right in Article 31 and the equality guarantee in Article 14 within its first litigation season, as in fact happened across Bihar, Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh in 1950 and 1951. A founder who wished to settle the second-order question had instruments available. Rau's discarded clause would have privileged the statute. A subordination clause of the kind the Supreme Court later wrote in Champakam would have privileged the right. The Assembly adopted neither. The charitable reading, Austin's, is that the founders trusted the future to work the balance in concrete cases. The severe reading, Jayal's, is that they could not agree and called the disagreement design. The evidence of the debates supports an uncomfortable compound: they could have agreed on a rule had they been willing to pay its political price, they calculated the price, and they chose the deferral with open eyes.

There is a further textual clue, hiding in the Constitution's own machinery, that the founders expected the principles to have legal effect even while denying them direct enforcement. Article 31C, when Parliament inserted it by the Twenty-Fifth Amendment in 1971, assumed that laws giving effect to specified principles might need protection from Articles 14 and 19; the assumption only makes sense because the principles already exerted gravitational force on legislation and litigation. Courts encountered the principles as interpretive aids from the earliest years, reading ambiguous statutes in their light and reading the rights themselves in the principles' company, a practice the Supreme Court blessed in the Kerala Education Bill reference of 1958. A text that was never meant to matter legally does not acquire that interpretive career. Article 37 closed one door, the writ door, and the legal system spent decades walking through the others, which is why the comparison table's single word, non-justiciable, tells the least interesting part of the story.

## K.T. Shah's Rejected Road: The Enforceability That Was Refused

The most instructive document in the founding record is one that failed. K.T. Shah carried his objection to non-justiciability beyond rhetoric into drafting, and the brief for this article directs attention to his 1948 note on the Directive Principles' enforceability, preserved in the Drafting Committee's records, which proposed making the principles partially justiciable. Shah's scheme, as Granville Austin recounts it in The Indian Constitution: Cornerstone of a Nation in 1966, carried a timetable: the principles should become enforceable rights within a specified period, so that a government could not plead the chapter's softness forever. His floor speeches of November 1948 supplied the argument's rhetoric. Principles left to the executive's convenience, he warned, would be honoured at the executive's convenience; a cheque drawn on a bank and payable when the bank's resources permit is a promise whose value the promisor alone decides. The Assembly's majority, speaking through Ambedkar, answered that the sanction of elections was sanction enough and that a constitution must not tie the people's hands on the social organisation they might choose. Shah's amendments fell, and the note survives as the road not taken.

The rejection deserves more scrutiny than it usually receives, because it clarifies what the majority thought it was buying. Rejecting Shah was not rejecting enforceability as a value; the same Assembly wrote enforceable rights with great care in Part Three and armed them with Article 32, which Ambedkar called the heart and soul of the Constitution on 9 December 1948. Rejecting Shah was rejecting a schedule. A timetable for justiciability would have committed the republic of 1955 or 1960 to judicially enforceable claims on education, work and livelihood regardless of the exchequer those years inherited, and it would have transferred the sequencing of the social programme from legislatures to courtrooms before the republic's administrative machinery existed. The majority's wager was that democratic politics would sequence the programme faster and more legitimately than litigation could. Jayal's reading, examined in full below, counts the wager's losers: the generations for whom the principles remained unenforceable promises while governments changed the subject. Austin's reading counts its winners: a policy space in which the Nehruvian state could legislate redistribution without first defeating a property clause in court every session. The wager's terms are visible only because Shah forced the Assembly to state them, and the standard accounts, which treat his intervention as colour, miss the analytic service the rejected note performs.

Shah also forces a correction to a lazy assumption in the comparison-table literature, the assumption that non-justiciability was the uncontested preference of the founding generation and enforceability a later judicial invention. The record shows a contested founding at which enforceability had named advocates, drafted language and a timetable, and lost. Directive Principles scholarship that begins in 1950 with the finished text mistakes a verdict for a consensus. The verdict's reasoning, moreover, was explicitly provisional: Ambedkar's defence rested on the electorate's future vigilance, a factual premise about Indian democracy that each succeeding decade has tested. When the Supreme Court began, from the 1970s, to read directive content into enforceable rights, the Court was not betraying the founding settlement so much as revisiting Shah's question under conditions in which Ambedkar's premise had worn thin. That revisiting is the bridge between the founding sections of this article and the jurisprudence that follows.

## Two Visions in One Binding: The Developmental State and the Liberal Charter

Beneath the drafting choices sat two coherent visions of the republic, and the ambiguity was their treaty. The developmental vision held that political freedom without material transformation would be a shell: the state had to direct resources, break concentrations of wealth, universalise education and protect the livelihood of the weakest, and the Constitution should command it to do so. Its carriers in the Assembly included Nehru, whose Objectives Resolution of 13 December 1946 promised safeguards and social justice in one breath, and the socialist current that Shah represented in a more doctrinaire register. The liberal vision held that a state armed with a transformative mandate would become the citizen's chief danger unless hemmed by enforceable entitlements, and its carriers included the lawyers of the Drafting Committee and the Fundamental Rights Sub-Committee, whose April 1947 report had separated court-enforceable claims from policy commitments precisely to keep the former hard. The treaty between the visions was the two-chapter text with Article 37's double formula, and the treaty's unwritten clause was that neither vision would be declared supreme. The relationship between the visions inside one founding generation is visible in miniature in [the founding triangulation that the directive-rights ambiguity reflects](/2018/12/17/patel-nehru-ambedkar-founding-triangulation/): Patel's order-centred statecraft, Nehru's programme and Ambedkar's legalism each needed the others and none would sign a hierarchy.

Ambedkar's own position resists the simplification that later camps performed on it, and recovering its complexity matters because both the subordination reading and the supremacy reading claim his authority. His defence of non-justiciability on 19 November 1948 was a defence of political enforcement, not of subordination; the principles, he insisted, were not mere pious declarations, and he grounded their force in the responsibility of governments before the electorate. At the same time he resisted, against Shah's Article 1 amendments, writing any single economic doctrine into the Constitution's identity clauses, because the people's future choices had to remain open. The two resistances are one philosophy: the Constitution should bind the state to the social programme as instruction while leaving each generation free to choose the programme's economics. That philosophy explains [Ambedkar's substantive role in shaping the directive-rights distinction](/2018/12/06/ambedkar-drafting-committee-constitution/) better than the hagiographic picture of a single author dictating terms, and it explains why the distinction he defended was a distinction of remedy, courts for rights and politics for principles, rather than a distinction of rank. The tragedy the jurisprudence records is that a distinction of remedy decayed, in the Supreme Court's first decade, into a distinction of rank, and the decay is where the case law begins.

The framers' treaty also had an external audience, and naming it disciplines any romantic reading of the ambiguity as pure wisdom. The Constitution had to be sold to a Constituent Assembly that doubled as a legislature facing elections under universal franchise by 1952, in a country where the Congress organisation contained zamindars and socialists in uneasy coalition. A hierarchy clause in either direction would have detonated a faction: privilege the principles, and the property-holding wing would fight the text; privilege the rights, and the socialist wing would denounce the charter as a landlord's settlement. Deferral kept the coalition inside the document. This is the element of truth in Jayal's deferred-adjudication reading that Austin's gentler vocabulary of accommodation tends to soften. Accommodation among whom, at whose expense, is the question Jayal teaches a reader to ask, and the honest answer for 1948 is that the expense was assigned, silently, to the future litigant whose statute or right would lose when the collision finally came to court. It came within fifteen months of the Constitution's commencement.

## Reading Part Four Closely: What the Principles Actually Command

An audit of the ambiguity should pause over the text that carries it, because the principles are usually summarised rather than read. Part Four opens with a definition and a mission. Article 36 borrows the meaning of the state from Part Three, so that the same governments bound by the rights are addressed by the principles. Article 38, recast by the Forty-Fourth Amendment in 1978 into its fuller form, directs the state to promote the welfare of the people by securing a social order informed by justice, social, economic and political, and to minimise inequalities in income, status, facilities and opportunities. The mission statement matters doctrinally: when courts later described the principles and the rights as two wheels of one chariot, they were paraphrasing Article 38's own integration of welfare with justice. Article 39 then lists the programme's economic core: citizens' right to an adequate means of livelihood, the distribution of ownership and control of material resources to subserve the common good, the prevention of concentration of wealth, equal pay for equal work for men and women, protection of workers' health and children's development against economic necessity. Clause (b) and clause (c) of Article 39 are the two provisions the entire doctrinal war of 1971 to 1980 was fought over, and a reader who has not seen them isolated cannot feel the stakes of Article 31C, which existed to protect laws enacting exactly those two clauses.

The middle of Part Four is the social charter. Article 40 directs the organisation of village panchayats with self-governing powers, a Gandhian commitment the Assembly debated as a question of where sovereignty should sit. Article 41 speaks of the right to work, to education and to public assistance in unemployment, old age, sickness and disablement, expressly within the limits of the state's economic capacity and development, a qualification that shows the draftsmen calibrating command to capacity in the text itself. Article 42 requires just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief; Article 43, a living wage, conditions of work ensuring a decent standard of life, and cottage industries; Article 43A, added in 1976, the participation of workers in the management of industries; Article 43B, added by the Ninety-Seventh Amendment in 2011, the promotion of autonomous and professionally managed co-operative societies, a reminder that Part Four remained a living site of amendment long after the founding. Article 44 holds out the uniform civil code for citizens throughout India, the principle whose deferral has its own long political career. Article 45, in its original form, promised free and compulsory education for every child until the age of fourteen within ten years of the Constitution's commencement, the only principle carrying a deadline, and the deadline's expiry in 1960 without fulfilment is the single clearest exhibit in Jayal's cost ledger. Article 46 commands special care for the educational and economic interests of the weaker sections, and of the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes in particular, the provision Madras invoked in Champakam and the provision through which the reservation jurisprudence flows. Article 47 makes the improvement of public health and the raising of nutrition and living standards a primary duty, and carries the prohibition commitment; Article 48 directs the organisation of agriculture and animal husbandry on modern and scientific lines; Article 48A, added in 1976, the protection of the environment, forests and wildlife; Article 49, the protection of monuments and places of national importance; Article 50, the separation of the judiciary from the executive in the public services, the one principle addressed to institutional design rather than welfare; and Article 51, the promotion of international peace and security, just relations between nations, and settlement by arbitration. The catalogue is heterogeneous by design, and its heterogeneity is the textual ground of the founding choice: no court could enforce this chapter as a code without becoming the government, and no government could read it honestly and conclude it had been told nothing.

Two features of the catalogue repay emphasis. The first is its addressee discipline: the principles speak to the state in all its organs, and the Supreme Court confirmed in later decades that the addressees include every authority within Article 36's borrowed definition, from Parliament to a panchayat, which is why a pay commission, a municipal corporation and a national legislature have all been measured against Part Four in litigation. The second is the catalogue's internal hierarchy of urgency, or rather its refusal of one: the text does not rank education against livelihood, or livelihood against public health, and the refusal repeats, inside the chapter, the same deferral the chapter's relation to Part Three performs outside it. Politics was to do the ranking, session by session and budget by budget. A constitution that wanted the courts to run the social programme would have written priorities and deadlines throughout, as it did once, in Article 45. The single deadline, missed and eventually answered by the Eighty-Sixth Amendment's conversion of the promise into the enforceable Article 21A in 2002, stands as the exception that illuminates the design: where the founders wanted enforceability, they knew how to write its instruments, and across the rest of Part Four they chose instruction instead.

## Champakam Dorairajan: The Subordination Settlement of 1951

The collision arrived wearing a Madras admissions list. The State of Madras maintained a communal order, inherited and continued after Independence, that distributed seats in medical and engineering colleges among communities in fixed proportions. Champakam Dorairajan, a Brahmin applicant denied a medical seat under the order, challenged it under Articles 15(1) and 29(2), and the State defended its distribution by pointing to Article 46, the Directive Principle directing the state to promote with special care the educational and economic interests of the weaker sections of the people. A seven-judge bench of the Supreme Court, in State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan, judgment delivered on 9 April 1951 and reported at AIR 1951 SC 226, held for the applicant. Justice S.R. Das, writing for the Court, reasoned that the Directive Principles, expressly made non-enforceable by Article 37, could not override the enforceable Fundamental Rights, and that the chapter on Fundamental Rights was sacrosanct and not liable to be abridged by the chapter on Directive Principles, which had to conform to and run as subsidiary to the rights chapter. The communal order fell. The first judicial word on the second-order question the founders had left open was subordination, stated without qualification.

The sentence in Justice Das's opinion deserves the weight this article places on it, because it converted Ambedkar's distinction of remedy into a distinction of rank without appearing to do anything dramatic. Article 37 said the principles could not be enforced by a court; the Champakam bench inferred that what cannot be enforced must yield, in every conflict, to what can. The inference has a logic, and the Madras government's argument supplied its target, since the State had presented Article 46 as a trump. But the inference also exceeded the founding record. The Assembly had considered and rejected a clause giving principle-implementing laws primacy; it had never adopted a clause giving rights primacy, and the debates of November 1948 show members on all sides treating the chapters as partners in a single enterprise whose relations politics would manage. The Court, faced with a concrete injustice to a named applicant, reached for a hierarchy the text had withheld, and in doing so taught a generation of lawyers that the comparison table's ordering, rights above principles, was constitutional law. Textbooks still reproduce the Champakam formula as the baseline position. The baseline was a judicial construction of 1951, not a founding decision of 1949, and recovering that provenance is the first step in reading the next sixty years correctly.

Parliament's reply came within weeks and at the highest level of constitutional politics. Nehru's government, which had just watched agrarian reform statutes fall in the Patna High Court's judgment on the Bihar Land Reforms Act in March 1951 and feared for its entire legislative programme, moved the Constitution (First Amendment) Bill in May 1951. The Amendment, enacted on 18 June 1951, inserted Article 15(4) to permit special provision for socially and educationally backward classes, added Articles 31A and 31B with the Ninth Schedule to shield agrarian reform laws from rights challenges, and tightened the permissible restrictions on speech. Nehru defended the measure in the provisional Parliament as necessary surgery on a Constitution whose working had already revealed the friction the founders had priced in. The First Amendment is usually filed under reservation or land reform. In the directive-rights story it is the moment the political branches refused the subordination settlement's full implications: the Court had ranked the chapters, and Parliament answered by carving protected spaces in which the principles' programme could proceed despite the ranking. The ambiguity the founders left had survived its first judicial resolution by migrating into amendment politics, where it would live for the next three decades.

## Zamindari, Compensation and the First Working of the Ambiguity

While the admissions case from Madras supplied the doctrine's first sentence, the land cases supplied its pressure. The abolition of zamindari was the principles' programme in its most concrete form: intermediaries between the state and the cultivator were to be extinguished, and the compensation terms would decide whether abolition was redistribution or purchase. Bihar enacted its Land Reforms Act in 1950; the Patna High Court struck it down in March 1951, in the Kameshwar Singh litigation, as a violation of the equality guarantee in Article 14, even while the Allahabad and Nagpur High Courts upheld the corresponding Uttar Pradesh and Madhya Pradesh statutes. The Supreme Court's eventual judgment in State of Bihar v Kameshwar Singh, delivered on 2 May 1952, sustained the Bihar Act in substance, but by then the First Amendment had already rebuilt the ground with Articles 31A and 31B and the Ninth Schedule, and the compensation battles migrated to Article 31 itself. There the Court's early reading, in State of West Bengal v Bela Banerjee on 11 January 1954, held that compensation for acquisition meant a just equivalent of what the owner had been deprived of, a market-value doctrine that made large-scale reform fiscally impossible; Parliament answered with the Fourth Amendment of 1955, which barred courts from questioning the adequacy of compensation. The pattern of the decade, call and response between Court and Parliament, is unintelligible inside the comparison table, where one chapter simply outranks the other. It is fully intelligible inside the deferred-question frame: the founders had left the price of the social programme undecided as between the exchequer and the expropriated, and the two institutions the Constitution empowered were bidding the price against each other, judgment by amendment by judgment.

The land decade also produced the ambiguity's most consequential administrative artefact, the Ninth Schedule. Conceived in the First Amendment as a list of thirteen statutes placed beyond rights challenge, the Schedule grew across the decades into a vault of several hundred enactments, many with no connection to agrarian reform, and its growth is the clearest material trace of Jayal's cost thesis in the directive-rights field. A founding settlement that had ranked the chapters would not have needed the vault. A political system that possessed the vault used it in place of doctrine, parking contested statutes beyond review rather than arguing their merits under a stated rule of balance, until the Court in Waman Rao in 1981 and definitively in I.R. Coelho in 2007 reclaimed the ground. The Schedule's career therefore cuts both ways in the scholarly adjudication this article conducts. For Jayal's reading, it is deferral's invoice, decades of constitutional evasion compiled in a list. For Austin's, it is the accommodation's machinery, the device by which the programme proceeded while doctrine matured. The synthesis holds both, because the device was genuinely both: it moved land to the landless in the 1950s on a scale litigation would have stalled for a generation, and it habituated governments to immunity in a way the republic spent two further generations unlearning.

## The Property Decade: From Shankari Prasad to Golak Nath

The arena in which the deferred question kept returning was property, and the sequence of decisions between 1951 and 1967 reads as an escalation ladder. In Shankari Prasad v Union of India, decided on 5 October 1951, a five-judge bench upheld the First Amendment and held that Parliament's amending power under Article 368 extended to the Fundamental Rights, reasoning that a constitutional amendment was not an ordinary law within Article 13. In Sajjan Singh v State of Rajasthan, decided on 30 October 1964, the Court maintained that position by a majority whose separate opinions carried visible unease; Justice Hidayatullah questioned whether the Fundamental Rights were intended to be a playground for a majority, and Justice Mudholkar wondered aloud whether a constitution could have basic features beyond amendment. The unease matured on 27 February 1967, when an eleven-judge bench in I.C. Golak Nath v State of Punjab held by six to five, through Chief Justice K. Subba Rao, that Parliament could not amend the Fundamental Rights at all, that the rights occupied a transcendental position, and that the holding would operate prospectively so that the First, Fourth and Seventeenth Amendments stood. A challenge that began with a Punjab family's land ceiling had produced the strongest judicial statement of the rights' supremacy in the Constitution's history.

Golak Nath's place in the directive-rights audit is double. On its face the case was about the amending power, not about Part Four. In substance it froze the directive programme at its most vulnerable point, because the reforms the principles demanded, land ceilings, acquisition, redistribution under Article 39(b) and (c), were precisely the reforms that kept meeting Articles 14, 19 and 31 in court. A Constitution in which the rights chapter could not be amended was a Constitution in which the Champakam hierarchy could never be revisited by the political branches; the subordination of 1951 would harden into permanence, and the Directive Principles would govern at the sufferance of the property clause. The Golak Nath majority did not present its holding in those terms, and the dissenters, who would have left the amending power intact, did not either. The directive-rights frame, the frame the comparison table suppresses, is the one in which the case's stakes come into focus: the question was never only whether rights could be amended, but whether the social programme of Part Four would remain a living instruction or become a preface. Parliament's answer came in two statutes of constitutional rank. The Twenty-Fourth Amendment of 1971 restored the amending power over every part of the Constitution and obliged the President to assent to amendment bills. The Twenty-Fifth Amendment of 1971 inserted Article 31C, providing that a law giving effect to the principles in Article 39(b) and (c), the distribution principles at the programme's core, could not be struck down for violating Articles 14, 19 or 31, and adding a declaration clause that attempted to oust judicial review of the connection between such a law and those principles. The stage was set for the largest bench in the Court's history.

## Kesavananda Bharati: The Balance Is Invented in 1973

His Holiness Kesavananda Bharati, the pontiff of the Edneer Mutt in Kasaragod, Kerala, challenged the Kerala land reform legislation and, in the course of the litigation, the Twenty-Fourth, Twenty-Fifth and Twenty-Ninth Amendments themselves. Thirteen judges of the Supreme Court heard the case across sixty-eight working days in the winter of 1972 and 1973, and on 24 April 1973 the bench delivered eleven separate opinions and a summary of conclusions signed by nine of the thirteen. By seven to six, the Court overruled Golak Nath: Parliament could amend the Fundamental Rights. By the same fractured majority, the Court held that the amending power did not extend to damaging or destroying the basic structure or framework of the Constitution. The doctrine arrived without a list of its contents, and its application to the directive-rights question came in the Court's treatment of Article 31C. The majority upheld the first limb of Article 31C, the shield for laws genuinely implementing Article 39(b) and (c) against Articles 14, 19 and 31, and struck down the second limb, the declaration clause that would have barred courts from examining whether a law in fact implemented those principles. Parliament could privilege specified principles over specified rights; it could not make the privilege self-certifying.

The opinions repay reading on the directive-rights point alone, because the judges reasoned in the idiom the founders had bequeathed and then moved past it. Justice Hegde and Justice Mukherjea, in their joint opinion, treated the Fundamental Rights and the Directive Principles as the two great limbs of the social revolution the Preamble promised, and warned against reading either as the other's enemy. Justice Khanna, whose concurrence supplied the basic-structure majority's decisive vote, framed the Constitution as a document neither of pure restraint nor of pure programme, in which the rights protected the individual and the principles charted the society the rights were meant to serve within. The harmony idiom was not new in 1973; the Court had used it in the Kerala Education Bill reference of 1958, where Chief Justice S.R. Das advised that the two chapters should be read together and neither given supremacy in construction. What Kesavananda added was enforcement machinery behind the idiom. Harmony was no longer a courtesy of interpretation. It was a structural limit on the most powerful political act the Constitution permits, amendment itself, and a legislature that ruptured the relation between the chapters could be stopped by a court invoking the framework of 1949.

For the deferred-question thesis, Kesavananda is the hinge of the whole seventy-year story, and its structure vindicates the synthesis this article defends. The founders' deferral had been deliberate; but a deferral cannot govern a republic forever, and the question of institutional design was who would eventually supply the missing rule, under what discipline, and at whose invitation. The Court supplied it, in a case about land reform, under the discipline of a thirteen-judge bench and a six-month argument, at the invitation of a Parliament that had pushed the amending power to its textual limit. The basic-structure doctrine, on this reading, is the deferred adjudication arriving at its appointed forum, twenty-four years after the Assembly declined to perform it. Jayal's cost accounting belongs here: the decades between 1950 and 1973 were not free. They were filled with statutes struck and re-enacted, with Ninth Schedule shields piled amendment upon amendment, and with a property jurisprudence whose uncertainty every landholder and every landless petitioner paid for. Austin's productivity accounting belongs here too: the open texture the founders left gave the Court of 1973 the raw material, the harmony language of the founding generation itself, from which to build a doctrine no founder had named. Both accounts are true of the same event, and only the synthesis keeps both in view.

## The Forty-Second Amendment: Supremacy Attempted and Answered

The Emergency Parliament attempted to reverse the polarity. The Constitution (Forty-Second Amendment) Act of 1976, the most extensive single revision of the constitutional text, enacted in November 1976 while opposition leaders were imprisoned and the press censored, amended Article 31C to extend its shield from the two distribution principles to all the Directive Principles in Part Four. Any law declared to give effect to any principle would stand immune from challenge under Articles 14 and 19, the equality and freedom guarantees through which most social legislation was attacked. The same amendment inserted clauses (4) and (5) into Article 368, declaring that no amendment could be questioned in any court and that there was no limitation on the amending power. The design's meaning for the directive-rights question was exact: the hierarchy Champakam had written in 1951 would be replaced by its mirror image, with the principles enthroned and the two most general rights subordinated across the whole field of social legislation. The Swaran Singh Committee, whose recommendations of 1976 supplied the amendment's blueprint, presented the changes as restoring the Constitution's original intent. The claim deserves to be stated at its strongest, as its authors made it: the founding generation had meant the principles to drive governance, the courts had frustrated the mandate for a generation, and a Parliament elected on a social programme was entitled to restore the balance the founders intended. Against it stood the record this article has traced, in which the founders intended no hierarchy in either direction, and in which the restoration claimed for 1976 was an invention of pedigree.

The political conditions of the amendment matter to its doctrinal fate, and stating them is not partisanship but provenance. The Forty-Second Amendment was passed by a Lok Sabha whose term had been extended beyond its elected five years, under Emergency rule proclaimed on 25 June 1975, with the usual channels of public contestation closed. When the Janata Parliament of 1977 came to repair the text, the Forty-Third and Forty-Fourth Amendments of 1977 and 1978 repealed or revised much of the Emergency revision, deleting the fundamental right to property from Part Three and relocating it to Article 300A as a legal right, a change that quietly removed the oldest battlefield of the directive-rights war. But the Janata Parliament could not muster the Rajya Sabha numbers to undo the Article 31C expansion, and so the supremacy clause survived the democratic correction and travelled to the forum the founders' deferral had appointed. It arrived there in the form of a dispute about a nationalised textile mill in Bombay, and the answer the Court gave on 31 July 1980 completed the doctrinal sentence that Champakam had begun in 1951.

## Minerva Mills: The Balance Itself Becomes Basic Structure

Minerva Mills, a textile company in Bangalore nationalised in 1974 under the Sick Textile Undertakings (Nationalisation) Act, challenged its takeover and, with it, the constitutional architecture of the Emergency amendments. A five-judge Constitution Bench delivered judgment on 31 July 1980 in Minerva Mills Ltd v Union of India, reported at (1980) 3 SCC 625, with Chief Justice Y.V. Chandrachud writing for a unanimous Court on the amending-power clauses and carrying four of five judges on Article 31C. The holdings were three. The clauses inserted into Article 368 by the Forty-Second Amendment, which barred judicial review of amendments and declared the amending power unlimited, were void, because a limited power cannot be used to make itself unlimited. The expansion of Article 31C to all Directive Principles was void, because it destroyed the balance between Part Three and Part Four. And that balance, the Court held, was itself an essential feature of the basic structure. The opinion's sentence on the third holding has become the canonical statement of the directive-rights settlement: the Constitution is founded on the bed-rock of the balance between Parts Three and Four, and to give primacy to one over the other is to disturb the harmony of the Constitution. The Court restored Article 31C to its Kesavananda dimensions, a shield for the two distribution principles of Article 39(b) and (c), judicially examinable, and no more.

The reasoning repays attention because it performs, in judicial prose, the adjudication the founders had declined to perform in constituent prose, and it does so by returning to the founding record rather than inventing values. Chief Justice Chandrachud's majority treated the two chapters as complementary instruments of one design, the rights restraining the state so that the principles' society could be built without tyranny, the principles directing the state so that the rights would not become the charter of a frozen order. A Parliament that privileged the principles across the board had not fulfilled the design but amended it out of existence, which Kesavananda forbade. The lone partial dissenter, Justice P.N. Bhagwati, would have upheld the expanded Article 31C as a legitimate instrument of the social programme, reading the Directive Principles' mandate as the Constitution's dominant purpose; his dissent is the judicial form of the supremacy argument, and its presence on the bench shows how live the founders' unanswered question remained in 1980. The majority's answer closed the hierarchy contest in both directions. Rights could not subordinate principles, as Champakam had held in the remedial sense only; principles could not subordinate rights; the relation itself was constitutional bedrock, alterable by no majority.

It is worth pausing on what kind of settlement Minerva Mills represents in the longer audit, because it is neither Austin's accommodation nor a simple verdict against deferral. The Court did not discover the balance in a single clause. It inferred the balance from the structure the founders built, two chapters, two commitments, no ranking, and it elevated the founders' refusal to rank into a rule that ranked refusals could not be overridden. The deliberate ambiguity, in other words, hardened over thirty years into deliberate doctrine, and the hardening is the strongest evidence for this article's synthesis. The deferral was real, and the deferral was where the doctrine grew. A founding generation that had written Champakam's hierarchy into the text would have left nothing for Kesavananda to build on; a founding generation that had written supremacy for the principles would have made Minerva Mills impossible. The space the Assembly left empty in November 1948 turned out to be load-bearing, and the Court's recognition of that fact, thirty-two years later, is the closest the constitutional system has come to an official interpretation of the founding silence. The silence was structural. The structure was the answer.

## The Other Enforcement: Legislation, Plans and the Harmonious Reading

Between the great confrontations, the principles were being enforced by the unglamorous route the founders had actually designed for them, and an audit that attends only to courtrooms will miss the bulk of their career. The legislative record of the first three decades is saturated with Part Four's instructions, often cited in the statements of objects and reasons that accompanied the bills. The abolition of zamindari across the states in the early 1950s enacted Article 39(b) before the courts had finished arguing about it. The Hindu code enactments of 1955 and 1956, which reformed marriage, succession, adoption and guardianship for the majority community, carried forward the uniform-code impulse of Article 44 in the partial form politics allowed. The Employees' State Insurance Act of 1948 and the Employees' Provident Funds Act of 1952 built the skeleton of Article 41's public assistance and Article 43's decent conditions before the Constitution's first decade closed; the Minimum Wages Act of 1948, carried into the republic, the Maternity Benefit Act of 1961 under Article 42's express command, the Equal Remuneration Act of 1976 under Article 39(d), and the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act of 1976 under Article 23 read with the principles, extended the pattern. The Five-Year Plans, administered by the Planning Commission created by cabinet resolution in March 1950, functioned as the principles' bureaucratic form: documents of instruction, revised by politics, unfitted to judicial enforcement, and consequential beyond any single statute. Ambedkar's political sanction operated here in its intended register. Governments campaigned on the programme the principles described, oppositions indicted governments for betraying it by name and article, and the chapter the comparison table calls unenforceable was enforced continuously by the only machinery its authors had designated.

The courts, for their part, developed the interpretive doctrine that made the two chapters read as one text well before the basic structure gave the doctrine teeth. The locus classicus is the Kerala Education Bill reference. In 1957 the President referred the Kerala Education Bill to the Supreme Court under Article 143, and the Court's advisory opinion of 22 May 1958, delivered by Chief Justice S.R. Das, the same judge who had written subordination into Champakam seven years earlier, advised that although the Directive Principles could not override the Fundamental Rights, in determining the scope and ambit of the rights the courts should not entirely ignore the principles, and that the attempt should be to give effect to both, adopting the principle of harmonious construction. The sentence is the seed of everything Minerva Mills later harvested. Harmonious construction began as a courtesy between chapters and ended as a structural limit on amendment; the career of that single interpretive maxim, from advisory opinion in 1958 to basic structure in 1980, is the doctrinal biography of the founders' ambiguity in its judicial register. The same maxim governed the rights-expanding cases of the late 1970s, when the Court in cases arising under Articles 14, 19 and 21 began to read the rights in the principles' light as a matter of method. By the time Minerva Mills declared the balance basic, the balance was already the Court's working habit, which is one reason the declaration, for all its grandeur, changed less in daily adjudication than the supremacy clause it destroyed would have changed.

## After Minerva Mills: The Quiet Working of Part Four

The decades after 1980 did not reopen the hierarchy contest, and their quietness is itself evidence that the settlement held. What the principles did instead was work through the channels the founding design and the case law had left open, above all through interpretation. The Supreme Court read Article 21, the right to life, in the light of the principles and found within it a right to livelihood in Olga Tellis v Bombay Municipal Corporation in 1985, where pavement dwellers facing eviction under a Bombay statute were held to hold a claim comprehended within life itself; a right to education in Mohini Jain in 1992 and, in disciplined form, in Unnikrishnan v State of Andhra Pradesh in 1993, which drew on Article 45 to hold that children held an enforceable right to free education until the age of fourteen; and a right to equal pay in Randhir Singh, where the principle of Article 39(d) was read into Articles 14 and 16 to reach a driver-constable's pay scale. The mechanism in each case was the harmony doctrine in its interpretive register: the principles supplied content, the rights supplied enforceability, and the litigant received a remedy that the comparison table, with its clean separation of the chapters, says cannot exist.

### What did the Supreme Court decide about equal pay in Randhir Singh v Union of India?

In Randhir Singh v Union of India, decided on 23 February 1982, a three-judge bench read the Directive Principle of equal pay for equal work in Article 39(d) into the enforceable guarantees of Articles 14 and 16. The Court held that a driver-constable could not be paid less than drivers doing identical work elsewhere in the same service.

The second quiet channel ran through the Ninth Schedule, and it closed with a nine-judge bench. In I.R. Coelho v State of Tamil Nadu, judgment delivered on 11 January 2007, the Court held unanimously that laws placed in the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973, the date of Kesavananda, remained open to challenge where they violated Articles 14, 15, 19 or 21 or the principles underlying them, and that insertion into the Schedule could not launder a statute that damaged the basic structure. Coelho matters to the directive-rights audit because the Ninth Schedule had been the political branches' oldest device for insulating the principles' programme from the rights, beginning with the First Amendment's protection of land reform in June 1951. The device survived; the immunity did not. A state could still pursue the programme, and Article 31C within its Kesavananda limits still shielded the distribution principles; what the state could no longer do was place its statute beyond the balance. The post-1980 order, taken whole, is the founders' treaty administered by courts: the programme proceeds, the rights restrain, the relation is policed, and the principles keep arriving in judgment after judgment as the Constitution's standing instruction to read the rights generously in the direction of the society Part Four describes. ## Austin Against Jayal: Accommodation, Deferral, and the Verdict

The scholarly disagreement this article must adjudicate is between two accounts of what the founders did in that winter, each anchored in a major work. Granville Austin, in Working a Democratic Constitution, published in 1999 as the sequel to his 1966 study of the founding, reads the directive-rights ambiguity as productive accommodation. On Austin's account, the Assembly's genius lay in drafting commitments that rival camps could accept because each camp could hope to realise its commitment through the politics of the new republic; the ambiguity kept the Constitution adoptable and kept its promises alive for future majorities to redeem. The principles, for Austin, were part of what he called the seamless web of the Constitution, national unity, democracy and social revolution woven as one fabric, and the web held because no strand was cut. His evidence is the founding record itself, committee papers and debates read across two volumes and four decades, and his reading has the authority of the scholar who knew that record most completely.

### What does Granville Austin mean by productive ambiguity in Working a Democratic Constitution?

In Working a Democratic Constitution, published in 1999, Granville Austin treats the unresolved relation between the Directive Principles and the Fundamental Rights as productive accommodation. Rival camps in the Assembly accepted one text because each could pursue its vision through future politics, and later generations inherited living commitments instead of a hierarchy that would have strangled one vision at birth.

Niraja Gopal Jayal reads the same record with a colder eye. In Citizenship and Its Discontents, published in 2013, her account of the founding treats the era's large promises, on citizenship and on social and economic transformation, as commitments whose hard choices the founding generation deferred, leaving later generations to undertake an adjudication that the founders had declined. The deferral, on this reading, was not costless wisdom. It transferred conflict from a constituent assembly, where it might have been settled with founding authority, into ordinary politics and constitutional litigation, where the parties least able to bear delay, the landless petitioner, the excluded applicant, the striking worker, bore it. Jayal's evidence is the same record read for its silences and for the distribution of the burden those silences created, and her method is the necessary complement to Austin's: she asks not only what the accommodation achieved but what it cost and who paid.

### Where does Niraja Gopal Jayal locate the cost of the founders' deferral?

In Citizenship and Its Discontents (2013), Jayal locates the cost of the founding deferrals in the generations that inherited them. Questions the Assembly left open, on citizenship as on the social programme, had to be adjudicated later in legislatures and courtrooms, and the burden of delay fell on citizens whose claims waited longest for a forum and a remedy.

A third reading helps the adjudication, and it is Madhav Khosla's. In India's Founding Moment, published in 2020, Khosla treats the Constitution as a pedagogical project, a code designed to teach a vast and largely unlettered electorate the practices of democratic citizenship, in which generality and deferral are features of a text meant to be inhabited over time rather than a code meant to be applied at once. On the pedagogical reading, ambiguity is the teacher's patience: the founders wrote principles as instruction because instruction was the mode in which a founding text could govern a society still learning the grammar of rights.

### What does Madhav Khosla mean by describing the Constitution as a pedagogical project?

Madhav Khosla argues that the Constitution was designed as a pedagogical project: a text to teach democratic citizenship to a new electorate by instructing its institutions over time. On his reading, the Directive Principles are lessons addressed to future governments, and their open texture is the patience of a teacher rather than the evasion of a draftsman.

The verdict of this article is the synthesis the brief proposes, defended here on the evidence assembled above. Austin is right about the intention and the mechanism: the deferral was deliberate, the record of November 1948 shows alternatives considered and refused, and the open texture did keep both visions alive inside one adoptable text. Jayal is right about the incidence: the deferral's costs were real, unevenly distributed, and paid in litigation and delay by claimants who had no seat in the Assembly. Khosla is right about the form: the principles were drafted as instruction, and instruction is a mode of binding that courts were never meant to exhaust. The synthesis is not a compromise for politeness. It is the only reading consistent with the whole sequence, because the basic-structure doctrine, the synthesis's institutional proof, is unintelligible except as the point where a deliberate deferral met its deferred adjudication. The founders left the second-order question open; the Court of 1973 and 1980 answered it by constitutionalising the openness itself, declaring that the balance, the refusal to rank, was the ranking. The ambiguity was productive and the ambiguity was costly, and the doctrine was the way the republic learned to bear the cost without surrendering the productivity. That sentence is this article's adjudication, and it stands against both a purely celebratory founding story and a purely accusatory one.

## The Surviving Shield: Article 31C After the Balance

The restoration of Article 31C to its Kesavananda dimensions left a live provision in the text, and its afterlife tests whether the balance doctrine could administer a genuine exception without dissolving into it. The test case arrived quickly. In Sanjeev Coke Manufacturing Company v Bharat Coking Coal Ltd, decided on 10 December 1982, a Constitution Bench upheld the Coking Coal Mines (Nationalisation) Act of 1972 against challenge under Articles 14 and 19, holding the statute protected by Article 31C as a law giving effect to Article 39(b), and reasoning that the distribution of material resources, in that case coking coal reserved for the steel industry, fell squarely within the protected principle. The judgment is sometimes read as a relapse into supremacy, and the reading is wrong in an instructive way. The shield operated because the Court itself examined the connection between the statute and the specified principles and found it real; the declaration clause that would have made the connection unexaminable had been struck in 1973 and the unexaminability had not returned. Minerva Mills had not killed Article 31C. It had constitutionalised the conditions of its use: two named principles, a genuine nexus, judicial verification. What survived 1980 was not the Emergency's supremacy but a licensed and supervised exception, and the supervision is the balance doctrine in its administrative form.

The same discipline governed the Ninth Schedule's long unwinding, and the two devices should be read as a pair. In Waman Rao v Union of India, decided on 13 November 1980, months after Minerva Mills, the Court held that amendments inserting laws into the Ninth Schedule after the Kesavananda judgment of 24 April 1973 would be open to challenge on basic-structure grounds, while earlier insertions stood undisturbed. The line the Court drew was chronological and pragmatic, a settlement between stability and principle, and I.R. Coelho's nine judges confirmed and generalised it in January 2007. The pair of devices, Article 31C and the Ninth Schedule, had been Parliament's two instruments for prosecuting the principles' programme against the rights; the Court's mature doctrine left both instruments in existence and subjected both to the balance. That outcome, neither abolition nor immunity, is the mature form of the founders' treaty and the strongest practical evidence for the synthesis this article defends. A judiciary bent on rights-supremacy would have dismantled the instruments. A judiciary indifferent to the rights would have left them unsupervised. The Court of the balance doctrine kept the programme's tools and confiscated their immunities, which is what administering a deferred settlement looks like when it is done in earnest.

## The Comparative Mirror: The Road of Justiciable Social Rights

The Indian choice comes into sharpest relief against the constitution that chose otherwise, with India's example open on the table. The Constitution of South Africa, adopted in 1996, made its social and economic rights directly justiciable: sections 26 and 27 guarantee rights of access to housing, health care, food, water and social security, qualified by the state's duty to take reasonable measures, within available resources, toward their progressive realisation. The South African Constitutional Court's judgment in Government of the Republic of South Africa v Grootboom, delivered on 4 October 2000, enforced the housing right through that reasonableness standard, holding a provincial programme unconstitutional for making no provision for people in desperate need, while declining to convert the right into an individually claimable minimum core. The comparison disciplines both Indian camps. Against the Indian settlement's critics, Grootboom shows that even a justiciable text delivers programme review rather than houses, and that a court enforcing social rights converges on supervision of government rationality, a terrain Indian courts reached through Article 21 without a justiciable Part Four. Against the settlement's celebrants, South Africa shows what India surrendered: a textual basis on which the desperate could appear in court as of right in 1950 rather than wait for the interpretive generosity of the 1980s. The Irish source text travelled the other way as well; Ireland's own courts, in cases across the later twentieth century, treated Article 45's directive principles as non-cognisable in the terms the Irish text prescribed, while occasionally allowing them interpretive influence, a trajectory closer to India's harmonious construction than to South Africa's rights model. Three constitutions, one device, three settlements: judicial enforcement in South Africa, interpretive partnership in India, near-dormancy in Ireland. The variation confirms this article's central claim from comparative ground. The directive device does not determine its own force. The force is settled by the politics and the courts of each republic over decades, which is precisely the open future the Indian founders, declining Shah's timetable and Rau's clause alike, chose to leave to theirs.

## Why the Settled-Doctrine Story Persists

If the record is this legible, why does the comparison table survive as the standard account, reproduced in textbook after textbook and answer key after answer key? Three reasons, each structural. The first is pedagogical economy: a table can be memorised in an evening and examined in a minute, and the market for constitutional summaries rewards compression over accuracy at exactly the point where the subject is a process rather than a proposition. The second is doctrinal habit inside the legal profession itself. Lawyers are trained on holdings, and the holdings, detached from their sequence, do look like a set of discrete rules: subordination here, a shield there, a balance elsewhere. The sequence is the meaning, and sequence is what a digest cannot carry. The third reason is the founding generation's own reticence. Because the Assembly never announced that it was deferring the second-order question, later readers could mistake the silence for absence, and the rich committee record in which the deferral was debated, Rau's discarded clause, Shah's rejected timetable, the November 1948 exchanges, sat in archives while the finished text circulated alone. Granville Austin's two volumes, published in 1966 and 1999, performed the archival recovery on which every serious account since depends, and the recovery is still working its way through the textbooks at the pace of syllabus revision. The settled-doctrine story persists because it is cheap to transmit. The deferred-question story requires the record, and the record requires reading. This article has proceeded on the conviction that the reading is worth it, because the two stories produce different citizens: one who believes the Constitution answered its hardest distributive question in 1949, and one who knows the answer was an institution for arguing about it, built deliberately, at a price the founders counted and their successors paid.

There is a competitive point here that the series to which this article belongs exists to make. Reference works summarise doctrine because summary is their form; coaching materials tabulate doctrine because tabulation is theirs. Neither form can hold an argument about a seventy-year process, and the space left by those forms is where a long-form adjudication wins its readers. The directive-rights question is not a fact to be looked up. It is a controversy with named parties, Austin against the deferral critics, Shah against Ambedkar, the Champakam bench against the Kesavananda majority, and a reader who has followed the controversy owns something no table can give: the ability to recognise the next round of the argument when it arrives, as it arrived in the Maratha litigation of November and December 2018, dressed in the vocabulary of weaker sections and equality codes that this article has traced to the winter of 1948.

## The Renegotiation Continues: Reservation, Quotas and the Standing Instruction

A settlement that has held since 1980 might be expected to fade from politics, and the directive-rights relation has done the opposite: it has become the standing grammar in which India's distributive politics argues. The reservation amendments of the 1990s and 2000s, the Seventy-Seventh Amendment of 1995 protecting reservation in promotion for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, the Eighty-First and Eighty-Second Amendments of 2000 on backlog vacancies and relaxed standards, and the Eighty-Fifth Amendment of 2001 on consequential seniority, were each Parliament working the Article 46 and Article 335 seam between the principles' command of special care and the rights chapter's equality code, and each was drafted inside the Minerva Mills grammar rather than against it. The Supreme Court's nine-judge decision in Indra Sawhney in 1992, capping reservation at fifty percent and excluding the creamy layer, belongs to the same grammar: the Court administered the balance as a working rule of distributive politics, not as a museum doctrine. When the Maharashtra legislature enacted the Socially and Educationally Backward Classes Act on 30 November 2018, reserving sixteen percent of seats and posts for the Maratha community after the Gaikwad Commission's report of that month, the statute's defence was framed, in the Assembly's debates and in the litigation that followed its enactment, in the vocabulary this article has traced: a Directive Principle of special care for weaker sections, an equality code that permits classification for substantive ends, and a ceiling doctrine that polices the relation between them. The founders' deferred question is the form in which twenty-first century India argues about who gets what, which is the surest sign that the deferral built a durable institution rather than a temporary embarrassment. The Constitution (One Hundred and Third Amendment) Act, passed by Parliament in January 2019, carried the grammar one step further than any predecessor: it inserted Articles 15(6) and 16(6) to reserve ten percent of seats and posts for economically weaker sections outside the Scheduled Caste, Scheduled Tribe and Other Backward Class quotas, breaching the fifty percent ceiling that Indra Sawhney had administered as a working rule and doing so in the name of the same Directive Principle of special care for weaker sections. A reservation justified by economic weakness rather than by caste was the developmental-state vision arguing in the equality code's own courtroom, and its defence was conducted entirely in the vocabulary of the deferred founding question: the principles commanding, the rights permitting, and the balance between them deciding. The renegotiation the Assembly scheduled in 1949 was still running on schedule seventy years later, which is what a productive ambiguity looks like when it becomes an institution.

The point generalises beyond reservation, and it is the point on which the House Thesis of this series turns. The directive-rights tension is one instance of [the third founding tension on directive-rights that the article works out](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/), the Assembly's habit of writing a commitment at full strength and a counter-commitment at full strength and leaving the reconciliation to the republic's future. The same habit produced the federal settlement's asymmetries and the secular settlement's principled distance, and in each domain the pattern of the working-out has rhymed: an early judicial or political attempt at hierarchy, a reaction, and an eventual doctrine that constitutionalises the balance rather than the victor. What distinguishes the directive-rights instance is the completeness of its documentary trail, seven holdings and amendments across seventy years, each one preserved in the timeline above, and the explicitness of the final doctrine. Minerva Mills did not merely resolve a case; it told the republic what kind of act the founders had performed in 1948, and the republic has governed itself inside the telling ever since.

## The Ambiguity at Work

The comparison table with which this article began can be rebuilt, honestly, on the evidence. Part Three and Part Four differ in remedy: rights are enforceable by courts, principles are not, and Article 37 says so in terms no reading may dissolve. They do not differ in rank. The founders, who considered giving the principles enforceability on K.T. Shah's timetable and considered giving principle-implementing laws primacy in B.N. Rau's discarded clause, adopted neither hierarchy and wrote a double formula whose second clause commands the state to treat the unenforceable principles as fundamental in governance. The courts then did, across Champakam and Golak Nath, Kesavananda and the Forty-Second Amendment's supremacy attempt, what courts do with deferred questions: they answered them provisionally, were corrected by amendment, answered again, and finally, in Minerva Mills on 31 July 1980, constitutionalised the deferral itself as the balance that no amendment may destroy. Austin's accommodation and Jayal's deferred adjudication name the same founding act from its two ends, and the doctrine is the proof that both names are earned. The Directive Principles were not aspirational guidance subordinate to Fundamental Rights. They were, and remain, the unresolved half of a deliberate constitutional ambiguity, and the resolution the republic eventually gave was to declare the irresolution structural. A reader who holds that sentence holds the comparison the table could not draw.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between Directive Principles and Fundamental Rights?

The Fundamental Rights in Part Three are enforceable entitlements held by persons against the state: a citizen whose rights under Articles 14, 19 or 21 are violated can move the Supreme Court under Article 32 or a High Court under Article 226 and obtain a remedy. The Directive Principles in Part Four are instructions addressed to the state itself, directing the policies it should pursue in welfare, distribution, education, health and allied fields, and Article 37 bars any court from enforcing them directly. The difference is therefore one of remedy and addressee, rights running from citizen against state and principles running from the Constitution to the state. It is not a difference of rank. From the Kerala Education Bill opinion of 1958 through Minerva Mills in 1980, the Supreme Court held that the two chapters must be read together, and that neither may be given primacy over the other.

### Q: Are Directive Principles enforceable in court?

Not directly. Article 37 provides in terms that the provisions of Part Four shall not be enforceable by any court, and no petitioner can obtain a writ merely because a government has failed to implement a Directive Principle. That bar, however, closes only one door. Courts use the principles to interpret statutes and to give content to enforceable rights, most prominently by reading directive commitments into Article 21, as happened with livelihood in Olga Tellis in 1985 and education in Unnikrishnan in 1993. Laws enacted to implement specified principles may also receive the protection of Article 31C against challenge under Articles 14 and 19. The accurate summary is that the principles are unenforceable as standalone claims and highly consequential inside other litigation, a combination the founders designed when they made the principles binding through politics rather than through courts.

### Q: Why did the founders include Directive Principles in the Constitution?

The founders wanted the Constitution to commit the new republic to a social and economic programme, not merely to restrain its government. A generation formed by the Karachi Resolution of 1931 and by mass poverty held that political freedom without material transformation would be hollow, and Part Four wrote the programme into the founding text: livelihood, distribution of resources, education, public health, and special care for the weaker sections. The device itself came from the Irish Constitution of 1937, whose Article 45 offered a model of principles guiding the legislature without judicial enforcement, adapted through the work of B.N. Rau and the Assembly's committees in 1947. The founders chose instruction over enforcement because a justiciable programme would have transferred the sequencing of social policy to courts and frozen one generation's economics into the text. The principles were the mechanism by which the Constitution could promise transformation while leaving each elected generation free to choose its means.

### Q: What did Champakam Dorairajan rule about Directive Principles?

In State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan, decided on 9 April 1951 and reported at AIR 1951 SC 226, a seven-judge bench struck down the Madras communal order that distributed college seats among communities in fixed proportions. The state had defended the order by invoking Article 46, the Directive Principle requiring special care for the educational interests of the weaker sections. Justice S.R. Das, for the Court, held that the Directive Principles could not override the Fundamental Rights in Articles 15(1) and 29(2), reasoning that since Article 37 made the principles non-enforceable, they had to conform to and run as subsidiary to the rights chapter, which the Court described as sacrosanct. The ruling established the subordination formula that textbooks repeated for a generation. Parliament answered within weeks: the First Amendment of June 1951 inserted Article 15(4), expressly permitting special provision for backward classes, and thereby restored by amendment the policy the judgment had struck down in the name of the principles' inferiority.

### Q: How does the basic-structure doctrine relate to Directive Principles?

The basic-structure doctrine, announced in Kesavananda Bharati on 24 April 1973, holds that Parliament may amend the Constitution but may not damage or destroy its basic structure. Its relation to the Directive Principles runs in two directions. In Kesavananda itself, the doctrine preserved the Directive Principles' strongest instrument, upholding Article 31C's shield for laws implementing Article 39(b) and (c) while striking the clause that barred judicial review. Seven years later, in Minerva Mills on 31 July 1980, the doctrine protected the principles from the opposite direction: the Supreme Court struck down the Forty-Second Amendment's attempt to give all Directive Principles primacy over Articles 14 and 19, holding that the balance between Part Three and Part Four is itself part of the basic structure. The doctrine thus neither enthrones nor subordinates the principles. It constitutionalises their relation to the rights, so that no parliamentary majority may settle by amendment the hierarchy the founders declined to settle in 1949.

### Q: What is Article 37 of the Indian Constitution?

Article 37 is the provision that defines the legal character of the Directive Principles, and it does so in two clauses that pull against each other. The first clause provides that the provisions contained in Part Four shall not be enforceable by any court. The second provides that the principles are nevertheless fundamental in the governance of the country, and that it shall be the duty of the state to apply these principles in making laws. Read whole, the article withdraws the principles from judicial enforcement while elevating them into a duty of lawmaking, a combination with no parallel elsewhere in the Constitution. Defending the draft on 19 November 1948, Ambedkar compared the principles to the Instruments of Instruction under the Government of India Act of 1935: directions that bound governments politically, through responsibility to the electorate, rather than legally through courts. Article 37 is therefore the textual site of the founding ambiguity this article audits, the place where the Constitution both commands and declines to enforce.

### Q: Did Ambedkar support making Directive Principles justiciable?

No. Ambedkar defended the non-justiciable design in the Constituent Assembly, most fully on 19 November 1948, and he did so on principle rather than from indifference to the principles' content. He argued that the principles were binding instructions to whoever held power, comparable to the Instruments of Instruction issued under the Government of India Act of 1935, and that their sanction was political: a government that ignored them would answer to the electorate. He also resisted fixing any single economic doctrine in the Constitution, telling the Assembly that what the social organisation of the state should be must be decided by the people according to time and circumstances, and he rejected K.T. Shah's proposals for enforceability and for writing socialist into the Constitution's identity clauses. His position was that enforcement through courts would freeze policy, while enforcement through democratic accountability kept the programme binding and its economics open. Later governments and courts tested that wager, but the founding position was his, stated and defended on the record.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court decide in Golak Nath v State of Punjab in 1967?

In I.C. Golak Nath v State of Punjab, decided on 27 February 1967, an eleven-judge bench held by six to five that Parliament had no power to amend the Fundamental Rights. Chief Justice K. Subba Rao's majority reasoned that a constitutional amendment was a law within Article 13, that the rights occupied a transcendental position in the Constitution, and that the amending power in Article 368 supplied only procedure, not a power to abridge Part Three. Fearing chaos if earlier reforms were undone, the Court applied its holding prospectively, so the First, Fourth and Seventeenth Amendments survived. The case arose from land ceiling legislation in Punjab, and its significance for the Directive Principles was structural: with the rights unamendable, the Champakam subordination of principles to rights could never be corrected by the political branches, and the social programme of Part Four would operate permanently at the sufferance of the property and equality clauses. Parliament reversed the premise, though not the technique, through the Twenty-Fourth Amendment of 1971, and Kesavananda Bharati overruled Golak Nath itself in 1973.

### Q: What did Minerva Mills strike down in 1980, and what part of the Forty-Second Amendment's design survived it?

Minerva Mills Ltd v Union of India, decided on 31 July 1980 and reported at (1980) 3 SCC 625, struck down two elements of the Forty-Second Amendment of 1976. The Court voided clauses (4) and (5) of Article 368, which had barred judicial review of constitutional amendments and declared the amending power unlimited, holding that a limited power cannot be exercised to make itself unlimited. It also voided the expansion of Article 31C, which had extended that provision's shield from the two distribution principles of Article 39(b) and (c) to every Directive Principle in Part Four against Articles 14 and 19. What survived was substantial. Article 31C in its original Kesavananda form remained good law, so a law genuinely giving effect to Article 39(b) or (c) still stands protected against Articles 14, 19 and 31, subject to judicial examination of the connection. The balance the judgment announced also survived as doctrine: the relation between Part Three and Part Four was declared part of the basic structure, and no later amendment has displaced it.

### Q: How did the Forty-Fourth Amendment of 1978 change the directive-rights balance by removing the fundamental right to property?

The Forty-Fourth Amendment, enacted by the Janata Parliament in 1978, deleted Article 19(1)(f) and Article 31 from the Fundamental Rights and inserted Article 300A, which provides that no person shall be deprived of property save by authority of law. Property thereby ceased to be a fundamental right and became a constitutional and legal right, enforceable against executive action without law but not armed with Article 32. The change transformed the directive-rights balance because property had been the balance's oldest battlefield: the compensation and acquisition clauses of Article 31 were the provisions against which land reform and redistribution under Article 39(b) and (c) had litigated from Bela Banerjee in 1954 through the bank nationalisation cases to Kesavananda. With the property right demoted, the sharpest recurring collision between the principles' programme and the rights chapter lost its textual basis, and later redistribution measures faced the equality and freedom clauses rather than a dedicated property guarantee. The amendment thus achieved by subtraction part of what the Forty-Second Amendment had attempted by supremacy, and it did so through ordinary democratic correction after the Emergency rather than under it.

### Q: Which Directive Principles have the courts read into the right to life in Article 21?

The Supreme Court's expansion of Article 21 after 1978 drew repeatedly on Part Four for content. In Olga Tellis v Bombay Municipal Corporation in 1985, the Court held that the right to life includes the right to livelihood, drawing on Article 39(a) and Article 41, so that pavement dwellers could not be evicted without regard to their means of living. In Randhir Singh in 1982, Article 39(d)'s principle of equal pay for equal work was read into Articles 14 and 16. In Unnikrishnan in 1993, Article 45, read with Article 41 and Article 46, grounded a child's enforceable right to free education until fourteen. The pollution and health cases of the 1990s drew on Articles 47 and 48A to build environmental content into Article 21, and the right to medical care drew on Articles 39(e), 41 and 47. The mechanism is consistent: the principles supply the substance, Article 21 supplies the remedy, and the litigant receives through interpretation what Article 37 withholds as a direct claim.

### Q: What subjects do the Directive Principles from Article 38 to Article 51 cover?

The principles span welfare, economics, social legislation and institutional design. Article 38 states the mission, a welfare state ordered by justice. Article 39 lists the economic programme: livelihood, distribution of material resources, prevention of concentrated wealth, equal pay, and protection of workers and children. Articles 40 to 43A cover village panchayats, the right to work and public assistance, humane conditions of work and maternity relief, a living wage and cottage industries, and worker participation in management, with Article 43B, added in 2011, covering co-operative societies. Article 44 holds out a uniform civil code; Article 45, originally children's education and after 2002 early childhood care; Article 46, special care for weaker sections; Article 47, public health, nutrition and prohibition; Article 48, agriculture and animal husbandry; Article 48A, the environment; Article 49, monuments; Article 50, separation of the judiciary from the executive; and Article 51, international peace and just relations between nations.

### Q: How did the Irish Constitution of 1937 influence India's Directive Principles?

The Irish Constitution of 1937 supplied the device itself. Its Article 45 set out directive principles of social policy for the general guidance of the Oireachtas, the Irish legislature, and declared that they were not to be cognisable by any court. B.N. Rau, the Constitutional Adviser to the Constituent Assembly, studied the Irish text during his preparatory tour and drafting work in 1946 and 1947 and carried the mechanism into the Indian process, where the Sub-Committee on Fundamental Rights adopted the division between enforceable rights and non-enforceable principles in its report of April 1947. The influence was structural rather than textual in detail: India took the form, a chapter of binding instruction without judicial remedy, and filled it with a far larger programme drawn from the Karachi Resolution of 1931, Gandhian commitments such as village panchayats and prohibition, and the socialist commitments of the freedom movement's left. Ireland also supplied the cautionary parallel, for Irish courts treated their principles as largely dormant, whereas India's became the working text this article traces through seventy years of litigation.

### Q: What did the Supreme Court say about harmonious construction in the Kerala Education Bill reference of 1958?

In 1957 the President referred the Kerala Education Bill to the Supreme Court under Article 143, and the advisory opinion delivered on 22 May 1958 by Chief Justice S.R. Das addressed the relation between the Fundamental Rights and the Directive Principles directly. The Court advised that although the principles could not override the rights, a court determining the scope and ambit of the Fundamental Rights should not entirely ignore the Directive Principles, and that the attempt should be to give effect to both chapters as far as possible, adopting what the opinion called the principle of harmonious construction. The statement is striking for its author: the same judge had written the subordination formula in Champakam Dorairajan seven years earlier. Harmonious construction began the doctrinal migration that ended at Minerva Mills, where the harmony of 1958 was elevated into a structural limit on constitutional amendment. Between the two dates, the maxim governed how courts read statutes and rights in the principles' light, and it remains the working rule of interpretation in directive-rights cases.

### Q: What is Article 31C, and how much of it survived Kesavananda Bharati and Minerva Mills?

Article 31C was inserted by the Twenty-Fifth Amendment in 1971 to protect the Directive Principles' economic core. It provides that a law giving effect to the policy of Article 39(b) or Article 39(c), the distribution of material resources and the prevention of concentrated wealth, cannot be struck down for violating Articles 14, 19 or 31. The original provision also contained a declaration clause stating that such a law could not be questioned on the ground that it did not in fact give effect to those principles. Kesavananda Bharati in 1973 split the provision: the shield survived, the declaration clause fell, because courts had to remain able to examine whether a law genuinely implemented the specified principles. The Forty-Second Amendment in 1976 expanded the shield to every Directive Principle. Minerva Mills in 1980 struck the expansion as destructive of the basic structure and restored the Kesavananda form. What survives, therefore, is a supervised exception: two named principles, a real and examinable connection between statute and principle, and protection against three specified rights.

### Q: How did the Unnikrishnan judgment and the Eighty-Sixth Amendment convert Article 45 into an enforceable right?

The original Article 45 directed the state to provide free and compulsory education for every child until the age of fourteen within ten years of the Constitution's commencement, a deadline that expired unmet in 1960. In Unnikrishnan v State of Andhra Pradesh, decided on 4 February 1993, a five-judge bench read Article 45 together with Article 21 and held that every child holds an enforceable fundamental right to free education until fourteen, with education beyond that age subject to the state's economic capacity. The judgment converted a directive into a right by interpretation, using the principles as the content of the life guarantee. Parliament then constitutionalised the conversion. The Eighty-Sixth Amendment of 2002 inserted Article 21A, making free and compulsory education for children from six to fourteen a fundamental right in terms, recast Article 45 as a directive for early childhood care and education for children below six, and added a corresponding fundamental duty for parents and guardians. The Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act of 2009 supplied the statutory machinery. Article 45 is thus the one principle that travelled the full road from instruction to entitlement.

### Q: What did K.T. Shah propose in 1948 for the Directive Principles, and why did the Assembly reject it?

K.T. Shah, the Bombay economist who sat on the Sub-Committee on Fundamental Rights, proposed that the Directive Principles should not remain permanently unenforceable. In a note preserved in the Drafting Committee's records and in amendments pressed during the November 1948 debates, he argued for making the principles justiciable, in Granville Austin's account within a specified period, so that governments could not plead the chapter's softness indefinitely. On the floor he warned that principles without enforcement were pious wishes, a cheque drawn on a bank payable only when the resources of the bank permit. The Assembly, answering through Ambedkar, rejected the scheme for two stated reasons. A timetable for justiciability would hand the sequencing of the social programme to courts before the republic's administrative and fiscal machinery existed, and it would bind future electorates to one generation's economic choices. Ambedkar's counter-design made the sanction political: governments would answer for neglect of the principles before the electorate. The rejection, not any enthusiasm for weak principles, fixed the non-justiciable form.

### Q: What does Article 51 direct, and why does a foreign-policy principle sit in Part Four?

Article 51 directs the state to promote international peace and security, to maintain just and honourable relations between nations, to foster respect for international law and treaty obligations in the dealings of organised peoples with one another, and to encourage settlement of international disputes by arbitration. Its presence in Part Four records the founding generation's understanding of the Constitution as a charter for the whole conduct of the state, not only for its domestic arrangements. Nehru's India intended to act in the world on stated principle, and the Assembly placed the intention where it placed the social programme: as a binding instruction to governments that no court would enforce and no citizen could plead as a personal right. The provision has nonetheless worked in the legal system. Courts have used Article 51, read with Article 253 and the international-law receptivity of the common law tradition, to draw international norms into domestic interpretation, most visibly in environmental and human-rights adjudication, where the Vishaka guidelines of 1997 drew on international conventions in the absence of domestic legislation. A directive addressed to foreign policy thus became one more channel through which Part Four shapes enforceable law.

### Q: How did the Supreme Court use the Directive Principles to frame a right to livelihood in Olga Tellis in 1985?

Olga Tellis v Bombay Municipal Corporation, decided on 10 July 1985, arose from the Bombay Municipal Corporation's decision to evict pavement and slum dwellers under the Bombay Municipal Corporation Act of 1888. A five-judge bench, through Chief Justice Y.V. Chandrachud, held that the right to life in Article 21 includes the right to livelihood, because a person deprived of the means of living is deprived of life in substance. The reasoning drew expressly on the Directive Principles, in particular Article 39(a), the citizen's right to an adequate means of livelihood, and Article 41, the right to work, treating the principles as the interpretive key to the content of the life guarantee. The eviction was not stopped outright; the Court permitted it to proceed under the statute with safeguards and notice, which is characteristic of the method. The principles supplied the substance of a right, Article 21 supplied the forum and the remedy, and the result was an enforceable claim assembled from a chapter the comparison table describes as unenforceable. Olga Tellis remains the standard citation for how Part Four works inside Part Three.

### Q: How do the Fundamental Duties in Part IV-A differ from the Directive Principles in Part IV?

The two chapters sit side by side in the constitutional text and differ in addressee, origin and sanction. The Directive Principles, in Part Four as adopted in 1950, are instructions addressed to the state, directing the policies governments must apply in making laws, and Article 37 declares them fundamental in governance while barring judicial enforcement. The Fundamental Duties, in Part IV-A, were inserted by the Forty-Second Amendment of 1976 on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee and are addressed to citizens: Article 51A lists duties that include abiding by the Constitution, cherishing the ideals of the freedom struggle, protecting public property and developing scientific temper. Ten duties were enacted in 1976; an eleventh, on parents and education, was added by the Eighty-Sixth Amendment in 2002. Like the principles, the duties are non-justiciable in themselves, and Parliament may enforce particular duties by statute, as it has done through laws protecting the national flag, public property and the environment. The structural difference is the direction of obligation: Part Four instructs the governors, Part IV-A instructs the governed, and the two chapters together frame the Constitution's pedagogy of citizenship.
