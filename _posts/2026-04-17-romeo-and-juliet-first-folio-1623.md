The most famous book in English literature holds a text of Romeo and Juliet that its printers never took from any manuscript of the playwright. When the First Folio appeared in 1623, seven years after Shakespeare's death, the tragedy of the Veronese lovers took its place among the tragedies in a volume whose title page promised works "Published according to the True Originall Copies." For this one play, that promise describes the book's ambition rather than its method. The Folio text was set in type from a printed quarto, a descendant of the Second Quarto of 1599 at two removes, and everything distinctive about it follows from that single bibliographical fact. Its readings are a quarto's readings filtered through one more printing house; its compositors corrected, miscorrected and occasionally corrupted a text that already stood in print; its one great structural gift, the division into acts and scenes, came from the printing house rather than the playhouse or the author. Yet the derivative text is also the text through which the play reached posterity, packaged as literature between hard covers, and the paradox defines the Folio's strange position in the play's history: least authoritative of the early witnesses for the words, most consequential of them all for the survival and the shape of the whole.

![The First Folio text of Romeo and Juliet - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-27.webp)

This study gives the verdict at the outset and then proves it from the evidence. For Romeo and Juliet, the First Folio is a witness of the third rank: behind it stand the Second Quarto, printed from copy close to the author's papers, and the First Quarto, a reported text of real if erratic value, while the Folio merely reprints the Second Quarto's line of descent with fresh error and a new skeleton of divisions. Editors therefore build the play's text on the Second Quarto and consult the Folio as a corrector of last resort, a source for a handful of plausible readings, and the origin of the act and scene grid that every reader inherits. What the Folio could not supply in authority it supplied in permanence. Bound into the collected works of 1623, the tragedy entered the libraries where books survive, passed into the Second, Third and Fourth Folios, and reached the eighteenth-century editors as a classic awaiting restoration. The cliché imagines the Folio as the source from which the play flowed. The documentary chain shows the reverse: a quarto flowed into the Folio, and the Folio, precisely because it was a monument rather than a manuscript, carried that quarto's text down the centuries under a grander name.

## The Paradox of the Derivative Monument

The Folio's derivative status surprises readers raised on the volume's legend, and the surprise rests on a confusion between the book and the copy behind each of its thirty-six plays. The printers of 1623 gathered copy wherever the syndicate's rights and the stationers' stocks allowed: unpublished manuscripts for roughly half the contents, promptbooks and transcripts for others, and printed quartos for the remainder. Each play in the volume therefore carries its own textual pedigree, and the prestige of the collection transfers to none of them automatically. A play printed in the Folio from a good manuscript, as several of the previously unpublished histories and comedies were, gains a witness of the first importance. A play printed from a quarto gains nothing but a reprint, and Romeo and Juliet belongs squarely to the second class. Its Folio text descends from the quarto tradition at the point where that tradition had already passed through two editions, and the descent can be demonstrated reading by reading, because the Folio inherits errors that could only have arisen in the quarto printings and adds a fresh crop of its own.

Demonstration matters more than assertion in textual scholarship, and the evidence here is unusually clean. The Second Quarto of 1599 was itself printed from copy that preserved, at several famous places, the debris of the author's drafting process: most famously the duplicated and half-cancelled lines in the dawn parting of Act 3, Scene 5, where two versions of the lovers' exchange stand one after the other in the printed text. A transcript or a promptbook prepared independently of the quartos would have resolved that debris into a single version. The Folio prints the passage in substantially the Second Quarto's unsettled state, carrying forward the marks of the earlier printing rather than the decisions of a playhouse. Where the third quarto of 1609, a page-for-page reprint of its predecessor, introduced or preserved small errors of its own, the Folio follows the third quarto's line rather than correcting back against the Second. Charlton Hinman's great analytical study of the Folio's printing, The Printing and Proof-Reading of the First Folio of Shakespeare (1963), established the physical sequence of the volume's production beyond reasonable dispute, and the textual critics who mapped the copy for each play, building on W. W. Greg's work in The Shakespeare First Folio: Its Bibliographical and Textual History (1955), reached a settled conclusion for this tragedy: the copy set before the Folio's compositors was a quarto of the Q3 line, most probably the third quarto itself, possibly with some consultation of the fourth. No independent manuscript stands behind a single page of the Folio's Romeo and Juliet.

The verdict that follows is sometimes mistaken for a slight on the volume, and the mistake should be refused. A derivative text is not a worthless text. The Folio's compositors and its correctors at press occasionally repaired obvious damage in their copy; its stage directions occasionally preserve a theatrical explicitness the quarto lacks; and its divisions reorganized the play for every subsequent reader. But authority in editing is a question of descent, and by descent the Folio stands downstream of the evidence, drinking from a stream the Second Quarto had already drawn from the spring. The sections that follow take the parts of that verdict in order: the book itself and the legend its preliminaries built; the chain of quartos that carried the text into Jaggard's printing house; the compositors whose habits mark every page they set; the variants, great and small, that the Folio contributes against its copy; the divisions it imposed; the promptbook question raised by its one famous omission; and the long afterlife in which the derivative text, embalmed in the monument, outperformed its betters.

### Does the Folio add any passage of verse that the quartos lack?

No passage of verse enters the play from the Folio, and no speech, soliloquy or exchange survives in its pages that the quarto tradition does not already contain. The Folio's additions are structural and accidental rather than substantive: act and scene headings, fuller stage directions at a few points, corrected or corrupted single words.

A reader searching the Folio for lost lines from the author's hand searches a reprint, and the reprint holds nothing that its printed copy had not already given it. That negative finding is itself a piece of evidence, because it closes the most attractive door through which Folio authority might have entered. Had the syndicate's copy for this play been a playhouse manuscript, the text would be expected to show the manuscript's independence in dozens of places: readings divergent from the quartos in patterned ways, stage business recorded from performance, perhaps passages the quarto printers had dropped. None of that pattern appears. The Folio's divergences from the Second Quarto are the small change of the printing house, a substituted word here, a modernized spelling there, a speech heading regularized, a direction expanded, and they behave exactly as the divergences of the third and fourth quartos behave, because they arise from the same mechanical process of setting type from a printed page. Editors who once hoped the Folio might descend from theatrical copy independent of the quartos, a hope John Dover Wilson entertained for parts of the canon, found for this play that the hope fails at the first collation. The Folio text is the quarto text wearing the collected works' livery, and its evidence must be weighed as a quarto's evidence, discounted by one more act of transmission.

The volume's own preface sharpens the irony to a point. Heminges and Condell commend the collected plays against the stolen and surreptitious copies that had maimed the texts in print, promising the genuine articles in their place. For the eighteen plays first printed in the volume, and for those set from good manuscripts, the promise had substance. For Romeo and Juliet the genuine article and the surreptitious tradition are the same tradition viewed from opposite ends: the text the fellows offered as cured descends, through the trade's own reprints, from the very quarto line their rhetoric deplored, differing from the 1609 reprint chiefly in dress, division and a scattering of fresh error. The preface is advertising, and advertising is not perjury; but generations of readers took the boast as bibliography, and the superstition that the Folio must stand nearer the author than any playbook, a superstition the eighteenth century's editors inherited and the twentieth century's bibliographers had to dismantle, was born in those sentences. The dismantling is the precondition of everything sound in the play's modern text.

## The Book Behind the Legend: Making the First Folio

The volume that carries this derivative text is the most deliberately monumental book of its age in English, and its making explains both its prestige and the accident of which copy its printers used. "Mr. William Shakespeares Comedies, Histories, & Tragedies" was published in London in late 1623, entered in the Stationers' Register on 8 November of that year by Edward Blount and Isaac Jaggard, and printed in the Jaggard printing house, the business founded by William Jaggard and carried on at his death in November 1623 by his son Isaac. The publishing syndicate joined the Jaggards and Blount with the stationers William Aspley and John Smethwick, who between them held rights in several of the previously printed plays. John Heminges and Henry Condell, senior actors of the King's Men and the playwright's fellow shareholders, lent the enterprise their names and their authority as its ostensible editors, signing the address "To the great Variety of Readers" and the dedication to the Earls of Pembroke and Montgomery. Their preface supplies the legend's founding sentences: the plays are offered as the author left them, cured and perfect of their limbs, where earlier printings had been "stolne, and surreptitious copies, maimed, and deformed by the frauds and stealthes of injurious impostors." The sentence advertises the volume against the quartos in general, and for plays the syndicate printed from manuscripts it had some warrant. For Romeo and Juliet, set from a quarto in the syndicate's own trade line, the boast collapses into irony: the stolen and surreptitious copies deplored in the preface include the very ancestors of the text the volume prints.

The physical book was a prestige object by design. A folio, in the technical sense examined later in this study's questions, is a volume made from sheets folded once, producing large pages suited to law, history and divinity rather than to plays, which the trade ordinarily issued in the cheap quarto format of the playbooks. To print plays in folio was to claim a shelf among the classics, following the precedent of Ben Jonson's Works of 1616, whose folio dignity Jonson was mocked for claiming on behalf of mere plays. The Shakespeare volume runs to some nine hundred pages, printed on decent paper, sold unbound at about fifteen shillings and bound at a pound or more, prices that placed it among the expensive books of the decade and aimed it at gentry and institutional libraries. Hinman's analysis of the surviving copies, of which rather more than two hundred are known, with the great Folger Shakespeare Library holding eighty-two, reconstructed a print run in the region of seven hundred and fifty copies, produced across roughly two years of presswork in 1622 and 1623. The book was a commercial speculation as much as a memorial, and its economics governed its copy: manuscripts were used where the syndicate had or could get them, and printed quartos were used where a quarto was what the syndicate owned, controlled or could most cheaply set.

The preliminaries built the author whose works the volume sold. Martin Droeshout's engraved portrait faces the title page, an image of a playwright seven years dead, engraved by an artist who can have had no sitting from life and whose plate became, through sheer placement, the face of Shakespeare for the world. Ben Jonson's verses opposite the portrait perform the volume's most quoted act of canonization, testifying that the engraver had a strife with nature to outdo the life, and commending the reader to look not on the picture but the book. Jonson's longer dedicatory poem inside supplies the epithets that hardened into the legend: the playwright as the soul of the age, the applause and wonder of the stage, a monument without a tomb, alive still while his book does live. Verses by Hugh Holland and Leonard Digges complete the frame, Digges pointing readers to the Stratford monument. The catalogue printed in the preliminaries sorts the thirty-six plays into Comedies, Histories and Tragedies, and it is that catalogue, not any theatrical practice, that fixes the generic address at which the play has been shelved ever since: Romeo and Juliet stands in the volume's order among the tragedies, its pages conventionally cited in modern scholarship by Charlton Hinman's through line numbering, the continuous count of the Folio's lines of type that lets a scholar cite any Folio reading by a single stable number. The apparatus of fame was complete before a line of the play was read: portrait, poems, catalogue, the names of the two fellow actors vouching for authenticity. Inside that frame, on the tragedies' pages, sat a text set from a three-and-sixpenny quarto's grandchild.

### How long did the Folio take to print, and where does the play fall in that labour?

The presswork occupied the Jaggard house across the better part of two years, from early 1622 into the autumn of 1623, interrupted and interleaved with the shop's other commissions. The play was set within that long labour from its quarto copy, by workmen whose Folio habits were already formed on the volume's earlier sheets.

The two years of presswork matter to the play's text because a book printed slowly, in stints, by several workmen, from copy delivered piecemeal, bears the marks of its own production at every opening. Hinman's method, the minute comparison of hundreds of copies to trace damaged types, spelling patterns and press corrections, showed the Folio to be the product of five compositors of differing experience, working from copy cast off in advance, with proof correction applied inconsistently, sometimes at press, sometimes not at all. For eighteen of the plays, that machinery worked on manuscripts, and its errors are the errors of misreading handwriting. For Romeo and Juliet it worked on a printed book, and its errors are of a different family: misreadings of type, substitutions of like-sounding words, omissions where the eye skipped between similar line endings in the quarto page. The distinction sounds technical and is in fact the whole story of the Folio text in miniature. Everything the Folio does to the play, good and ill, was done to a printed page by printers, and an editor reading its variants reads the habits of a printing house, not the mind of an author or the memory of a company.

## The Descent of the Copy: From the Second Quarto to the Folio

The chain that carried the play into the Folio begins in 1599, when the Second Quarto presented the tragedy to the book trade as a corrected and enlarged replacement for the short, corrupt First Quarto of 1597. The history of that foundation text is fully told in [The Second Quarto of 1599: The 'Good' Quarto](/2026/04/03/second-quarto-1599-good-quarto/), and the short reported text behind it in [The First Quarto of 1597: The 'Bad' Quarto](/2026/03/27/first-quarto-1597-bad-quarto/); the present study takes up the chain where those studies end, at the point where the Second Quarto's text begins its career as copy for further printings. That career ran in two channels. The first channel is the quarto line itself: the third quarto of 1609, printed for John Smethwick, reprinted the Second Quarto page for page; the edition of 1622, printed by John Norton for Smethwick, reprinted the third, adding the playwright's name to its title where its predecessors had been anonymous; and the fifth quarto of 1637, printed by R. Young for Smethwick, continued the line after the Folio had appeared. The second channel is the detour through the collected works, where the text was lifted out of the quarto succession and set again, enlarged by the Folio's divisions and dressed in the Folio's typography, to be reprinted in the Second Folio of 1632, the Third of 1663 and 1664, and the Fourth of 1685. The quarto line and the Folio line thereafter influenced the play's editing jointly, but they are not independent witnesses: both flow from the Second Quarto, the Folio line simply through more hands.

Naming the Folio's exact copy requires care, because the quarto line's members resemble one another as siblings resemble, and the evidence consists of small agreements in error rather than any document of the transaction. The Folio follows the third quarto's tradition in its substantive readings, departing from the Second Quarto where the third had already departed or where its own compositors strayed afresh. Greg, whose bibliographical history of the Folio remains the standard account of its copy, concluded that the printers set the play from the third quarto, an exemplar of the 1609 edition, and subsequent scholarship, including the collations behind Brian Gibbons's Arden edition and Jill Levenson's Oxford edition, has worked within that conclusion, allowing at most a glance at the fourth quarto as a supplementary source for a few readings. The 1622 quarto complicates the nomenclature in a way that delights bibliographers: printed two years before its traditional number suggests in some catalogues and long counted as the fourth quarto, it was in fact the third reprint, so that the edition the Folio's printers most plausibly also knew is Q4 by its date order and Q3's child by descent. Precision about the chain, Q2 to Q3 to F with Q4 standing as Q3's twin offspring rather than as the Folio's parent, is not pedantry. Every claim made for a Folio reading depends on where in that chain the reading entered, and a reading that entered in 1609 or 1623 belongs to a printing house, while a reading that stood in 1599 may belong to the playwright.

One further possibility has been canvassed and should be recorded with its proper weight: that the exemplar of the quarto used for the Folio was not a clean copy but an annotated one, marked up with occasional reference to a playhouse manuscript, so that some Folio variants might carry theatrical authority at second hand. The hypothesis is respectable in the abstract, since annotated quartos demonstrably served as copy for other Folio plays, and W. W. Greg himself allowed the general mechanism. For this play, the variants that would need to be explained by annotation are too few and too evenly matched by compositor error to require it, and the dominant pattern, agreement with the quarto line in error after error, points the other way. The judicious conclusion is the one the standard editions embody: the Folio was set from a quarto of the third quarto's line, unassisted by any manuscript whose readings can be identified, and its text is therefore a tertiary witness to the play, valuable exactly in proportion to the closeness with which its printers followed a copy whose own value is independently known. The quarto they followed survives; the Folio's deviations from it can be exhaustively listed; and a witness whose every deviation can be listed and explained has, by that fact, surrendered any claim to independent authority.

The economics of the syndicate made the quarto channel nearly inevitable for this title. John Smethwick, a member of the Folio syndicate, was the play's trade owner: the third quarto of 1609 had been printed for his shop, the 1622 quarto likewise, and the rights in the copy, in the period's sense of a stationer's property in a text, were his to contribute to the venture. A syndicate assembling thirty-six plays against a deadline and a budget had every reason to set the titles it already controlled from the printed stock it already held, reserving its manuscript-hunting for the unpublished plays that were the volume's commercial novelty. The Folio's Romeo and Juliet is thus not the product of a failed search for better copy; no search was needed, because the enterprise's logic classified the play from the outset as a reprint item, one of the known quantities whose quarto text the great book would dignify rather than replace. Bibliography and book-trade history converge on the same page: the chain Q2 to Q3 to F is what the syndicate's structure predicts, what the surviving variants confirm, and what the play's editors have ever since assumed.

The quarto line did not stop at the Folio's door, and its continuation sharpens the picture of two channels carrying one water. The fifth quarto of 1637 appeared fourteen years after the Folio, printed for Smethwick's successor interests, and it perpetuates the quarto tradition in its own right, a reminder that the playbook market and the library market ran in parallel rather than succeeding one another. Editors collate it as a late derivative of value chiefly for the history of the text's reception. The Folio line, meanwhile, passed through the Second Folio of 1632 and its successors, each a reprint of the 1623 text with a fresh accretion of compositorial change, so that a reader of 1685 met the tragedy at five removes from the Second Quarto, through Q3, F1, F2, F3 and F4 in succession. The stemma of the play, the family tree of its witnesses that textual scholars draw, shows the structure at a glance: one authoritative line from the author's papers to the Second Quarto, one reported line to the First Quarto, and thereafter a delta of reprints in which the Folio is merely the broadest channel. Every editorial decision about the play's text is a decision about position in that delta, and the Folio's position, broad and late and much traversed, is the one this study has described.

### Was the Folio text set from a manuscript or from a printed quarto?

From a printed quarto. The Folio inherits the Second Quarto's distinctive debris, including passages where the earlier printing preserved rival draft readings in an unsettled state, and it reproduces errors that arose when the third quarto reprinted the second. No pattern of independent manuscript readings appears anywhere in its pages.

The copy laid before the Jaggard compositors was a book from the same trade line as the text it printed, a quarto descended from the edition of 1599 through the reprint of 1609. The distinction between printed and manuscript copy is visible in the texture of the Folio's errors, and it repays a closer look, because the same test applied to other plays in the volume returns the opposite answer and thereby validates the method. Where the Folio prints a play from manuscript, its text typically shows expansions, contractions and verbal divergences from any printed predecessor that follow the grain of a written document behind it, and stage directions of a fullness that bespeaks the playhouse or the author's papers. Where it prints from a quarto, the verbal fabric matches the quarto's fabric thread for thread, and the divergences are of the mechanical kinds: a word misread through the similarity of letterforms in the quarto's type, a phrase adjusted to justify a line, a speech heading expanded or contracted to fit the Folio's narrower measure, since the Folio's pages set the play in double columns of a smaller type area than the quarto's single broad page. Romeo and Juliet matches the second pattern at every point of the collation. Its Folio page is the quarto's page re-imposed, and its differences from the quarto are the differences that re-imposition, re-setting and hasty proofing explain without remainder. Bibliographical analysis rarely achieves certainty, but on this play it comes close: the chain Q2, Q3, F is as well established as any fact in the play's documentary history.

## How the Descent Was Proved: Shared Error and the Bibliographer's Method

The chain asserted throughout this study rests on a method worth stating in the open, because the method is what separates the conclusion from an opinion. Textual descent is proved by shared error. Where two witnesses share a truth, the agreement proves nothing, since either might have reached the truth independently; where they share a distinctive mistake, a word gone wrong in a way no two workmen would independently contrive, descent or contamination is the only economical explanation, and the direction of descent follows from which witness can be shown to have generated the error and which merely inherited it. The Folio's relation to the quartos was established by exactly this logic, applied at scale. A. W. Pollard's Shakespeare Folios and Quartos (1909) first sorted the early editions into their families and cleared the good quartos of the piracy charge the Folio's own preface had fastened on them; Greg's bibliographical history then mapped the Folio's copy play by play, showing for the present tragedy a pattern of agreements with the third quarto's line in readings where that line departs from the Second Quarto, including departures that are errors by any standard. The pattern is the proof. A Folio set from manuscript would agree with the quartos in truth and differ in its own way; the actual Folio agrees with one branch of the quarto family in that branch's identifiable mistakes, and no hypothesis but reprinting explains the distribution.

The method's second instrument is the analysis of the physical book, and its application to this play has already appeared in the account of the compositors. Spelling clusters, type damage and press variants allow the scholar to reconstruct the order and agency of production inside the Jaggard house, and that reconstruction cross-checks the stemma from an independent direction: the men who set the play can be watched, stint by stint, behaving as men behave when the copy before them is print, misreading letterforms, expanding contractions to fill a measure, adjusting headings to the column. Nothing in their behaviour resembles the handling of a difficult hand, the hesitations, expansions and misreadings of secretary script that mark the Folio's manuscript-derived pages elsewhere in the volume. Two independent methods, the logic of shared error and the archaeology of the press, converge on one conclusion, and convergence of independent methods is as close to demonstration as historical scholarship comes. It is worth adding that the conclusion has survived its severest tests: the editors who most wished the Folio well, those who have adopted its readings at individual points, have adopted them as corrections within a Second Quarto text, thereby conceding the descent their adoptions presuppose.

A corollary of the method disciplines every claim made anywhere in this study, and readers should hold it in reserve for the questions that close the article. Because the Folio's copy was print, and print of a known edition in a known state, the Folio's evidence can never outweigh its copy's evidence about the words; it can only, at best, preserve a state of the copy's page that the surviving exemplars of the quarto happen to have lost, or embody a corrector's lucky repair. Claims that the Folio preserves the play as performed, or as the author finally wished it, or as the company treasured it, all require a channel between the playhouse or the study and the Jaggard frames that the shared errors prove did not exist for this title. The romance of the great book dissolves under the method, and what remains is more interesting than the romance: a fully documented case of how a literary classic was manufactured, in which every step from the author's papers to the monument can be assigned to its agent, its date and its instrument.

## Compositors B and E: The Hands in the Type

A printed text is the residue of particular workmen's days, and for the Folio those workmen have been identified with a precision that transforms the reading of its variants. Hinman's analysis distinguished five compositors, lettered A through E, whose stints can be told apart by their spelling habits, their treatment of speech headings and stage directions, and the recurrence of individual damaged types in the pages each man set. Compositor B, the most experienced and the most interventionist of the group, set the largest share of the volume and the whole of Romeo and Juliet's later portion in Hinman's allocation; Compositor E, identified as an apprentice in the shop, set the play's opening portion and betrays his inexperience in every column. The stints meet within the play, and the seam matters, because the two men handled their quarto copy with opposite temperaments. B read his copy with the confidence of a journeyman who expected to improve it, modernizing spellings, regularizing headings and silently adjusting readings he took to be errors, some of which were nothing of the kind. E followed his copy letter by letter where he understood it and garbled it where he did not, producing blunders of a crudeness that B's pages seldom show. An editor weighing a Folio variant must therefore first ask whose page it stands on: a B variant may be a deliberate correction, wrong or right; an E variant is likelier to be an accident of the type case and the eye.

The identification of the two men's work rests on evidence of a charm peculiar to analytical bibliography. Early modern compositors spelled common words according to habits as personal as handwriting: the distribution of spellings such as do and doe, go and goe, here and heere across the Folio's pages clusters into stints that match the physical evidence of type damage and presswork. Hinman plotted those clusters for the whole volume, and the tragedy divides along them cleanly, E taking the opening pages of the play and B the rest, a division that also explains the play's uneven texture, its opening columns being markedly more corrupt than its close. E's signature is visible in the kind of error no experienced man makes twice, the substitution of a real word for the copy's quite different word under the pressure of setting by small memorized phrases rather than by continuous sense. Charlton Hinman's work has been refined since 1963, and some attributions at the margins have shifted, Paul Werstine and others having counselled caution about building editorial arguments on compositor identification alone. The caution is healthy and the play's main division of labour survives it: whoever exactly set the opening pages set them badly, and the Folio's Romeo and Juliet begins in a fog of small corruptions from which its later pages partly clear.

A single passage shows the stakes. In Act 5, Scene 3, Capulet, arriving at the tomb and seeing his daughter bleed afresh, speaks one of the play's most quoted sentences of parental recognition: "O heavens! O wife, look how our daughter bleeds!" In the Second Quarto the sentence stands entire. The Folio's handling of the scene's prefixes and lines in this stretch is characteristically compressive, and collators from the eighteenth century onward have used this scene to demonstrate how the derivative text, pressed by its compositors into the column, trims and flattens the copy's contours. The more instructive case stands earlier, in the scene of the lovers' parting at dawn, where the Second Quarto preserves the famous duplicated lines, the fossil of the author's revision, and where the Folio simply reprints the fossil without comment or repair. A compositor working from a promptbook, or an editor working from any document with theatrical use behind it, would have been forced by the needs of performance to choose between the rival versions. The Folio's men were under no such compulsion, because their copy was a book to be reproduced, not a script to be played from, and their fidelity to its oddities is the surest internal evidence of what lay on their frames. The point generalizes across the play: wherever the quarto text is difficult, the Folio either reproduces the difficulty or blurs it, and it nowhere dissolves a difficulty by recourse to knowledge the quarto lacks. That is the behaviour of a reprint, and editors from Capell's generation to Levenson's have treated it accordingly.

### How can scholars tell which compositor set a given Folio page?

By habits too small for the workmen themselves to have noticed. Each compositor favoured particular spellings of common words, particular forms of speech headings, and particular ways of spacing and justifying lines, and those preferences cluster across consecutive pages into stints. Recurring damaged letters confirm the stints the spellings reveal.

By mapping the clusters against the physical evidence of the presswork, Hinman assigned the Folio's pages to five men, and the assignments let editors weigh a variant by the reliability of the hands that set it. Compositor study also disciplines the editor's imagination in a second way, by attaching a rate of error to the Folio's testimony. A witness whose habits are known can be cross-examined: B's tendency to modernize means that a Folio reading in modern dress proves nothing about the copy, since B dressed readings as he went; E's tendency to substitute means that a Folio reading in the opening pages that flatly contradicts the quarto's sense carries the presumption of blunder. The Folio thus becomes, under analysis, not one witness but a panel of witnesses of unequal credit, deposing about a copy that is itself extant. The courtroom metaphor is exact. When the original document survives, the depositions matter only where the document is damaged or ambiguous, and the play's copy-text situation is precisely that happy case: the Second Quarto survives in good copies, and the Folio's depositions are needed only at the points where the Second Quarto itself falters. Those points are the subject of the sections that follow, after a necessary account of the physical process that produced every one of them.

## Reading the Folio Page: The Look of the Tragedy in the Great Book

A reader who turns to the play in a surviving Folio meets a page unlike any modern edition, and the page's furniture quietly instructs the reader in the volume's claims. The text stands in two columns of roman type, the tragedy's title carried in the headline across each opening, the speech headings set off in a contrasting face, the stage directions in italic, the whole ruled and ordered with a regularity the quartos never attempt. Where a quarto page looks like a pamphlet, hasty and expendable, a Folio page looks like a chronicle or a statute book, designed for consultation across centuries. Act openings receive a decorated initial letter, so that the architecture the volume imposed on the play is visible as ornament before it is legible as structure. The visual argument precedes the textual one: before a word is read, the page announces that the matter in the columns is literature of record, the kind of writing a library keeps. For a play whose Folio text was set from a pamphlet, the transformation of dress is the transformation that mattered, since it was the dress, far more than the readings, that persuaded the succeeding century to shelve the tragedy among the classics and to take the volume's version of the words as the authorized one.

The page also preserves, for eyes trained to see it, the archaeology of its own making. Catchwords at the foot of pages await their matching first words overleaf; the signatures of the gatherings stand in the lower margins; the columns vary almost imperceptibly in their crowding as the compositors managed their cast-off stints; and at the points where press correction intervened, two surviving copies of the volume will differ in a word or a turned letter, so that the Folio text resolves, under the bibliographer's lens, into the small constellation of states described in the following section. None of this was visible to the book's first readers as evidence; it was simply the texture of a fine book. Scholarship learned to read the texture as a document, and the reading reversed the page's own argument: the furniture that proclaimed permanence became the means of proving derivation, as the type damage, the spelling clusters and the correction states testified, against the title page's promise, that the words in the columns had come out of a stationer's quarto by way of a printing house's ordinary labour.

## Inside the Printing House: Casting Off, Proof-Reading and the Unidentical Copies

The Folio was not printed as a reader encounters it, page following page in order, but as sheets: large leaves printed on both sides, folded once, and gathered at the end into the bound volume. Before a line could be set, the copy for each play had to be cast off, that is, measured and divided among the compositors by estimating how much printed copy would fill how much Folio page, since men setting different parts of the same play worked simultaneously on different sheets. Casting off a quarto to fill folio columns was arithmetic performed on a text with its own layout, and where the estimate ran wrong, the compositors had to compress or stretch their setting to meet the sheet's boundary, squeezing lines together or spreading them with extra spacing. Some of the Folio text's local oddities in this play, the compressed prefixes and tightened passages of the tomb scene among them, are the visible scars of that arithmetic. The copy the men worked from was therefore not merely a quarto; it was a quarto pre-divided into stints marked by the caster-off's hand, a working tool of the shop, and its pre-division is one more mediation between the play and the page.

Proof-reading in the Jaggard house was real but intermittent, and its intermittency is written into every surviving copy of the volume. Correction happened at the press: a sheet would be read, errors marked, the type adjusted, and printing resumed, while the sheets already printed with the uncorrected state of the type went into the heap with the rest, to be gathered indifferently into copies. The consequence, demonstrated by Hinman's collation of copy after copy, is that no two First Folios are exactly alike, and the Folio text of Romeo and Juliet exists not as a single state but as a small family of states, differing at the points where press correction intervened between the printing of one sheet and its next impression. Where the corrector caught a blunder, later-printed sheets carry the repair and earlier sheets carry the error, and both circulate under the same date and imprint. Scholarship's siglum F therefore conceals a plurality, which is why rigorous citation specifies, where it matters, the state of the reading and the copy consulted, and why Hinman's through line numbering, fixed to the ideal page, was such a liberation: it gave the family a single address system despite its members' differences.

For this play the corrected points are few, and their fewness is informative. A text set from a manuscript of any difficulty generates corrections wherever the corrector puzzled over the hand; a text set from a clean printed quarto generates few, because the copy was legible and the errors were of the kind that only collation against sense, not against copy, would catch. The Jaggard correctors worked against the copy, checking that the type matched the page before them, a procedure that catches literals and turned letters but passes sense-errors faithfully, since the sense-error also matches, in the compositor's phrase, what the eye expected. The Folio's Romeo and Juliet accordingly preserves its compositors' blunders with a fidelity that manuscripts would not have afforded: the proofing system was designed to reproduce the copy, and the tragedy it reproduced included, embedded in the copy's own descent and freshly added by its own workmen, every error the system had no mandate to remove. An editor who understands the presswork stops asking why the Folio failed to correct the quarto tradition and starts asking the only question the evidence supports, which is how faithfully, sheet by sheet and stint by stint, the reproduction was executed. Hinman's answer for the volume as a whole, painstaking reproduction under commercial pressure with uneven skill, is the answer the play's pages give as well.

## What the Folio Changes: Variants Against the Second Quarto

Collation of the Folio against the Second Quarto yields a harvest of differences numbering in the hundreds, the great majority of them accidental in the technical sense: spellings, punctuation, capitalization, the expansion or contraction of speech headings, the handling of verse lining where prose and verse crowd the column. These accidentals are not negligible, since punctuation steers delivery and lining steers interpretation, but they carry no authority, for they reflect the Folio printing house's conventions rather than any document behind the quarto. The substantive differences, the places where the Folio prints a different word or words with a different sense, are far fewer, and they divide into families that an editor learns to recognize on sight. There are modernizations, where B substitutes the current form for the copy's older one and sense survives intact. There are trivializations, where a compositor replaces a harder or less familiar reading with the phrase that first came to hand, flattening the copy's precision. There are press corrections, where a proof-reader at the Jaggard house caught an obvious quarto error and set it right, giving the Folio a handful of its genuinely useful readings. And there are the plain blunders, concentrated in E's stint, where the Folio's word makes worse sense or none and survives only to be bracketed in a collation note.

The blunders are individually small, and their collective shape matters more than any single specimen. In the long tomb scene of Act 5, Scene 3, the Folio's text drifts from its copy in a scatter of substitutions, transpositions and compressed speech headings, each trivial alone, together forming the pattern collators recognize as high-speed resetting: a compositor holding a phrase of the quarto in memory and setting its gist rather than its letters. The opening pages set by the apprentice hand show the same drift in coarser form, misreadings of the quarto's words that no one corrected at press. What no Folio blunder in the play ever does is equally diagnostic: none imports a reading from outside the quarto tradition, none agrees with the First Quarto against the Second in a way that would suggest theatrical copy, and none touches the play's substance beyond the sentence in which it stands. The general truth the pattern illustrates is the one this study has pressed from the start: the Folio's divergences are events in a printing house, datable to 1622 and 1623, attributable to identifiable workmen, and explicable by the mechanics of setting type from a printed page under time pressure. They are not transmissions from the playhouse or from the author's papers.

Against that background, the Folio's useful readings deserve their precise due, neither inflated nor denied. At a small number of points the Second Quarto's text is damaged, ambiguous or manifestly astray, and the Folio prints the word the sense requires. Some of those rescues are press corrections, the Jaggard corrector repairing his copy's obvious slips; some may descend from the third quarto's own attempts at repair, and a very few may embody correction against another quarto impression within the shop. Whatever their route, the readings enter modern editions on their merits, adopted one by one with the Folio's siglum in the collation, never wholesale. The modern text of the play is an eclectic construction on a Second Quarto base, and the Folio's contribution to it resembles the contribution of a diligent early reader whose marginal guesses occasionally hit the mark: consulted everywhere, obeyed rarely, and never mistaken for the author. That is the accurate measure of the Folio text's critical value, and it stands in instructive contrast to the volume's cultural value, which is immeasurable. The remaining sections take up the two areas where the Folio's contribution exceeds the occasional lucky correction: the structure it imposed on the play, and the afterlife it guaranteed.

### How many of the Folio's differences from the Second Quarto actually change the sense?

A small minority. The collation runs to hundreds of entries, but the overwhelming share are accidentals: spellings, punctuation, capitalization, expanded or compressed speech headings, and lining adjusted to the Folio's narrower columns. The substantive differences, where a different word carries a different sense, number in the dozens, and most are small substitutions within a single sentence.

A second family of Folio readings deserves its own paragraph, the stage directions, because here the derivative text occasionally outruns its copy in explicitness and has misled the unwary into crediting it with playhouse knowledge. The Folio's directions are fuller than the Second Quarto's at a scattering of points, specifying entries, groupings and properties with a tidiness the quarto's terser directions lack. Tidiness, however, is a printing-house virtue as much as a theatrical one: B and his fellow compositors worked in a shop that was regularizing directions across the whole volume, and the Folio's expansions elsewhere in the canon demonstrably arise from the compositors' and correctors' tidying rather than from prompt copy. For this play the directions add no business that the dialogue does not already imply, record no property or movement beyond the quarto's implication, and settle no staging question the quarto leaves open. They are garnish on the reprint, attractive on the page and empty as evidence, and the history of editing bears out the assessment: the directions editors print for the play are the Second Quarto's, supplemented sparingly, while the Folio's ornaments live in the collation notes where curiosities belong.

## The Accidentals: Punctuation and Spelling as Printing-House Weather

Beneath the substantives lies the stratum editors call accidentals, and for a derivative text the stratum is where the Folio's true character shows most continuously. Every page of the Folio's Romeo and Juliet respells and repunctuates its copy: the quarto's spellings give way to the preferences of B and E, its capitals settle into the Folio house pattern, its contractions expand or contract to the column's needs. None of this carries authority, since none of it descends from any document but the quarto itself, but all of it conditions how the text strikes a reader, and the history of the play's editing includes a long argument about how much of it to keep. Percy Simpson's Shakespearian Punctuation (1911) mounted the most influential case ever made for the eloquence of early punctuation, arguing that the pointing of the early editions, the quartos' especially, was rhetorical rather than grammatical, a score for the speaking voice marking breath and emphasis where a modern comma marks syntax. Simpson's case was built chiefly on the good quartos and the manuscript-derived Folio texts, and its implication for the present play runs against the Folio with unusual neatness: if pointing is evidence, it is the Second Quarto's pointing, standing near the author's papers, that deserves an editor's reverence, while the Folio's repunctuation is the record of two compositors re-scoring a text for their own habits of reading. Later scholarship has qualified Simpson's system, treating early pointing as a mixture of authorial, scribal and compositorial practice varying from text to text, and the qualification only sharpens the local verdict. In a Folio text set from print, the pointing is compositorial by definition, and the modern editor who follows the Second Quarto for accidentals, modernizing its spellings while respecting its rhetorical pointing where the sense allows, is applying to the stratum the same law of descent that governs the words.

The verse lining belongs to the same analysis. The Second Quarto prints the tragedy's prose as prose and its verse as verse with the rough fidelity of copy close to a working dramatist's habits, while the Folio, setting the same matter in narrower columns under cast-off pressure, occasionally redivides lines, runs verse together, or breaks prose into lengths that mimic verse. Editors since the eighteenth century have silently repaired the Folio's lining from the quarto's, and actors, who learn the play's rhythms from modern editions, speak the Second Quarto's lineation while believing they speak the Folio's play. The point deserves emphasis because it completes the inventory of the derivative text's dependence: not only the words but the very shape of the verse on the page, the unit in which the poetry is memorized and quoted, descends in the modern book from 1599, and the Folio's contribution even here is a set of small disturbances that scholarship has spent three centuries ironing out.

## The Gift of Division: Acts, Scenes and the Shape of Reading

Against the Folio's failures of transmission stands its single structural triumph, the gift that every reader of the play receives from the 1623 volume without knowing its source. The quartos print the tragedy as a continuous stream of dialogue, articulated by entrances and exits but innocent of the act and scene architecture in which the play is universally read, taught and cited. The Folio divides the stream. Its pages mark five acts, and within the acts a sequence of scenes, each headed and numbered, so that the tragedy acquires the skeleton of "Act 3, Scene 1" and "Act 2, Scene 2" by which the world refers to its parts. The full history of that architecture, from classical precedent to the modern edition, belongs to [Who Divided the Play Into Acts and Scenes?](/2026/05/29/act-and-scene-divisions-history/); the Folio's own contribution can be stated compactly. The divisions were imposed in the printing house or in the preparation of the quarto copy for the press, by an agent whose identity is unknown and whose work was competent in outline and careless in detail. Acts in the Folio follow the play's broad movements with reasonable fidelity, and the scene divisions generally observe the classical rule of a cleared stage, but the execution slips in ways that betray its workmanship. The great tomb scene of the finale is cut in two, a new scene opening in the middle of the churchyard action where no clearing of the stage justifies it, and several genuine breaks elsewhere go unmarked. The pattern is that of a workman dividing a printed text by eye and by rule of thumb, not of a playhouse document recording how the play was actually staged.

The consequences of the division outran its workmanship. Nicholas Rowe, editing the plays in 1709, took the Folio's grid as the frame of the work itself, regularized its scene headings, added locations to them, and bequeathed the arrangement to every edition after; the subsequent history traced in [Editing Romeo and Juliet: Rowe to Malone](/2026/06/05/editing-rowe-to-malone-history/) is in part the history of editors refining a skeleton the Folio supplied. Modern editions still print the play in the Folio's five acts, correct its scene joints where the joints are plainly wrong, and cite its passages by the coordinates the division created. The gift is real and it is also a warning, because a structure imposed in 1623 by an unknown hand has acquired the authority of the author's design in the minds of readers who meet the play in no other shape. Generations of criticism have reasoned about the tragedy's five-act form, its symmetries and its turning points, as if the form descended from the playwright's desk, when the documentary record shows a play written for continuous performance in two hours' traffic, articulated by its author through entrances, exits and the two Chorus speeches, and articulated by its first collected edition into the five boxes posterity mistakes for its anatomy. The Folio's most influential reading of Romeo and Juliet is the one it never wrote a word of: the shape on the page.

### Did Shakespeare compose the play in the five-act shape the Folio prints?

The five-act shape belongs to the book, not to the composition. The quartos, which stand closer to the play as written and first staged, print the action continuously, and nothing in their pages marks act intervals. The play was built for the continuous two hours' traffic its own Prologue advertises.

Its movements are articulated by entrances, exits and Chorus speeches rather than by act breaks. Honest assessment requires the counterweight to be stated, since the five-act idea was not invented in 1623. Roman drama had canonized the five-act norm, Horace had prescribed it, and the academic and courtly stages of the sixteenth century observed it; the public playhouse of the 1590s, performing without intervals in its early years, had less use for the grid, and the adoption of act intervals at the indoor playhouses belongs to the following reign. The Folio's divisions for the early plays therefore represent a Jacobean regularization of Elizabethan material, the book trade dressing the repertory of the 1590s in the architectural taste of 1623. For this play the regularization is benign in most places and violent in one, the bisection of the tomb scene, where the printed break bisects continuous action and every modern edition silently accepts or quietly repairs the wound. The scene division that matters most to classroom citation, the separation of the balcony scene as Act 2, Scene 2, is Folio work that happens to match the action's own articulation; the division that matters most to scholarship is the one that does not. Between those poles the whole gift sits, indispensable, imperfect, and, in the strictest sense, an editorial interpolation on a monumental scale, executed in type by workmen whose names the division does not record.

One historical irony completes the division's story. Within a few years of the play's first performances, London's indoor playhouses were normalizing the act interval, with music between the acts, and the King's Men themselves, at the Blackfriars after 1608, played in a house where such intervals were expected. The five-act grid the Folio imposed on the tragedy of the 1590s thus anticipated the conditions under which the company itself would later perform, and the volume can be read as regularizing the repertory not merely for the library but for a stage practice that had caught up with the classical model. The regularization remains an imposition on the text as composed, and editors are right to treat the divisions as the Folio's rather than the author's; but the imposition was not vandalism. It was the book trade and the theatre converging on the same architecture from opposite directions, and the play's subsequent life in five acts, on the page and in the playhouse alike, is the joint product of that convergence.

## The Missing Chorus and the Promptbook Question

One Folio departure from its copy is large enough to have generated a theory, and the theory tests the whole account of the Folio's descent. The Second Quarto prints two Chorus speeches, the sonnet Prologue and the second sonnet that opens the action after the feast, meditating on desire's delay: "Now old desire doth in his deathbed lie, / And young affection gapes to be his heir" (Chorus, Act 2 prologue, lines 1-2, in the Second Quarto's text). The Folio prints the first speech and drops the second entirely. An omission of fourteen lines is a different order of event from a substituted word, and scholars have asked what kind of copy loses exactly such a passage. One answer proposes that the Folio's exemplar, though a quarto, had been annotated or supplemented from a promptbook in which the second Chorus stood cancelled, cut in performance as companies commonly cut prologues and choruses once a play's novelty faded. Another answer, favoured by the bibliographical evidence as a whole, treats the omission as one more accident of transmission, whether a compositor's or a marker's eye passing over a page boundary in the exemplar, or a deliberate abridgement in the printing house to save space in a volume whose cast-off copy was running long. The question cannot be settled by the passage alone, and its scholarly interest lies in the discipline it imposes: a single omission, however suggestive, cannot carry the weight of reclassifying the Folio's copy as theatrical when the play's hundreds of other agreements bind that copy to the quarto page.

The Folio's treatment of the first Chorus speech is the necessary companion evidence. The opening sonnet the Folio retains, heading its speaker as the quartos do and printing the fourteen lines with the usual freight of compositorial respelling and repunctuation, but entire. A copy marked for the stage by a company that had dropped the second Chorus might equally have dropped the first, which shares its function and its speaker; a copy abridged by a printing house pressed for room might drop either. The pattern of retention, one speech kept whole and its twin cleanly absent, fits a mechanical or casting accident at least as comfortably as a theatrical cancellation, and it fits the rest of the Folio's behaviour in the play far better. Modern editions register the case exactly so: the second Chorus stands in the text, drawn from the Second Quarto, its absence from the Folio noted without romance.

The verdict of this study is that the omission is best explained without the promptbook. Performance copies of the period lose far more than a Chorus when they are marked for the stage: they adjust entrances, cut lines throughout, and annotate business, and the Folio shows no accompanying pattern of such intervention anywhere in the play. What the Folio shows, everywhere except at this one passage, is a quarto reprinted with quarto fidelity and quarto error. The economical reading treats the missing sonnet as the largest of the Folio's accidents rather than the sole survivor of a different kind of copy, and the standard editions encode exactly that judgement when they print the second Chorus from the Second Quarto without hesitation and record the Folio's silence in a note. The episode is worth its section because it demonstrates the method by which derivative texts are kept honest: every anomaly is weighed against the mass of the evidence, and the mass, in this play, is not in doubt.

## Why Editors Still Build on the Second Quarto

The editorial order of battle for this play was settled once the chain of descent was proved, and it has not been seriously disturbed since. W. W. Greg's theory of copy-text, articulated in his 1950 British Academy lecture "The Rationale of Copy-Text," taught editors to distinguish the authority a witness holds over a work's accidentals, the spelling and punctuation that follow the copy-text, from the judgement to be exercised over its substantives, word by word. Applied to Romeo and Juliet, the theory ratifies what the collations had already shown: the copy-text is the Second Quarto, the early witness closest to the author's papers, and the Folio enters the edition as a source of conjectural corrections where the copy-text is defective, each adoption argued on the reading's merits and recorded in the apparatus. George Walton Williams, editing the tragedy for the Pelican Shakespeare, and Brian Gibbons for the Arden, built their texts on that foundation; Jill Levenson's Oxford edition, the most exhaustively documented of the modern texts, collates the Folio throughout and adopts from it sparingly, printing its few persuasive corrections while refusing its hundreds of refashionings. René Weis's Arden edition follows the same order. The unanimity is itself evidence: scholars who agree on little else about the play agree that its words descend from 1599 and that 1623 is a commentator upon them, not a rival source.

The disagreement the brief for this study requires is genuine, and it concerns the Folio's margin of credit rather than its rank. Greg's own assessment of Folio texts printed from quartos was austere: such texts, in his phrase for the class, could claim no authority independent of their copy, and their variants stood condemned in advance as the corruptions of reprinting unless a reading commended itself as an obvious correction of an obvious error. Fredson Bowers, Greg's great American ally and the systematizer of the copy-text method, allowed the Folio's correctors a somewhat freer hand in principle, treating press correction in the Jaggard house as a process that might embody genuine editorial intelligence drawn from the trade's accumulated knowledge of the texts. The practical difference is narrow but real: on Greg's austere view, a Folio variant at a point where the Second Quarto makes tolerable sense is dead on arrival; on the more permissive view, such a variant earns a hearing as a possible sophistication worth weighing. This study adjudicates the disagreement in Greg's favour, with a qualification. The compositor and corrector evidence assembled by Hinman shows a printing house working at speed, correcting sporadically and without collation against any superior document, so that its interventions deserve no presumption of intelligence; the presumption should run the other way, and a Folio reading should be adopted only where the Second Quarto is manifestly wrong and the Folio manifestly right. The qualification is that manifest rightness does occur, a handful of times in the play, because even hasty correctors repair obvious nonsense, and an editor who refused those repairs on principle would be printing known error for doctrine's sake. Levenson's practice embodies exactly that adjudication, and the play's modern text is the better for it.

### If the Folio is derivative, why do editions still print some of its readings?

Because derivation condemns a witness's presumption, not its every word. The Folio's printers worked from a good quarto and corrected it at press in places; where the Second Quarto is manifestly astray and the Folio's word restores the sense, an editor adopts the Folio reading as the best available repair.

The adoption is recorded in the collation, and the Second Quarto remains copy-text everywhere else. The settlement was reached the hard way, against a century in which the Folio's prestige had inverted the true order. Rowe edited from the Fourth Folio, the tradition's most distant member. Pope, in 1725, printed the quartos' readings as degradations and relegated matter he disliked to the foot of the page, treating the collected volume as the playwright's authentic monument and the playbooks as the players' corruptions of it. Lewis Theobald's Shakespeare Restored of 1726 began the reversal, demonstrating by collation that the Folio tradition itself was corrupt at points where the quartos preserved sense, and Capell's mid-century edition, the first built on systematic collation of the early copies, restored the Second Quarto's priority for this play in practice. The New Bibliography of the early twentieth century supplied the theory and the proof: A. W. Pollard's rehabilitation of the good quartos as a class, Greg's descent analysis for the Folio's copy, and Hinman's presswork together converted Capell's practical instinct into demonstrated bibliography. The Greg-versus-permissive adjudication above is thus the final form of an argument three centuries long, and its outcome, the Second Quarto enthroned and the Folio pensioned as an honoured corrector, is among the most secure results in the play's scholarship.

A reader who grasps that settlement grasps the play's whole textual politics. Authority flows downhill from the fountainhead through channels that can be mapped, and the editor's task is hydrography: to know at every point which water is being drawn. The Folio in that map is a broad, navigable, much-travelled reach of the river, downstream of narrower and purer streams, celebrated because the river's traffic passes along it and consulted with the caution due to any water that has passed through many hands. The map also explains the persistence of the opposite myth. Readers meet the play first in the Folio's architecture and often in Folio-derived texts, and the channel through which a work arrives acquires, in the imagination, the dignity of its source. Scholarship's correction of that instinct is not antiquarian spite; the assignment of readings to their true origins decides, at hundreds of small points and a few large ones, what the play actually says.

## The Folio as Monument: How a Derivative Text Carried the Play Down the Centuries

The afterlife reverses the hierarchy one final time, and the reversal is the reason the derivative text deserves its own study rather than a footnote in the Second Quarto's. The quarto line, for all its authority, ran in cheap playbooks printed for the trade, read hard, and discarded; the First Quarto survives in two copies, the Second in a handful, and had the play's text depended on the quartos alone, its survival into an age of libraries would have hung on those few fragile gatherings. The Folio changed the odds categorically. A large, expensive, library-bound book in seven hundred and fifty copies does not vanish as playbooks vanish, and more than two hundred Folios survive, the largest single reservoir of the play's early text in existence, shelved across the world's collections from the Folger's eighty-two copies outward. Through the seventeenth century the collected volume, in its second, third and fourth impressions, was the form in which the play was owned, read and quoted; the quartos' fifth and last printing of 1637 marks the end of the playbook line, after which the Folio text, errors and architecture and all, was simply the play for readers until the eighteenth-century editors re-collated the early editions and slowly restored the Second Quarto's priority. Garrick's audiences in the following century still met a tragedy descended from the Folio tradition, its catastrophe softened and its tomb scene rewritten, and the scholarly recovery of the quarto text, from Capell's pioneering collations through the New Bibliography of Greg, Pollard and Hinman, is a story of the following centuries reversing the seventeenth's settlement.

The Folio succession itself worked steadily against the recovery, because each successive Folio reprinted the last with the serene confidence of a trade that believed it was improving a classic. The Second Folio of 1632, printed by Thomas Cotes for Robert Allot and his partners, reset the whole volume page for page from the First, its compositors modernizing spellings, smoothing punctuation and correcting, by their own lights, hundreds of readings across the canon; its Romeo and Juliet is the 1623 text at one more remove, carrying the First Folio's quarto-derived errors plus a fresh layer of 1632 housekeeping, and prefaced, in a fitting emblem of the volume's cultural ascendancy, by the young John Milton's first published poem, an epitaph on the playwright that the monument had by then made monumental. The Third Folio of 1663, reissued in 1664 with Pericles and six spurious plays added, and the Fourth of 1685, continued the line, each enlarging the book while diluting the text. By the Fourth Folio the play stood at the end of a chain of six printings from the Second Quarto, and the text the Restoration and early Augustan stage inherited, the text Garrick still substantially worked from, was the Folio tradition's text, its act divisions canonical, its errors venerable, its quarto original half forgotten in the trade that had supplanted it. The ark had not merely preserved the play; it had replaced it, and the editors' recovery of 1599 had to begin by dethroning the book that had kept the play alive. That recovery is the subject of the editing history linked earlier in this study; its precondition is the fact established here, that there was a monumental text to recover from, preserved by the very qualities, bulk, price and prestige, that made the Folio a bad witness and a magnificent ark.

### Would the play have survived at all without the First Folio?

Probably, since the quartos also carry the text and two copies of the short First Quarto alone might have preserved a version of the tragedy. But survival as a classic, continuously owned, shelved and reprinted through the seventeenth century, was the Folio's doing. The quartos were ephemera; the collected volume was furniture for libraries, and furniture endures where ephemera perish.

Jonson, who wrote the volume's great commendatory poem, understood what the book was for more clearly than its publishers' preface admitted. His lines make no claim that the Folio's texts are pure; the claim is that the book is a monument, that the playwright who had no tomb and asked for none had raised himself one in print, alive as long as the book lived and had lovers of his work to keep it alive. As bibliography the poem is no evidence at all. As a prediction it was exact. The monument preserved the play's name, fame and broad text through the century when both might have slipped into the condition of the lost plays of the period, known by title alone; and when scholarship at last returned to the quartos to recover the words in their purest early form, the quartos were recoverable partly because the Folio's celebrity had made everything bearing the playwright's name worth a collector's keeping. The derivative text thus stands in a relation to its betters that posterity understands instinctively and bibliography must state plainly: the Folio's Romeo and Juliet is a corrupted copy of a good text, bound in the book that ensured the good text would still exist to be preferred over it. The cliché that the Folio is the source of the play is wrong in the direction of its flow and right in its instinct for where the play's survival was decided. Restoring the chain, quarto to quarto to Folio, gives the volume its true office: not the spring of the text, but its reservoir.

## The Contrast with the Other Folio Plays: What Manuscripts Gave the Neighbours

The surest way to feel the Folio's poverty for this play is to set it beside the riches the same volume brought to others. Eighteen of the thirty-six plays had never been printed before 1623, and for those the Folio is not a witness among others but the sole early authority, the only bridge between the playwright's company and posterity: Macbeth, Twelfth Night, The Tempest, Antony and Cleopatra and Julius Caesar survive because the syndicate's manuscripts reached the Jaggard house, and had the volume failed, those plays would stand where the lost Cardenio stands, as titles in other men's records. Among the previously printed plays, several gained substantially from the Folio's copy. The Folio Hamlet prints passages absent from the Second Quarto of that play and lacks others the quarto prints, the mark of a theatrical text independent of the quarto and indispensable to any complete edition. The Folio Othello adds lines beyond its quarto, including fuller matter in the willow song scene, and preserves readings that editors weigh as equals against the quarto's. The Folio King Lear differs from its quarto so extensively that modern scholarship prints the two as distinct versions of the work, the Folio text plausibly shaped by revision. In each case the Folio's value springs from the same source: copy that came from manuscript, from the playhouse or the author's papers, carrying information the printed tradition lacked.

Romeo and Juliet received none of that bounty, and the contrast is structural rather than accidental. The plays that gained from the Folio were plays whose copy the syndicate had to seek, in the company's archives or the scriveners' shops, because the trade's printed stock was absent, defective or not theirs to use. The tragedy of Verona was a trade property in good standing, its quarto text current, its rights held inside the syndicate itself, and the enterprise's rational course was the course taken: dignify the existing text with the volume's format and divisions and spend the manuscript budget elsewhere. The Folio's Romeo and Juliet is therefore the limiting case that proves the volume's textual rule, the rule that the Folio's authority for any play is exactly the authority of the copy its printers happened to use, no more and no less. Where the copy was a manuscript, the result is a treasure; where the copy was a quarto at two removes, the result is a handsome reprint. Readers who approach the volume as a uniform reliquary, every page equally near the author, misunderstand the object in their hands. The First Folio is a portfolio of thirty-six separate textual histories bound in one cover, and the history this study has traced is the history of a quarto wearing a monument's clothes.

## The Verdict on the Folio Text

The evidence assembled across this study supports a judgement in three clauses. First, as a witness to the words of Romeo and Juliet, the First Folio ranks third among the early texts, behind the Second Quarto it reprints at two removes and, for the passages where report preserves what authority corrupts, behind the First Quarto's independent testimony; its variants are the products of the Jaggard printing house, datable, attributable and devoid of any channel to the author's papers or the company's promptbook. Second, as an influence on the play's form, the Folio ranks first and alone: its act and scene divisions created the architecture of reference in which the tragedy is read, cited and criticized, an interpolation so successful that four centuries of readers have mistaken it for the author's design. Third, as an agent of survival, the Folio ranks with the two or three decisive artefacts in the play's entire history, the monument that carried a quarto's text through the centuries in which quartos perished, and delivered the tragedy to the age of editors with its fame intact and its words awaiting purification. A derivative text, a structural author, an ark: the Folio's Romeo and Juliet is all three at once, and understanding the play's documents means holding the three verdicts together, granting the volume everything it did for the play except the one thing its title page claimed and its printers never had, an original copy of the words.

For the reader who cites the play, the practical consequences are plain. Quotations follow a modern edition built on the Second Quarto, and the Folio enters an essay as evidence about transmission, structure and survival, not as a court of appeal for contested words. Where a Folio reading is preferred in an edition, the preference is the editor's argued correction of the copy-text, and it should be credited to the argument, not to 1623. Where the five acts are discussed, they are discussed as the book's architecture, illuminating for the play's reception and performance history, inadmissible as direct evidence of the author's compositional plan. And where the volume's greatness is praised, the praise is most accurate when it praises the right thing: not the purity of a text the printers took from a quarto, but the permanence of a book that carried that quarto's words, its own divisions and its gathering fame intact across the centuries that consumed the playbooks. The Folio saved the play the way a reservoir saves a river, by standing downstream of the source and refusing to run dry.


## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the First Folio, and when was it published?

The First Folio is the first collected edition of Shakespeare's plays, a large-format volume of thirty-six plays published in London in 1623, seven years after the playwright's death. Its full title names the contents as "Mr. William Shakespeares Comedies, Histories, & Tragedies," and it was entered in the Stationers' Register on 8 November 1623 before going on sale late that year. The syndicate behind it joined the printers William and Isaac Jaggard with the stationers Edward Blount, John Smethwick and William Aspley, while the actors John Heminges and Henry Condell fronted the enterprise as the author's fellows. Eighteen of its plays had never been printed before. For the eighteen that had, the volume's value varies play by play with the copy its printers used, and for Romeo and Juliet that copy was a printed quarto, which is why the Folio's splendour as a book must not be confused with authority over the tragedy's words.

### Q: Who published the First Folio, and who printed it?

The First Folio was printed in the London printing house of William Jaggard and his son Isaac, and published by a syndicate of stationers: Edward Blount, Isaac Jaggard, William Aspley and John Smethwick. The volume was entered in the Stationers' Register under Blount's and Isaac Jaggard's names on 8 November 1623. William Jaggard died in November 1623, as the work finished, leaving Isaac to complete the enterprise. John Heminges and Henry Condell, leading actors of the King's Men and the playwright's former fellow shareholders, presented the volume to the world with a dedication to the Earls of Pembroke and Montgomery and an address to readers, and their names have carried the credit for the edition ever since. The actual labour belonged to the Jaggard workmen: the compositors who set the type stint by stint, the correctors who read proof at the press, and the pressmen who printed the sheets across roughly two years of work.

### Q: How many plays does the First Folio contain, and under what heading does Romeo and Juliet appear?

The First Folio contains thirty-six plays, sorted by its printed catalogue into comedies, histories and tragedies, and Romeo and Juliet appears among the tragedies. The classification is the volume's own: the catalogue in the preliminaries assigns each play to one of the three kinds, and the tragedy of the Veronese lovers has been shelved as a tragedy in the collected works ever since, a generic address fixed by the book trade rather than by any playhouse record. Thirty-six was not the whole of the playwright's dramatic output, since Pericles and The Two Noble Kinsmen stand outside the volume, and collaboration complicates several inclusions; but the catalogue's threefold order became the canonical shape of the works. Within that order the tragedies form the volume's final and gravest division, and the play's place in it completed the transformation of a popular 1590s stage success into a classic text, bound in the company of Hamlet, Othello and King Lear between the same covers.

### Q: Why did the Folio's publishers use a printed quarto as copy for this play?

Because the syndicate already owned the play in print and had no commercial reason to seek a manuscript. John Smethwick, a member of the publishing syndicate, held the trade rights in Romeo and Juliet, and the quarto line had been printed for his shop: the third quarto of 1609 and the quarto of 1622 both came from his business. A collected edition assembled against a deadline set its unpublished plays from manuscripts, since manuscripts were the venture's novelty, and set its securely owned titles from the printed stock at hand. The choice was economic before it was textual, and its consequences were permanent: the Folio text descends from the Second Quarto through the third quarto, inheriting that line's errors and adding a printing house's fresh crop, while no document from the playhouse or the author's papers entered the transmission at any point. The syndicate's ledger, not any editor's judgement, decided the Folio text's pedigree.

### Q: What is the difference between the Folio text and the Second Quarto text of the play?

The Folio text is the Second Quarto's text at two removes, reprinted from the third quarto with the changes a printing house makes. The substantive differences number in the dozens: single words substituted, modernized or blundered, a scattering of readings where the Folio's correctors repaired obvious damage, and one large omission, the Act 2 Chorus sonnet, which the Folio drops entirely. The accidental differences number in the hundreds: respelled words, repunctuation in the compositors' habits, expanded and compressed speech headings, and verse lining adjusted to the Folio's narrower double columns. The structural difference outweighs all the rest, since the Folio divides the play into the five acts and numbered scenes that the quartos lack and that all later reading inherits. Nothing of substance enters the play from the Folio: no passage, speech or soliloquy stands in its pages that the quarto tradition does not already contain. The modern edited text therefore rests on the Second Quarto and treats the Folio as a corrector and a frame.

### Q: Who were Compositors B and E, and why do they matter for this play?

Compositors B and E were two of the five workmen in the Jaggard printing house whose hands Charlton Hinman distinguished in the setting of the First Folio, and between them they set Romeo and Juliet. Compositor B, the shop's most experienced and most interventionist typesetter, set the greater share of the volume and the latter portion of the tragedy; Compositor E, an apprentice, set the play's opening pages. They matter because their temperaments differ in ways an editor can use. B read his copy intelligently and improved it as he went, modernizing and regularizing, so his variants may be deliberate corrections, right or wrong. E followed copy mechanically and stumbled where it puzzled him, so his variants carry a presumption of blunder. Hinman identified the stints by the men's spelling habits and by recurring damaged type, and the identification lets scholarship weigh every Folio variant in the play by the reliability of the hands that produced it.

### Q: What is through line numbering, and why do scholars cite the Folio by it?

Through line numbering is Charlton Hinman's system of numbering every line of type in the First Folio continuously through the volume, so that any Folio reading can be cited by a single stable number regardless of edition or facsimile. Hinman devised the system for his study of the Folio's printing and his edition of its text, and the Norton Facsimile of the Folio prints the numbers in its margins. The system solves a practical problem created by the book's own manufacture: since correction at the press left different surviving copies in slightly different states, a citation by page and column alone can mislead, while a through line number fixes the exact line of type under discussion in any copy. Scholars citing the play's Folio readings give the act, scene and line of a modern edition for the passage and the through line number for the Folio's witness to it, a double address that keeps the derivative text precisely placed in every argument about the words.

### Q: What act and scene divisions does the Folio give the play that the quartos lack?

The Folio gives the play its entire architecture of reference: five acts, each divided into numbered scenes, where the quartos print the action as a continuous stream marked only by entrances and exits. The division was imposed in the printing house or in the preparation of the copy for the press, by an unknown agent working from the printed quarto, and its execution is uneven: the broad movements of the action are caught well, most scene breaks observe the rule of a cleared stage, but the great tomb sequence of the finale is cut into two scenes in the middle of continuous action, and some genuine breaks elsewhere go unmarked. Nicholas Rowe's edition of 1709 took the grid over, regularized the headings and added the locations that editions still print. Every act and scene citation in every classroom and edition descends from this Folio workmanship, a fact worth remembering whenever the play's five-act design is praised as the author's: the design is the book's.

### Q: Why do some Folio stage directions look fuller than the Second Quarto's?

Because the Jaggard compositors and correctors tidied directions as they set them, expanding the copy's terser indications into the fuller, more regular forms the collected volume affects throughout. At scattered points the Folio specifies entries, groupings and business that the Second Quarto leaves to implication, and the additions have occasionally tempted readers to credit the Folio with playhouse knowledge, as if a promptbook stood behind its page. The pattern forbids the inference: the expansions occur across the volume, including in plays demonstrably set from print, they add no business the dialogue does not already imply, and they settle no staging question the quarto leaves open. They are a feature of the book's dress, part of the regularization that made the collected edition look authoritative, and editors accordingly print the Second Quarto's directions as the text's directions, recording the Folio's ampler versions in the collation where their ingenuity can be admired without being obeyed.

### Q: How many copies of the First Folio survive, and where are most of them kept?

Rather more than two hundred copies of the First Folio survive from a print run in the region of seven hundred and fifty, a survival rate that itself testifies to the book's status as a library object rather than a disposable playbook. The largest collection by far belongs to the Folger Shakespeare Library in Washington, which holds eighty-two copies, a concentration assembled by Henry Clay Folger precisely so that the copies could be compared, the comparison that made Charlton Hinman's analysis of the printing possible. Other copies rest in the British Library, the Bodleian, university and national collections across several continents, and a small number in private hands, where a complete copy commands a price among the highest paid for any printed book. The survival matters to the play's text in a practical way: the Folio's press variants can be mapped only because so many copies endure to be collated, and the map confirms the volume's derivative handling of the tragedy sheet by sheet.

### Q: How much did the First Folio cost when it was new?

The First Folio sold at about fifteen shillings unbound and at a pound or more in a binding, prices that placed it among the expensive books of its decade. For scale, a single play in quarto cost about sixpence, so the collected volume cost roughly thirty times the price of the playbook in which Romeo and Juliet had circulated since 1597. The pricing was a positioning decision: the syndicate aimed the book at gentry, professional and institutional buyers, the purchasers whose shelves preserved books, rather than at the playgoing readership of the quartos. The strategy worked as preservation even where it limited circulation, since expensive books are kept and cheap ones are read to pieces. The two surviving copies of the First Quarto and the handful of the Second, against more than two hundred Folios, measure the difference in survival that the price bought. The tragedy reached posterity in the costly dress, carrying a text the cheap dress had printed better.

### Q: What is a folio, and how does a folio differ from a quarto as a book?

A folio is a book made from sheets of paper folded once, giving two leaves and four pages to the sheet, while a quarto is made from sheets folded twice, giving four leaves and eight smaller pages. The folio was therefore the large, costly format of law, history, theology and collected works, and the quarto the standard cheap format of the single play. Format carried a social meaning in the trade: to publish plays in folio, as Ben Jonson had done with his Works in 1616, was to claim for stage pieces the dignity of literature, a claim the trade and its wits initially mocked. The 1623 Shakespeare volume pressed the claim at full scale, some nine hundred pages in the prestige format, and the claim succeeded so completely that the book is known by its format alone. The format also shaped the play's text physically, since the Folio's narrower double columns, against the quarto's broad single page, forced the lining and spacing adjustments that mark the Folio text throughout.

### Q: Did Shakespeare himself prepare the First Folio for publication?

No. Shakespeare died in 1616, seven years before the First Folio appeared, and no evidence connects him to the planning of a collected edition of his plays. The volume was the work of the publishing syndicate and the Jaggard printing house, fronted by John Heminges and Henry Condell, his former fellows in the King's Men, whose preface presents the plays as he left them. For some plays the syndicate obtained manuscripts from the company's stocks, and those texts may stand close to the author's papers; but the collection was a posthumous commercial and commemorative enterprise, not an author's edition supervised from Stratford. The point carries a direct consequence for Romeo and Juliet, whose Folio text was set from a printed quarto in the syndicate's own trade line. Whatever authority the volume possesses derives from the documents its printers used, play by play, and for this play that document was a book any stationer could supply, not a legacy from the author's desk.

### Q: How did eighteenth-century editors treat the Folio text of the play?

With a deference the evidence did not warrant, until collation taught them better. Nicholas Rowe, editing in 1709, worked from the Fourth Folio, the tradition's latest and most diluted member, and inherited its text along with its act and scene architecture. Alexander Pope's edition of 1725 treated the Folio line as the authentic monument and the early quartos as players' corruptions, demoting readings he disliked on that principle. Lewis Theobald's Shakespeare Restored of 1726 exposed the error by showing the Folio tradition corrupt where the quartos preserved sense, and Edward Capell's edition, built on the first systematic collation of the early copies, restored the Second Quarto's priority for the play in practice. The sequence is the Folio myth in miniature: prestige first, evidence later. The twentieth century's bibliography, from Pollard through Greg to Hinman, completed the reversal by proving the descent, and the modern order, the Second Quarto as copy-text with the Folio as honoured corrector, is the settlement that collation earned.

### Q: What is the Second Folio, and how does its text of the play differ from the First Folio's?

The Second Folio is the 1632 reprint of the collected plays, printed by Thomas Cotes for Robert Allot and partners, nine years after the First. Its text of Romeo and Juliet is the First Folio's text at one more remove: the 1632 compositors reset the volume page for page from the 1623 edition, modernizing spellings, adjusting punctuation and silently correcting or corrupting readings by their own judgement as they worked. Nothing in the Second Folio descends from any document independent of the First, so its variants add a layer of printing-house change without adding evidence, and editors cite it for the play only as a stage in the Folio tradition's drift. The volume is remembered culturally for its preliminaries, which include the first published poem of the young John Milton, an epitaph on the playwright, and textually as proof of the first volume's authority in the trade: the classic was now worth reprinting whole, errors, architecture and all, for a new generation of library shelves.

### Q: How reliable are the Folio's punctuation and spelling for an editor?

Not reliable at all as evidence of the author, since both were supplied by the printing house as it reset a printed quarto. Each Folio compositor spelled by his own habits, which is precisely how Hinman identified their stints, and the pointing was recast in the same process, so the Folio's commas and capitals record how Jaggard's workmen read the play in 1622 and 1623, not how any earlier document presented it. Percy Simpson's Shakespearian Punctuation argued that the early editions' pointing was rhetorical, a score for the voice, and the argument, where it holds, holds for the witnesses nearest the author's papers; for this play that witness is the Second Quarto, whose heavier rhetorical pointing editors respect accordingly. Modern editions therefore follow the Second Quarto for the accidentals of spelling and punctuation as for the words, modernizing the spelling for readers while treating the Folio's accidentals as compositorial weather: real, pervasive, and without authority over the text's climate.

### Q: What did the Folio's title page claim about the texts inside the volume?

The title page claims that the plays are "Published according to the True Originall Copies," and the claim is the founding sentence of the Folio legend. As a description of the whole volume it will not stand: roughly half the plays were indeed printed from manuscripts, some standing very close to the originals the phrase invokes, but the remainder, Romeo and Juliet among them, were printed from earlier printed editions, and a text set from the third quarto is nobody's true original copy. Heminges and Condell's address to readers pressed the same claim in warmer language, contrasting the volume's cured and perfect texts with the stolen and surreptitious copies that had deformed them in print. For the tragedy of Verona the rhetoric consumes itself, since the Folio text descends from the very quarto tradition the preface deplores. The phrases are best read as the trade's advertising at its most confident, and their long afterlife as bibliography, in readers who trusted them, is a caution against taking a title page at its word.

### Q: Who wrote the commendatory verses printed in the First Folio?

Ben Jonson wrote the volume's principal commendatory verses: the lines facing the Droeshout portrait, testifying to the engraver's strife with nature, and the long poem "To the memory of my beloved, the Author," which calls the playwright the soul of the age and a monument without a tomb. Hugh Holland contributed a sonnet on the author's remains, and Leonard Digges a poem pointing readers to the Stratford monument and prophesying that the book would outlast it. The verses frame the enterprise as a national act of commemoration, and Jonson's authority as the age's leading dramatist-scholar gave the frame its weight: the rival and friend who had teased the playwright's small Latin and less Greek now certified him for posterity. The poems promise fame, not textual purity, and in that they are the volume's most honest preliminaries. The fame they promised arrived; the purity the title page promised for this play had never been in the building.

### Q: Why does the belief that the Folio saved Shakespeare mislead readers of this play?

Because it confuses the survival of the book with the authority of the text, and for Romeo and Juliet the two pull in opposite directions. The Folio did preserve the play magnificently as an object: a costly library volume, reprinted through the century, ensured that the tragedy never faced the extinction that consumed the cheap quartos, of which a handful of copies survive. But the words the Folio preserved were a quarto's words at two removes, and the belief that the great book must carry the truest text inverted the editorial order for more than a century, until collation restored the Second Quarto's priority. The accurate sentence is narrower and stronger: the Folio saved the play's fame, its architecture and its broad text, while the truest early words survived in the fragile playbook the Folio's prestige eclipsed. Readers who hold both halves of that sentence understand the volume's real office in the tragedy's history, neither idol nor impostor, but ark and interpreter at once.

The InsightCrunch Folio note is this study's reference table setting the Folio text's derivation and added features side by side: its copy in a quarto of the third quarto's line, its compositors B and E, its imposed act and scene divisions, its missing second Chorus, its dozens of substantive variants and hundreds of accidentals, and its standing as a tertiary witness behind the Second Quarto.
