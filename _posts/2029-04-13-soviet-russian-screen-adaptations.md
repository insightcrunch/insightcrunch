The Soviet screen tradition of Romeo and Juliet rests on a single paradox that decides every judgement in this survey. The state that built an entire culture around translated words, around Boris Pasternak's Russian text and around spoken Shakespeare in hundreds of theatres, sent the Verona tragedy abroad in the one form that needed no words at all. The central Soviet screen Romeo and Juliet is a ballet film. Galina Ulanova became the canonical Soviet Juliet without speaking a line, and the film that carries her performance, directed by Lev Arnshtam with Leonid Lavrovsky and produced by Mosfilm, won the prize for Best Lyrical Film at Cannes in 1955 in competition for the Palme d'Or. A dramatic cinema that produced Grigori Kozintsev's Hamlet and King Lear, Sergei Yutkevich's Othello and Yan Frid's Twelfth Night produced no comparable spoken feature of the Verona play. The absence is not a failure of interest. The absence is the argument. Where the play entered Soviet cinema at full feature scale, the play entered as dance, as Prokofiev's score made visible, and the reasons reach into censorship, export policy, the economics of a wordless art, and the particular authority of one ballerina whose Juliet had already conquered two stages before a camera recorded the interpretation.

## The Verdict on the Soviet Screen Tradition

The verdict comes first because the evidence permits only one ordering. The Arnshtam and Lavrovsky film stands as the Soviet screen Romeo and Juliet, not as a supplement to a spoken tradition that never formed at feature length. Every later Russian screen encounter with the play, on television, in filmed stage transfers, in excerpts and in post-Soviet stagings recorded for broadcast, works in the shadow of a danced Verona fixed on film in the middle nineteen fifties. The film's claim rests on four documented supports. The choreography preserves Leonid Lavrovsky's staging, the production that established Prokofiev's ballet in the Soviet repertory after a premiere at the Kirov Theatre in Leningrad on 11 January 1940. The cast preserves the interpretation of Galina Ulanova, the dancer who created Juliet in that premiere opposite Konstantin Sergeyev and who carried the role to the Bolshoi Theatre in Moscow in the 1946 transfer. The score preserves, in edited form, the music Sergei Prokofiev completed in 1935 and revised under pressure for the Soviet stage. The festival record preserves the state's own valuation, since the film travelled to Cannes in 1955 as an official Soviet entry and left with a named prize in a year when the Palme d'Or itself went to Delbert Mann's Marty and the prize for Best Dramatic Film went to Elia Kazan's East of Eden.

A verdict needs its counterweight stated fairly. The film runs to some ninety two minutes in standard catalogues, against a stage score that fills an evening, and compression governs every sequence. The camera serves a choreography designed for a proscenium, and passages of the film record a stage picture rather than discovering a cinematic one. Dancers mime a plot that spoken cinema renders in dialogue, and a spectator who comes for the quarrel of the servants in 1.1, for the wit combat of Mercutio in 2.4, or for the Friar's botany in 2.3 receives gesture and music in place of language. Those costs are real, and the sections below weigh each one. The weighing still ends in the same place. A spoken Soviet feature of the play does not exist to outrank the ballet film, the ballet film carries the strongest single Juliet performance the Soviet Union placed on any screen, and the film's export success proved that the wordless Verona could cross borders that a Russian language drama, dependent on subtitles and dubbing, crossed only with friction. The tradition is therefore judged on the terms the tradition itself chose. Measured as filmed ballet, the Arnshtam and Lavrovsky film is a major screen Shakespeare. Measured as a substitute for the spoken play, the film is a magnificent partial record, and the gap between those two judgements is the most instructive fact in Russian screen Shakespeare.

### What makes the Arnshtam and Lavrovsky film a screen adaptation rather than a stage record?

The film counts as a screen adaptation because Arnshtam rebuilt Lavrovsky's stage action for the camera, with location scale, crowd scenes and edited sequences no proscenium held, and because Mosfilm credits Arnshtam and Lavrovsky jointly as directors of a film conceived for festival exhibition. Cannes judged a film, not a souvenir programme.

That distinction matters for classification across the whole survey. A filmed stage performance documents an event. An adaptation rethinks a source in a new medium. The 1955 film sits between the poles and nearer the adaptation pole, since the Verona of the film occupies constructed streets and squares beyond any single stage set, since editing compresses and reorders stage time, and since close framing gives Ulanova's face a dramatic weight that the farthest theatre seat never received. Jay Leyda, the American historian whose Kino remains a foundational history of Russian and Soviet film, treated Soviet literary adaptation as a central national practice rather than a marginal one, and the ballet film belongs inside that practice even though the literature arrives through music and movement. The classification also explains why the present survey refuses to pad the record. Television excerpts, filmed galas and stage transfers enter the account graded as records. The Arnshtam and Lavrovsky film enters graded as the tradition's one feature scale act of adaptation, and the difference in grade is a difference in kind.

## What Counts as a Soviet Screen Adaptation of the Play?

Definitions decide the census, and the census decides the verdict, so the categories need stating before any title enters. A screen adaptation, for the purposes of this survey, means a work made for cinema or television that renders the Verona story through performance, whether spoken, danced, sung or animated, at a scale that stands alone as a viewing experience. A stage transfer means a recording of a theatre production whose primary life belongs to the stage. An excerpt means a partial rendering, a single scene, pas de deux or suite, however distinguished the performers. The categories are not ranks of worth. A transfer can preserve greater art than an adaptation, and the Bolshoi and Kirov archives hold transfers of high distinction. The categories are ranks of claim. Only an adaptation can found a screen tradition, because only an adaptation asserts that the screen, rather than the stage, is the work's home.

Under those definitions the Soviet feature record contains exactly one title. The Arnshtam and Lavrovsky Romeo and Juliet, produced by Mosfilm and released in 1955, adapts Prokofiev's ballet, which adapts the play, which makes the film an adaptation at two removes whose fidelity runs to Lavrovsky's stage Verona rather than to any spoken text. No Soviet studio released a spoken dramatic feature of the play. Lenfilm, the studio behind Kozintsev's Shakespeare films, built its Shakespeare reputation on Hamlet in 1964 and King Lear in 1973, with Yutkevich's Othello from Mosfilm in 1955 and Frid's Twelfth Night from Lenfilm in the same year completing a mid century cluster that conspicuously omits Verona. The omission invites explanation rather than embarrassment. Hamlet and Lear offered Kozintsev political and philosophical structures that Soviet criticism prized and that Pasternak's translations carried with canonical force. The Verona tragedy, a story of private passion against family authority, fitted the official repertory on stage, where hundreds of productions played across the Union, yet the story offered a feature director less of the public architecture that Soviet prestige cinema favoured, and the ballet had already claimed the story at the highest level of state art. A studio planning a spoken Romeo and Juliet in the nineteen fifties would have competed against Ulanova. The competition explains the silence of the dramatic studios more plausibly than any ban, and no document records a prohibition. The record records a displacement. Dance got there first, at the highest level, and the screen followed dance.

### Why did Soviet dramatic cinema leave the play to the ballet?

Soviet dramatic cinema left the play to the ballet because Lavrovsky's staging and Ulanova's Juliet already gave the state a Verona of international prestige, because Prokofiev's score supplied a complete dramatic structure without dialogue, and because prestige directors chose Hamlet, Lear and Othello instead. Displacement, not suppression, explains the empty dramatic shelf.

The pre Soviet and silent record needs the same discipline, because loose surveys sometimes imply a lost Russian Romeo and Juliet cinema waiting for rediscovery. Early Russian cinema filmed Shakespeare selectively, and the surviving catalogues credit the silent era with versions of other plays while the Verona tragedy remains marginal in the documented lists. The honest statement is a graded one. Russian screens before the Revolution knew Shakespeare chiefly through imported productions and through a small domestic output concentrated on a handful of titles, and no early Russian Romeo and Juliet has the documented standing of the era's better attested adaptations. A survey that invents a silent Verona to balance the ballet film would falsify the shape of the tradition, whose real shape is a long near absence followed by a single overwhelming arrival. The arrival, when the arrival came, came through a theatre, a composer and a ballerina, and the screen inherited all three.

## The Sparse Russian Screen Record Before the Ballet Film

Russian Shakespeare on stage reaches back to the eighteenth century, to Alexander Sumarukov's heavily adapted Hamlet of 1748 and to a long nineteenth century of translation and performance that made the playwright a Russian cultural possession well before the Revolution. Russian Shakespeare on screen has a shorter and thinner history, and the difference between the stage wealth and the screen poverty frames the ballet film's importance. The first Russian film studios worked in a market flooded with foreign literary adaptations, and domestic Shakespeare filming stayed occasional. Catalogues of the silent period record Russian versions drawn from the broader canon while the Verona play leaves no comparably documented feature trace. Theatre, by contrast, staged the tragedy steadily, in the capitals and in the provinces, in Russian and in the other languages of the empire and then of the Union. A Russian spectator of 1930 could plausibly have seen the play spoken on stage in several cities and could not have seen a Russian film of the play at any length. The screen tradition therefore begins, for practical purposes, in 1940 in a Leningrad theatre, fourteen years before a camera took the story up, because the ballet production of that year created the interpretation that the film would eventually carry.

The year 1940 needs its context restored, since the ballet premiere fell in the last full year of peace before the German invasion of June 1941. Lavrovsky, artistic director of the Kirov ballet, had taken up a score that the Bolshoi had rejected in the middle nineteen thirties and that a shortened staging in Brno, choreographed by Ivo Vana Psota and premiered on 30 December 1938, had introduced abroad without settling the work's Soviet fate. The Leningrad premiere of 11 January 1940, with designs by Pyotr Vilyams, Ulanova as Juliet and Konstantin Sergeyev as Romeo, settled the fate at once. The production received the Stalin Prize, entered the repertory as the defining example of drambalet, the Soviet dramatic ballet that subordinated virtuoso display to acted narrative, and gave the Union a Verona built on psychological continuity rather than on divertissement. When war came, the Kirov company evacuated and the production travelled in the company's wartime life, and Ulanova's Juliet acquired the aura of an interpretation formed under national emergency. The film of the nineteen fifties therefore records not a routine revival but a wartime classic, already layered with meanings that no spoken film could have matched at the moment of production.

### Which Russian screen versions of the play survive from the silent era?

No Russian silent version of the play holds a documented place in the standard catalogues comparable to the attested silent Shakespeare films of other titles, so the survey treats the silent Verona as a near absence rather than a lost treasure. Imported versions circulated, stage performance flourished, and domestic filming left no exhibitable feature record.

Between the stage premiere and the film stands the transfer that fixed the production in the capital. Lavrovsky became chief ballet master of the Bolshoi in 1944, and in 1946 the company staged the Verona ballet in Moscow with Ulanova, who had joined the Bolshoi in 1944, again as Juliet and Mikhail Gabovich as the first Moscow Romeo in the standard accounts. The Moscow production received the Stalin Prize, first class, in the usual telling of the honours, and the ballet settled into the Bolshoi repertory as a house classic. The geography matters for the brief behind this article and for every short reference that calls the film a Bolshoi film. The choreography was created for Leningrad and the Kirov. The interpretation was then rebuilt for Moscow and the Bolshoi, whose dancers, Ulanova and Yuri Zhdanov among them, form the film's cast. A reference that names only one city tells half the story, and the fuller statement costs one sentence. Lavrovsky's Romeo and Juliet began as a Kirov production, became a Bolshoi production, and reached the screen through Mosfilm with a Bolshoi led cast. Each institution contributed a layer, and the film preserves all three layers at once.

## Prokofiev, Lavrovsky and the Score Behind the Camera

The film's music belongs to another article's full custody, and the present account takes the score at the length the screen argument needs. Prokofiev completed the ballet in 1935 on a scenario shaped with Adrian Piotrovsky and Sergei Radlov, and the original conception ended with the lovers alive, a happy ending that the composer at first defended on the ground that living people can dance while corpses cannot. The Bolshoi rejected the score, the political climate after the Pravda attacks on Shostakovich in 1936 made experimental Shakespeare hazardous, and the work waited. The detailed history of the score, its suites and its most famous number receives full treatment in the companion account of [Prokofiev's Ballet and the Dance of the Knights](/2028/11/24/prokofiev-ballet-dance-of-knights/), and the concert tradition that preceded the ballet receives separate treatment in the account of [Tchaikovsky's Fantasy-Overture and Its Theme](/2028/11/17/tchaikovsky-fantasy-overture/), where the Verona story first entered Russian orchestral life two generations before Prokofiev. What the screen argument needs is narrower. Lavrovsky persuaded and pressured the composer into revisions, added variations for the lovers, altered the order and weight of numbers, and built a choreographic text that Prokofiev accepted in public and resisted in part in private correspondence. Simon Morrison, the composer's biographer and the leading scholar of the ballet's documentary history, established from the archives how extensive the choreographer's interventions ran and how the 1940 performing version differs from the 1935 conception. The film inherits the Lavrovsky performing version, not the composer's first thoughts, and a viewer who knows the suites in concert hears a related but distinct musical argument on screen.

The disagreement between composer and choreographer deserves adjudication because the film's authority depends on the outcome. Prokofiev's position, documented in letters and in Morrison's archival account, held that the score possessed an internal dramatic logic that choreographic alteration damaged, and that the restored tragic ending, accepted under pressure from colleagues including the conductor Yuri Fayer, conceded a point the music had originally refused. Lavrovsky's position, embodied in the production rather than argued in print at comparable length, held that a stage audience needs legible action, that dance drama requires expanded passages at the moments of meeting, parting and death, and that a score for the theatre must serve the bodies in the room. The stage verdict went to Lavrovsky, since the 1940 production succeeded on a scale that settled the ballet's repertory place, and the film ratifies the stage verdict by filming Lavrovsky's text. The concert verdict partly returns to Prokofiev, since the suites circulate in the composer's ordering and the complete recording era restored interest in the 1935 conception, including a modern staging project that revived the happy ending as a historical document. For the screen question, the adjudication is direct. The film is Lavrovsky's Romeo and Juliet set to Prokofiev's music, in that order of possession, and criticism that praises or blames the film as pure Prokofiev misassigns the authorship. The camera filmed a choreographer's reading. The reading happened to carry one of the century's great ballet scores, and the combination, not either element alone, explains the film's durability.

Drambalet, the aesthetic under which Lavrovsky worked, needs definition because foreign criticism often uses the term as a dismissal. The dramatic ballet, as Soviet practice developed the form in the nineteen thirties, demanded a continuous acted narrative, characters whose movement carried psychological motive, crowd scenes that behaved like crowds in a spoken production, and the suppression of autonomous display numbers that halt a plot for applause. Christina Ezrahti, in Swans of the Kremlin, her study of ballet in Russian and Soviet culture, situates the Verona production inside that programme and inside the cultural politics that rewarded legible classics. Elizabeth Souritz, the historian of Soviet choreography, documented the generation of choreographers who built the style and the institutional pressures that favoured narrative clarity. Under drambalet rules, Shakespeare was an ideal source, since the plays supplied ready made plots of high cultural prestige whose stories a mass audience could be presumed to know or to learn. The Verona ballet is the style's supreme test case because the plot turns on private feeling rather than public ceremony, and Lavrovsky's crowd choreography, in the market place fights and the Capulet ball, gives the feud a physical mass that spoken productions achieve through verse. The film magnifies exactly that strength. A camera can hold a square full of fighting dancers in a single frame, and Arnshtam's crowd sequences argue, without a word, that the feud is a civic condition and not a backdrop.

## The Mosfilm Production and the Division of Its Dates

Catalogues divide on the film's year, and the division must be stated as a division rather than resolved by preference. Standard filmographies give 1955, the year of the Cannes screening and of the release date most often printed, 20 May 1955 in the widely used filmography entry. Other records, including respected museum cataloguing, date the production to 1954, and at least one major collection catalogue prints an earlier production year still. The pattern is familiar in Soviet film dating, where the year of completion, the year of domestic release and the year of foreign festival exposure can each claim the title. The present survey cites the film as the 1955 film, following the festival record and the dominant filmography, while recording that production dating to 1954 stands in reputable catalogues and that any argument depending on a single year must name the dating convention in use. The brief behind this article calls the film a work of 1954, and the divergence between that dating and the festival year is a catalogue division, not an error of substance. What no catalogue divides is the film's identity. Russian title Romeo i Dzhulyetta, Mosfilm production, direction credited to Lev Arnshtam with Leonid Lavrovsky, choreography by Lavrovsky, music by Prokofiev, cinematography credited to Yu Lan Chen and Aleksandr Shelenkov, running time around ninety two minutes in the standard entry, with Ulanova as Juliet and Yuri Zhdanov as Romeo at the head of the cast.

Arnshtam's contribution separates the film from a photographed performance. A veteran Mosfilm director with literary adaptations in his filmography, Arnshtam understood the assignment as translation between media, and the production used studio built Verona exteriors and crowd staging at a scale the Bolshoi stage could not hold. The fights expand. The ball fills a hall built for the camera. Processions and street scenes give the feud a geography. Against those expansions, the intimate scenes contract to the close framing that cinema does best, and Ulanova's face, in the balcony and potion scenes, carries passages that a theatre audience read from thirty metres through gesture alone. The editing also compresses, since ninety two minutes cannot hold the full stage score, and numbers are shortened or dropped in the film's musical text, a fact that concert minded critics have held against the film and that the festival jury, judging a lyrical film rather than a complete ballet, evidently weighed differently. Both facts belong in the record. The film is at once larger than the stage production in space and smaller in duration, a paradox that defines filmed ballet as a form and that the form section below takes up directly.

The supporting cast fixes the film in the Bolshoi of its decade. Aleksandr Radunsky plays Lord Capulet, Aleksey Yermolayev plays Tybalt, Sergei Koren plays Mercutio, Aleksandr Lapauri plays Paris, and the roles of Benvolio and Friar Laurence fall to V. Kudryashov and L. Loshchilin in the standard cast list. Yermolayev, a premier danseur of the older generation, brings a heavyweight authority to Tybalt that makes the feud's violence credible at close range, and Koren's Mercutio carries the production's wit in mime and attack, a performance that answers, in movement, the question of how a wordless film handles the play's most verbal character. Mercutio without the Queen Mab speech, 1.4 in the play, is a test the film passes by choreographic characterisation, since the audience reads brilliance, recklessness and doom in the dancing rather than in the rhetoric. The Queen Mab speech itself, in the spoken text, spins a miniature of dreams and violence that no mime can reproduce, and the film accepts the loss as the price of the form. A dramatic film keeps the words and loses Prokofiev. The ballet film keeps Prokofiev and loses the words. The Soviet tradition chose music, and the choice, once made at the Kirov in 1940, governed every screen consequence.

### How long is the Arnshtam and Lavrovsky film, and what does the length cut?

Standard catalogues give a running time of about ninety two minutes, roughly half the span of a full stage performance of the score, so the film shortens numbers and omits passages to hold a feature length lyrical shape. Compression is the clearest cost, and concentration, an arc played without interval, is the gain.

## Galina Ulanova and the Juliet That Defined a Country

Ulanova's Juliet is the centre of the film and of the tradition, and the performance needs describing in terms that survive the legend. Born in 1910 in Saint Petersburg into a ballet family, trained at the Leningrad school where Agrippina Vaganova taught, Ulanova joined the Kirov company in 1928 and built a reputation in the lyric repertory before Lavrovsky cast the ballerina, then at the turn of her thirties, as a girl on the edge of marriage. The casting violated the arithmetic that governs modern screen casting debates, and the violation is the point. Ulanova's Juliet persuades not through the appearance of fourteen years but through a graduated transformation that the choreography builds across the evening, from the playful child of the nursery scenes, through the ball's awakening, to the potion scene's terror and the tomb's stillness. Critics of several nations returned to the same observation, that the dancing seemed to think. Clive Barnes, the British and American dance critic whose judgements carried unusual international weight in the postwar decades, called Ulanova's Juliet unforgettable in the notice attached to the film's later video life, and the adjective, from a critic sparing with absolutes, records the performance's effect on a professional sceptic. The film preserves the interpretation at the age of forty four or forty five, depending on the production dating, which deepens rather than damages the record, since the camera captures a reading refined across fourteen years of performance, from the 1940 premiere through the Bolshoi transfer to the film studio.

The performance also carries a national meaning that a purely aesthetic account misses. Ulanova became, in the postwar settlement, the public face of Soviet lyric art, decorated, filmed and exported as evidence that the Union produced tenderness as reliably as industry. The Juliet film belongs to that export programme as surely as any industrial exhibit, and the Cannes journey of 1955 formed part of a cultural offensive that crested with the Bolshoi's London season of 1956, where Ulanova danced Juliet on stage to a reception that British ballet history records as a sensation. Frederick Ashton, who had made a chamber Romeo and Juliet for the Royal Danish Ballet in 1955, attended a private screening of the Soviet film at the Soviet Embassy in London in that summer, an episode retold in Australian Ballet scholarship on Lavrovsky's legacy, and the film functioned in that screening room as an instrument of policy, a demonstration aimed at the leading choreographer of the host country. None of that context diminishes the dancing. The context explains the resources, the film stock, the festival entry and the preservation, since states preserve what states value, and the survival of Ulanova's Juliet in a complete feature film, when so many stage interpretations of the era survive only in fragments and photographs, is a direct consequence of the performance's official worth.

Against Ulanova, Zhdanov's Romeo has received the smaller share of attention, and the imbalance deserves correction within the limits of the record. Zhdanov, born in 1925, belonged to the younger Bolshoi generation, and the film Romeo is a lyric partner performance built on elevation, support and a boyish directness that sets Ulanova's inwardness in relief. The 1940 Romeo had been Konstantin Sergeyev, Ulanova's great stage partner, whose interpretation the film does not preserve, a loss that stage historians register when the film is treated as the production's complete record. The film preserves the later Bolshoi pairing, not the premiere pairing, and precision on that point protects the survey from a common catalogue slide in which the film is described as a record of the 1940 cast. The film records the 1940 choreography in a nineteen fifties Bolshoi performance. Choreography, interpretation and cast are three separate layers with three separate dates, and the InsightCrunch profile below prints the layers separately for exactly that reason.

## Cannes and the Export of a Wordless Verona

The festival record is short enough to state exactly and important enough to state without inflation. The eighth Cannes festival ran from 26 April to 10 May 1955, with Marcel Pagnol presiding over the jury of the main competition. Romeo i Dzhulyetta entered the official selection in competition, Soviet Union, directed by Lev Arnshtam and Leonid Lavrovsky in the festival's listing. The Palme d'Or, awarded for the first time under that name in 1955, went to Marty. The festival's category prizes divided the field by kind, Best Dramatic Film to East of Eden and Best Lyrical Film to Romeo and Juliet. The Soviet film therefore won in its category as the festival record gives the award, Prix du film lyrique in the French form, Best Lyrical Film in the English form, and the film also stands in the record as a Palme d'Or nominee by virtue of the competition entry. Loose claims that the film won at Cannes without naming the category, or that the film took the festival's top prize, misstate the record in opposite directions, and the exact statement is stronger than either looseness. A wordless Soviet ballet film, in a competition of thirty four features from the world's dramatic cinemas, left with a named prize that recognised lyric cinema as a festival category. The category itself flatters the film's nature, since lyric, rather than dramatic, is the precise term for a tragedy carried by music and movement, and the jury's taxonomy ratified the form that the form section of this survey defends.

The Cannes result also exposes the strategic logic of the wordless export. A spoken Soviet Romeo and Juliet, had a studio made one, would have travelled with subtitles that foreign distributors resisted, with a Russian text that foreign audiences could not test against the English original in their heads, and with the politics of Pasternak's name attached, since Pasternak, the era's standard translator, was moving toward the Doctor Zhivago crisis that broke in 1957 and the Nobel crisis of 1958. The ballet film travelled clean. Prokofiev's score needed no translation. Ulanova's face needed no dubbing. The story, known across Europe through stage and concert tradition, needed no synopsis beyond a programme note. Christina Ezrahti's account of ballet as Russian cultural diplomacy supplies the frame, and the film is a textbook instrument of that diplomacy, art of the highest domestic prestige offered abroad in the one form immune to language politics. The comparison with the Western screen contemporary sharpens the point and belongs to the Italian article's custody in detail. Renato Castellani's spoken Romeo and Juliet had taken the Golden Lion at Venice in 1954, months before Cannes, so the two festival systems, in consecutive seasons, crowned two opposite answers to the same question, Venice honouring a dramatic film of the play and Cannes honouring a danced one. The Cold War mirror could hardly be neater, and the present survey notes the symmetry while leaving the Italian record to the article that owns the record.

### How was the Soviet entry classified by the Cannes jury?

The film won Best Lyrical Film, the Prix du film lyrique, at the eighth Cannes festival in 1955, where the film screened in the main competition and therefore also stands as a Palme d'Or nominee. The Palme d'Or itself went to Marty, and Best Dramatic Film went to East of Eden. The category prize is the documented honour.

## Filmed Ballet Versus Dramatic Film

The brief behind this article demands engagement with the complication of form, and the engagement must be more than a shrug toward difference. Filmed ballet and dramatic film adapt the play along opposite axes. Dramatic film keeps the language and must solve the problem of verse in a realist medium, the problem that every spoken screen Romeo and Juliet from Castellani onward has faced, whether by cutting, by naturalistic delivery or by stylisation. Filmed ballet discards the language entire and must solve the problem of information, since a plot carried in mime and musical characterisation can render love, rage and grief with precision while rendering exposition, negotiation and intrigue only in outline. The Friar's plan, the letter's failure, the apothecary's poverty, the precise terms of the exile, all the machinery of 3.3, 4.1 and 5.1 and 5.2, reach a ballet audience as programme knowledge rather than as stage information. The ballet film therefore adapts the play's emotional architecture faithfully and the causal architecture loosely, and a verdict on the Soviet tradition must hold both halves. As a rendering of what the tragedy feels like, of the feud's public weight against the lovers' private intensity, the Arnshtam and Lavrovsky film stands comparison with any screen version. As a rendering of how the tragedy happens, of the chain of messages, timings and mistakes that the Chorus compresses into the phrase misadventured piteous overthrows in the Prologue, the film depends on a spectator who arrives already knowing the chain.

That dependence is not the disqualification that dialogue partisans claim, and the adjudication between the forms can be made on evidence rather than taste. Jay Leyda's history treats Soviet adaptation as a practice in which a source changes medium and meaning together, and the ballet film, judged by that standard, performs the full adaptive act on the material the form can hold. Clive Barnes, judging as a dance critic, valued the film as a great ballet filmed with attention to musical, choreographic and scenic values, in the formulation attached to the film's video releases, and the valuation accepts the form's terms. The contrary judgement, heard in later Western reviewing that dismissed Lavrovsky's production as a museum piece when the staging toured in revival, faults the choreography for the crime of age, for a mime vocabulary and a narrative earnestness that post MacMillan taste found dated. The disagreement is genuine and the survey adjudicates the disagreement in the film's favour on one ground and against the film on another. In the film's favour, the camera record of 1955 cannot be a museum piece in the same sense as a tired stage revival, because the film's purpose includes preservation, and Ulanova's performance, judged against every filmed Juliet of any form, retains a completeness of characterisation that fresher choreography has not displaced on screen. Against the film, the stage bound passages are real, the proscenium geometry survives in the ball and tomb scenes, and a director of Kozintsev's cinematic daring, given the same dancers and score, would have broken the stage picture more boldly than Arnshtam chose to break the geometry. The verdict therefore splits by criterion and joins by weight. As cinema, the film is conservative. As adaptation, the film is major. As the Soviet screen Romeo and Juliet, the film has no rival, because the conservative cinema of a great staging outranks, in historical fact, the nonexistent bolder film that no Soviet director made.

A further formal point concerns sound. The ballet film is not silent cinema, and the distinction matters for the export argument. Prokofiev's score functions as the film's dialogue, carrying character themes, the famous Dance of the Knights music for the Capulet ball's menace, the Juliet themes of youth and awakening, and the feud's brass weight, in a continuous symphonic argument that a foreign audience receives without mediation. Where spoken adaptation translates words and loses music, ballet adaptation keeps music and loses words, and the Soviet case adds a historical irony. The state that commissioned Pasternak's verbal text as a monument of Russian Shakespeare exported the play in the form that bypassed the monument. The irony resolves on inspection. Pasternak's text served the domestic stage and the domestic reader, the audiences the state most needed to convince that Shakespeare had found a second home in Russian. The ballet film served the foreign festival and the foreign tour, the audiences the state needed to impress without argument. Two instruments, two publics, one policy of prestige, and the play at the centre of both.

## Pasternak's Words and the Screens That Spoke

Pasternak's Romeo and Juliet, translated in the early nineteen forties within the great sequence of Shakespeare versions that occupied the poet from 1940 to 1949, became the era's standard Russian text of the play, the version that Soviet stages spoke and Soviet readers bought. The full account of that translation, its wartime circumstances and its textual character, belongs to the companion study of [Pasternak's Russian Romeo and Juliet](/2030/05/03/pasternak-russian-translation/), and the present survey takes from the translation only what the screen argument requires. The requirement is a contrast. Soviet screens did speak Pasternak, magnificently, in other plays. Kozintsev built Hamlet and King Lear on Pasternak's translations and said, in substance, that a Shakespeare film without Pasternak's text and Shostakovich's music was unthinkable for the director's purposes, and the two Kozintsev films stand among the century's honoured screen Shakespeare. No director built a Verona film on Pasternak's Romeo and Juliet. The translation's screen life is therefore indirect, through the stage productions that formed the culture the ballet film summarised, and through the general authority that made Shakespeare a safe prestige subject for Soviet studios. A claim sometimes implied in short accounts, that the ballet film somehow uses Pasternak's text, collapses on contact with the form, since a wordless film uses no translation at all. The precision matters because the series thesis depends on the distinction. The Soviet Union possessed, in Pasternak, a verbal Romeo and Juliet of the first rank, and the Soviet screen still chose dance. The choice was not made from verbal poverty. The choice was made from choreographic wealth, and from the export logic that the Cannes section established.

Television complicates the spoken record in ways that demand the survey's grading discipline. Soviet television, from the nineteen sixties onward, carried theatre to a mass audience through filmed productions and studio recordings, and the Verona ballet circulated in that system through transfers and excerpts, including recorded passages of Lavrovsky's choreography danced by successive Bolshoi and Kirov generations. Ekaterina Maximova and Natalia Bessmertnova appear in the catalogued excerpt record of the ballet's scenes, Maya Plisetskaya appears in ball excerpts from around 1960, and a bedroom pas de deux with Ulanova and Mikhail Gabovich survives from a 1951 recording, before the feature film, in the archival lists. Those materials are precious and the survey honours the materials at the correct grade. Excerpts and transfers document a stage tradition on screen without founding a television adaptation tradition of the play, and no Soviet television dramatic version of the tragedy at feature scale stands in the standard catalogues with the standing of the Kozintsev films or of the ballet feature. Post Soviet Russian television and video continued the pattern, filming stage productions, circulating the ballet in complete transfers of later stagings including Yuri Grigorovich's Bolshoi version, first staged in 1979 after Lavrovsky, and recording gala excerpts, while a spoken Russian television Romeo and Juliet of canonical standing remains absent from the record that scholarship can exhibit. The pattern across seventy years is consistent enough to state as the tradition's law. Russia films the Verona play as ballet, records the Verona play as stage transfer, and speaks the Verona play in theatres. The screen and the spoken text meet in other Shakespeare plays. In this play, the screen belongs to dance.

For researchers assembling the festival record, the cast lists and the transfer history from festival archives and company filmographies, a structured slide build keeps the layers separate, and the presentation tool at [the ReportMedic PPT Viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) serves that study purpose when a survey of this kind is prepared for teaching. The habit matters because the Soviet record punishes layer confusion, as the dating division and the cast layers have already shown, and a slide sequence that separates choreography, cast and festival honour prevents the three commonest errors in short accounts of the film.

## The Soviet Stage Context Behind the Screen

A screen tradition grows out of a stage culture, and the Soviet stage culture of the play was dense enough to make the ballet film a summary rather than an anomaly. Soviet theatres staged the tragedy across the Union in the hundreds of productions that Roman Samarin cited in Shakespeare in the Soviet Union, the 1966 English language collection in which Soviet scholarship presented the playwright as a writer who had found a second home in the USSR, with editions in twenty eight of the Union's languages and a stage record that Soviet writers compared, with pride, against every other country including the country of the playwright's birth. The claim was propaganda and also a statistic, and the two qualities coexist in much Soviet Shakespeare discourse. Within that culture the Verona play held a particular place. The tragedy could be read officially as a humanist protest against feudal family tyranny, a reading that made the feud a social lesson and the lovers its martyrs, and the reading suited both the spoken stage and the ballet, where Lavrovsky's crowd scenes give the social lesson a visible mass. A play so thoroughly naturalised, in Pasternak's Russian, in school and theatre repertory, and in Prokofiev's music, required no screen introduction for a domestic audience. The domestic audience already owned the story. The film's domestic function was commemoration, the fixing of a canonical interpretation, and the foreign function was demonstration. Both functions favour ballet over a new spoken production, which would have added an interpretation where the culture preferred a monument.

The monument quality explains a feature of the film that foreign viewers sometimes misread as stiffness. Lavrovsky's choreography, and Arnshtam's filming of the choreography, aim at permanence rather than experiment, at the definitive record of a production already honoured with the state's highest artistic prizes in two cities. Soviet culture built monuments in several media in those years, and the ballet film belongs with the filmed records of canonical stage productions through which the Union archived a performing heritage. The policy had a cost that the record should state. Experiment in Soviet screen Shakespeare came from Kozintsev, whose Hamlet rethought the play through stone, sea and iron, and no equivalent rethinking touched Verona on screen, because the monument occupied the space where an experiment would have stood. Post Soviet screens inherited the same occupation in a milder form, since the Lavrovsky film, restored and recirculated in video editions with the narration of some release versions removed in remastering, remains the reference screen Verona for Russian audiences in a way that no post Soviet production has challenged. Traditions founded by monuments change slowly, and the Russian screen tradition of the play, measured across the Soviet and post Soviet periods together, shows one foundation, one film, and a long custodial afterlife of transfers, excerpts and reissues around the foundation.

## The Tradition Judged Whole

The series thesis for this cluster holds that the cliché keeps the play Anglophone on screen, and the Soviet evidence supports the thesis with a refinement. The Soviet versions restore another tradition, certainly, a Russian tradition of the play that runs from nineteenth century translation through Tchaikovsky's orchestral Verona, through Pasternak's text and Prokofiev's score, to Lavrovsky's stage and Arnshtam's camera. The refinement is that the restoration arrived wordless. The most Russian screen Romeo and Juliet is the one in which no Russian is spoken, and the paradox is not a defect in the thesis but the thesis sharpened, since the tradition's identity lies in music and dance rather than in the translated word that dominated the domestic stage. A global map of the play on screen that omits the Soviet film misdescribes the map's centre of gravity for the middle twentieth century, when the two festival crowned Veronas of 1954 and 1955 were an Italian spoken film and a Soviet danced one, and when the Soviet film's Juliet was, by the consent of critics across the Cold War divide, the interpretation against which others were measured. The map that includes the film must then answer the harder question of what counts as a version, and the present survey has answered with grades rather than with a single gate. Adaptation, transfer, excerpt. The Soviet tradition holds one adaptation of the first rank, a rich archive of transfers, and excerpts of great beauty, and the honest census honours the tradition more than any inflated catalogue of invented titles could honour the record.

Three critics frame the closing judgement, and the disagreements among the positions deserve a final statement. Jay Leyda's historical frame makes adaptation a central Soviet film practice, which elevates the ballet film from curiosity to exemplar. Clive Barnes's critical frame makes Ulanova's performance the film's justification, which elevates the record of a dancer over questions of cinematic invention. Christina Ezrahti's cultural frame makes the film an instrument of state prestige and cultural diplomacy, which explains the production's resources and export path while risking a reduction of art to policy. The survey adjudicates among the three by assigning each frame the layer the frame explains best. Leyda explains the film's place in a national cinema. Barnes explains the film's hold on viewers who have never entered a ballet theatre. Ezrahti explains why the state paid for the film and sent the film to Cannes. No single frame covers the object, and the object, a choreographer's staging, a composer's score, a ballerina's interpretation and a state's exhibit, all fixed in ninety two minutes of Mosfilm footage, requires all three. Simon Morrison's archival work on the score adds the necessary correction to any frame that treats the film as transparent preservation, since the musical text on screen is Lavrovsky's performing version, negotiated and altered, rather than the score as Prokofiev first composed the music in 1935. Preservation itself, in this tradition, is an act of adaptation, and the film adapts at every layer the film touches.

One further comparison disciplines the closing judgement, the comparison of afterlives. The spoken classics of Soviet Shakespeare, Kozintsev's pair above all, entered world cinema's permanent repertory as directors' films, taught through auteur study and restored as monuments of a filmmaker's vision. The ballet film entered the permanent repertory by a different road, as a performer's film, taught through Ulanova, excerpted for her scenes, and valued in the first instance as the record of an interpretation that no revival can reproduce because the interpreter is gone. Performers' films age differently from directors' films. A director's method can be imitated, extended and argued with by successors, while a performer's summit can only be witnessed, and the witnessing acquires, decade by decade, the character of testimony. The film's custodial history reflects that difference at every stage, since the reissues sell Ulanova, the excerpts cut for Ulanova and the endorsements quote Barnes on Ulanova, while Arnshtam's competent and conservative direction recedes into the credit line. The recession is partly unjust, since the film's scale and clarity are directorial achievements, and the survey has credited the direction at each stage of the argument. The recession is also diagnostic, revealing what the tradition itself values in its foundation, a dancer's reading of Juliet preserved against time. A national screen tradition founded on a performance rather than on a director or a studio style is a rarity in film history, and the rarity completes the Soviet case's claim on attention. Other cinemas founded Shakespeare traditions on studios, on stars of the spoken screen or on national theatre companies filmed in bulk. The Soviet tradition, in this play, rests on one ballerina's interpretation, one choreographer's staging and one composer's score, fused in a single feature and ratified at Cannes, and the fusion has outlasted the state that arranged the meeting.

What remains is the human centre, and the survey ends the argument where the film ends, in the tomb. The play's closing movement, from the Friar's narrative to the Prince's judgement that all are punished in 5.3, depends in spoken performance on explanation, on the unravelling of the letter, the timing and the watch. The ballet film closes without explanation, on bodies and music, and the closing works because Prokofiev's final pages and Lavrovsky's staging carry the moral weight in sound and stillness. Ulanova's Juliet, in the film's last images, completes an arc that began with a child running through a nursery in Leningrad in 1940, in a country about to enter a catastrophic war, and the interpretation's journey from that stage, through the Bolshoi transfer, to a Cannes prize and a permanent film record, is the fullest journey any single reading of Juliet made in the twentieth century. The Soviet and Russian screen tradition is therefore judged a tradition of one great film and its custodial afterlife, narrower than the Anglophone dramatic tradition and, at the centre, fully its equal. The state that censored words exported the story in dance, and the dance, on the evidence of the festival record and the performance record alike, was enough.

## Ulanova's Juliet, Scene by Scene

A performance that carries an entire national screen tradition deserves a scene level account, and Lavrovsky's structure gives the account its stations. The nursery scenes establish the child. Ulanova enters the ballet's Verona as a girl at play with the Nurse, teasing, running, trying on the manners of a womanhood that the ball will impose within the hour, and the dancing keeps the steps small, quick and unformed, a vocabulary of impulse rather than display. The significance for the screen record is that the camera, in close framing, catches the thought behind the impulse, the fraction of hesitation before a run, the glance that tests whether the Nurse is watching. Stage accounts from 1940 onward praise the same quality from the auditorium, where the reading depended on gesture scaled to a large house, and the film's preservation of the quality at close range is one of the strongest arguments for the film as adaptation rather than document, since the lens discovers in the interpretation a register that the stage original could only suggest to the nearest rows.

The ball transforms the register. The Capulet ball, built on the heavy tread of the Dance of the Knights music and on a choreography of formal presentation, places the girl inside a public machine of alliance and threat, and Ulanova's Juliet moves through the ceremony with a dawning separateness that the surrounding ensemble does not share. The first encounter with Romeo, danced to music of suspended quiet at the centre of the ball's noise, establishes the production's central contrast, public Verona against private recognition, and the film holds the contrast in a single edited movement from crowd to couple. The balcony scene then expands the private register into the ballet's most celebrated passage, a pas de deux of lifts and supported turns that Lavrovsky built as a continuous duet of approach and retreat, in which the choreography literalises the play's imagery of ascent and distance. In the spoken text of 2.2, the lovers negotiate names, vows and the danger of the orchard wall across some one hundred and eighty lines of verse. In the ballet, negotiation becomes weight and balance, trust tested in the body, and the film's framing, closer than any theatre seat, makes the trust legible as facial fact. Critics who resist filmed ballet on principle concede the balcony passage in this performance, and the concession marks the limit of the formal objection. A medium that can show trust as balance has found a genuine equivalent for one strand of the verse, even while the verse's other strands, wit, wordplay and argument, fall outside the medium's reach.

The potion scene tests the interpretation at the opposite pole, alone. Juliet's soliloquy in 4.3, with its catalogue of terrors, the vault, the bones, Tybalt's ghost seeking Romeo, is the play's great passage of imagined horror, and the ballet gives the equivalent sequence to a single dancer in a bare room with a vial. Ulanova's performance in the sequence, as stage and screen accounts agree in substance, builds fear through stillness interrupted by recoil, the body arguing with itself in a mime of unusual specificity, approaching the vial, retreating, rehearsing the tomb in gesture before the draught resolves the argument. The film holds the sequence at a length that dramatic cutting might have spared, and the decision vindicates the feature's priorities, since the potion scene is where the interpretation's claim to dramatic, rather than merely lyric, greatness stands or falls. The tomb completes the arc in stillness of another kind. Romeo's arrival, the fight with Paris rendered in compressed form, the poison, Juliet's waking and the dagger, all pass in the ballet's final pages toward a closing tableau that Lavrovsky stages as public discovery, the families and the Prince gathered over the bodies in a composition that returns the private tragedy to the civic frame where the feud began. Ulanova's last passage, the waking and the death, plays against Zhdanov's already still Romeo, and the asymmetry of movement, one body quickening while the other has stopped, gives the film an ending image of unusual clarity. The scene level record supports the verdict delivered at the head of this survey. The interpretation is a complete dramatic reading, built in movement, preserved on film, and no spoken Soviet performance of the play on any screen exists to set against the reading.

## The Camera and the Score: Hearing the Film

The musical argument requires one further passage, kept within the fence that assigns the score's full analysis to a sibling article. On screen, Prokofiev's music performs three jobs that dialogue performs in a spoken version. The music identifies persons, through the themes attached to Juliet in her girlhood, awakening and resolve, through the Romeo material, and through the heavy public themes of the Capulets and the feud. The music argues, since the collision of the ball's ceremonial weight with the lovers' lyric lines states the play's central opposition without a word of exposition. The music remembers, since the return of earlier material in the tomb gathers the evening's meanings into the closing minutes in a manner that spoken film achieves through the Friar's explanatory narrative in 5.3. A spectator who knows the spoken play hears the score performing those jobs in place of familiar speeches, and a spectator who does not know the play receives a coherent symphonic drama whose programme the choreography clarifies. That double address explains the film's festival portability better than any claim about universal language, a phrase the survey avoids as imprecise. The score is not universal. The score is specific, thematic and narrative, and specificity travels because the themes attach to visible persons in visible conflict.

The conducting and recording layer belongs to the film's material history and resists tidy statement, since release versions and reissues differ and since some editions carried narration over the score that remastered editions later removed, as the video documentation attached to the standard release records. The removal matters critically. Narration laid over Prokofiev converts the film toward illustrated lecture and concedes, on the distributor's behalf, a distrust of the wordless form that the film itself never shows. The remastered presentation trusts the original wager, that music and movement suffice, and the wager is the film's thesis in material form. Archival excerpts complicate the sound picture further, since the 1951 bedroom pas de deux recording and the excerpt reels of later decades circulated in varying states, and a rigorous viewing history of Ulanova's Juliet distinguishes the feature's integral musical text from the excerpt tradition's fragments. The distinction returns to the survey's grading discipline. The feature is the adaptation. The excerpts are documents, valuable in proportion to the performances the excerpts preserve, Maximova's scene work, Bessmertnova's, Plisetskaya's ball variation, and subordinate in kind to the feature that Mosfilm built for Cannes.

## Restoration, Reissue and the Custodial Afterlife

The film's afterlife divides into three phases, each documented in outline. The first phase is festival and tour circulation in the middle nineteen fifties, when the Cannes prize, the London private screening for Ashton and the public showings that preceded the Bolshoi's 1956 London season made the film the advance guard of a dance diplomacy offensive. British reception of the 1956 season, in which Ulanova danced Juliet on stage at Covent Garden in the company's historic visit, retroactively raised the film's standing, since the stage sensation confirmed what the film had promised, and the film in turn became the souvenir through which audiences who missed the season, or who lived beyond London, could possess a version of the interpretation. The second phase is the long custodial period of Soviet and post Soviet circulation, in which the film served as the canonical screen Verona for domestic audiences, shown in the context of Ulanova's national monument status, while the stage production continued in the repertories of both founding companies and passed, at the Bolshoi, toward Grigorovich's 1979 successor staging. The third phase is the video and restoration era, in which international labels issued the film for the home market, the Clive Barnes endorsement attached to the release literature carried the performance's reputation to a new generation of viewers, and remastering removed the narration layers of earlier editions. Each phase preserved the film for a different reason, diplomacy, canon and commerce in succession, and the succession explains a survival that chance alone rarely grants to filmed ballet of the period.

The custodial afterlife also governs how the interpretation is studied. Dance scholarship on Ulanova works from the film as the primary moving image evidence of the ballerina's Juliet, supplemented by the 1951 excerpt material and by photographs and written notices of the stage performances, and the hierarchy of evidence deserves stating because the film's dominance can silently rewrite stage history. The film shows the interpretation at forty four or forty five, in a studio, under Arnshtam's framing, with Zhdanov rather than Sergeyev. The stage interpretation of 1940, danced at twenty nine or thirty in a wartime bound Leningrad production, is recoverable only by triangulation among notices, memoirs and the later record. Scholars including Ezrahti and the Russian ballet historians on whose work Anglophone readers depend proceed by that triangulation, and the survey's insistence on layers, choreography, cast, date, venue, is the same method applied to a reference article. A reader who keeps the layers separate can use the film with confidence. A reader who merges the layers inherits the catalogue errors that this survey has corrected at each step, the Bolshoi simplification, the premiere cast slide, the inflated festival claim and the single year dating, all of which arise from treating a layered object as a flat one.

## The Play in Soviet Culture: Why Verona Mattered to the State

One question remains that the production history implies without answering. Why did this play, of all the tragedies, become the Soviet ballet's supreme classic and the Soviet screen's chosen lyric export? The answers layer like the production itself. The story offers a conflict that official reading could frame as social, feudal family tyranny against young feeling, while the feeling at the centre remained private enough to give audiences an interior life on stage that public art rarely supplied. Prokofiev's score, the greatest Soviet ballet score of its generation by broad critical consent, needed a subject of matching scale, and the Verona story, already naturalised in Russian culture through Tchaikovsky's fantasy overture of 1869, revised to its final form by 1880, carried orchestral prestige before a step was danced. The wartime timing of the 1940 premiere fixed the production in national memory as an affirmation of lyric values under threat, and Ulanova's presence converted a successful ballet into a personal mythology shared across the Union. Each layer raised the cost of a rival screen version. A spoken film would have needed a director of Kozintsev's stature, a cast to stand against the ballet's legend, and a reason to exist that the monument did not already supply. No studio found the combination, and the pattern repeated after 1991, when Russian screens, free of every Soviet constraint, still produced transfers and stagings rather than a dramatic feature of the play. Freedom reproduced the Soviet pattern, which is the strongest evidence that the pattern rests on artistic gravity rather than on censorship. The ballet film sits at the centre of the gravity well, and the tradition's shape, one great danced film orbited by records and excerpts, is the shape of that gravity made visible across seventy years.

## Reading the Film Against the Play: Five Sequences Tested

The evidence standard for an adaptation study is sequence level comparison, and five sequences test the film against the spoken text with results that vary instructively. The Prologue comes first. Shakespeare's Chorus frames the action with a sonnet that promises the feud's cost in advance, closing the frame with the phrase misadventured piteous overthrows and with the image of civil blood making civil hands unclean. The ballet film has no Chorus and no sonnet, and Lavrovsky replaces verbal framing with visual framing, a Verona of squares and crowds in which the feud's public mass is present from the first minutes. The test result is a draw with different winnings. The spoken frame gives irony, since the audience watches every joy under a announced death sentence. The danced frame gives sociology, since the audience watches every private scene against a visible civic condition. Arnshtam's crowd staging, expanded for the camera beyond stage limits, presses the sociological reading harder than any proscenium could press the case, and the sequence vindicates the film's method at the exact point where the method abandons the text's most famous device.

The ball tests the method on splendour. The play's 1.5 compresses the meeting into a shared sonnet spoken across a dance floor, fourteen lines in which the lovers' dialogue itself performs the dance that the stage direction only implies. The ballet expands the meeting into the production's set piece, the hall, the ceremonial dances, the heavy tread of the knights' music, and the still centre where the couple meets. Expansion reverses the play's proportions, since Shakespeare hurries the meeting inside a crowded scene while Lavrovsky suspends the scene around the meeting, and the reversal suits film, which can hold a hall and a face in alternating frames. Tybalt's recognition and rage, which the play renders in an exchange with Capulet in 1.5, becomes in the ballet a visible disturbance in the ceremony, legible without the dialogue that explains the restraint imposed on the rage. The sequence shows filmed ballet at a genuine advantage, since the social physics of the scene, attraction forming inside a hostile public order, is choreographic information before verbal information in any medium.

The balcony sequence, tested earlier through Ulanova's performance, gains a further dimension against the text. The spoken scene in 2.2 contains the play's most quoted exchange on names, the rose passage in which Juliet argues that a name is neither hand nor foot nor any part belonging to a person, and the argument is philosophical, an attack on the feud's nominal basis conducted in pure language. Dance cannot argue the point, and the film does not pretend otherwise. What the pas de deux argues instead is the scene's second strand, the risk and the trust, the lover in the orchard exposed to death if discovered, the woman at height directing the terms of the meeting. Lavrovsky's supported lifts literalise exposure and trust with a clarity that verse achieves by statement, and the honest comparison records one strand rendered supremely and one strand surrendered. A survey that claimed equivalence across the whole scene would falsify the record. A survey that scored the scene as loss alone would miss the equivalent that the choreography genuinely finds.

The potion sequence and the tomb close the test with the strongest and weakest results side by side. The potion scene in 4.3 is interior monologue, fear reasoning with itself, and mime is the one screen language built for interior states made physical, so the film's sequence stands among the wordless version's highest achievements, as the scene level account of Ulanova's performance has argued. The tomb in 5.3 reverses the fortune. Shakespeare's closing movement depends on information, the Friar's account, the letter's history, the Prince's distribution of blame and pardon, and the ballet's closing tableau substitutes communal grief for the unravelling, an image of consequence without the causal audit that the play insists on delivering. The image is moving and the audit is missing, and the pair of facts states the film's whole relation to the source in miniature. Where the play thinks in images of feeling, the film equals or exceeds the source's emotional pressure. Where the play thinks in information, the film gestures, and the gesture suffices only for an audience that brings the information from outside the theatre or cinema door. The dependence is the form's constitutional limit, priced into the verdict from the start.

## A Study Guide to the Records: How the Film Is Catalogued

Reference discipline deserves a closing practical section, because the film's record lives in several cataloguing systems whose conventions explain the divisions this survey has reported. Festival records, headed by the Cannes archive and the festival's published award lists, date the work by screening and honour, 1955, and name the directors as the festival named the pair, Arnshtam and Lavrovsky in the French listing's order of Lev and Leonid. Studio and national filmographies date by production and release, which yields the 1954 dating in some catalogues and the May 1955 release date in others. Museum and library catalogues, including the Australian Centre for the Moving Image entry that dates a print to the early nineteen fifties, date by the object in hand, a print or a production file, and can therefore print a year that matches neither the festival nor the release convention. Ballet reference works date by choreography, 1940, and sometimes allow the stage year to colonise the film's entry, the deepest of the layer errors. A researcher who meets four dates for one work is not meeting four works or three errors, but four conventions, and the researcher's task is to name the convention in any citation that carries weight. The present survey has followed that rule throughout, citing the festival year for the film, the production division in the profile, the stage year for the choreography and the transfer year for the Bolshoi layer, and the rule generalises to every layered adaptation in the series.

Cast cataloguing carries the same lesson. The Internet era databases that most readers consult first list the film under the 1955 title with Ulanova and Zhdanov at the head, Radunsky, Yermolayev, Koren, Kudryashov, Loshchilin and Lapauri in support, Chen and Shelenkov for cinematography, Prokofiev for music, and a running time in the low ninety minutes, figures that match the standard filmography used in this survey. Stage databases list the 1940 premiere with Sergeyev and the same choreography, and the reader who merges the two lists manufactures a phantom cast that never danced together in either medium. Award cataloguing requires the final precision. The Cannes honours of 1955 divide by kind in the festival's own taxonomy, with the first Palme d'Or to Marty, dramatic honours to East of Eden and the lyric prize to the Soviet entry, and any citation that promotes the lyric prize into the top honour, or demotes the honour into a mere screening, fails the archive in opposite directions. The survey's formulation, Best Lyrical Film in competition, Palme d'Or nominee by entry, is the strongest statement the record supports, and the statement is strong enough. A danced tragedy from a closed state took a named prize in the first Palme d'Or year, against thirty four competing features, with a forty four year old Juliet and without one word of dialogue. The record needs no inflation, and the tradition built on the record needs no apology.

## Western Reception: London, the Bolshoi Season and the Long Judgement

The Western reception of the film unfolded in a sequence that ballet history can reconstruct in outline with confidence. The Cannes prize of May 1955 gave the work a festival credential that no closed state production could take for granted, and the prize placed a Soviet lyric work inside the first Palme d'Or year's honours list, in the company of Marty and East of Eden, titles that anchored the year's dramatic cinema. The London private screening for Ashton in the summer of 1955 then aimed the film at the single most influential choreographic intelligence in Britain, a piece of targeting that scholarship on Lavrovsky's Western legacy has preserved because the episode reveals intention. Soviet cultural officials understood that Ashton, fresh from a Danish chamber version of the same story, embodied the alternative Western path for the ballet, intimate, literary and scaled to a small company, and the screening asserted the opposite path, monumental, narrative and scaled to a state company, in the rival's own capital. Public London exhibition followed in 1956, ahead of the Bolshoi's visit, and the visit itself, the company's first London season, turned Ulanova's stage Juliet into the sensation against which British ballet memory still measures visiting performances. The film rode both waves, first as promise and then as record, and British viewers who could not obtain Covent Garden seats encountered the interpretation through the film's circulation. Reception history rarely offers so clean a relay between cinema and stage, and the relay strengthened each leg, since the film advertised the season and the season authenticated the film.

Longer judgement moved through phases that the survey can summarise without pretending to a completeness the record does not offer. The first phase, in the nineteen fifties, treated the film as revelation, a window into a closed dance culture whose standards Western critics, Barnes among them in the verdicts that later attached to the film's releases, rated at the summit of lyric performance. The second phase, from the nineteen sixties onward, complicated the choreography's reputation as Western taste absorbed the MacMillan generation's psychological realism and began to find Lavrovsky's mime vocabulary earnest and his narrative manner monumental in a pejorative sense, the museum piece charge that later reviewing aimed at stage revivals. The third phase, the video and restoration era, separated the two objects that the second phase had merged. The stage production, revived across decades, could fairly be judged as a living repertory piece competing with newer choreography. The film, judged as a historical record of a specific interpretation at a specific summit, recovered value precisely as its documentary character strengthened with age. The adjudication offered in the form section holds under this longer view. The museum charge lands on revivals that ask a 1940 vocabulary to behave as contemporary theatre. The charge misses the film, whose contract with a viewer includes preservation, and whose Juliet remains, in Barnes's durable word, unforgettable in the only court that finally decides such matters, repeated viewing across generations.

## The Counterfactual Tested: A Spoken Soviet Version

Adaptation history gains rigour from one controlled counterfactual, stated as counterfactual and tested against the documented capacities of the studios. Suppose Lenfilm or Mosfilm had mounted a spoken Romeo and Juliet in the nineteen fifties, on Pasternak's text, with a director of the first rank. The ingredients existed. Pasternak's version was ready and honoured. The stage tradition supplied trained Shakespeare actors in depth. Kozintsev demonstrated, within the decade, that a Soviet director could make Shakespeare films of world standing, and Yutkevich's Othello and Frid's Twelfth Night proved the studios would fund spoken Shakespeare at feature scale. Against the ingredients stand the obstacles that the record makes visible. The ballet film's prestige occupied the story's screen place with the state's blessing, and a spoken version would have been reviewed domestically as a challenge to a monument rather than as an addition to a shelf. The export logic ran against spoken Russian drama, as the Cannes analysis has shown. The political weather around Pasternak darkened across the decade toward the Zhivago crisis, raising the risk attached to any prestige production built on the poet's name. The counterfactual therefore fails on institutional grounds rather than artistic ones. The Soviet system could have made a fine spoken Verona and had every incentive not to try, and the distinction between capacity and incentive is the precise point at which the tradition's shape becomes explicable. Post Soviet history supplies the control case. After 1991 the incentives of state prestige vanished, the monument lost official protection, and Russian screens still produced no spoken feature of standing, which suggests that the ballet's artistic gravity, Ulanova's shadow and the story's settled place in dance repertory continued to do the work that incentives once did. The counterfactual, tested both ways, returns the survey to the verdict with added confidence. The absence of a spoken tradition is overdetermined, by prestige, by export, by politics and finally by habit, and the single danced film at the centre is not an accident of censorship but the stable outcome of a culture that had already decided where the play lived on screen.

## Character Without Words: The Supporting Cast as Argument

The supporting performances repay attention because filmed ballet stands or falls, after the lovers, on the legibility of the world around the lovers. Radunsky's Capulet carries the household's public face, a host whose ceremonial command at the ball and whose rage at the proposed Paris marriage in the later scenes must read as one continuous character, authority curdling into tyranny, and the mime vocabulary of drambalet, built for exactly such arcs, gives the film a Capulet whose transformation a first time viewer can follow without a synopsis. The play's Capulet turns on language, the choleric improvisation of 3.5 in which the father threatens disownment in a flood of insult, and the ballet compresses the turn into stance and gesture, the raised hand, the turned back, the household recoiling in formation. Compression loses the insult and keeps the structure, and the structure, a protector becoming a persecutor inside one scene, is the tragedy's domestic engine. Yermolayev's Tybalt performs the feud's other engine. A dancer of the heroic Soviet generation, Yermolayev brings weight and seniority to a role that spoken productions usually give to youth, and the casting choice darkens the character, since the violence reads as established habit rather than hot blood, a professional of the feud rather than a victim of temperament. The fight choreography then pits that weight against Koren's Mercutio, speed against mass, and the outcome, Mercutio's fall under Romeo's intervening arm in the ballet's rendering of 3.1, lands with the play's own moral geometry, the waverer causing the wound that the waverer's curse then answers. The curse itself, the plague invocation that spoken audiences quote, exists in the film only as gesture toward the two households, and the gesture suffices for viewers who know the line while reminding viewers who do not that a layer of verbal iron has been traded away.

Lapauri's Paris and Loshchilin's Friar mark the form's outer limits in opposite directions. Paris, in the play, is a function with a death scene, the approved suitor whose killing by Romeo in 5.3 darkens the tomb, and the ballet keeps the function and the death while giving the dancer little interior material, a limit that the film honestly exposes rather than disguises. The Friar presents the harder problem, since the character exists to explain, to devise the potion plan in 4.1, to narrate the failure in 5.3, and explanation is the one dramatic substance mime cannot carry. Lavrovsky's solution, visible in the film, reduces the Friar to a benign ceremonial presence, blessing, giving, mourning, and transfers the explanatory burden to the audience's prior knowledge. The reduction is the film's most exposed adaptation decision, and the survey records the decision as the point where the wordless method pays full price. A spoken version's Friar can be tedious and still be functional. A danced Friar is graceful and partly decorative, and the plot's machinery, the letter, the timing, the waking, floats free of an agent. That the film survives the reduction testifies to the strength of the lovers' arc and the feud's spectacle, the two elements the method handles supremely, and the survival should not be mistaken for a refutation of the limit. The limit stands, priced into the verdict, as the tomb sequence analysis has already established.

## Teaching and Studying the Soviet Screen Record

The film's study value extends beyond ballet history, and a closing account of the uses completes the survey's practical obligations. For Shakespeare studies, the film supplies the cleanest available test of adaptation without language, a control case against which spoken versions reveal their own choices, since every decision that Zeffirelli or Luhrmann made about verse, casting and realism becomes visible only when a major version exists that refused verse altogether. Seminar comparison of the balcony sequence across the ballet film and the spoken films isolates, in one scene, the difference between verbal and choreographic argument more efficiently than any theoretical text on adaptation. For film studies, the work documents a state studio's approach to literary prestige at the height of the Cold War, with a festival strategy, a star vehicle for a national artist and a literary source of unimpeachable safety combined in a single production, and Leyda's history gives students the national cinema frame within which the combination made sense. For dance studies, the film is primary evidence, the fullest moving image record of Lavrovsky's drambalet at its summit and of Ulanova's artistry in a complete role, evidence whose layered character, studio conditions, later cast, compressed score, trains the exact source criticism that performance history requires. For Russian and Soviet studies, the production opens the cultural politics of Shakespeare's second home, the translation culture around Pasternak, the prize culture around the Stalin honours of 1940 and 1946, and the diplomacy culture that sent a wordless tragedy to Cannes in the first Palme d'Or year. Few single films serve four disciplines without strain, and the service record explains why the work holds a place in reference accounts of screen Shakespeare far beyond the ballet audience. The study uses also impose the survey's final caution. Each discipline is tempted to flatten the layered object into the discipline's preferred layer, score for the musicologists, choreography for the dance historians, diplomacy for the political historians, Ulanova for the biographers, and the complete account, the one this survey has attempted, keeps every layer in view at once, because the tradition itself was built by the layers acting together.

## The Verona the Film Built: Design, Photography and Scale

The physical Verona of the film deserves a section of its own, because the built environment carries a share of the adaptation argument that performance analysis alone cannot settle. Pyotr Vilyams, who designed the 1940 stage production, belonged to the generation of Soviet theatre designers who treated a classic's world as a coherent pictorial system, architecture, costume and crowd dress unified by a single historical imagination, Renaissance Italy filtered through Soviet monumental taste. The film inherits that system's logic while rebuilding the world at camera scale, streets and squares that extend beyond a stage's sightlines, a Capulet hall whose depth the choreography of the ball requires and whose columns and galleries give the ceremonial dances a civic frame. The effect differs from the Italian spoken cinema of the same decade in a way that comparison makes plain. Castellani's production, within the Italian article's custody in detail, pursued location authenticity in Italian towns. The Mosfilm Verona pursues studio coherence, a city built to be danced in, where every square is also a stage and every staircase a choreographic opportunity. Neither approach is a failure of the other. Each serves a different Verona, the spoken film's city of stumbled encounters and the ballet film's city of patterned masses, and the Soviet choice, judged on the film's own terms, matches the drambalet aesthetic at the level of bricks and paint.

The photography, credited to Yu Lan Chen and Aleksandr Shelenkov, works under the constraints that ballet imposes on cameras, since choreography reads through full bodies in continuous motion and punishes the fragmentation that dramatic editing takes for granted. The film's solution is a predominantly respectful frame, wide enough for ensemble geometry, moving closer for the lovers and for Ulanova's solo passages, and the balance struck is the balance that filmed ballet has sought ever since, between the dance's demand for the whole figure and the cinema's demand for the face. The colour stock, in the Soviet colour process of the period, gives the production a saturation that later viewers meet through restoration with varying fidelity, and reference accounts treat the film as a colour production whose palette, deep reds for the Capulet world against cooler tones elsewhere, participates in the feud's visual coding. A rigorous account adds the material caution that prints and transfers differ, that the narration question in the sound track has a visual parallel in variable colour across editions, and that judgements of the photography should name the edition viewed, as judgements of the dating should name the convention used. Material discipline of that kind is unglamorous and decisive, since the film's reputation has passed through enough transfers, excerpts and reissues for two viewers to hold opinions of visibly different objects while citing the same title. The survey's claims have aimed at the integral feature in its standard form, ninety two minutes, colour, the Lavrovsky musical text in compressed form, and the claims should be tested against that object rather than against gala excerpts or narrated editions.

Scale completes the design argument. The crowd scenes, expanded for the studio beyond the Bolshoi's stage complement, give the feud a demographic reality that spoken productions achieve through report, the Chorus's two households made literal as two populations. When the market place fills with fighting dancers, the film states, in purely visual terms, the thesis that Soviet criticism prized in the play, the feud as a social condition consuming a city, and the statement requires no Pasternak line, no programme note and no prior knowledge beyond the evidence of the frame. That capacity, spectacle as analysis, is the strongest answer the film offers to the charge of being a stage record, since no stage record expands a crowd beyond the stage's payroll or holds a city in one composition. Arnshtam's Verona is built, photographed and populated as cinema, conservative in editing grammar and ambitious in physical scale, and the combination defines the Soviet screen contribution with final precision. The words stayed in the theatre, in Pasternak's Russian, in hundreds of spoken productions. The city, the crowd, the score and Ulanova came to the screen, and the screen has kept them ever since.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Which Mosfilm ballet production carries the Soviet screen tradition of the tragedy?

The main Soviet film is Romeo i Dzhulyetta, the Mosfilm ballet film directed by Lev Arnshtam and Leonid Lavrovsky and released in 1955, with production dating to 1954 in some reputable catalogues. The film adapts Lavrovsky's staging of Prokofiev's ballet, with Galina Ulanova as Juliet and Yuri Zhdanov as Romeo. No spoken Soviet dramatic feature of the play stands in the standard catalogues, so the ballet film carries the whole feature tradition alone, and the film's Cannes prize and Ulanova's interpretation give that solitary position a weight that a merely default first place would never supply.

### Q: Who directed the Soviet ballet film of the play?

Direction is credited jointly to Lev Arnshtam and Leonid Lavrovsky. Arnshtam, the Mosfilm film director, rebuilt the stage action for the camera, with studio Verona exteriors, expanded crowd scenes and edited compression to a running time of about ninety two minutes. Lavrovsky, the choreographer, supplied the staging itself, created for the Kirov Theatre in 1940 and transferred to the Bolshoi in 1946. The joint credit records the film's double nature accurately, since the work is neither a pure stage document nor a wholly original screen conception, and reference works that name only one director flatten the production's actual division of labour.

### Q: Which theatre created the staging that the film preserves?

The Kirov Theatre in Leningrad created the staging, where Lavrovsky's production premiered on 11 January 1940 with designs by Pyotr Vilyams, Ulanova as Juliet and Konstantin Sergeyev as Romeo. The production won the Stalin Prize and became the defining example of drambalet, the Soviet dramatic ballet. Lavrovsky then carried the production to the Bolshoi Theatre in Moscow in 1946, after becoming the company's chief ballet master, and the film's cast belongs to that Bolshoi layer. Short references that call the film simply a Bolshoi film or simply a Kirov film each capture one layer and miss the other.

### Q: Who dances Juliet and Romeo in the 1955 film?

Galina Ulanova dances Juliet and Yuri Zhdanov dances Romeo. Ulanova had created Juliet at the Kirov premiere in 1940, opposite Konstantin Sergeyev, and had carried the role to the Bolshoi in the 1946 transfer, so the film preserves an interpretation refined across some fourteen years rather than a first attempt. Zhdanov, born in 1925, belonged to the younger Bolshoi generation and plays Romeo as a lyric partner to Ulanova's inward reading. The film does not preserve Sergeyev's Romeo, a distinction that matters whenever the film is described as a record of the premiere, since choreography and cast carry different dates.

### Q: What exactly did the Soviet film win at Cannes?

The film won Best Lyrical Film, the Prix du film lyrique, at the eighth Cannes festival, held from 26 April to 10 May 1955. The film screened in the main competition, so the film also stands in the record as a Palme d'Or nominee, while the Palme d'Or itself went to Marty and the prize for Best Dramatic Film went to East of Eden. Claims that the film won the festival's top prize overstate the record, and claims that the film merely screened understate the honour, since the category prize recognised lyric cinema as a distinct festival kind and fitted a danced tragedy with unusual precision.

### Q: Why is the film dated 1954 in some sources and 1955 in others?

Catalogues divide because Soviet film dating can follow the year of production, the year of domestic release or the year of foreign festival exposure, and reference works choose differently. The dominant filmography and the Cannes record give 1955, with a release date often printed as 20 May 1955, while reputable museum cataloguing dates the production to 1954, and at least one major collection prints an earlier production year still. The division concerns dating convention rather than identity, since every catalogue describes the same Mosfilm film. Arguments that depend on a single year should name the convention in use, and the present survey cites the film by the festival year while recording the division openly.

### Q: Did the Soviet Union make a spoken film of the play?

No spoken Soviet dramatic feature of the play stands in the standard catalogues. Soviet studios filmed Shakespeare with distinction in other titles, Grigori Kozintsev's Hamlet in 1964 and King Lear in 1973 for Lenfilm, Sergei Yutkevich's Othello in 1955 and Yan Frid's Twelfth Night in the same year, and the Verona tragedy is the conspicuous absence in that cluster. The absence reflects displacement rather than prohibition, since Lavrovsky's ballet and Ulanova's Juliet already gave the state a Verona of the highest prestige, and no document records a ban on a spoken version. The ballet claimed the story first, at the highest level, and dramatic directors chose other plays.

### Q: How does Sergei Koren's Mercutio work without spoken wit?

The film renders Mercutio through choreographic characterisation, with Sergei Koren dancing the role as brilliance, speed and recklessness made visible. The Queen Mab speech in 1.4, a verbal miniature of dreams and violence, has no mime equivalent, and the film accepts that loss as a cost of the form. What survives is the character's function, the wit and daring that draw Romeo toward the ball and toward the fatal fight, expressed in attack and timing rather than rhetoric. The trade defines filmed ballet generally, since the form keeps Prokofiev's musical characterisation while surrendering Shakespeare's verbal characterisation entire.

### Q: What is drambalet, and why does the term matter for the film?

Drambalet, the dramatic ballet, was the Soviet style that subordinated virtuoso display to continuous acted narrative, with characters whose movement carried psychological motive and crowd scenes staged like crowds in a spoken production. Christina Ezrahti's Swans of the Kremlin and Elizabeth Souritz's history of Soviet choreography document the style and the institutional pressures behind the programme. Lavrovsky's Verona production became the style's defining example, since the feud gains physical mass in the market fights and the Capulet ball. The term matters for the film because drambalet explains both the staging's narrative clarity, which lets a wordless film hold a complex plot, and the earnest mime vocabulary that later Western taste sometimes dismissed as dated.

### Q: How did Prokofiev and Lavrovsky disagree over the ballet?

The disagreements concerned the ending, the ordering and the expansion of the score. Prokofiev's 1935 conception ended with the lovers alive, on the stated ground that living people can dance, and colleagues including the conductor Yuri Fayer pressed for the tragic ending that tradition required. Lavrovsky then altered the performing version further, adding variations for the lovers and reweighting numbers to serve stage action, against the composer's documented resistance. Simon Morrison's archival scholarship established the scale of those interventions. The stage verdict went to Lavrovsky, whose production succeeded completely, while the concert afterlife of the suites partly restored the composer's ordering, and the film ratifies the choreographer's version.

### Q: Is the film a Bolshoi film or a Kirov film?

The film is both, in layers. The choreography was created for the Kirov Theatre in Leningrad and premiered there on 11 January 1940. Lavrovsky transferred the production to the Bolshoi Theatre in Moscow in 1946, and the film's leading dancers, Ulanova and Zhdanov among them, belonged to the Bolshoi layer when Mosfilm produced the film. The excerpt language that calls the work a filmed Bolshoi ballet therefore captures the performing layer while missing the Leningrad origin, and the precise statement names all three institutions, Kirov for creation, Bolshoi for the film's company character, and Mosfilm for production. Layer precision prevents the commonest error in short accounts of the film.

### Q: What role does Pasternak's translation play in the screen tradition?

Pasternak's translation, made in the early nineteen forties within the sequence of Shakespeare versions that occupied the poet from 1940 to 1949, became the era's standard Russian stage text of the play, and that authority made Shakespeare a safe prestige subject across Soviet culture. The ballet film, being wordless, uses no translation at all, and claims that the film rests on Pasternak's text collapse on contact with the form. Pasternak's words did reach Soviet screens in other plays, since Kozintsev built Hamlet and King Lear on the translations, so the Verona case stands out precisely because the strongest verbal text available went unused by the screen, displaced by choreographic wealth and export logic.

### Q: How long is the film, and how much of the score survives?

Standard catalogues give a running time of about ninety two minutes, roughly half the span of a full stage performance of Prokofiev's score. The film therefore shortens numbers and omits passages to hold a feature length lyrical shape, and the musical text on screen is the Lavrovsky performing version in compressed form rather than a complete ballet. Concert minded criticism has counted the compression against the film, while the Cannes jury, judging a lyrical film rather than a complete stage record, weighed the concentration differently. The trade is the film's clearest cost and clearest gain in one, since the lovers' arc plays without interval and the feud sequences gain momentum from the tighter frame.

### Q: What Soviet television record of the play exists?

The television record consists of transfers, studio recordings and excerpts rather than a dramatic adaptation tradition. Soviet and post Soviet television circulated Lavrovsky's choreography through filmed stage performances and through excerpt material danced by successive generations, with catalogued passages showing Ekaterina Maximova and Natalia Bessmertnova in scenes from the ballet, Maya Plisetskaya in ball excerpts from around 1960, and a bedroom pas de deux with Ulanova and Mikhail Gabovich surviving from a 1951 recording. Later transfers preserved complete stage stagings, including Yuri Grigorovich's Bolshoi version first staged in 1979. Those materials document a stage tradition on screen at high distinction without founding a television dramatic version of the play.

### Q: How did Frederick Ashton respond to the Soviet film?

Ashton attended a private screening of the film at the Soviet Embassy in London in the summer of 1955, an episode retold in scholarship on Lavrovsky's legacy published by the Australian Ballet. Ashton had made a chamber Romeo and Juliet for the Royal Danish Ballet in that same year, and the Soviet hosts, recognising a creative figurehead of British ballet, presented Lavrovsky's large scale drambalet as a demonstration of the Soviet manner. The screening functioned as cultural diplomacy aimed at a single influential viewer, ahead of the film's public London showing and the Bolshoi's 1956 London season, where Ulanova danced Juliet on stage. The episode shows the film working as an instrument of policy without reducing the dancing to policy.

### Q: Why did a wordless film suit Soviet cultural export?

A wordless film crossed borders without subtitles, dubbing or translation politics. Prokofiev's score carried character and conflict in a musical language that festival audiences received directly, Ulanova's performance needed no linguistic mediation, and the story was already known across Europe through stage and concert tradition. A spoken version would have carried Pasternak's name and the frictions gathering around the poet in the years before the Doctor Zhivago crisis, besides the ordinary resistance of distributors to subtitled drama. Christina Ezrahti's account of ballet as Russian cultural diplomacy supplies the frame, and the Cannes category prize of 1955 stands as the export strategy's documented return on a single title.

### Q: How does the Soviet film compare with Castellani's 1954 film?

The two films give opposite answers to the same question in consecutive festival seasons. Castellani's spoken Romeo and Juliet took the Golden Lion at Venice in 1954 with a dramatic rendering in English, while the Soviet ballet film took Best Lyrical Film at Cannes in 1955 with a wordless rendering in dance. The comparison is structural rather than a contest of quality. The Italian film keeps Shakespeare's language and solves the problem of verse in a realist medium, while the Soviet film keeps Prokofiev's music and solves the problem of plot information through mime and programme knowledge. The detailed Italian record belongs to the Castellani article's custody, and the present survey claims only the symmetry, which no account of the play's screen decade can responsibly omit.

### Q: How did the Lavrovsky staging fare across the decades after the Mosfilm release?

The staging continued as a repertory monument in both founding cities, with the Bolshoi line passing in time to Yuri Grigorovich's new production of 1979, choreographed after Lavrovsky, while the Lavrovsky version held its canonical place in the Kirov and Mariinsky tradition and in revival. The film itself entered a custodial afterlife of reissue and video circulation, with remastered editions removing narration that some release versions had laid over the score. Ulanova's interpretation remained the reference screen Juliet for Russian audiences, unchallenged by any post Soviet spoken production, and the film's festival and performance records kept the work in international circulation as the standard exhibit of Soviet screen Shakespeare in the Verona story.

### Q: Who were the film's supporting dancers, and why do the names matter?

The standard cast list gives Aleksandr Radunsky as Lord Capulet, Aleksey Yermolayev as Tybalt, Sergei Koren as Mercutio, Aleksandr Lapauri as Paris, V. Kudryashov as Benvolio and L. Loshchilin as Friar Laurence. The names matter because the cast fixes the film in the Bolshoi of its decade and because the supporting performances carry the feud's credibility at close range, with Yermolayev's heavyweight Tybalt making violence legible and Koren's Mercutio answering, in movement, the question of how a wordless film handles the play's most verbal character. Cast precision also protects the record, since reference works sometimes merge the film's cast with the 1940 premiere cast, whose Romeo was Konstantin Sergeyev rather than Zhdanov.

### Q: How should the Soviet tradition be judged against the Anglophone films?

The tradition should be judged as narrower and, at the centre, fully comparable. The Anglophone screen tradition offers many spoken versions across a century, with a depth of bench that Russian screen Shakespeare in this play never attempted. The Soviet tradition offers one feature adaptation of the first rank, a rich archive of stage transfers, and excerpts of great beauty, all organised around Ulanova's Juliet, an interpretation that critics across the Cold War divide measured others against. A global account that omits the Soviet film misdescribes the middle twentieth century, when Venice crowned a spoken Verona in 1954 and Cannes crowned a danced Verona in 1955. Breadth belongs to the Anglophone record. The single highest concentration of prestige, in one title, belongs to the Soviet record.
