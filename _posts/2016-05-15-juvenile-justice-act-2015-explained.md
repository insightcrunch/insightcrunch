The Juvenile Justice Act 2015 asks one statute to do two jobs that share almost nothing. For a child who has been abandoned, trafficked, beaten, put to work or left without anyone to feed her, the Act is a welfare code: it builds committees, homes, foster care, sponsorship and adoption, and its whole object is to get the child safe and then get her back to a family. For a child accused of theft, assault or murder, the same Act is a criminal procedure code: it builds boards, inquiries, bail rules, an offence classification and a mechanism by which a sixteen-year-old accused of a grave offence can be moved out of the juvenile system and tried as an adult. The two systems run through the same district, use some of the same buildings and staff, and are governed by one set of principles, yet they answer different questions, apply different tests and end in different kinds of order. Nearly every confusion about this statute, in public argument and in practice, begins with treating it as one system rather than two.

![The Juvenile Justice Act 2015 and its two tracks of care and offence proceedings before Boards and Committees - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-55.webp)

One further peculiarity has to be stated at the outset, because it is the kind of trap this series exists to mark. The statute is called the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015, and 2015 is the year in its short title, the year of its passage and the year searchers use. Its Act number, however, is Act 2 of 2016. The Bill cleared the Rajya Sabha on 22 December 2015, received the assent of the President on 31 December 2015, and was published as the second Act of the following year; it was brought into force on 15 January 2016 by notification. Anyone citing the statute as Act 43 of 2015, or hunting for it in the 2015 volume of the Gazette, is looking in the wrong year. The Act of 2015 is, in the official numbering of Indian legislation, an Act of 2016, and every citation in this article follows the official number.

## What stood before, and why Parliament replaced it

India has legislated for children in trouble for well over a century, through reformatory schools legislation in the colonial period and then a patchwork of state children Acts, each with its own age limits and institutions, so that a child's treatment depended on which side of a state border the child stood. A central Juvenile Justice Act in 1986 gave the subject a uniform frame, and the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2000, Act 56 of 2000, rebuilt that frame after India acceded to the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child on 11 December 1992. The 2000 Act fixed eighteen as the age of a child across the country, created Juvenile Justice Boards for children in conflict with law and Child Welfare Committees for children in need of care and protection, and adopted the vocabulary of care rather than punishment. It was amended in 2006 and 2011, principally to tighten procedures and time limits.

The 2000 Act's own reforms are worth isolating, because the 2015 Act kept its skeleton and replaced much of its muscle. It displaced a landscape in which a central Children Act of 1960 governed the Union territories and state children Acts governed elsewhere, with the age of a juvenile differing not only between states but, under the 1986 Act and several of the state Acts, between boys and girls, a boy passing out of the juvenile system at sixteen while a girl remained within it until eighteen. The 2000 Act fixed a single age of eighteen for every child in the country, replaced the juvenile courts of the earlier law with Boards in which social workers sat beside the magistrate, created Child Welfare Committees as the care track's decision maker in place of the courts that had handled neglected children, substituted the language of the child in conflict with law for the language of the delinquent juvenile, and organised the institutions into the observation homes, special homes and children's homes whose descendants the present Act retains. What it did not do, and what a decade of implementation exposed, was to grade offences, to give the registration of institutions a penal edge, to create offences against children of its own, or to discipline its own processes with the layered timelines and pendency reviews the successor statute builds. Its gravest gap, in the event, was the one its drafters had regarded as its principle: an absolute bar on treating any person below eighteen otherwise than as a child, with a single maximum disposition of three years in a special home however grave the act and however close to eighteen the actor. The 2013 and 2014 litigation showed the courts would not bend that bar; the politics of the Delhi case showed the bar would not survive unchanged; and the 2015 Act's transfer mechanism is the precise, narrow shape of the bend Parliament eventually chose, which is why understanding what the 2000 Act was, and what it refused to be, is the necessary preliminary to judging its successor.

By the middle of the following decade the 2000 Act was under pressure from two directions at once. From the care side, the record of unregistered and uninspected institutions, of committees that existed on paper and boards that sat irregularly, had been documented in inspection after inspection, and the statute's registration and monitoring provisions were widely treated as the weakest part of its design. From the offence side, a single case had become a national argument. In December 2012, one of the persons who committed the gang rape and murder in Delhi that convulsed the country was a few months short of eighteen. Tried under the 2000 Act, he received the maximum the statute allowed, three years in a special home, while his adult co-accused faced the gallows. The political demand that followed was not for a better welfare code. It was for a route by which an older child accused of a grave offence could be tried as an adult, and that demand, carried through an election and a change of government, is what the 2015 Act answered. The passage debate itself, and the question whether that answer was justified, belong to the article on the [juvenile age debate in India](/2016/06/01/juvenile-age-debate-india/); what matters here is the mechanism Parliament built and the rest of the statute that travelled with it.

The Bill was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 12 August 2014 by the Minister of Women and Child Development, Maneka Gandhi, passed the Lok Sabha on 7 May 2015, and was examined in between by the parliamentary Standing Committee on Human Resource Development, which advised against the adult-trial mechanism. Parliament enacted it anyway. Alongside the transfer provision, the 2015 Act rewrote the care side: it made registration of every child care institution mandatory with a real penalty behind it, created a full chapter of offences against children, gave the Central Adoption Resource Authority a statutory basis, inserted timelines into both tracks, and restated the governing principles of the system in a single section. Section 111 repealed the 2000 Act, deeming things done under it to have been done under the corresponding provisions of the new one, and Section 25 confirms that cases pending on commencement continue under the old law, a transition rule whose consequences the comparison of the two statutes takes up in [the 2000 and 2015 Acts compared](/2016/08/15/juvenile-justice-2000-vs-2015/).

## One title, two statutes: the architecture of the Juvenile Justice Act 2015

The Act is arranged in ten chapters and 112 sections. Chapter I defines. Chapter II states sixteen general principles. Chapters III and IV build and regulate the offence track: the Juvenile Justice Board and the procedure for a child in conflict with law. Chapters V and VI do the same for the care track: the Child Welfare Committee and the procedure for a child in need of care and protection. Chapter VII covers rehabilitation and social re-integration, including the registration of institutions. Chapter VIII is a self-contained adoption code. Chapter IX creates offences against children. Chapter X gathers the miscellaneous machinery, from the District Child Protection Unit to the rule-making powers. The frame within which both tracks operate is drawn by [the guide to child protection laws in India](/2016/04/01/child-protection-laws-india-guide/), which sets this Act beside the protection of children from sexual offences legislation, the child labour statutes and the commissions for protection of child rights; this article stays inside the one statute.

### Why does one Act run two separate systems?

Because the two groups of children need opposite things from the state. A child in need of care and protection has committed no offence and is owed rescue, shelter and restoration to a family. A child in conflict with law is accused of an offence and is owed a fair, speedy, non-stigmatising inquiry. One system protects; the other adjudicates.

The two categories are defined separately in Section 2, and the definitions do different work. A child, by Section 2(12), is a person who has not completed eighteen years of age. A child in conflict with law, by Section 2(13), is a child who is alleged or found to have committed an offence and who had not completed eighteen years on the date of commission of the offence. The date that matters is therefore the date of the act, not the date of arrest, charge or trial; a person of twenty who is apprehended for something done at seventeen is dealt with under this Act, and Section 6 says so expressly. A child in need of care and protection, by Section 2(14), is defined by an enumerated list of situations rather than by a single test: a child found without any home or settled place of abode and without ostensible means of subsistence; a child found working in contravention of labour laws, or found begging, or living on the street; a child living with a person who has injured, exploited, abused or neglected the child, or who has threatened to do so with a reasonable likelihood of the threat being carried out, or who has killed or abused another child; a child who is mentally ill or physically challenged or suffering from a terminal disease with no one to support him; a child whose parent or guardian is unfit or incapacitated; a child without parents, or whose parents have abandoned or surrendered him; a missing or runaway child whose parents cannot be found; a child who has been or is likely to be abused, tortured or exploited for sexual abuse or illegal acts; a child vulnerable to induction into drug abuse or trafficking; a child being abused for unconscionable gains; a child who is a victim of armed conflict, civil unrest or natural calamity; and a child at imminent risk of marriage before the lawful age of marriage. The breadth of that list is the point. The care track is not a residual category for the destitute; it is a jurisdiction over vulnerability, and most children whom the statute touches enter through it.

The two tracks can also meet in one child. A runaway picked up for theft may be both accused and abandoned, and the Act anticipates the overlap: Section 8 requires the Board, where a child before it appears also to be in need of care and protection, to transfer the care dimension to the Committee, and Section 17 requires the Board that finds a child has committed no offence, but is in need of care, to refer the child across. The district apparatus, the District Child Protection Unit established under Section 106 as the focal point for implementation in the district, serves both. What the tracks do not share is a destination: the offence track ends in an order about responsibility for an act, the care track in an order about where and with whom a child will live.

Section 3 then lays sixteen principles over both tracks, and they are drafted as directions to the governments, the Board, the Committee and every other agency administering the Act. They include the presumption of innocence of any mala fide or criminal intent up to eighteen; dignity and worth; participation, the child's right to be heard in every process affecting him; best interest as the primary consideration in every decision; family responsibility, placing primary care on the biological, adoptive or foster family; safety; positive measures; non-stigmatising semantics, a bar on adversarial or accusatory words in processes concerning a child; non-waiver of rights; equality and non-discrimination; privacy and confidentiality throughout the judicial process; institutionalisation as a measure of last resort; repatriation and restoration to the family at the earliest; the principle of fresh start, that all past records of a child under the system should be erased except in special circumstances; diversion away from judicial proceedings; and the principles of natural justice. These are not decoration. The fresh start principle is what Section 24 later enforces by destroying records, and the last-resort principle is the standard against which every institutional placement under either track is supposed to be measured.

The single table below is the article's map of the whole statute, and it is worth pausing on, because most practical errors in this field are track errors: a child produced before the wrong body, a committee asked to punish, a board asked to place a child for adoption. The two systems are set side by side so that any child can be located in the right one.

The constitutional ground had been tested before Parliament moved. Under the 2000 Act, the uniform age of eighteen was challenged as arbitrary and as a denial of justice to victims, and in Salil Bali v. Union of India in 2013 the Supreme Court rejected the challenge and upheld the eighteen year line as a constitutionally permissible classification resting on the Convention and on the developmental character of childhood. A further attempt to have the age read down, in proceedings brought by Subramanian Swamy after the Delhi case, was also declined in 2014, the Court treating the fixing of the age as a matter for the legislature. The 2015 Act is therefore best read as Parliament exercising a choice the courts had twice told it was its own to make: it kept eighteen as the age of a child, kept the Board as the forum, and carved a single exception, for a single grade of offence, through a screening mechanism that did not exist anywhere in the earlier law. Nothing else about the offence track's philosophy changed. The vocabulary remained that of conflict with law rather than delinquency, the orders remained reformative rather than punitive, and the maximum the Board itself can impose stayed at three years in a special home, exactly where the 2000 Act had left it.

The sixteen principles of Section 3 repay attention as working instructions rather than preamble, because later provisions keep executing them. The principle of non-stigmatising semantics is the reason the statute speaks of a child in conflict with law and a child found to have committed an offence, and never of an accused, a convict or a delinquent; reports and orders that use the older vocabulary are not merely impolite, they are off the statute's text. The principle of diversion directs that measures for dealing with children in conflict with law without resorting to judicial proceedings be promoted unless diversion is against the best interest of the child or of society, and it is the principle behind the Board's power to close a matter with advice, admonition and counselling rather than a placement. The principle of participation is executed by the duties, repeated through both tracks, to hear the child and to take his views into account with due regard to age and maturity, and it is why Section 37 requires the Committee to weigh the wishes of a child mature enough to form a view before choosing between restoration and placement. The principles of privacy and confidentiality are executed by Section 74's identity bar and by Section 99, which treats the reports of the probation officer concerning a child as confidential. The principle of institutionalisation as a measure of last resort disciplines every placement order in both tracks, and the principle of fresh start is executed, as the assessment section below shows, by the destruction of records under Section 24. Reading the principles against the operative sections is the fastest way to see that the Act's two tracks, for all their separation, are governed by a single theory: the state's intervention in a child's life is justified by the child's need or by the child's act, is limited by the child's development, and is meant to end with the child restored to ordinary life with no permanent mark.

## The offence track: the Board, the inquiry and bail

The Juvenile Justice Board is the forum the Act substitutes for a criminal court. Section 4 requires a Board for every district, and Section 8 gives it exclusive power to deal with all proceedings under the Act relating to children in conflict with law in its area; the powers may also be exercised by the High Court and the Children's Court when proceedings come before them in appeal, revision or under Section 19. The Board's design is a deliberate hybrid, part court and part welfare body, and its composition is where that design is most visible.

### Who sits on a Juvenile Justice Board?

A Board is a bench of three: a Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the First Class with at least three years of experience, called the Principal Magistrate, and two social workers, of whom at least one must be a woman, each with seven years in child welfare work or a degree in child psychology, psychiatry, sociology or law.

The magistrate supplies the legal discipline; the social workers are there so that the decisive questions, which concern the child's circumstances, maturity and prospects rather than contested points of law, are not answered by a lawyer alone. Section 4 disqualifies any person with a past record of violation of human rights or child rights, a conviction for an offence involving moral turpitude, a dismissal from government service, or a history of child abuse or employment of child labour. The State Government must provide induction training and sensitisation to every member, including the Principal Magistrate, within sixty days of appointment. The Board sits as a bench, decides by majority with the Principal Magistrate's opinion prevailing where there is no majority, and must have at least two members including the Principal Magistrate present at final disposal. Its proceedings, by Section 7, are to be child friendly, in a venue that does not resemble a regular court and is not intimidating to the child. Its functions under Section 8 run well beyond adjudication: ensuring the child's informed participation and legal aid through the legal services institutions, directing a social investigation report within fifteen days of first production, inspecting residential facilities at least once a month, inspecting adult jails to check that no child is lodged in one, and ordering the registration of a first information report where an offence has been committed against a child before it.

Procedure at the front end is built around speed and separation from the ordinary criminal process. Under Section 10, a child apprehended by the police is placed under the charge of the special juvenile police unit or the designated Child Welfare Police Officer, and never in a police lockup or a jail; the child must be produced before the Board within twenty four hours, excluding journey time. A child who is not released is kept only in an observation home. Section 13 requires the parents or guardian and the probation officer to be informed, and a social investigation report on the child's antecedents, family background and material circumstances to be prepared for the Board within two weeks. Section 5 deals with the child who turns eighteen while an inquiry is pending: the inquiry continues, and orders may be passed as if the person had remained a child.

### What must happen within twenty four hours of a child being apprehended?

The child must be produced before the Juvenile Justice Board. Apprehension passes the child to the special juvenile police unit or the designated Child Welfare Police Officer, never to a police lockup or jail, and production before the Board must follow within twenty four hours, excluding only the time the journey takes.

Bail under this Act reverses the ordinary instinct of criminal procedure. Section 12 provides that a person who is apparently a child, apprehended for a bailable or non-bailable offence, shall be released on bail, with or without surety, or placed under the supervision of a probation officer or a fit person, notwithstanding anything in the Code of Criminal Procedure or any other law. The exceptions are three and they are about the child's safety and the integrity of the process, not about the gravity of the charge: bail is to be refused only where there are reasonable grounds for believing that release is likely to bring the child into association with any known criminal, or expose the child to moral, physical or psychological danger, or defeat the ends of justice, and the Board must record its reasons. A child refused bail goes to an observation home or a place of safety, not to a prison. Section 12 also protects the child who cannot meet bail conditions: a child unable to fulfil them within seven days must be produced before the Board for their modification. The design is unmistakable, and the gap between it and station-house practice, where children are held because no one has read the section, is one of the recurring findings of implementation reviews. The general position in this article is a description of the statute and its cases, not advice on any individual matter, and anyone facing an actual proceeding should take advice on the facts of that case, since bail orders turn on recorded reasons that vary from Board to Board.

The inquiry itself is timed. Section 14 requires the Board to complete its inquiry within four months of the child's first production, extendable by two months for recorded reasons; for serious or heinous offences a further extension may be granted by the Chief Judicial Magistrate or Chief Metropolitan Magistrate. Proceedings for petty offences that remain inconclusive after the extended period stand terminated. Petty cases are disposed of through summary proceedings, serious cases by following the procedure for summons cases, and heinous cases concerning a child below sixteen in the same summons-case manner; the separate route for a child above sixteen accused of a heinous offence is the subject of the next section. The Board must satisfy itself at the outset that the child has not been ill-treated by the police or anyone else, conduct the proceedings in as simple a manner as possible, and give the child the opportunity to be heard and to participate. Section 16 adds a pendency review: the Chief Judicial Magistrate or Chief Metropolitan Magistrate reviews the Board's pendency every three months, and a high level committee chaired by the Executive Chairperson of the State Legal Services Authority reviews it every six months.

Where the Board finds the child has committed no offence, Section 17 directs an order to that effect, with a referral to the Committee if the child is in need of care and protection. Where the Board is satisfied on inquiry that a child has committed a petty or serious offence, or a heinous offence while below sixteen, Section 18 gives it a graduated set of dispositions, chosen on the nature of the offence, the need for supervision or intervention, the social investigation report and the child's past conduct: the child may be allowed to go home after advice or admonition following inquiry and counselling of the child and the parents; directed to participate in group counselling; ordered to perform community service under supervision; ordered, or the parents ordered, to pay a fine; released on probation of good conduct under the care of a parent, guardian or fit person for up to three years; released on probation under a fit facility; or sent to a special home for up to three years for reformative services including education, skill development, counselling, behaviour modification therapy and psychiatric support, with a power to send the child instead to a place of safety where his conduct makes a special home placement against his interest or the interest of other children there. Additional orders may direct school attendance, vocational training, a therapeutic centre, exclusion from a specified place, or a de-addiction programme. No disposition under the Act includes imprisonment, and the ceiling of three years in a special home is the offence track's ordinary maximum intervention, which is precisely the ceiling the transfer mechanism was built to break through in the narrow class of cases it covers.

The social investigation report is the document around which a Board inquiry turns, and the Act treats its preparation as urgent rather than administrative. On apprehension, the Child Welfare Police Officer or the special juvenile police unit must inform the parent or guardian and direct them to be present when the child is produced, and must inform the probation officer, or where none is available a Child Welfare Officer, who prepares the report for the Board within two weeks, covering the child's antecedents, family background and the other material circumstances likely to assist the inquiry. Section 8 separately obliges the Board to direct a social investigation, through the probation officer, the Child Welfare Officer or a social worker, within fifteen days of first production, directed at the circumstances in which the alleged offence was committed. The report is the Board's evidence about the child as opposed to the police's evidence about the act, and Section 18 makes it one of the stated foundations of the final order, together with the nature of the offence, the need for supervision or intervention and the child's past conduct. A Board that disposes of a case without the report is not applying the section, and the quality of social investigation, done in most districts by probation officers carrying many other charges, is the quiet variable on which the offence track's reformative promise stands or falls. Legal aid runs alongside: Section 8 charges the Board with ensuring the availability of legal aid for the child through the legal services institutions, and with providing an interpreter or translator where the child does not understand the language of the proceedings. Section 90 allows the Board or the Committee to require the attendance of a parent or guardian at any proceeding, and Section 91 allows the child's own attendance to be dispensed with where it is not essential, the inquiry continuing in his absence, with travel reimbursement for the child and one escort where attendance is required. Section 26 covers the child who runs away from a special home, observation home, place of safety or placement: a police officer may take charge of the child and produce him before the Board that passed the original order or the nearest Board, which ascertains the reasons and passes appropriate orders, and no additional proceeding may be instituted against the child for the running away itself.

Two further features of the offence track's daily operation deserve separate statement, because both are litigated constantly. The first is the construction the courts have given Section 12. The three grounds on which bail may be refused are stated in the proviso as grounds about the consequences of release for the young person and for the process, and the High Courts have read them, in a settled line of decisions, as exhaustive of the matter: the gravity of the alleged offence, the nature of the accusation and the strength of the police case are not among them, and a Board that refuses bail because the charge is heinous, without findings that release would bring the young person into association with a known criminal, expose him to moral, physical or psychological danger, or defeat the ends of justice, has applied the wrong test. The refusal must also be reasoned, since the section requires the Board to record the reasons for denying bail and the circumstances that led to the decision, and an unreasoned refusal does not survive revision. The practical consequence is that bail litigation under this Act is not a smaller version of bail litigation under the Code; it is a different inquiry, into the circumstances of release rather than the merits of the accusation, and it is meant to be concluded at the first production rather than across a series of hearings. The second feature is the fairness scaffolding Section 14(5) builds into every inquiry. The Board must satisfy itself at the threshold that the young person has not been ill treated by the police or by any other person, including a lawyer or probation officer, and must take corrective steps where ill treatment appears; the proceedings are to be conducted in as simple a manner as possible, in a child friendly atmosphere; the young person must be given the opportunity of being heard and of participating; and the procedures are graded to the offence, summary proceedings for petty offences and the summons case procedure for serious ones, so that the formality of the process rises with its stakes rather than standing at the level of a sessions trial throughout. A young person who is not released remains, throughout, in an observation home or a place of safety, and the observation home is itself a registered institution under the Act, with the services and inspection regime of Chapter VII applying to it, so that even pre-decision custody sits inside the welfare architecture rather than in the penal one.

## The classification of offences and the preliminary assessment

The 2015 Act introduced something the 2000 Act never had: a grading of offences by the punishment they carry, because the route a case takes depends entirely on the grade. Section 2(45) defines petty offences as those for which the maximum punishment under the Indian Penal Code or any other law is imprisonment up to three years. Section 2(54) defines serious offences as those for which the punishment is imprisonment between three to seven years. Section 2(33) defines heinous offences as those for which the minimum punishment under the Penal Code or any other law is imprisonment for seven years or more. The definitions are mechanical: they look to the punishment prescribed for the offence, not to what the child did, and the whole transfer architecture hangs off the third definition.

Section 15 is the provision around which the Act is argued about. Where a heinous offence is alleged to have been committed by a child who has completed or is above the age of sixteen years, the Board shall conduct a preliminary assessment with regard to the child's mental and physical capacity to commit the offence, his ability to understand its consequences, and the circumstances in which he allegedly committed it. The Board may take the assistance of experienced psychologists, psycho-social workers or other experts. The section carries an Explanation that is quoted in argument constantly: the preliminary assessment is not a trial, but an assessment of the capacity of the child to commit and understand the consequences of the alleged offence. If the Board is satisfied on the assessment that the matter should be disposed of by the Board itself, Section 15(2) directs it to follow the procedure for trial in summons cases, and that order is appealable. If the Board concludes there is a need for trial of the child as an adult, Section 18(3) empowers it to order transfer of the trial to the Children's Court having jurisdiction.

Two features of this mechanism are persistently misdescribed, and both matter. The first is that transfer is not automatic. A grave charge against a seventeen-year-old does not move the case anywhere by itself; the Board must conduct the assessment, must be satisfied of the need, and must pass an order, and the assessment itself must be disposed of within three months of the child's first production under Section 14(3). The second is that the Board's order is not the last word. The Children's Court, defined by Section 2(20) as a court established under the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005 or a Special Court under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012 where one exists, and otherwise the Court of Sessions having jurisdiction, receives the assessment and decides for itself under Section 19. It may decide that there is a need for trial of the child as an adult under the Code of Criminal Procedure, passing appropriate orders after trial subject to Sections 19 and 21, considering the special needs of the child, the tenets of fair trial and a child friendly atmosphere. Or it may decide that there is no need, in which case it conducts the inquiry as a Board and passes orders under Section 18. Two bodies must therefore independently conclude that adult trial is warranted before a child faces it, and either can stop the transfer.

The protections that survive transfer are written into the same part of the Act. Section 21 provides that no child in conflict with law shall be sentenced to death or to life imprisonment without the possibility of release, for any offence, under this Act, the Penal Code or any other law; a child tried as an adult can receive a long sentence, including life imprisonment with the possibility of release, but the two sentences that extinguish a life outright are closed. Section 19(3) requires that a child found in conflict with law by the Children's Court be sent to a place of safety until he attains twenty one years, and transferred to a jail only thereafter, with reformative services, education, skill development, counselling and psychiatric support provided during the stay, and an annual follow up report by the probation officer, the District Child Protection Unit or a social worker evaluating the child's progress and confirming there is no ill-treatment. Section 20 governs the twenty first birthday where the term of stay is unexpired: the Children's Court must evaluate, with the progress records and expert evaluation before it, whether the person has undergone reformative change and can be a contributing member of society, and may then release him on conditions, including a monitoring authority for the remainder of the term, or direct that the remainder be completed in jail. The final order in every Children's Court case must include an individual care plan for the child's rehabilitation. Section 23 bars any joint proceedings of a child with a person who is not a child, so a transferred child is tried separately from adult co-accused. Section 22 excludes the security proceedings of Chapter VIII of the Code of Criminal Procedure against any child.

Section 24 then deals with the future. A child who has committed an offence and been dealt with under the Act shall not suffer any disqualification attaching to a conviction under any other law, and the Board or the Children's Court must direct the destruction of the relevant records of conviction after the expiry of the period of appeal or a prescribed reasonable period. Both protections carry a deliberate exception, inserted for the transfer cases: neither the removal of disqualification nor the destruction of records applies where a child above sixteen has been found in conflict with law by the Children's Court under Section 19(1)(i), that is, tried as an adult for a heinous offence; in that class the Children's Court retains the records, and the conviction carries its ordinary consequences. The line the Act draws is therefore not between children and adults in the abstract. It is between a child dealt with inside the juvenile system, whose record is wiped and whose future is protected, and the narrow class transferred out of it, who loses both protections along with the shelter of the Board.

### How long does a Board inquiry take?

The statute sets four months from the child's first production, extendable by two months for reasons recorded in writing. A preliminary assessment in a heinous case must be disposed of within three months of first production, and petty cases that are still inconclusive after the extended period stand terminated altogether.

Age is the gateway to everything in this part of the Act, and the Act guards the gateway carefully. A claim of juvenility may be raised before any court, and Section 9 requires the court before which it is raised, including a Magistrate not empowered as a Board, to inquire into it, taking evidence other than an affidavit, and to record a finding stating the person's age as nearly as may be. The proviso to Section 9(2) is one of the most consequential sentences in the statute: the claim shall be recognised at any stage, even after final disposal of the case, and shall be determined under the Act even if the person has ceased to be a child on or before the date the Act commenced. If the court finds the person was a child on the date of the offence, Section 9(3) forwards him to the Board and any sentence passed is deemed to have no effect. Section 94 supplies the method of determination where the Board or Committee faces the question: the age is to be determined on the evidence of the date of birth certificate from the school, or the matriculation or equivalent certificate, failing which the birth certificate given by a corporation, municipal authority or panchayat, and only in the absence of both, by medical opinion from a duly constituted medical board; the age so recorded is deemed to be the true age for the purposes of the Act. In Rishipal Singh Solanki v. State of Uttar Pradesh, decided on 18 November 2021, the Supreme Court restated the principles governing such claims: a claim of juvenility may be raised at any stage of a criminal proceeding, even after final disposal, delay in raising it is no ground for rejection, and it may be raised for the first time before the Supreme Court itself. The reckoning rule beneath all of this, that age is counted at the date of the offence rather than the date of the proceeding, had been settled by a Constitution Bench in Pratap Singh v. State of Jharkhand in 2005 under the earlier Act, and Section 2(13) wrote it into the definition.

The single most important judicial gloss on the 2015 Act concerns the definition of a heinous offence, and it narrowed the transfer class materially. Section 2(33) requires a minimum punishment of seven years or more. A large family of grave offences, however, prescribes no minimum at all, or a minimum below seven years, while allowing a maximum well above seven: culpable homicide not amounting to murder is the standard example. Such offences fit none of the three definitions, being too heavy for the serious category as defined and lacking the minimum the heinous category demands. In Shilpa Mittal v. State of NCT of Delhi, decided on 9 January 2020 by a bench of Deepak Gupta and Aniruddha Bose JJ and reported at (2020) 2 SCC 787, the Supreme Court held that an offence which does not provide a minimum sentence of seven years cannot be treated as a heinous offence, and that the unclassified fourth category of offences, those with a maximum above seven years but no minimum or a minimum below seven years, shall be treated as serious offences within the meaning of the Act, in exercise of the Court's power under Article 142 of the Constitution, from the date the 2015 Act came into force, until Parliament takes the call on the matter. The practical consequence is large: for offences in that fourth category, no preliminary assessment arises at all, no transfer can be ordered, and a child of seventeen accused of them is dealt with by the Board under the summons-case procedure whatever the facts. Any account of the transfer mechanism that omits this narrowing describes a power wider than the one the law confers.

The assessment has since been the subject of the Supreme Court's closest procedural attention. In Barun Chandra Thakur v. Master Bholu, decided in 2022, the Court examined what a preliminary assessment demands of a Board and held that it is a delicate and demanding task, not a formality to be completed on the papers: the assessment concerns the capacity of a particular child, formed at a particular stage of development, and a Board dealing with a heinous allegation against a child above sixteen should ordinarily take the assistance of experienced psychologists or psycho-social workers, as Section 15 permits, rather than treating the exercise as one of judicial impression. The judgment's insistence matters because the proviso to Section 15 makes expert assistance available rather than compulsory, and the record in the early years of the Act included assessments conducted without any psychological input at all. Read with Shilpa Mittal, the position is that the transfer gate is narrow in law and is meant to be slow in practice: the definition excludes the fourth category of offences, the Board must assess capacity rather than react to gravity, expert assistance is the expected route to that assessment, and the Children's Court must then be independently satisfied before any adult trial begins.

Appeals and supervision complete the offence track's frame. Section 101 provides an appeal from the orders of the Board and the Committee, ordinarily to the Children's Court, within thirty days, with a bar on second appeals; the appeal against a Board's order passed after a preliminary assessment lies instead to the Court of Sessions, which may take the assistance of experienced psychologists and medical specialists other than those whose assistance the Board obtained, a fresh-eyes safeguard built into the transfer route itself; no appeal lies from an order of acquittal made by the Board except in the case of a heinous offence by a child above sixteen, or from a Committee's finding that a person is not a child in need of care and protection; and an appeal from an order of the Children's Court lies to the High Court in accordance with the Code of Criminal Procedure. Section 102 gives the High Court a revisional power, exercisable of its own motion or on application, to call for the record of any proceeding before a Board, a Committee or a Children's Court and satisfy itself as to the legality or propriety of the order. Section 103 provides that proceedings under the Act are not to be invalidated by defects in the appointment of members or other technical grounds the section specifies, a provision whose purpose is to keep welfare orders from collapsing on formalities. Above the district machinery sit the periodic reviews already noticed: the Chief Judicial Magistrate or Chief Metropolitan Magistrate over Board pendency, the District Magistrate's quarterly review of the Committee, and the reporting of pendency by both bodies, so that delay, the system's characteristic failure, is at least made visible to an authority with power to act on it.

A transferred case deserves one further pass, because the Children's Court stage is where the Act's two natures, penal and protective, are forced into a single courtroom. On receiving the preliminary assessment, the Court is not conducting an appeal from the Board and is not bound by the Board's satisfaction; Section 19 gives it the decision in its own right, and a Court that finds the assessment perfunctory, or the capacity evidence thin, has both the power and the duty to decide that there is no need for trial as an adult and to proceed as a Board. Where it decides the other way, the trial that follows is a trial under the Code of Criminal Procedure, with the charges framed, the evidence recorded and the standard of proof the ordinary one, but it is conducted subject to the Act's own conditions: the special needs of the young person are to be considered, the tenets of fair trial maintained, and a child friendly atmosphere kept, an instruction that governs everything from the arrangement of the courtroom to the language in which the young person is addressed. The sentence, where the trial ends in conviction, is drawn from the penal law of the offence, cut down only by Section 21, so the Court may impose imprisonment for a term, or imprisonment for life where the offence carries it, provided the life sentence carries the possibility of release; what it may not do is sentence the young person to death or to a whole life term without that possibility, whatever the adult co-accused in the separate trial receives. Execution of the sentence is then re-routed through the welfare architecture: the young person goes not to a Central Jail but to a place of safety, a registered and inspected institution, where the reformative services of education, skill development, counselling, behaviour modification therapy and psychiatric support are to be provided, and the probation officer, the District Child Protection Unit or a social worker reports on his progress to the Court every year, so the sentencing court retains a supervisory thread over the sentence it passed, a feature with no counterpart in adult sentencing. At twenty one the review described in Section 20 is a genuine second decision, taken on the accumulated progress reports and expert evaluation, between release under conditions with a monitoring authority appointed for the remainder of the term and transfer to a jail to complete it; the Act thus postpones the final penal character of the sentence until the young person has been observed as an adult in a reformative setting. The individual care plan that Section 19(2) requires in the final order, covering rehabilitation and the follow up by the probation officer, the Unit or a social worker, is the document that ties these stages together, and its absence from a Children's Court order is a defect of substance and not of form, since the annual reports and the twenty one review both presuppose a plan against which progress can be measured.

Two features of the surrounding caseload shape how all of this works in practice, and both can be stated as patterns in the official record without resting on any single year's figures. The first is the composition of the offence track's business. The National Crime Records Bureau's annual Crime in India reports have, across the life of this Act and of the one before it, recorded apprehensions of children in conflict with law concentrated in property and other ordinary offences, theft and burglary prominent among them, with the heinous categories forming a small share of the whole. The system's everyday work is therefore not the assessment of seventeen year olds for murder trials, which dominates its public image, but the processing of large numbers of minor allegations against poor children through Boards that the Act expects to dispose of petty matters summarily and to terminate them if they cannot be concluded in the extended period. A Board resourced for that reality looks different from a Board imagined for the headline cases, and much of the pendency the review machinery records is the accumulation of small cases in under staffed districts rather than the difficulty of large ones. The second pattern concerns age itself. A substantial share of the litigation the Act generates is not about what a young person did but about how old he was when he did it, fought over school records that do not exist, birth registration that was never made and medical opinions with margins of error on both sides of the eighteenth birthday. The documentary hierarchy of Section 94 is the Act's answer, preferring the school record and the municipal record to the ossification test precisely because the medical method is the least exact, and Rishipal Singh Solanki drew the working distinction that governs the forum of the fight: where the question arises before a court, it is dealt with under sub-sections (2) and (3) of Section 9, the court taking such evidence as may be necessary and recording the age as nearly as may be; where the person is brought before a Board or a Committee, Section 94 governs, the age recorded there being deemed the true age for the purposes of the Act. The same judgment, restating the settled line of authority, holds that the claim may be raised for the first time before the Supreme Court itself and that delay is no answer to it, from which it follows that a conviction recorded against a person later shown to have been a child on the date of the offence cannot stand on the sentence passed, Section 9(3) deeming it to have no effect and sending the person to the Board for appropriate orders. The gateway question, once raised, is therefore never time barred, and every actor in the ordinary criminal process, police, prosecutor, Magistrate and appellate court, carries the duty of recognising it, which is why the Act places the claim in the general procedure chapter rather than in the Board's own.

## The care track: the Committee, restoration and the road to a family

The Child Welfare Committee is the care track's counterpart to the Board, and the Act deliberately mirrors the Board's bench structure while changing its character. Section 27 requires the State Government to constitute one or more Committees for every district. The Committee consists of a Chairperson and four other members, of whom at least one shall be a woman and another an expert on matters concerning children. No person may be appointed unless he has been actively involved in health, education or welfare activities pertaining to children for at least seven years, or is a practising professional with a degree in child psychology or psychiatry or law or social work or sociology or human development. A member's term cannot exceed three years, appointment can be terminated after inquiry for misuse of power, a conviction involving moral turpitude, or persistent failure to attend, and the Committee functions as a bench with the powers of a Metropolitan Magistrate or a Judicial Magistrate of the First Class. The District Magistrate reviews its functioning quarterly and is its grievance redressal authority. Where the Board's authority runs to children accused of offences, Section 29 gives the Committee exclusive power, save as otherwise provided in the Act, over all proceedings relating to children in need of care and protection.

### Which children does the care track reach?

Any child inside the list in Section 2(14): one without a home or means of subsistence, one working in breach of labour laws or found begging or living on the street, one abused or threatened by the person he lives with, a runaway, and one at risk of early marriage or trafficking.

Entry is deliberately open. Section 31 permits a child in need of care and protection to be produced before the Committee by a police officer or the special juvenile police unit or a Child Welfare Police Officer or an officer of the District Child Protection Unit or a labour inspector; by any public servant; by Childline services or a recognised voluntary organisation; by a Child Welfare Officer or probation officer; by any social worker or public spirited citizen; by a nurse, doctor or the management of a nursing home, hospital or maternity home; or by the child himself. Production must be within twenty four hours, excluding journey time. Section 32 imposes a duty on anyone who finds and takes charge of an abandoned, lost or orphaned child to report the fact within twenty four hours to Childline, the nearest police station, the Committee or the District Child Protection Unit, or to hand the child to a registered institution, and Sections 33 and 34 make failure to report an offence punishable with imprisonment up to six months or a fine of ten thousand rupees or both. The design assumption is that children in need rarely arrive through a single gate, so the statute opens every gate it can identify and penalises the bystander who closes one.

The Committee must sit at least twenty days a month under Section 28, and a visit to a child care institution counts as a sitting. Its functions under Section 30 run from receiving children and conducting inquiry on all issues affecting their safety and well being, through directing social investigation, declaring fit persons, directing foster care placements, and selecting the registered institution for each child requiring institutional support, to inspecting residential facilities at least twice a month, certifying surrender deeds, restoring lost children to their families, declaring orphan, abandoned and surrendered children legally free for adoption, taking suo motu cognizance of children not produced before it, and acting on complaints of abuse in any child care institution. Section 36 disciplines the inquiry: the social investigation is to be completed within fifteen days so that the Committee can pass its final order within four months of the child's first production, and the Committee reports its disposal and pendency quarterly to the District Magistrate, who may direct remedial measures and, where pendency persists unaddressed, set in motion the termination and reconstitution of the Committee itself.

On being satisfied through the inquiry that a child is in need of care and protection, Section 37 arms the Committee with the orders that define the care track's outcomes: a declaration that the child is in need of care and protection; restoration of the child to parents, guardian or family, with or without supervision; placement in a Children's Home or fit facility or Specialised Adoption Agency, for long term or temporary care, where the family cannot be traced or restoration is not in the child's best interest; placement with a fit person; foster care orders under Section 44; sponsorship orders under Section 45; directions to the persons and institutions holding the child covering shelter, medical attention, psychiatric and psychological support, counselling, skill training, legal aid, education and follow up; and a declaration that the child is legally free for adoption. Restoration is not merely one option among others. Section 40 makes the restoration and protection of the child the prime objective of every Children's Home, Specialised Adoption Agency and open shelter, and restoration means return to parents, adoptive parents, foster parents, guardian or fit person after the Committee determines suitability. Section 35 governs surrender: a parent or guardian who, for physical, emotional and social factors beyond their control, wishes to surrender a child produces the child before the Committee, executes a surrender deed after inquiry and counselling, and is given two months to reconsider. Section 38 then governs the declaration that a child is legally free for adoption: for an orphan or abandoned child, after efforts to trace the parents, within two months of production for a child up to two years of age and within four months for an older child, the decision to be taken by at least three members of the Committee; and no first information report may be registered against a biological parent in the course of that inquiry, a protection designed to keep frightened parents inside the legal process rather than outside it.

Around these orders the rehabilitation chapter builds the non-institutional ladder the principles demand. Section 39 bases rehabilitation and social re-integration on the child's individual care plan, preferably through family based care: restoration, adoption or foster care. Section 43 provides open shelters as community based facilities for children needing short term residential support, to protect them from abuse and from a life on the streets. Section 44 provides foster care, including group foster care, in a family other than the child's own. Section 45 provides sponsorship, financial and other support to families and institutions to improve the quality of a child's life. Section 46 provides aftercare: a person who has completed eighteen but not twenty one, and has left institutional care, may be given financial support to re-integrate into the mainstream of society. Section 53 lists the services institutions must provide, from food, shelter, clothing and medical care to education, vocational training, recreation and counselling, and Section 54 requires inspection committees for every district and state, with power expressly extending to institutions that house children without being registered at all.

## A care case in sequence

The care track likewise resolves into a sequence, and its logic is the mirror image of the offence track's: the first orders are interim and protective, and they harden into permanence only as the inquiry excludes the faster, family based outcomes. A case may begin with a telephone call, since Childline services are among the persons entitled to produce a young person before the Committee, and Section 32's reporting duty, backed by the offence of non-reporting in Sections 33 and 34, means that a hospital, a police officer or a member of the public who takes charge of a lost, abandoned or apparently orphaned young person must pass the information, within twenty four hours, to Childline, the nearest police station, the Committee or the District Child Protection Unit, or hand the young person to a registered institution, the information being uploaded on the portal the Central Government specifies so that a missing young person in one district can be matched against a found one in another. Production before the Committee follows within twenty four hours, before an individual member if the Committee is not in session. The Committee's first business is immediate safety: interim placement in a Children's Home, a fit facility or with a fit person, medical attention where it is needed, and the direction of a social investigation, to be completed within fifteen days, into the young person's family, history and circumstances. The inquiry then tests, in the statutory order of preference, whether restoration is possible: the family is traced where it is unknown, its suitability is assessed where it is known, and the wishes of a young person mature enough to hold a view are taken into account. The four month period for the final order is the discipline within which tracing, assessment and reconsideration must all occur; for a surrendered young person the two month reconsideration period for the parents runs inside it, and for an orphan or abandoned young person the declaration of being legally free for adoption has its own shorter clocks under Section 38, two months from production up to the age of two years and four months above it, taken by at least three members of the Committee. The final order under Section 37 chooses among restoration with or without supervision, placement in a home or fit facility, placement with a fit person, foster care and sponsorship, and it is a living order: the situation of a young person placed in a home or with a fit facility, person or foster family is reviewed by the Committee as prescribed, restoration remains the prime objective of the institution holding the young person under Section 40, and the rehabilitation process of Section 39, built on the individual care plan, prefers family based care at every stage, keeping siblings together unless their best interest requires otherwise. Where no family outcome is found, the sequence runs on into the longer institutions of the Act: foster care under Section 44, sponsorship under Section 45, adoption where the young person has been declared legally free, and, at the far end of childhood, aftercare under Section 46, financial support for the young adult of eighteen to twenty one who has left institutional care and must join the mainstream of society without the family network the system was unable to restore. The pendency discipline matches the offence track's: the Committee reports disposal and pendency quarterly to the District Magistrate, persistent unaddressed pendency leads towards the Committee's termination and reconstitution, and a standing panel of eligible persons is to be maintained so that a new Committee can be constituted without losing time, with the Committee of a nearby district assuming responsibility in any interval.

## Institutions and the registration duty

The institutions of the two tracks are distinct and their names carry their functions. For the offence track, Section 47 provides observation homes, where a child is kept pending inquiry; Section 48 provides special homes, where a child found in conflict with law is placed under a Section 18 order; and Section 49 provides places of safety, for children above sixteen alleged or found to have committed a heinous offence, for persons above eighteen apprehended for an offence committed as a child, and for transferred children awaiting or undergoing adult trial, every place of safety being by definition a place that is not a police lockup or a jail. For the care track, Section 50 provides Children's Homes for children in need of care and protection, and Section 65 provides Specialised Adoption Agencies for the placement of orphan, abandoned and surrendered children. Section 51 allows the Committee or the Board to recognise a governmental or voluntary facility as a fit facility for a particular child for a specific purpose, and the definition of a fit person performs the same office for individuals.

The provision that changed the obligations of everyone running such a home is Section 41. Every institution, whether run by a State Government or by a voluntary or non-governmental organisation, which is meant wholly or partially for housing children in need of care and protection or children in conflict with law, must be registered under the Act, regardless of whether it receives government grants. Registration is granted for a recorded capacity and a recorded purpose, as a Children's Home, open shelter, Specialised Adoption Agency, observation home, special home or place of safety; it runs for five years and is renewable; provisional registration may be granted within a month to bring an institution inside the Act; and the State Government may cancel or withhold registration of an institution that fails to provide the rehabilitation and re-integration services Section 53 specifies, managing the institution itself in the interval. Section 42 supplies the teeth the 2000 Act lacked: a person in charge of an institution housing such children who fails to comply with Section 41 is punishable with imprisonment which may extend to one year, or a fine of not less than one lakh rupees, or both, and every thirty days of delay in applying for registration is a separate offence. The pairing of a duty with a priced penalty converted registration from an administrative request into a legal exposure, and it was aimed at a documented problem: homes housing children entirely outside the inspection system, answerable to no committee, in which abuse could continue unrecorded because the state had no lawful occasion to enter. The Act gives it that occasion anyway, since Section 41(9) empowers the inspection committee to enter and inspect any institution housing children, registered or not, to determine whether it houses children in need of care and protection.

Two pieces of district machinery hold the institutional system together and appear throughout both tracks. The first is the police interface. Section 107 requires a special juvenile police unit for every district, drawn from the district's police, and the designation of a Child Welfare Police Officer in every police station; it is this officer, and not the ordinary station staff, who is the statutory point of contact for a child apprehended, a child produced and a child reported lost, which is why the Act's front end works only where the designation has actually been made and the officer actually trained in the role. The second is the District Child Protection Unit, established by the State Government for every district under Section 106 and defined by Section 2(26) as the focal point to ensure the implementation of the Act and other child protection measures in the district. The Unit provides the Committee's secretariat, supports the Board, runs the district's child protection data, coordinates the non-institutional services of sponsorship, foster care and aftercare, and is the office to which inspection committees and Committees report. Its staffing is the system's most common single point of failure: a district in which the Unit exists as one officer with additional charge has, in substance, chosen to run the care track without an engine. Section 105 adds a Juvenile Justice Fund, created by the State Government for the welfare and rehabilitation of children dealt with under the Act, fed by voluntary donations and contributions, which is the statutory recognition that some of the Act's promises, from aftercare support to sponsorship, are spending promises and not merely procedural ones.

The fit person and fit facility devices deserve notice because they are the Act's principal answer to the last resort principle in daily practice. A Committee or a Board faced with a child who cannot go home, and for whom a large institution is the only registered alternative, may recognise a governmental or voluntary facility as a fit facility for that child for a specific purpose under Section 51, or identify an individual, after inquiry, as a fit person prepared to take responsibility for the child for a specific purpose. The device personalises placement inside a system otherwise built of institutions: a child can be placed with a named family, a small hostel, a treatment centre or a mentor household, under the continuing authority of the body that made the order, and the placement can be reviewed and changed in a way a general admission to a Children's Home cannot. Much of the care track's better practice, and the whole of foster care under Section 44, grows out of this statutory habit of preferring a named person to an unnamed institution wherever a named person can be found.

The registration machinery has more moving parts than the duty alone suggests, and an institution manager needs all of them. At registration the State Government determines and records the institution's capacity and purpose and registers it in its proper category, so a home may not lawfully house more children than its certificate states or children of a category it is not registered for. The registration runs for five years and is renewable for successive periods of five years, and an institution whose registration is under renewal consideration continues inside the system while the State Government manages or supervises as the section directs. A registered institution is duty bound to admit children as directed by the Committee, subject to its recorded capacity, whether or not it receives grants from the Central or a State Government, which prevents a registered home from selecting only the children it finds convenient. Against default, the State Government may, after following the prescribed procedure, cancel or withhold the registration of an institution that fails to provide the rehabilitation and re-integration services Section 53 specifies, and it manages the institution itself until registration is renewed or granted, so that enforcement does not punish the children for the management's failure by closing the roof over them. The services themselves, set out in Section 53, are the standard against which cancellation is judged: food, shelter, clothing and medical care; appropriate education, including special education and vocational training; recreation; counselling and mental health interventions; life skill education; and measures for reintegration, with the section's fuller list running to the equipment and support a child with disability requires. Section 54 completes the scheme with inspection committees for the state and for every district, appointed by the State Government from representatives of the government, the Board and the Committee, medical and social work professionals and child welfare organisations, visiting institutions and reporting on their functioning; and it is the inspection committee that Section 41(9) arms with the power to enter an institution that houses children without any registration at all, which is the point at which the state's knowledge of an unregistered home lawfully begins.

Inside the institutions, Section 53 builds two participatory devices that are easy to overlook and that distinguish the Act's homes, in conception, from the custodial institutions of the older law. Every institution is to have a management committee, set up for the management of the institution and the monitoring of the progress of every child resident in it, so that the individual care plan of each child has a named body answerable for its movement. And the section provides for children's committees within the institution, through which the resident children themselves take part in activities and voice their experience of the home, an institutional form of the participation principle of Section 3: the subjects of the system are made, in a small and supervised way, participants in running the place that holds them. Both devices depend for their reality on the inspection regime that stands behind them, since a management committee that never meets and a children's committee that is never convened are indistinguishable, from the child's side of the wall, from their absence, and it is the inspection committee's reports, the Committee's twice monthly visits on the care side and the Board's monthly inspections on the offence side, that are supposed to detect the difference between the institution on paper and the institution in fact.

## The offences the Act creates against children

Chapter IX is the part of the statute directed not at children but at the adults around them, and it is routinely underused relative to its design. Section 74 opens the chapter sideways, with the identity prohibition: no report in any newspaper, magazine, news-sheet or audio-visual media or other form of communication regarding any inquiry, investigation or judicial proceeding shall disclose the name, address or school or any other particular which may lead to the identification of a child in conflict with law, a child in need of care and protection, or a child victim or witness of a crime, and no picture of such a child shall be published, unless the Board or Committee holding the inquiry permits disclosure in writing because it is in the child's best interest. Contravention is punishable with imprisonment up to six months or a fine up to two lakh rupees or both, and the police may not disclose a child's record for a character certificate or otherwise once a case is closed or disposed of. In Shilpa Mittal the Supreme Court, noticing that the High Court's judgment had itself named the child, directed the correction of the judgment: the discipline binds courts and newsrooms alike.

Section 75 punishes cruelty to a child by whoever has actual charge or control of the child: assault, abandonment, abuse, exposure or wilful neglect likely to cause unnecessary mental or physical suffering carries imprisonment up to three years or a fine of one lakh rupees or both, with the abandonment of a child by biological parents in circumstances beyond their control presumed not wilful; where the offender is employed by or managing an organisation entrusted with the child's care and protection, the punishment rises to rigorous imprisonment up to five years and a fine up to five lakh rupees, and where the cruelty leaves the child physically incapacitated, mentally ill or at risk to life or limb, rigorous imprisonment of three to ten years with a fine of five lakh rupees. Section 76 punishes employing or using a child for begging with imprisonment up to five years and a fine of one lakh rupees, rising to rigorous imprisonment of seven to ten years and a fine of five lakh rupees where the child is amputated or maimed for the purpose; the child so used is never treated as a child in conflict with law and is produced before the Committee for rehabilitation. Section 77 punishes giving a child intoxicating liquor, a narcotic drug, tobacco products or a psychotropic substance, except on a qualified medical practitioner's order, with rigorous imprisonment up to seven years and a fine up to one lakh rupees, and Section 78 punishes using a child to vend, peddle, carry, supply or smuggle such substances on the same scale. Section 79 punishes exploitation of a child employee, engaging a child ostensibly and keeping him in bondage for employment or withholding his earnings, with rigorous imprisonment up to five years and a fine of one lakh rupees. Section 80 punishes adoption outside the prescribed procedures with imprisonment up to three years or a fine of one lakh rupees or both, with withdrawal of a recognised adoption agency's registration added where the agency offends. Section 81 punishes the sale or purchase of a child for any purpose with rigorous imprisonment up to five years and a fine of one lakh rupees, rising to three to seven years where the offender had actual charge of the child, including hospital, nursing home and maternity home staff. Section 82 punishes corporal punishment in a child care institution, by a fine of ten thousand rupees on first conviction and imprisonment up to three months thereafter, with dismissal and a bar on working directly with children, and a heavy penalty on an uncooperative management. Section 83 punishes the recruitment or use of a child by a non-state militant group, and the use of children by any adult or adult group for illegal activities, with rigorous imprisonment up to seven years and a fine of five lakh rupees. Section 84 applies the Penal Code's kidnapping and abduction provisions to children under this Act, Section 85 doubles the penalty for offences under the chapter committed on a disabled child, Section 87 punishes abetment, and Section 88 provides that where an act is an offence under this Act and another law, the punishment is the greater of the two. Section 86, as enacted, classified these offences: those punishable with more than seven years are cognizable, non-bailable and triable by a Children's Court; those punishable with three years and above but not more than seven are cognizable, non-bailable and triable by a Magistrate of the First Class; and those below three years or punishable with fine only are non-cognizable, bailable and triable by any Magistrate.

## A Board case in sequence

The offence track reads differently when its steps are laid end to end, because the deadlines interlock and a failure at any step changes the legal position at the next. The sequence begins at apprehension, when the young person passes to the special juvenile police unit or the Child Welfare Police Officer, is told, in substance, of the allegation in language he understands, and is produced before the Board within twenty four hours, production before an individual member being permissible when the Board is not in sitting. At that first production the bail question is decided, on the Section 12 standard, and the young person leaves either on bail, with or without surety, under the supervision of a probation officer or a fit person, or to an observation home; there is no third destination in a police station or a jail, and the seven day rule for unfulfilled bail conditions starts running from the order. The Board at the same stage sets the social investigation in motion, the parents or guardian having been informed and directed to attend, and the fifteen day clock for the report runs against the probation officer or Child Welfare Officer. When the report arrives, the inquiry proper begins under Section 14, with the Board satisfying itself about ill treatment, informing itself of the young person's participation and legal aid, and proceeding by the graded procedure the offence grade dictates. Four months is the statutory allowance, two more are available for recorded reasons, and for serious and heinous matters a further extension lies with the Chief Judicial Magistrate or the Chief Metropolitan Magistrate, whose quarterly review of pendency under Section 16 means the file's age is known outside the Board room. Where the allegation is heinous and the young person was above sixteen at the offence, the assessment is fitted inside the same period and disposed of within three months of first production, so the transfer question is decided early, while the evidence of the act is still to be inquired into; the young person and his family are entitled to know that the assessment is not a trial, that it examines capacity and circumstances rather than guilt, and that an order keeping the case is appealable while an order of transfer takes the case to a second, independent decision maker. If the inquiry ends in a finding that no offence was committed, Section 17 closes the matter with the care referral where one is needed. If the finding is against the young person, Section 18's ladder is climbed only as high as the findings justify, from admonition at its foot to the special home at its top, with the additional orders, schooling, training, therapeutic attendance, exclusion from a place, de-addiction, available to fit the order to the life rather than to the label. The final order, under Section 8, includes an individual care plan for rehabilitation, with follow up by the probation officer, the District Child Protection Unit or a non-governmental member, so the case does not end at disposal but at the completion of a plan. Every stage has a named owner and a stated period, and the most productive question a lawyer, a parent or an inspector can ask of any file is the simplest: which stage is this at, and whose clock is running.

## Foundations: the Constitution, the Convention and the definitions that do quiet work

The Act's preamble is unusually informative about its own sources, and it identifies the two foundations on which the whole structure rests. The first is constitutional. The preamble recites clause (3) of Article 15, which empowers the state to make special provisions for children; clauses (e) and (f) of Article 39, which direct policy towards securing that children are not abused and that childhood is protected against exploitation and moral and material abandonment; Article 45; and Article 47. A statute for children is therefore not an ordinary welfare measure in the constitutional scheme: it is the discharge of a direction the Constitution itself gives, and the special provision power of Article 15(3) is what sustains the classifications the Act draws between children and adults and, inside childhood, between the two tracks. The second foundation is international. India acceded to the Convention on the Rights of the Child on 11 December 1992, and the preamble names the standards the re-enactment takes into account: the Convention itself, the United Nations Standard Minimum Rules for the Administration of Juvenile Justice, 1985, known as the Beijing Rules, the United Nations Rules for the Protection of Juveniles Deprived of their Liberty, 1990, and the Hague Convention on Protection of Children and Co-operation in Respect of Inter-country Adoption, 1993. The Beijing inheritance shows in the offence track's insistence on diversion, on detention as a last resort and on the erasure of records; the Hague inheritance shows in Chapter VIII's insistence that inter-country adoption move only through authorised agencies and a central authority, with a no objection certificate from the Authority as the gate. When a provision of the Act is ambiguous, these instruments are the materials its interpreters reach for, and the Supreme Court's juvenile justice jurisprudence has consistently read the statute in their light.

Several definitions in Section 2 do more work than their brevity suggests, and practitioners trip on them. An abandoned child, by Section 2(1), is a child deserted by his biological or adoptive parents or guardians who has been declared as abandoned by the Committee after due inquiry: the declaration is a legal act, not a description, and until the Committee makes it the adoption machinery cannot start. An orphan, by Section 2(42), is a child without biological or adoptive parents or legal guardian, or whose legal guardian is not willing to take, or capable of taking, care of the child, a definition that deliberately extends beyond the death of parents to the practical absence of a carer. Best interest of child, by Section 2(9), is defined as the basis for any decision taken regarding the child, to ensure fulfilment of his basic rights and needs, identity, social well being and physical, emotional and intellectual development, which converts the Section 3 principle into a test that must be answered decision by decision. Child friendly, by Section 2(15), means any behaviour, conduct, practice, process, attitude, environment or treatment that is humane, considerate and in the best interest of the child, and it is the standard Section 7 applies to the Board's venue and Section 19 to the Children's Court's atmosphere. Aftercare, by Section 2(5), is the making of provision of support, financial or otherwise, to persons who have completed eighteen years but not twenty one and have left institutional care to join the mainstream of society, so the Act's concern does not terminate at the institution's gate or at the eighteenth birthday. And the definition of a child care institution in Section 2(21), gathering Children's Homes, open shelters, observation homes, special homes, places of safety, Specialised Adoption Agencies and fit facilities recognised under the Act, is what fixes the reach of the registration duty in Section 41: if a facility houses children for care and protection and answers to one of those descriptions, it is inside the Act whether its managers have heard of the Act or not.

## The two tracks at work: two pathways through the statute

The architecture is easiest to test against concrete cases, and two will show nearly every provision in motion. Take first a boy of seventeen years and four months, apprehended on an allegation that a stabbing during a street fight caused a death. The charge the police record will determine the first question, and it is a question about the Penal Code's punishment clause, not about the facts. If the allegation is framed as culpable homicide not amounting to murder, the punishment prescribed runs to imprisonment for life, or imprisonment up to ten years, with no minimum stated; after Shilpa Mittal that offence sits in the fourth category and is treated as a serious offence, so no preliminary assessment arises, the Board follows the summons case procedure, and the maximum outcome is three years in a special home. If the allegation is framed as murder, for which the punishment is death or imprisonment for life, the offence is heinous because its minimum punishment exceeds seven years, and the machinery changes entirely: bail is still the rule under Section 12 unless a recorded exception applies; the social investigation report is prepared within the statutory weeks; the Board completes a preliminary assessment within three months of first production, taking psychological assistance on the boy's capacity to commit the act, his ability to understand its consequences and the circumstances in which it occurred; and it either keeps the case, following the summons case procedure and the Section 18 dispositions, or records its satisfaction that trial as an adult is needed and transfers the case under Section 18(3). The Children's Court then decides the question afresh. If it agrees, the boy is tried under the Code of Criminal Procedure in a child friendly atmosphere, with the special needs of the child considered; if convicted, he cannot be sentenced to death or to life imprisonment without the possibility of release, he is sent to a place of safety until he is twenty one with annual progress reports, and at twenty one the Court reviews his reformative progress before deciding between release on conditions with a monitoring authority and transfer to a jail for the remainder. If the Children's Court disagrees with the transfer, it inquires as a Board and the ordinary ceiling returns. The same death, on the same street, can therefore lawfully end anywhere between admonition and a life sentence with the possibility of release, and the variables that move the outcome are the charge's punishment clause, the assessment of one adolescent's capacity, and two successive satisfactions recorded by two different benches.

Take second a girl surrendered at a hospital at birth by parents who state, in the language Section 35 contemplates, that physical, emotional and social factors beyond their control leave them unable to raise her. No offence is alleged against anyone, and the offence track never opens. The hospital, a person entitled to produce a child under Section 31, places her before the Committee within twenty four hours; the Committee directs her interim placement, she being below six years of age, in a Specialised Adoption Agency; the surrender deed is executed before the Committee after inquiry and counselling, and the parents are given two months to reconsider, a period the Committee certifies and during which the child remains in the Agency. If the parents do not return, the Committee, by a decision of at least three of its members, declares her legally free for adoption under Section 38 within the period the section prescribes, and informs the State Agency and the Authority of the declaration. She has become adoptable without any court having been approached at any point in the process, and the adoption that follows under Chapter VIII is what converts the Committee's welfare jurisdiction into a permanent family. Between the two pathways there is no shared procedure, no shared bench and no shared vocabulary beyond the definitions and principles of the first two chapters, which is the article's central claim demonstrated rather than asserted: this is one title over two statutes, and it misleads everyone who reads it as one.

Chapter IX operates differently from the rest of the Act, and the difference explains both its promise and its thin enforcement record. Everywhere else, the Act proceeds by production, inquiry and order before the Board or the Committee; a person aggrieved by cruelty in a home, or by a child being used for begging at a crossing, is usually inside a welfare process before any question of prosecution arises, and the Committee's power under Section 30 to inquire into a complaint of abuse in an institution and to direct the police, the District Child Protection Unit, the labour department or Childline to act is the channel through which most Chapter IX conduct first surfaces. Prosecution, when it comes, follows the classification of Section 86 as enacted: the gravest offences, those punishable with more than seven years, are cognizable and non-bailable and triable by a Children's Court; the middle band, punishable with three years and above but not more than seven, are cognizable and non-bailable before a Magistrate of the First Class; and the lowest band is non-cognizable and bailable. Two provisions keep the chapter coherent with the rest of the design. Section 88 provides that where an act is an offence under this Act and under any other law, the offender is liable to the punishment that is greater in degree, which prevents the Act's penalties from becoming a discount where, for example, the same conduct is trafficking or a sexual offence under another statute. Section 89 provides that a young person who commits an offence under the chapter, as where a seventeen year old is made the visible seller in a drug peddling operation directed by adults, is treated as a child in conflict with law under the Act, so the chapter's targets remain the adults who organise, employ and profit. The non-reporting offence of Sections 33 and 34 belongs to the same scheme: the Act first conscripts everyone who encounters an unaccompanied young person into the reporting chain, and only then punishes the conduct the chain uncovers. The enforcement weakness most often recorded is not in this text but in the sequence it assumes: the offences are used where Committees, inspection committees and Child Welfare Police Officers are functioning well enough to detect the conduct and set the police in motion, and they lie unused where that machinery is dormant, which is a further reason the capacity question, treated in the assessment below, is the statute's real centre of gravity.

## The adoption chapter, and the statute's neighbours

Chapter VIII made this Act the single legislative home of adoption process for orphan, abandoned and surrendered children, whatever the religion of the adopters, sitting alongside the personal law routes it did not displace. Section 56 grounds adoption under the Act in the regulations framed by the Authority; Section 57 sets the eligibility of prospective adoptive parents, who must be physically fit, financially sound, mentally alert and highly motivated, with the age and marital conditions the chapter prescribes, a single male being barred from adopting a girl child; Section 58 prescribes the procedure for resident Indian parents adopting an orphan, abandoned or surrendered child, through a Specialised Adoption Agency and the Child Adoption Resource Information and Guidance System; Section 59 does the same for inter-country adoption; Section 61, as enacted, placed the passing of the adoption order in the courts; Section 63 gives the adoption its effect, the child becoming the child of the adoptive parents for all purposes with the ties to the birth family replaced; Section 67 obliges every state to set up a State Adoption Resource Agency; and Section 68 constitutes the Central Adoption Resource Authority as a statutory body, with the steering and regulatory functions Chapter VIII assigns it. The chapter's timelines and the declaration that a child is legally free for adoption under Section 38 are the hinge between the care track and a permanent family. Around the chapter's core, the Act builds a permanent adoption infrastructure rather than leaving the process to courts and custom. Every institution housing children must furnish details of those declared legally free for adoption to the Specialised Adoption Agency under Section 66, so that a declaration by a Committee in one proceeding becomes visible to the placement system in another. The State Adoption Resource Agency under Section 67 supervises and coordinates adoption within the state, and the Central Adoption Resource Authority under Section 68, working through a Steering Committee constituted under Section 69 and reporting annually under Section 71, frames the adoption regulations, operates the centralised matching system through which prospective parents are referred children in order of seniority and choice, issues the no objection certificate without which no inter-country adoption can be completed, and accredits the authorised foreign adoption agencies through which applications from abroad must move. The design premise, inherited from the Hague Convention the preamble invokes, is that adoption across a market of willing parents and a scarcity of legally free children will be abused unless matching, eligibility and the movement of children are taken out of private hands entirely, and the chapter therefore reads less like family law than like the charter of a regulated exchange, with the Committee's declaration as its listing decision and the Authority as its regulator. The offences of Section 80, punishing adoption outside the prescribed procedure, and of Section 81, punishing the sale and procurement of children, are the criminal walls of the same structure. The working detail of that machinery, the eligibility disputes and the inter-country process belong to the article on [adoption law and CARA in India](/2016/07/15/adoption-law-india-cara-explained/), which owns them.

The Act also has to be read beside the statute with which it shares its most sensitive cases. Where the allegation against a child is a sexual offence, the case sits at the junction of this Act and [the POCSO Act 2012 explained](/2016/04/15/pocso-act-2012-explained/): the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act supplies the offence definitions and the Special Court, and its own procedure provision directs that a child who commits an offence under that Act be dealt with under this Act, while the child victim of the same incident is a child in need of care and protection before the Committee, Section 30 expressly charging the Committee with acting for the rehabilitation of sexually abused children reported to it by the special juvenile police unit or the local police under the sexual offences statute. A single incident can therefore lawfully generate three proceedings under three logics at once: a Board inquiry into the child accused, a Committee process for the child harmed, and, where the accused is transferred, a trial before the very Special Court that the sexual offences statute created, and the district machinery's ability to keep those proceedings distinct, confidential under Section 74 and directed at different ends is the severest ordinary test of the system's design. The Children's Court that tries a transferred child is, where one has been designated, the POCSO Special Court itself, which is why the two statutes cannot be applied in ignorance of each other.

## The layer above the district: rules, monitoring and movement between states

A statute of this kind is only partly self-executing, and the Act's closing sections show where its promises are converted, or fail to be converted, into working systems. Section 110 gives the rule-making power to the State Governments, with the Central Government empowered to frame model rules that apply to a state, with the necessary changes, until that state makes its own, and that the state's rules must conform to. The matters left to rules are not peripheral: they include the qualifications and terms of Board and Committee members, the manner of registration of institutions and of cancelling it, the procedure of foster care and the inspection of foster families, the duration of sponsorship, the manner of financial support on leaving institutional care, the standards of the several kinds of home, the recognition of fit facilities and fit persons, the destruction of records under Section 24, and the functioning of the State Child Protection Society and the District Child Protection Units under Section 106. The practical consequence is that the Act a family meets in one state is the central text filtered through that state's rules, and two districts in different states can run visibly different systems under identical sections; the Act's uniformity is a uniformity of structure, deliberately, and not of detail. Section 109 assigns the monitoring of implementation to the National Commission for Protection of Child Rights and the State Commissions, which, while inquiring into any matter relating to an offence under the Act, carry the same powers they hold under the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005, and must include their work under this Act in their annual reports. Section 55 empowers the Central or State Government to cause an independent evaluation of the functioning of the Boards, Committees, special juvenile police units, registered institutions and recognised fit facilities and persons, so the system contains, on paper, three distinct external checks: the Commissions' monitoring, the governments' evaluation power, and the inspection committees of Section 54. Section 95 governs the movement of a young person between institutions and across state lines, permitting transfer to an institution of a corresponding character in another state with the concurrence the section requires, and providing travelling allowance for the escorting staff, a provision of real consequence for the care track, where restoration routinely means returning a trafficked or runaway young person to a home state whose Committee then takes charge. Section 92 allows a young person suffering from a disease requiring prolonged treatment, or a mental or physical complaint that will respond to treatment, to be sent to a recognised fit facility for the purpose, and Section 93 allows the transfer of a young person who is mentally ill or addicted to alcohol or other drugs to a psychiatric hospital or nursing home, and onward to an Integrated Rehabilitation Centre for Addicts, under the machinery those sections build on the mental health legislation. Section 112, finally, gave the Central Government a two year power, from commencement, to remove difficulties in giving effect to the Act by order laid before Parliament, the conventional closing device of a statute that expects its first years to expose drafting gaps the legislature cannot conveniently return to.

One office recurs at the edge of both tracks in the Act as enacted, and its original, limited role explains the shape of the 2021 amendment better than any commentary. The District Magistrate in the 2015 text is a reviewer and a recipient, not a decision maker in individual cases. The Magistrate conducts the quarterly review of the functioning of the Child Welfare Committee under Section 27 and is the grievance redressal authority for the Committee, before whom anyone connected with the child may file a petition. The Board furnishes its pendency information to the District Magistrate quarterly under Section 16, and the Committee submits its quarterly report on disposal and pendency to the District Magistrate under Section 36, whose review may produce directions for remedial measures, a report to the State Government, and, where pendency continues unaddressed three months after such directions, the termination of the Committee and the constitution of a new one. The design is a deliberate separation: the executive head of the district watches the system's speed and regularity, receives its grievances and reports its failures upward, while the decisions about individual children, their custody, their placement and their responsibility, remain with the Board, the Committee and the courts. The 2021 amendment's transfer of adoption orders to that same office, and its enlargement of the Magistrate's supervisory remit, therefore moved a genuinely judicial act across a line the original Act had drawn with some care, which is the ground on which the change has been both defended, as an administrative acceleration of a largely non-adversarial process, and criticised, as a dilution of the judicial scrutiny adoption had received since the Supreme Court first regularised inter-country adoption. The headline position is stated here; the working argument belongs to the adoption article.

## The amendment arc: the Act of 2021

The 2015 Act has been amended once, by the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Amendment Act, 2021, Act 23 of 2021, which received assent on 7 August 2021 and was brought into force on 1 September 2022. At the headline level, the amendment did four things. It transferred the power to issue adoption orders from the courts to District Magistrates, with an appeal to the Divisional Commissioner, a change whose adoption working belongs to the adoption article. It strengthened the eligibility conditions for Child Welfare Committee members, adding educational qualifications and experience requirements to the original activity test. It enlarged the District Magistrate's supervisory role over the district's child protection apparatus, including additional review of Committees and institutions. And it reclassified offences: the amendment redefined serious offences and altered Section 86 so that offences under the Act punishable with imprisonment of three years and above but not more than seven years became non-cognizable, a change challenged before the Supreme Court by the Delhi Commission for Protection of Child Rights on the ground that it weakens the reporting of offences against children, including cruelty, exploitation of a child employee and the use of children for peddling drugs. The amendment did not touch the transfer mechanism, the classification thresholds for children in conflict with law, or the Shilpa Mittal problem, which remains where the Court left it: awaiting a legislative call.

The identity prohibition has its own enforcement story, and it belongs in any honest account of the Act in operation. Section 74 is breached, in the ordinary course of crime reporting, with a regularity that no other provision of the Act quite matches: names altered by a syllable, schools and neighbourhoods named, photographs published with faces blurred in ways that identify rather than conceal, and details assembled from the reporting of the same incident across outlets until the young person is unmistakable to anyone who knows him. The provision's defences are real but slow: the penalty, imprisonment up to six months or a fine up to two lakh rupees, requires a prosecution that competes for police and court time with the underlying case itself, and the young person's remedy usually arrives, if it arrives, after the identification has done its work. The Supreme Court's direction in Shilpa Mittal, ordering the correction of a High Court judgment that had named the child before it, is the sharpest available statement that the discipline is not a courtesy extended by the press but a condition of the system's legitimacy, binding on courts most of all; a system that promises a young person a fresh start while permitting his name to be permanently attached to the worst act of his adolescence, in searchable form, has broken the promise at the point where it matters most to his future. The same tension runs through Section 74(2), which bars the police from disclosing a young person's record for a character certificate or otherwise once the case is closed or disposed of: the fresh start of Section 24 is only as strong as the record keeping discipline of the stations and registries that hold the paper, and the destruction the Board or the Children's Court directs is an order that someone must physically carry out. These are small provisions carrying the statute's largest promise, and their enforcement, largely invisible when it works, is one of the truest measures of whether the Act's theory of childhood survives contact with its administration.

## The honest assessment

On the offence track, the argument about Section 15 continues, and the stance of this series is to give it in the voices that hold it. The case for the transfer mechanism, made by Maneka Gandhi as the minister who carried the Bill and by the campaign that followed the Delhi case of December 2012, is that a person of seventeen who plans and commits a murder or a gang rape has exercised the capacities the criminal law exists to answer, that a maximum of three years in a special home is not a proportionate response either for victims or for public confidence, and that the assessment safeguard answers the objection: no child is transferred on the charge alone, but only after a Board has examined his capacity, his understanding and his circumstances. The case against, made by the Standing Committee that examined the Bill, by UNICEF in its submissions on the legislation, and by child rights organisations such as the HAQ Centre for Child Rights, whose co-director Enakshi Ganguly has argued the point across the Act's life, is that the assessment demands a determination of mental capacity and understanding that Boards are not equipped or staffed to make reliably; that the children who come before Boards are drawn overwhelmingly from poor families without the resources to contest an assessment, so a discretion of this kind carries a class risk in its application; that transfer severs the child from the fresh start principle for reasons of the offence's gravity rather than the child's culpability; and that no evidence establishes that trying children as adults reduces offending by children. Those are the strongest forms of both positions; their resolution is an argument about evidence and about what a justice system owes a child, and it is conducted in full in the age debate article. What can be said on the text is narrower, and Shilpa Mittal has already said part of it: the transfer class is smaller than the debate usually assumes, bounded by the minimum punishment requirement, by the double decision of Board and Children's Court, and by the survival of the Section 21 protections after transfer.

On the care track, the weaknesses are of a different kind: they are weaknesses of capacity rather than of design. The chapter's reach depends on Committees that sit twenty days a month, conduct social investigations in fifteen days and inspect the institutions in their district twice a month, and on Boards that finish inquiries in four months; yet the recurring findings of reviews and inspections are of Committees constituted late or left with vacancies, members without the training Section 27 assumes, districts where the District Child Protection Unit is a single officer with additional charges, and homes that continue unregistered years after Section 41 made registration a condition of lawful operation, with prosecutions under Section 42 rare enough to be notable. In Sampurna Behura v. Union of India in 2018, the Supreme Court, reviewing the working of the Act's machinery across the country, issued directions directed at exactly this layer: the constitution and staffing of Boards and Committees, the setting up of the special juvenile police units, and the inspection and registration of institutions. A practitioner working through a district's Board and Committee papers can keep the inspection reports, social investigation reports and case records that these bodies generate in a single ordered file; readers who handle such papers in the spreadsheet and document formats in which they circulate can open them with [the ReportMedic office file viewer for Excel, Word and PowerPoint files](https://reportmedic.org/tools/office-file-viewer-excel-docx-pptx.html) rather than chasing the application that produced each one. The verdict the record supports is asymmetrical. The offence track's contested provision operates in a small number of cases under heavy judicial supervision, while the care track's uncontested provisions govern the lives of the far larger number of children the statute touches, and it is the care track, the one the public argument ignores, where the distance between the text and the district remains widest. One title, two statutes: the Act should be judged as two, and on the evidence the welfare code it contains is the larger achievement and the larger failure at once.

The same capacity gap shows on the care track's paper obligations. A Committee's orders are only as good as the social investigation beneath them and the individual care plan above them, and both documents depend on Child Welfare Officers and probation officers whose numbers the Act assumes rather than creates. Where those posts are vacant or doubled up, the Committee's fifteen day investigation becomes a form, its four month inquiry becomes a queue, and its twice monthly inspection of institutions becomes a signature. The criticism made of Committees, that persons without legal training decide questions of custody, guardianship and adoption readiness, with uneven reasoning and little consistency between districts, is a criticism the Act anticipated in part: Section 27 builds in eligibility conditions, induction training within two months of constitution, a three year term limit, quarterly District Magistrate review and a grievance route to the District Magistrate, and the 2021 amendment tightened the eligibility side further. The remaining deficit is not in the text. It is in the willingness of State Governments to constitute the bodies on time, fill them with the people the section describes, and pay for the staff the district unit needs; and it recurs, in almost identical words, in reviews of the Board system, where Principal Magistrates routinely carry the Board as an additional charge beside a full ordinary docket. For the reader who must use the system rather than judge it, the operational conclusions are plain enough. A police officer's duties are front loaded and unforgiving: charge to the Child Welfare Police Officer, no lockup, production in twenty four hours, intimation to parents and probation officer. A person in charge of any home housing children, however small and however privately funded, is inside Section 41 and exposed under Section 42, and the thirty day separate offence rule means delay compounds. A parent or advocate in the offence track should test every bail refusal against the three statutory grounds and every assessment for the expert assistance and the Children's Court's independent satisfaction the Act requires. And every reader should hold the two tracks apart, because the statute's benefits and its dangers are distributed unevenly between them, and the argument conducted about one is no measure of the other.

For the examination candidate, on whom this statute is a perennial subject, the marks lie in a small set of distinctions that this article has kept separate throughout, and they are worth gathering in one place. The Board adjudicates and the Committee protects, and neither may do the other's work; the preliminary assessment decides the forum and never the guilt; the classification turns on the punishment the law prescribes and not on the facts of the case, and after Shilpa Mittal an offence without a seven year minimum is never heinous however grave its maximum; bail is the rule and its refusal the reasoned exception; the twenty four hour production rule governs both tracks; the death sentence and life imprisonment without the possibility of release are barred against every child in every forum, including a transferred child in a Children's Court; records are destroyed and disqualification removed for a child dealt with inside the system, and both protections are withheld from the child tried as an adult; and the Act that bears the year 2015 in its name is Act 2 of 2016 in the Gazette. A candidate who holds those nine lines apart has the statute; a candidate who merges any two of them has the confusion the Act's critics and its examiners both trade in.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does the Juvenile Justice Act 2015 deal with?

The Act deals with two groups of children under one title. It governs children in conflict with law, meaning children alleged or found to have committed an offence before turning eighteen, through Juvenile Justice Boards, a graded classification of offences and a preliminary assessment that can transfer a child above sixteen accused of a heinous offence to a Children's Court for trial as an adult. It also governs children in need of care and protection, through Child Welfare Committees, registered child care institutions, foster care, sponsorship and adoption. Sixteen principles in Section 3, including best interest, family responsibility and a fresh start, govern both tracks.

### Q: Who is a child in conflict with law?

A child in conflict with law is a child who is alleged or found to have committed an offence and who had not completed eighteen years of age on the date the offence was committed, under Section 2(13) of the Act. The reckoning date is the date of the act, not the date of arrest or trial, so a person apprehended at twenty for an offence committed at seventeen is dealt with under this Act, and Section 6 confirms that such a person is treated as a child through the inquiry. The category covers every offence grade in the Act, petty, serious and heinous, and it ends in orders by the Juvenile Justice Board or, after transfer, the Children's Court.

### Q: Who is a child in need of care and protection?

Section 2(14) defines the category by a list of situations rather than a single test. It includes a child without a home or settled place of abode and without ostensible means of subsistence; a child working in contravention of labour laws, found begging or living on the street; a child abused, neglected or threatened by the person he lives with; a child whose parent or guardian is unfit or incapacitated; a child without parents, or abandoned or surrendered; a missing or runaway child; a child likely to be abused or exploited, trafficked or inducted into drug abuse; a child affected by armed conflict, civil unrest or calamity; and a child at imminent risk of marriage before the lawful age.

### Q: How are offences classified under the Juvenile Justice Act?

The Act grades offences by the punishment they carry. Petty offences, under Section 2(45), are those punishable with imprisonment up to three years. Serious offences, under Section 2(54), are those punishable with imprisonment between three to seven years. Heinous offences, under Section 2(33), are those for which the minimum punishment is imprisonment for seven years or more. The grade decides the procedure: petty cases go through summary proceedings, serious cases follow the summons case procedure, and heinous cases involving a child above sixteen trigger the preliminary assessment. In Shilpa Mittal v. State of NCT of Delhi (2020), the Supreme Court held that offences with a maximum above seven years but no minimum, or a minimum below seven, form a fourth category treated as serious offences until Parliament acts.

### Q: What does a preliminary assessment by the Juvenile Justice Board decide?

Under Section 15, where a child who has completed sixteen years is alleged to have committed a heinous offence, the Board assesses the child's mental and physical capacity to commit the offence, his ability to understand its consequences and the circumstances in which it was allegedly committed, taking expert help where needed. The assessment is not a trial and decides nothing about guilt. It decides the route of the case: whether the Board disposes of the matter itself following the summons case procedure, or whether there is a need for trial as an adult, in which case the case is transferred to the Children's Court, which then makes its own independent decision under Section 19.

### Q: Can a child be sentenced to life imprisonment in India?

A child in conflict with law cannot be sentenced to death or to life imprisonment without the possibility of release, for any offence, under Section 21 of the Act, and that bar survives even where a child above sixteen is transferred and tried as an adult by a Children's Court. A transferred child can receive a long sentence, including life imprisonment with the possibility of release, but must first be kept in a place of safety until the age of twenty one, with reformative services and annual progress reviews, and transferred to a jail only thereafter. A child dealt with by the Board itself faces no imprisonment at all; the Board's strongest ordinary order is placement in a special home for up to three years.

### Q: Must a child care institution be registered?

Yes. Section 41 requires every institution meant wholly or partly for housing children in need of care and protection or children in conflict with law to be registered under the Act, whether it is run by a State Government or by a voluntary or non-governmental organisation, and whether or not it receives government grants. Registration records the institution's capacity and purpose and runs for five years, renewable. Section 42 makes non-registration an offence: the person in charge is punishable with imprisonment up to one year, or a fine of not less than one lakh rupees, or both, and every thirty days of delay in applying counts as a separate offence. Inspection committees may inspect even unregistered homes.

### Q: Does a juvenile conviction stay on record?

Ordinarily, no. The principle of fresh start in Section 3 requires that a child's past records under the system be erased except in special circumstances, and Section 24 removes every disqualification attaching to a conviction from a child dealt with under the Act and directs the destruction of the relevant records of conviction after the period of appeal expires or a prescribed reasonable period passes. The police may not disclose a child's record for a character certificate once a case is closed or disposed of. The exception is deliberate: where a child above sixteen is tried as an adult and found in conflict with law by the Children's Court for a heinous offence, the disqualification bar does not apply and the Children's Court retains the records.

### Q: What is a Children's Court under the Juvenile Justice Act?

Section 2(20) defines a Children's Court as a court established under the Commissions for Protection of Child Rights Act, 2005, or a Special Court under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012, wherever such courts exist, and where they have not been designated, the Court of Sessions having jurisdiction to try offences under the Act. Its distinctive role is the second stage of the transfer process: it receives the Board's preliminary assessment, decides for itself whether the child should be tried as an adult or inquired into as a Board would, and, where it tries the child as an adult, must include an individual care plan for rehabilitation in its final order and keep the child in a place of safety until twenty one.

### Q: When can a claim of juvenility be raised?

At any stage. The proviso to Section 9(2) provides that a claim that a person was a child on the date of the offence may be raised before any court and shall be recognised at any stage, even after final disposal of the case, and even if the person has ceased to be a child before the Act commenced. The court inquires into the claim on evidence other than an affidavit and records a finding of age; if the person was a child on the date of the offence, the case goes to the Board and any sentence passed is deemed to have no effect. The Supreme Court in Rishipal Singh Solanki v. State of Uttar Pradesh (2021) confirmed that delay in raising the claim is no ground for rejecting it.

### Q: How is the age of a child determined under the Juvenile Justice Act?

Section 94 prescribes an order of evidence. The Board or Committee first looks to the date of birth certificate from the school the child first attended, or the matriculation or equivalent certificate where available. Failing those, it looks to the birth certificate issued by a corporation, a municipal authority or a panchayat. Only in the absence of both may age be determined by medical opinion, through an ossification test or another medical age determination test conducted by a duly constituted medical board. The age recorded by the Board or Committee on this evidence is deemed to be the true age of the person for the purposes of the Act, and the exercise is an inquiry on documents, not a contest on affidavits alone.

### Q: Can a child in conflict with law get bail?

Yes, and the Act makes bail the rule rather than the exception. Section 12 provides that a person who is apparently a child, apprehended for a bailable or non-bailable offence, shall be released on bail with or without surety, or placed under the supervision of a probation officer or a fit person, overriding the ordinary code. Bail may be refused only on three recorded grounds: that release is likely to bring the child into association with any known criminal, expose the child to moral, physical or psychological danger, or defeat the ends of justice. A child refused bail is kept in an observation home or a place of safety, never a lockup or jail, and a child who cannot fulfil bail conditions within seven days must be brought back to the Board for the conditions to be modified.

### Q: What orders can the Juvenile Justice Board pass after an inquiry?

Where the Board finds that a child has committed a petty or serious offence, or a heinous offence while below sixteen, Section 18 gives it a graduated set of orders: the child may go home after advice or admonition with counselling of the child and parents; be directed to group counselling; be ordered to perform community service under supervision; be ordered, or the parents ordered, to pay a fine; be released on probation of good conduct under a parent, guardian, fit person or fit facility for up to three years; or be sent to a special home for up to three years for reformative services including education, skill development, counselling and therapy. The Board may add orders for school attendance, vocational training, a therapeutic centre or a de-addiction programme, and it cannot order imprisonment.

### Q: What orders can the Child Welfare Committee pass?

On finding a child to be in need of care and protection after inquiry, Section 37 empowers the Committee to declare the child in need of care and protection; restore the child to parents, guardian or family with or without supervision; place the child in a Children's Home, a fit facility or a Specialised Adoption Agency for temporary or long term care; place the child with a fit person; pass foster care orders and sponsorship orders; direct the persons or institutions holding the child to provide shelter, medical attention, psychiatric and psychological support, counselling, skill training, legal aid and education; and declare the child legally free for adoption. Restoration to the family is the system's stated first objective, and institutional placement is structured as the measure of last resort.

### Q: What happens when a child tried as an adult turns twenty one?

A child found in conflict with law by a Children's Court after trial as an adult is kept in a place of safety, not a jail, until he attains twenty one years, receiving reformative services and an annual follow up report on his progress. Section 20 then requires the Children's Court, when the person turns twenty one and the term of stay is not complete, to evaluate with the progress records and expert opinion whether he has undergone reformative change and can be a contributing member of society. The Court may release him on conditions, including appointment of a monitoring authority for the remainder of the term, or direct that the remainder of the term be completed in a jail, to which he is then transferred.

### Q: Can a child be tried together with an adult for the same offence?

No. Section 23 prohibits any joint proceedings of a child alleged to be in conflict with law with a person who is not a child, notwithstanding the joint trial provisions of the Code of Criminal Procedure or any other law. Where a child and an adult are accused in the same incident, the child's case proceeds separately before the Board, or before the Children's Court if the transfer route is engaged, and if during an inquiry the person is found not to be a child, he is not tried along with a child either. The separation works with Section 21, which bars the death sentence and life imprisonment without possibility of release, to keep even a transferred child's trial distinct in forum, exposure and consequence from the trial of adult co-accused.

### Q: What offences does the Juvenile Justice Act create against children?

Chapter IX creates offences by adults against children. Section 75 punishes cruelty by a person in charge of a child, with higher punishment where the offender works for an organisation entrusted with the child's care or the child is incapacitated. Section 76 punishes employing a child for begging, Section 77 giving a child intoxicating liquor, narcotic drugs, tobacco or psychotropic substances, Section 78 using a child to vend or smuggle such substances, and Section 79 exploiting a child employee by keeping him in bondage or withholding his earnings. The chapter also punishes adoption outside the prescribed procedure, the sale and procurement of children, corporal punishment in child care institutions, and the recruitment or use of children by militant groups or by adults for illegal activities, with doubled penalties where the child is disabled.

### Q: Can a media report disclose the identity of a child in conflict with law?

No. Section 74 prohibits any report in a newspaper, magazine, news-sheet, audio-visual media or other form of communication regarding an inquiry, investigation or judicial proceeding from disclosing the name, address, school or any particular that may lead to the identification of a child in conflict with law, a child in need of care and protection, or a child victim or witness of a crime, and prohibits publishing the child's picture. The only exception is written permission by the Board or Committee holding the inquiry, given for recorded reasons because disclosure is in the child's best interest. Contravention is punishable with imprisonment up to six months or a fine up to two lakh rupees or both, and the Supreme Court has directed the correction of a judgment that named a child.

### Q: What is a place of safety under the Juvenile Justice Act?

A place of safety is an institution, expressly not a police lockup or a jail, established under Section 49 for children above sixteen who are alleged or found to have committed a heinous offence, for persons above eighteen apprehended for an offence committed while a child, and for children transferred to a Children's Court for trial as an adult. A child found in conflict with law by a Children's Court is kept in a place of safety until the age of twenty one and transferred to a jail only after the review Section 20 requires. During the stay the institution must provide reformative services, education, skill development, counselling, behaviour modification therapy and psychiatric support, and the child's progress is reported to the Children's Court every year.

### Q: What did the Juvenile Justice Amendment Act 2021 change?

The Amendment Act, Act 23 of 2021, assented to on 7 August 2021 and brought into force on 1 September 2022, changed the administration of the Act rather than its core design. It transferred the power to issue adoption orders from the courts to District Magistrates, with an appeal to the Divisional Commissioner. It strengthened the eligibility criteria for Child Welfare Committee members, enlarged the District Magistrate's supervisory role over Committees and child care institutions, and reclassified Chapter IX offences punishable with imprisonment of three years and above but not more than seven years as non-cognizable, a change the Delhi Commission for Protection of Child Rights has challenged as weakening the reporting of abuse. The preliminary assessment, the offence classification for children and the Board and Committee structure were left untouched.
