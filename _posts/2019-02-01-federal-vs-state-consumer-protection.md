## Two Systems for One Wrong

The comparison described as federal vs state consumer protection begins, in most instances, with a single transaction that went wrong. A buyer paid for a repair that a shop never completed, signed a credit contract whose price differed from the price quoted at the counter, or relied on an advertisement that described a product in terms the product could not meet. The loss is often modest, a few hundred dollars or a damaged credit record, and the conduct is rarely dramatic. The legal question, however, is structural. Two systems of consumer law claim authority over the same deception, one enacted by Congress and administered from Washington, the other enacted by fifty state legislatures and administered close to the place of sale. A consumer cheated in a transaction has to learn which body of law belongs to that consumer in a practical sense: which statute supplies a claim, which official can act on a complaint, and which forum will hear the dispute.

![Federal vs state consumer protection compared across legislatures, agencies, and courts - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)


That choice confronts three distinct audiences. The injured consumer encounters the choice first, usually after a complaint to the seller has failed, when the issue is whether a court will recognize a personal claim or whether relief depends on persuading a public agency to take the matter up. The student encounters the choice as a problem of design, because the two systems were built in different periods and with different assumptions about who should enforce a prohibition on deception. The practitioner encounters the choice as a problem of selection, since pleadings, defenses, and settlement value all shift with the governing statute. A lawyer evaluating a deceptive practice in 2018, for example, had to decide whether to plead a state claim, a sectoral federal claim, or both, and had to anticipate motions addressed to preemption and to arbitration.

American consumer protection law did not begin as a pair of parallel systems. For much of the nineteenth century, a cheated buyer relied on common law actions for deceit and breach of warranty, doctrines that demanded proof of a false statement, knowledge of falsity, intent to induce reliance, actual reliance, and damages. Those elements protected sellers as much as buyers, because a seller could escape liability by characterizing a claim as opinion, or by omitting a fact rather than misstating one. Early state legislatures answered portions of that problem with narrow criminal measures against false advertising, often called printer's ink statutes after a model promoted in 1911. The federal system arrived in 1914, and the state systems in the modern sense arrived mainly in the 1960s and 1970s. Sequence matters, because state law was often a deliberate reaction to limits perceived in federal law.

The federal system rests on a single broad prohibition surrounded by narrower statutes addressed to particular markets. Congress created the Federal Trade Commission in 1914 through the Federal Trade Commission Act, and Section 5 of that Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. 45, supplied the prohibition around which the federal structure was later assembled. In the form enacted in 1914, Section 5 declared unfair methods of competition unlawful and empowered the Commission to prevent such methods, language directed principally at injury to competitors. A deceptive advertisement fell within that language when deception injured a rival or distorted competition. The text gave the new agency an open standard rather than a catalogue of forbidden acts, and that openness became the defining feature of federal consumer law.

The Wheeler-Lea Act of 1938 changed the center of gravity in Section 5. By amendments signed on March 21, 1938, Congress added a declaration that unfair or deceptive acts or practices in commerce were unlawful, separate from the prohibition on unfair methods of competition, and added provisions addressed to false advertising of food, drugs, devices, and cosmetics. After 1938, the Commission no longer had to connect consumer deception to injury to a competitor before acting under Section 5. A practice that misled purchasers, or that treated purchasers unfairly, could violate federal law because of the injury to consumers alone. That amendment converted a competition statute with consumer benefits into a consumer statute in its own right, and Section 5 became the broadest federal standard in the field. Its operative terms received content through adjudication, policy statements, and court review after 1938, including a deception policy statement in 1983 and an unfairness standard that Congress codified in 1994 at Section 5(n), requiring substantial injury that consumers could not reasonably avoid and that countervailing benefits did not outweigh.

Breadth at the center did not prevent Congress from building specific statutes around Section 5. In credit disclosure, the Truth in Lending Act of 1968 required creditors to disclose the cost and terms of consumer credit in a standardized form, so that a borrower could compare an annual percentage rate and a finance charge across competing offers. In credit reporting, the Fair Credit Reporting Act of 1970 regulated consumer reporting agencies, the businesses that furnish information to those agencies, and the users of consumer reports, and gave consumers rights to obtain file disclosures and to dispute inaccurate information. In debt collection, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act of 1977 restricted third party debt collectors, barred harassment and false representations in collection, and required validation information for disputed debts. In product safety, the Consumer Product Safety Act of 1972 created the Consumer Product Safety Commission and authorized federal safety standards, bans of hazardous products, and corrective action for products presenting substantial hazards. In warranty law, the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act of 1975 regulated written warranties on consumer products, required disclosure of warranty terms before sale, and restricted disclaimer of implied warranties when a written warranty was given. Each statute has a defined market, a defined set of duties, and a defined enforcement scheme. Other federal measures filled adjoining ground, including the Equal Credit Opportunity Act of 1974 and the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010, which created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau with authority over unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices in consumer financial products. None displaced Section 5, and the federal system at February 1, 2019 was layered, with a general prohibition enforced by the Federal Trade Commission and market specific statutes whose remedies varied.

The Commission historically proceeded through investigation, administrative complaint, hearing, and cease and desist order, subject to review in a federal court of appeals, and a respondent that violated a final order faced civil penalties. Congress expanded the toolkit in stages. Amendments in 1973 added Section 13(b), which authorized the Commission to seek injunctions in federal district court and which courts read as support for restitution and disgorgement in appropriate cases. Amendments in 1975 gave the Commission substantive trade regulation rulemaking authority under Section 18, and rules adopted under that authority carried civil penalty consequences for knowing violations. The structure gave a single national agency power to set a uniform floor against deception, at the cost of dependence on agency priorities and appropriations.

Against that background, the absence of a private right under the Federal Trade Commission Act stands out as a deliberate structural fact rather than an oversight awaiting correction. Section 5 grants enforcement authority to the Commission, and the statute contains no language authorizing an injured consumer to file suit for a Section 5 violation. Federal courts confronted the question directly in litigation of the early 1970s, and the answer given in that period governed the field afterward. The mechanics of Commission action matter here, because an agency that proceeds through rules, guides, adjudication, and consent orders leaves a different record from a legislature that creates a damages action, and [an account of how the Federal Trade Commission writes its consumer rules](/2019/01/15/ftc-consumer-protection-rulemaking/) supplies the institutional detail behind that distinction. A consumer who identifies deception that violates Section 5, without more, holds a complaint to present to the Commission rather than a federal claim to file in court, and the significance of state law in the comparison derives in large part from that gap.

### Can a consumer sue under the Federal Trade Commission Act?

No. Courts held by 1973 that Section 5 gives enforcement to the Federal Trade Commission alone, and a consumer harmed by deception has no claim under the Federal Trade Commission Act itself. Relief for that consumer must come from a sectoral federal statute or from state law.

The leading decision was Holloway v. Bristol-Myers Corporation, decided by the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit in 1973, which held that the Act conferred no private right of action on consumers. The court in Holloway examined the text and legislative history of the Act as enacted in 1914 and amended in 1938 and found strong indication that Congress intended enforcement through the Commission, with its screening function and its ability to weigh public interest considerations that a private suit does not replicate. Other circuits reached the same result, including the Ninth Circuit in Carlson v. Coca-Cola Company in 1973. The holding rested on allocation, a judgment that a broad prohibition, enforceable at agency discretion, should not convert every deceptive act into a federal damages case. That allocation explains why many sectoral statutes do create express private actions with statutory damages and fee provisions, because Congress supplied in those statutes the element that Section 5 withholds.

The state system developed on the far side of that allocation. By the early 1980s, and in most states considerably earlier, every one of the fifty states had enacted an unfair and deceptive acts and practices statute, a family of laws commonly abbreviated as UDAP statutes and often nicknamed little FTC Acts. The nickname captures a genuine lineage. Most state statutes were modelled on the federal trade statute, on the uniform acts circulated in the 1960s, or on a combination of federal language and uniform language adapted to local institutions. Washington acted early with a Consumer Protection Act in 1961 that tracked Section 5 closely. Massachusetts enacted Chapter 93A in 1967. Other states adopted general prohibitions in the same period or in the surge of enactments that ran through the 1970s. The statutes share a recognizable core, a ban on unfair or deceptive conduct in trade or commerce, and diverge in almost every operational detail that determines whether a claim succeeds.

Two model texts account for a large share of that shared core. The Uniform Deceptive Trade Practices Act, promulgated by the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws in 1964 and revised in 1966, listed specific deceptive practices, such as passing off goods or services as those of another, causing confusion about source or sponsorship, and advertising with intent not to sell as advertised, and closed the list with a general clause against conduct that similarly created a likelihood of confusion or misunderstanding. That uniform act grew out of unfair competition concerns, and its characteristic remedy was injunctive relief, supplemented after the 1966 revision by attorney fee provisions in defined circumstances. The Federal Trade Commission model, formally the Model Unfair Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Law, was developed by the Commission, adopted through the Council of State Governments as suggested state legislation, published in 1967, and amended in 1969 and 1970. That model joined a general prohibition modeled on Section 5 to public enforcement and a private action with damages and fees. State legislatures borrowed from one text, from the other, or from Section 5 directly, and then added local choices on exemptions, scienter, reliance, class treatment, and remedies.

Administration at the state level follows a pattern distinct from the federal pattern. A state attorney general serves as the principal public enforcer of the UDAP statute in most states, acting through a consumer protection division that receives complaints, investigates patterns of conduct, and files enforcement actions for injunctions, restitution, and civil penalties. Alongside attorneys general, many states maintain consumer agencies with complaint handling, licensing, and education functions, and some statutes assign roles to district attorneys or specialized regulators. Several states authorize an attorney general to adopt rules defining deceptive practices, and Massachusetts offers a prominent example, since Chapter 93A empowered the attorney general to issue regulations that incorporate certain federal standards by reference. A state office can pursue a practice confined to one state that would rarely justify expenditure of federal resources.

The substantive relationship between the two systems is one of overlap without identity. A deceptive practice frequently violates Section 5 and a state UDAP statute at the same time, and many state statutes instruct courts to consider federal guidance when state courts construe parallel state language. Federal guidance in that setting includes Commission decisions, federal court decisions under Section 5, and policy statements that articulate deception as a representation, omission, or practice likely to mislead a consumer acting reasonably, where the representation or omission is material. State law nevertheless remains independent law. A state legislature can prohibit conduct that federal law leaves untouched, can excuse proof of individual reliance in defined settings, and can attach remedies that federal law does not provide under Section 5. Conversely, a state statute can contain exemptions for regulated industries or for conduct permitted under other state law, exemptions with no counterpart in the federal standard.

Preemption and arbitration complicate that overlap from opposite directions. Federal law can displace state law under the Supremacy Clause when Congress has expressed an intent to occupy a field, when compliance with both laws is impossible, or when state law stands as an obstacle to federal purposes, and several sectoral statutes contain express preemption clauses, as the Fair Credit Reporting Act did in provisions expanded in 1996 and made permanent in 2003. Other federal statutes contain savings clauses that preserve state law, and state UDAP claims often survive preemption analysis because general prohibitions on deception regulate conduct rather than impose requirements specific to a federally regulated label or disclosure. Arbitration operates through a contract rather than through a hierarchy of legislatures. A consumer contract containing an arbitration clause and a class action waiver can remove a state UDAP claim from court altogether, under the Federal Arbitration Act of 1925 as construed in AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion in 2011 and American Express Company v. Italian Colors Restaurant in 2013, even when the underlying state statute remains valid and even when a class proceeding was the only economically rational vehicle for a small claim.

The five axes that organize the balance of the comparison separate those effects. The first axis is the private right of action, the threshold question of who may invoke a prohibition in court. On that axis the contrast is stark at the general standard level and narrower at the sectoral level, since the Federal Trade Commission Act withholds a private action while most state UDAP statutes grant one in express terms, and many federal sectoral statutes grant one as well. The second axis is preemption, which asks whether a state claim may operate at all in an area that federal law regulates, and which turns on the text of the particular federal statute and the presence of an express preemption clause or a savings clause. The third axis is remedies, covering actual damages, statutory minimum damages, multiple damages for willful or knowing conduct, restitution, injunctive relief, civil penalties payable to the state, and awards of attorney fees and costs, a set of choices that determines whether a meritorious claim can attract representation. The fourth axis is enforcers, public and private, including the Federal Trade Commission, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau after 2010, sector regulators, state attorneys general, and private plaintiffs acting individually or through class actions. The fifth axis is arbitration, which asks whether a predispute agreement diverts a claim from the forum that the governing statute presupposes, and whether a state rule against class waivers can survive the Federal Arbitration Act after Concepcion. Each axis isolates a different mechanism, and a system that prevails on one axis can lose practical effect on another.

Two cautions govern use of those axes. A comparison stated at the level of systems can conceal variation that decides real cases, because state UDAP law is fifty bodies of law rather than one, and federal law is a general standard plus sectoral exceptions rather than a single code. A claim involving a deceptive credit disclosure in California in 2018, for example, implicated a state statute with a broad private action and a federal statute with an express preemption clause, while a claim involving a deceptive home repair in Illinois in the same year implicated a state statute with fee shifting and a federal standard with no private action at all. Doctrines also shifted at identifiable moments, in 1938, in the 1960s and 1970s, in 1994, and in 2011 with Concepcion. Statements about either system carry an implicit date, and the comparison fixes that date at February 1, 2019.

Stated as institutions, the federal system offers a national standard of great breadth, public enforcement by specialized agencies, and sectoral statutes that supply private rights in defined markets, while the state system offers a parallel standard in each state, public enforcement by an attorney general with local complaint intake, and a general private action that in most states reaches the ordinary consumer transaction directly. The federal system trades private access for uniformity and administrative screening. The state system trades uniformity for access and local responsiveness. Neither trade can be evaluated in the abstract, because preemption can narrow the state standard, remedies can weaken the private action that a state statute creates on paper, and an arbitration clause can prevent a court from reaching either body of law. The verdict at the close of the comparison assigns weight among the axes rather than treating the five as equivalents. On the evidence assembled through 2018, the arbitration axis carried unusual weight, since a clause in a standard form contract could decide the outcome before preemption, remedies, or the identity of the enforcer had any opportunity to matter.

## The Private Right of Action and the Remedies That Follow

The first axis of comparison concerns the identity of the plaintiff. Federal consumer protection law, in its general form, assigns enforcement to an agency and withholds the courtroom from the consumer. Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, enacted in 1914 and expanded by the Wheeler-Lea Amendments of 1938, declares unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce unlawful. The statute contains no provision authorizing a person injured by such a practice to file suit under Section 5. A consumer deceived by a false advertisement or charged a fee that disclosure concealed held, under the federal general statute, a grievance without a federal cause of action. The right to complain existed. The right to sue under Section 5 did not.

The controlling authority for that proposition, as the law stood through 2018, was Holloway v. Bristol-Myers Corp., 485 F.2d 986, decided by the United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit in 1973. The plaintiffs in Holloway alleged that advertising for analgesic products violated Sections 5, 12, and 14 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, and sought declaratory and injunctive relief together with compensatory and punitive damages. The district court dismissed the statutory claims, and the court of appeals affirmed. The opinion reasoned from the text, the legislative history of the 1914 enactment and the 1938 amendments, and the architecture of the enforcement scheme that Congress had entrusted enforcement of the Act to the Federal Trade Commission and had created no private right of action by implication. Congress had built a specialized tribunal with investigative and adjudicative machinery, and implication of a parallel private action would have disturbed the balance that the design embodied. Later courts followed Holloway without serious division, and a complaint that pleaded Section 5 as a private claim invited dismissal.

Exclusion of the private plaintiff did not leave the federal statute toothless, because the Commission held enforcement tools of considerable force, each with limits built in. The original instrument was administrative adjudication. Under Section 5(b) of the Act, the Commission could issue a complaint, conduct a hearing, make findings, and enter a cease and desist order directing the respondent to stop the unlawful practice. The order operated prospectively, and violation of a final order exposed the respondent to civil penalties in a separate action, but the proceeding itself produced no payment to the consumers whom the deception had injured. The asymmetry shaped the whole field: the federal general statute could stop a practice across an industry, and the same statute could not, through its central proceeding, return money to the households that had paid for the deception.

Congress addressed part of that gap in 1975, when the Magnuson-Moss Warranty and Federal Trade Commission Improvement Act added Section 19, codified at 15 U.S.C. 57b. Section 19 authorized the Commission to file suit in federal court for consumer redress, including refund of money and return of property, after a final cease and desist order had issued against the respondent for the practice in question. The redress action carried a condition that expressed congressional caution about monetary relief: the Commission had to show that the act or practice was one that a reasonable person would have known was dishonest or fraudulent. Ordinary deception and unfairness without a dishonest or fraudulent character fit awkwardly within the standard, and the sequencing requirement meant that redress followed adjudication rather than accompanying the initial enforcement decision. Section 19 also supplied a redress route for violations of Commission trade regulation rules, where no antecedent order was necessary. Outside rule violations and litigated orders, the path from a deceptive practice to money in consumer hands was long, conditional, and entirely in agency hands.

The Commission found a faster courtroom route in Section 13(b), codified at 15 U.S.C. 53(b), a provision added in 1973 that authorized the Commission to seek preliminary relief and, in proper cases, a permanent injunction in federal district court. As the provision stood in 2018, Section 13(b) had become the Commission's principal litigation vehicle in consumer protection matters. The agency filed suit directly in court, without a prior administrative adjudication, and courts of appeals in most circuits had read the grant of injunctive authority to carry the full range of equitable powers, including restitution and disgorgement. That reading allowed the Commission to obtain monetary judgments for consumers in the same action that halted the practice, and by 2018 the Commission routinely sought such relief in cases involving phantom debt collection, deceptive lending, and fraudulent business opportunities. The textual basis was inference rather than express language, since Section 13(b) speaks of injunctions and does not name restitution, and the contrast with Section 19 generated a structural objection that scholars and defense counsel pressed with increasing energy during the 2010s. The objection had not prevailed in most circuits by the end of 2018. Federal enforcement as of that date thus presented two halves: an agency with no private counterpart under Section 5, and an agency with a monetary program of real scale on an inferred foundation.

Against that federal background, the state side of the comparison presents a near inversion. Beginning in the 1960s and continuing through the 1970s, state legislatures enacted general consumer protection statutes modeled on Section 5, borrowing the federal prohibition of unfair or deceptive acts or practices and, in many states, directing courts to give weight to federal interpretations of the federal model. The statutes acquired a collective label that recorded the borrowing: little FTC Acts. The borrowing, however, was selective in the one respect that mattered most to an injured consumer. The typical little FTC Act conferred enforcement authority on the state attorney general, and then went a step the federal act had refused to take, by creating a private right of action for a person injured by a prohibited practice. The result, settled well before 2018, was that the operative law for an individual consumer claim was almost always state law. A dispute over a deceptive car sale or a home repair contract procured by misrepresentation proceeded, if the dispute proceeded in court at all, under a state unfair and deceptive practices statute, with federal general law in the background as interpretive guidance rather than as a source of claims. The federal statute supplied much of the vocabulary. The state statute supplied the plaintiff and the judgment.

Variation among the states was wide. States differed on the practices covered, on exemptions for regulated industries, on reliance and public interest requirements, on class actions, and on limitations periods. On the axis under examination, who may sue and what a successful plaintiff recovers, a common pattern appeared across otherwise divergent statutes. The private action existed, and the remedial scheme attached to the private action was built, in state after state, from a small set of recurring components: actual damages, a statutory minimum or statutory damages in place of proof of loss, multiplication of damages for knowing or willful conduct, injunctive relief, and an award of attorney fees and costs to a prevailing consumer. Each component addressed a known failure of ordinary private litigation in consumer cases, and the combination explains why state claims carried practical force that the federal general claim delivered only through enforcement discretion.

Massachusetts illustrated the pattern in a developed form. The Massachusetts Consumer Protection Act, Chapter 93A of the Massachusetts General Laws, prohibited unfair or deceptive acts or practices, and Section 9 gave a consumer a private action with a remedial structure of unusual precision. A prevailing consumer recovered actual damages or twenty five dollars, whichever was greater, so that a proven violation with trivial loss did not produce a trivial judgment. Where the court found a willful or knowing violation, the statute directed an award of up to three times, but not less than two times, actual damages, converting culpability into a mandatory multiplier rather than a discretionary enhancement. Section 9 also required an award of attorney fees and costs to a prevailing consumer. Layered over the damages scheme was a procedural requirement with its own remedial consequences: at least thirty days before filing suit, a consumer claimant had to send a written demand letter identifying the claimant, describing the practice and the injury, and stating the relief demanded. The letter was a jurisdictional prerequisite in the ordinary consumer case, subject to exceptions for counterclaims and for respondents without a place of business or assets in the Commonwealth. A reasonable settlement tender within the thirty day window, if rejected, limited later recovery, while a refusal to grant relief in bad faith itself supported multiplication of damages. The letter thus disciplined both sides, forcing a stated claim and an investigated answer on a record a court could later examine for good faith.

Texas supplied a second illustration, explicit in its treatment of mental anguish. The Texas Deceptive Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Act, Subchapter E of Chapter 17 of the Texas Business and Commerce Code, enumerated false, misleading, and deceptive acts, added liability for breach of warranty and for unconscionable conduct, and gave a consumer a private action for economic damages, defined as compensatory damages for pecuniary loss. A finding of knowing conduct, defined in terms of actual awareness of the falsity, deception, or unfairness of the conduct, opened two further recoveries: damages for mental anguish, and additional damages that could raise the award to as much as three times the amount of economic damages. A finding of intentional conduct, defined as actual awareness coupled with a specific intent that the consumer rely on the act to the consumer's detriment, allowed the additional award to reach three times the combined amount of economic and mental anguish damages. A prevailing consumer also recovered court costs and reasonable and necessary attorney fees, and the Texas scheme required pre-suit written notice sixty days before filing, subject to exceptions for limitations emergencies and counterclaims. Mental anguish damages marked a real divergence from statutes confined to pecuniary loss, because deception in housing and insurance transactions characteristically produced distress that a purely economic measure left uncounted, and the Texas legislature had chosen, for knowing and intentional conduct, to count that distress and to multiply the count.

California demonstrated that a state system could divide the remedial labor between two statutes, one equitable and one legal, and the division cautions against assuming that a private right under state law automatically meant damages. The California Unfair Competition Law, Section 17200 of the California Business and Professions Code, defined unfair competition broadly to include unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business acts or practices, and a private plaintiff who had lost money or property could sue under the statute after Proposition 64, adopted in 2004, narrowed standing. The remedies under the Unfair Competition Law, however, were equitable only: a court could enjoin the practice and could order restitution of money or property obtained by means of the unfair competition, but the statute authorized no damages and no punitive damages in a private action. Restitution measured what the defendant had taken, not the full loss suffered. Damages came instead from a companion statute, the Consumers Legal Remedies Act, Civil Code Section 1750 and following sections, which declared a list of deceptive practices in sale or lease transactions unlawful and, in Section 1780, authorized a consumer who suffered damage to recover actual damages, an injunction, restitution, punitive damages, and any other relief the court deemed proper, with fees and costs to a prevailing plaintiff and a floor of one thousand dollars on the total damages award in a class action. California practice consequently paired the statutes, and the pairing represented the state position on the remedies axis.

Washington supplied a fourth configuration, in which multiplication existed but a ceiling confined the multiplication. The Washington Consumer Protection Act, Chapter 19.86 of the Revised Code of Washington, declared unfair or deceptive acts or practices in trade or commerce unlawful and authorized a private action by a person injured in business or property. A prevailing private plaintiff recovered actual damages, together with costs and reasonable attorney fees, and the court held discretion to increase the award to an amount not exceeding three times the actual damages sustained, subject to a statutory limit under which the treble damages award could not exceed twenty five thousand dollars. The Washington plaintiff also had to establish a public interest impact, an element that filtered out purely private disputes. The cap altered the arithmetic in higher loss cases: trebling operated in full at modest damage levels and compressed as actual damages rose, so that the Washington scheme concentrated its enhancement where losses were individually small. Set beside Massachusetts, where the multiplier ran without a comparable ceiling, and Texas, where intentional conduct multiplied mental anguish as well as economic loss, the Washington statute showed how one design choice could redirect the deterrent weight of a private enforcement regime.

An honest comparison cannot stop at the general statutes, because the federal side of the line changes character in the federal sectoral statutes. Several carried private rights with remedial schemes of the same family as the state schemes, complicating any tidy claim that private enforcement was a state invention and public enforcement a federal one. The Fair Credit Reporting Act of 1970 authorized a consumer to sue a person who failed to comply with the statute. For negligent noncompliance, under 15 U.S.C. 1681o, the consumer recovered actual damages together with costs and reasonable attorney fees. For willful noncompliance, under 15 U.S.C. 1681n, the consumer recovered actual damages or statutory damages of not less than one hundred dollars and not more than one thousand dollars, together with such punitive damages as the court allowed and an award of costs and fees. The statutory damages alternative mattered because credit reporting injuries were often real but difficult to quantify. The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act of 1977 displayed the same architecture in a different market. A debt collector who violated that statute was liable, under 15 U.S.C. 1692k, for actual damages, for additional damages up to one thousand dollars in an individual action, and for costs together with a reasonable attorney fee, with class action additional damages capped at the lesser of five hundred thousand dollars or one percent of the debt collector's net worth. The federal position as of 2018 was therefore twofold: no private action under the general statute, and private actions with statutory damages, punitive damages in defined settings, and fee shifting under a patchwork of sectoral statutes.

The remedies axis, the third axis of the comparison, cannot be understood by cataloging damage categories alone, because the categories operated through a mechanism that determined which claims entered the legal system at all. The characteristic consumer injury was small in amount. A deceptive fee, an undisclosed charge, or a misrepresented repair typically produced a loss measured in tens, hundreds, or low thousands of dollars, and the cost of litigating a claim through counsel, filing fees, and motion practice exceeded that loss by a wide margin under ordinary American practice, in which each side bore its own attorney fees. A rational consumer holding a claim for three hundred dollars and facing several thousand dollars in legal costs abandoned the claim, and a rational attorney declined a contingent engagement that could not support an hour of work. The abandonment was arithmetic, and any remedial scheme that left the arithmetic untouched conferred a right that functioned, for the mass of small injuries, as decoration.

Statutory minimums and multiplied damages changed the arithmetic at the recovery end. A minimum award, such as the twenty five dollar Massachusetts floor or the statutory range for willful credit reporting violations, guaranteed that a proven violation produced a judgment with some content even where provable loss approached zero. Multiplication changed the arithmetic more powerfully. Trebling a two thousand dollar loss produced a six thousand dollar exposure, and trebling combined with mental anguish in Texas or with punitive damages under the credit reporting statute and the California Consumers Legal Remedies Act pushed exposure into a range at which settlement became a business decision rather than a courtesy. Exposure of that kind also supported aggregation: a practice that took a small sum from each of thousands of consumers generated collective exposure proportioned to the scale of the practice, and that prospect was the principal private deterrent against business models built on small, widely distributed overcharges.

Fee shifting changed the arithmetic at the cost end, and fee shifting was the component on which the whole structure rested. An award of reasonable attorney fees and costs to a prevailing consumer severed the connection between the size of the loss and the viability of the suit. Counsel could evaluate a case on the merits of the violation rather than on the depth of the client's loss, and could accept a small claim with a clear violation in the expectation that success would carry compensation for the work required. The one way character of most consumer fee shifting, under which a prevailing defendant recovered fees only in narrow circumstances such as bad faith or frivolous filing, expressed a legislative judgment that the risk allocation should favor the bringing of meritorious small claims. Defense interests contested that judgment throughout the period, arguing that fee shifting and multiplication invited strike suits, while consumer advocates answered that unremedied small deception dwarfed abusive filings and that demand letter requirements, settlement offer mechanisms, and judicial control of fee awards supplied the correctives. The statutes in force in 2018 embodied compromises between the positions: pre-suit notice in Massachusetts and Texas, a damages cap in Washington, equitable limits in the California Unfair Competition Law, and culpability thresholds for multiplication nearly everywhere.

The mechanism also explains the distribution of enforcement labor between the two systems. The Federal Trade Commission, operating without a private counterpart under Section 5, chose cases for market significance: a practice affecting millions of consumers, or a scheme causing large aggregate loss. Between agency cases, the run of ordinary deception fell to whatever private law made litigation possible, and state legislatures, by attaching private actions, minimums, multipliers, and fee shifting to the little FTC Acts, had made litigation possible across a far wider band of cases than agency enforcement could reach. Where a state weakened one component, the band narrowed, and where a federal sectoral statute supplied the full set of components, as the debt collection and credit reporting statutes substantially did, federal courtrooms filled with private consumer cases despite the absence of a private action under the general federal statute. The private right of action and the remedial scheme were therefore not two separate features. The right decided who held the key, and the remedies decided whether the key turned, and the comparison as of the first of February 2019 turned, on these two axes, on the distance between a federal general statute that reserved enforcement to an agency with inferred monetary powers and state statutes that handed enforcement to the injured consumer and then paid, through fee shifting and enhanced damages, for the handing over.

## Preemption as a Floor, a Ceiling, and a Settlement

Preemption determines how much room a state legislature retains after Congress has entered a field of consumer protection. The statutes governing consumer credit, debt collection, credit reporting, and product safety give a graded answer rather than a binary one. Congress set a national baseline in most of those fields and preserved state authority to build upon the baseline. In credit reporting, Congress built a ceiling over several subjects and barred states from adding requirements of their own. In banking, Congress revisited the balance in 2010 and wrote a negotiated settlement into law.

A floor and a ceiling produce opposite incentives. A floor establishes minimum protections that apply in every state, while preserving state authority to demand more. A ceiling treats the federal rule as both the minimum and the maximum. Compliance becomes simpler across state lines, but state legislatures lose the capacity to test stronger protections that Congress has not adopted.

### Does federal consumer protection law preempt state law?

Federal law sometimes preempts state consumer protection law, but the pattern varies by statute. Most federal statutes preserve state law that is not inconsistent and that protects consumers more. Credit reporting imposes a broader ceiling, and national bank law applies a separate, fact specific preemption standard.

The instrument that creates a floor is the savings clause, sometimes captioned as a provision on the relation to state laws. The recurring language provides that a federal statute does not annul, alter, or affect state law, and does not exempt any person from complying with state law, except to the extent that state law is inconsistent with the federal statute, and then only to the extent of the inconsistency. That formulation rejects field preemption, the theory that federal entry into a subject displaces state law across the subject, and replaces displacement with a comparison between particular provisions. Any displacement is confined to the point of actual conflict, so a state statute can lose one provision and retain the rest. Several savings clauses add a greater protection proviso, directing the administering agency not to treat a state law as inconsistent if that law gives greater protection to the consumer or to the credit applicant. The proviso separates difference from conflict: a stricter state rule will often differ from a federal rule, yet added protection is treated as compatible with the federal purpose.

The Truth in Lending Act, enacted in 1968, supplies the template. Its effect on other laws provision, codified at 15 U.S.C. 1610, provides in section 1610(a)(1) that the Act does not annul, alter, or affect state laws relating to the disclosure of information in connection with credit transactions, except to the extent that those laws are inconsistent with the Act, and then only to the extent of the inconsistency. The subsection assigns the administering agency the task of determining whether an inconsistency exists, and limits that task by providing that the agency may not determine a state law to be inconsistent if the state law gives greater protection to the consumer. A state could therefore require disclosure of an item that federal law did not cover, or require a more detailed itemization than federal law required, without supplying a ground for preemption, provided the added disclosure did not contradict a federal disclosure in form or substance. Consistency of the federal disclosure vocabulary, centered on the finance charge and the annual percentage rate, differed from exclusivity, and a creditor operating in several states faced additional state disclosures layered upon the federal statement in some states.

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, enacted in 1977, repeats the pattern in collection. Its relation to state laws provision, codified at 15 U.S.C. 1692n, provides that the Act does not annul, alter, or affect state laws with respect to debt collection practices, and does not exempt any person from complying with state laws, except to the extent of an inconsistency, and then only to the extent of the inconsistency. Section 1692n then states the greater protection rule directly. For purposes of that section, a state law is not inconsistent with the federal Act if the protection the state law affords any consumer is greater than the protection provided by the federal Act. The companion provision at 15 U.S.C. 1692o adds an exemption mechanism. Under section 1692o, the agency shall by regulation exempt a class of debt collection practices within a state from the federal requirements if state law subjects that class to requirements substantially similar to the federal requirements and adequate provision for enforcement exists. The federal standard remains the benchmark against which substantial similarity is measured.

State activity in collection illustrates the breadth that the floor allowed. The federal Act defined its central term, debt collector, around persons collecting debts owed to another, and state law retained room to license collection agencies, to regulate conduct by a wider set of actors, to add state remedies, and to set stricter rules on validation, harassment, and communication with third parties. Because greater protection was deemed consistent by statute, a state rule did not need to mirror the federal categories to survive, and a state could close gaps that the federal definition left open. State innovation in collection remained broad because the federal text invited supplementation.

The Equal Credit Opportunity Act, enacted in 1974 and expanded in 1976, applies the same savings approach to credit discrimination. The relevant language appears at 15 U.S.C. 1691d. Subsection 1691d(f) provides that the Act does not annul, alter, or affect state laws with respect to credit discrimination, and does not exempt any person from complying with those laws, except to the extent of an inconsistency, and then only to the extent of the inconsistency. The agency may not determine a state law to be inconsistent if the agency determines that the state law gives greater protection to the applicant. Subsection 1691d(g) supplies a parallel exemption mechanism for a class of credit transactions where state law imposes substantially similar requirements, or gives greater protection to the applicant, and provides adequately for enforcement. A floor in that field allowed states to prohibit discrimination on grounds beyond the federal list and to tighten application procedures beyond the federal core duties.

Product safety presents a more complicated variant. The state standards provision, codified at 15 U.S.C. 2075, operates in two zones. Where no federal consumer product safety standard covers a product and a risk of injury, section 2075 erected no express barrier, and states retained authority to set safety requirements. Where a federal standard was in effect for a risk of injury associated with a consumer product, section 2075(a) barred a state or political subdivision from establishing or continuing a standard prescribing requirements on matters such as performance, composition, design, construction, packaging, or labeling designed to deal with the same risk, unless those state requirements were identical to the federal standard. In that second zone, the Act behaved as a ceiling for nonidentical state standards aimed at the same risk.

Section 2075 nevertheless contained two safety valves. Subsection 2075(b) preserved authority to set a more protective safety requirement for a product purchased for government use, even if not identical to the federal standard. Subsection 2075(c) allowed a state to apply to the Consumer Product Safety Commission for an exemption from subsection 2075(a), which the Commission could grant by rule if the state standard provided a significantly higher degree of protection and did not unduly burden interstate commerce, considering feasibility, compliance cost, product distribution, likely applications from other states, and the need for a national uniform standard. States may and do impose additional requirements in product safety through that channel and in the zone without a federal standard, although the channel is narrower than the open invitation in lending and collection.

The Fair Credit Reporting Act breaks the pattern, and the break is the most consequential drafting choice on the preemption axis. The Act, enacted in 1970, contains a general provision at 15 U.S.C. 1681t(a) resembling the savings clauses in neighboring statutes: except as provided in subsections 1681t(b) and 1681t(c), the Act does not annul or affect state laws on the collection, distribution, or use of information on consumers, except to the extent of an inconsistency. Subsection 1681t(b), however, withdraws whole subjects from that general rule. The analysis in [the guide to the Fair Credit Reporting Act](/2018/11/01/fair-credit-reporting-act-1970-guide/) places that withdrawal at the center of the federalism of the statute, and the text supports the placement.

Subsection 1681t(b)(1) opens with a prohibition of a different kind from an inconsistency test. No requirement or prohibition may be imposed under the laws of any state with respect to any subject matter regulated under the sections that the subsection enumerates. The enumerated subjects include prescreening of consumer reports, regulated under subsections 1681b(c) and 1681b(e), under which a consumer reporting agency furnishes a list of consumers meeting stated criteria for use in firm offers of credit or insurance. The enumeration also includes the time by which a consumer reporting agency must take action in a procedure related to disputed accuracy, regulated under section 1681i; the duties of a person who takes adverse action, regulated under subsections 1681m(a) and 1681m(b); and the duties of persons who use a consumer report in a credit or insurance transaction not initiated by the consumer, regulated under subsection 1681m(d). Each subject was removed from state supplementation, even where a proposed state rule would have protected consumers more.

The enumeration continues into the content of reports and the obligations of firms that supply data. Subsection 1681t(b)(1)(E) covers subject matter regulated under section 1681c, relating to information contained in consumer reports. Section 1681c contains the federal rules on obsolete information, including the time periods after which specified adverse items, such as bankruptcies, civil judgments, arrests, and accounts placed for collection, may no longer be reported, subject to statutory exceptions. A state that wished to shorten a federal reporting period would be imposing a requirement with respect to subject matter regulated under section 1681c, and the greater protection logic of the neighboring statutes does not appear in subsection 1681t(b) as a saving qualification. Subsection 1681t(b)(1)(F) covers the responsibilities of persons who furnish information to consumer reporting agencies, regulated under section 1681s-2. Furnishers include banks, card issuers, retailers, and collection agencies that report account performance, and their duties on accuracy, correction, and dispute investigation were placed beyond state addition or subtraction by the same mechanism.

Affiliate information sharing received parallel treatment through two routes. Subsection 1681t(b)(1)(H) covers subject matter regulated under section 1681s-3, relating to the exchange and use of information to make a solicitation for marketing purposes. Subsection 1681t(b)(2) separately bars state requirements with respect to the exchange of information among persons affiliated by common ownership or common corporate control, subject to a narrow exception for a Vermont provision in effect on September 30, 1996. The federal design rested on notice and an opportunity to opt out. A state that preferred an opt in model, under which sharing required affirmative consent, would be regulating the exchange itself, and subsection 1681t(b)(2) barred that choice. In collection law, a state could demand more than Congress demanded. In affiliate sharing, a state could not demand consent where Congress had settled for an opt out.

The exceptions inside subsection 1681t(b) confirm the breadth of the rule by their narrowness. Several subparagraphs preserve specified state laws in effect on September 30, 1996, including laws on dispute timing and on information contained in reports, and the furnisher subparagraph preserves two identified provisions of Massachusetts and California law as in effect on that date. Later paragraphs, added in 2003, preserve identified state disclosure provisions as in effect on December 4, 2003, and preserve state regulation of credit based insurance scores. Grandfathering protects reliance on law enacted at a fixed date. Grandfathering does not restore a general power to legislate in the enumerated subjects after the fixed date, and the frozen date is the point.

The 2003 amendments converted a temporary ceiling into a permanent one. The preemption provisions added in 1996 had been scheduled to lose effect for state laws enacted after January 1, 2004, under a sunset mechanism that would have reopened the enumerated subjects to state legislation giving greater protection. The Fair and Accurate Credit Transactions Act of 2003, enacted on December 4, 2003, struck that sunset and made the preemptions permanent, and added further preemptive paragraphs covering disclosures to consumers, the frequency of free disclosures, and conduct required by specified identity theft provisions. The Congressional Research Service summarized the change as making permanent the expiring preemptions. After December 2003, a state legislature considering a new rule on prescreening, furnisher duties, reporting periods, or affiliate sharing faced a statutory bar with no expiration date.

The reason for the asymmetry lies in the structure of the regulated activity, as Congress framed the activity in 1996 and again in 2003. A debt collection rule operates mainly within the state where the collector acts and the consumer resides, and a lending disclosure operates within a transaction that state law has long governed. A consumer report moves through a national system in which furnishers report data in standardized formats, agencies assemble files from many states, and users rely on reports across state lines. Support in Congress for the ceiling rested on the claim that divergent state rules on report content, furnisher duties, and prescreening would fragment that system. Opposition in Congress rested on the claim that a ceiling would block states from answering local abuses and from shortening the period during which old adverse information followed a consumer. The enacted text resolved the dispute for the enumerated subjects in favor of uniformity. State credit reporting innovation therefore operated at the margins section 1681t left open: subjects outside the enumerated list, grandfathered laws, general consumer protection statutes, enforcement authority, and the protections expressly preserved for state insurance score regulation and for identity theft prevention and mitigation in subsection 1681t(a).

The financial reform statute of 2010, the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, enacted on July 21, 2010, addressed preemption as a deliberate federalism settlement carried by two provisions. Section 5551, in Title X, codified at 12 U.S.C. 5551, provides in subsection (a)(1) that Title X, other than the sections governing national bank preemption, may not be construed as annulling or affecting state statutes, regulations, orders, or interpretations, or as exempting any person from complying with state law, except to the extent of an inconsistency, and then only to that extent. State consumer financial laws are therefore not preempted merely because federal law exists on the same subject. Section 5551(a)(2) states the greater protection rule: a state provision is not inconsistent with Title X if the protection afforded to consumers is greater than the protection provided under Title X. Section 5551(c) added an upward channel, under which a resolution enacted by a majority of the states in support of a consumer protection regulation required the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau to issue a notice of proposed rulemaking.

Section 5551(b) provides that Title X may not be construed as modifying, limiting, or superseding the operation of any provision of an enumerated consumer law that relates to the application of state law. The 2010 Act therefore did not convert the Fair Credit Reporting Act into a floor statute, because section 1681t is such a provision and section 5551(b) directed that such provisions continue to operate. The settlement restored a floor as the default for the new framework, while leaving the statute specific choices in the older laws, including the credit reporting ceiling, in place.

Section 25b addresses the distinct problem that national banks had claimed broad immunity from state consumer law. In the decade before 2010, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency had adopted regulations asserting preemption across categories of state law, and the Supreme Court in Watters v. Wachovia Bank in 2007 had extended national bank preemption to a state licensing regime as applied to a national bank operating subsidiary. The 2010 Act answered with a standard drawn from an earlier decision. Under 12 U.S.C. 25b(b)(1), a state consumer financial law is preempted as applied to a national bank only if application of the law would have a discriminatory effect on national banks in comparison with a bank chartered by that state, or the law prevents or significantly interferes with the exercise by the national bank of its powers in accordance with the legal standard in Barnett Bank of Marion County, N.A. v. Nelson, 517 U.S. 25 (1996), or the law is preempted by another provision of federal law. Section 25b defines a state consumer financial law as a nondiscriminatory state law that directly and specifically regulates the manner, content, or terms and conditions of a financial transaction, or an account related to a transaction, with respect to a consumer.

Barnett Bank itself involved a federal statute authorizing national banks in places with populations not exceeding five thousand to sell insurance, and a Florida statute that barred that activity. The Supreme Court in 1996 held the Florida statute preempted because the state law stood as an obstacle to the federal grant of authority, while earlier decisions collected in the opinion had applied state laws of general application to national banks without preemption. Codification made significantly interferes the measure, and ordinary state rules do not meet the measure merely by imposing cost. Procedural limits reinforced the standard. A preemption determination under the Barnett Bank prong may be made by a court, or by the Comptroller of the Currency on a case by case basis. A Comptroller determination must rest on substantial evidence, the statute directs review of determinations every five years, and a reviewing court assesses the thoroughness of the consideration and the validity of the reasoning. Section 25b also provided that a state consumer financial law applies to a subsidiary or affiliate of a national bank to the same extent as to other persons, removing the categorical shelter associated with Watters v. Wachovia Bank.

That layered outcome explains why preemption does not by itself decide the practical balance between federal and state consumer protection. A legislator or litigant reading the field in February 2019 had to ask which statute governed the activity, whether the regulated firm was a national bank or a subsidiary, and whether a state law that survived preemption would be invoked in a forum that allowed the claim to proceed, a question governed by arbitration provisions treated elsewhere in the comparison. A state rule can be valid, applicable, and more protective, and can matter little if the claim under that rule is channeled into a forum that a contract selected. Preemption sets the boundary of state authority. Procedure decides how much of the authority inside the boundary is exercised.

## The Enforcers: Two Governments With Overlapping Authority

A comparison of federal and state consumer protection law changes character at the point of enforcement. Substantive prohibitions can look similar on paper, while the practical law for a business depends on which official notices the conduct, has jurisdiction over the firm involved, and commands the staff and political incentive to act. As the law stood on February 1, 2019, neither level held a monopoly. Federal enforcement supplied national reach, uniformity of interpretation, and agencies built for particular markets. State enforcement supplied proximity to complainants and a practiced habit of acting in combination. Successive statutes preserved state authority while adding federal authority, and the financial reform statute of 2010 went further by handing state officials a direct role in enforcing federal law itself. The identity of the enforcer often mattered as much as the wording of the prohibition being enforced.

The oldest general federal enforcer was the Federal Trade Commission, created by the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914. Section 5 of that statute, codified at 15 U.S.C. 45, declared unfair methods of competition unlawful, and the Wheeler-Lea Act of 1938 amended Section 5 to declare unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce unlawful as well. Acting under Section 5, the Commission investigated conduct across most of the economy, issued complaints, conducted administrative proceedings, entered cease and desist orders, sought federal court injunctions, and promulgated trade regulation rules defining particular practices. The Commission also enforced narrower statutes covering credit reporting, debt collection by outside collectors, telemarketing, and product labeling, subject to statutory exceptions that removed some of the largest consumer sectors from its hands.

Those exceptions shaped the federal map as much as the grant of authority did. Section 5 withheld Commission power over banks, savings and loan institutions, and federal credit unions, and it also excepted common carriers subject to the Acts to regulate commerce, air carriers, and persons subject to the Packers and Stockyards Act of 1921, except as that statute provided otherwise. Insurance occupied a distinct position under the McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945, which made the Federal Trade Commission Act applicable to the business of insurance only to the extent that such business was not regulated by state law. The Supreme Court gave effect to that limitation in Federal Trade Commission v. National Casualty Co., 357 U.S. 560 (1958). The consequence was a federal generalist with holes in its jurisdiction corresponding to sectors assigned to other regulators. A deceptive practice by a retailer fell to the Commission, a deceptive practice by a bank fell elsewhere, and a deceptive practice by an insurer fell in the first instance to state insurance departments. The identity of the seller, rather than the character of the deception, determined the enforcer.

The sectors removed from Commission jurisdiction were assigned to sectoral regulators whose primary missions often lay elsewhere. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency supervised national banks, the Federal Reserve supervised state member banks and holding companies, the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation supervised other insured state banks, and the National Credit Union Administration supervised federal credit unions. Those prudential regulators examined institutions for safety and soundness and also enforced consumer compliance, including the prohibition on unfair or deceptive practices as applied to banks through Section 8 of the Federal Deposit Insurance Act. Other federal agencies enforced consumer rules inside defined markets: the Securities and Exchange Commission policed deception in securities markets, the Federal Communications Commission regulated communications carriers and billing practices, and the Department of Transportation regulated air carriers. The arrangement produced expertise inside each sector and inconsistency across sectors, because an agency charged principally with institutional soundness weighed consumer enforcement against different concerns than an agency charged principally with consumer welfare.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau altered that balance for financial products. Title X of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, signed on July 21, 2010, created the Bureau, and the Bureau formally began operating on July 21, 2011. Congress transferred to the Bureau rulemaking and enforcement authority under the enumerated consumer financial laws, including the Truth in Lending Act, the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, and the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, and Congress added a new prohibition. Under 12 U.S.C. 5531, the Bureau could identify and prohibit unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices in connection with consumer financial products or services, and under 12 U.S.C. 5536, covered persons were forbidden to engage in such conduct. The Bureau supervised insured depository institutions with more than ten billion dollars in assets and defined categories of nonbank providers, including mortgage lenders and servicers, payday lenders, and private education lenders. A single agency thus held consumer financial protection as its principal mission, with a single director, funding drawn from the Federal Reserve System rather than annual appropriations, and authority crossing the charter lines dividing banks from nonbanks.


State enforcement began from a different base. During the 1960s and 1970s, most states adopted statutes modeled in part on Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act and known collectively as unfair and deceptive acts and practices statutes, or UDAP statutes. Each state statute had its own scope, exemptions, and remedies, and many were construed with reference to federal interpretations of the Federal Trade Commission Act, but the enforcing officer was ordinarily the state attorney general. Attorneys general used civil investigative demands and subpoenas to obtain documents and testimony before filing suit, sought injunctions and restitution in state court, and recovered civil penalties where state law authorized penalties. State banking departments, insurance commissioners, and licensing agencies enforced sector rules alongside the attorney general, so a lender, insurer, or seller could face a state sectoral regulator for licensing or solvency questions and the attorney general for deceptive conduct arising from the same transaction.

The attorney general brought institutional features that no federal agency replicated. In forty-three states, the attorney general was chosen by popular election; in the remaining states, appointment by the governor, election by the legislature, or appointment by the state supreme court supplied the officer. An elected attorney general answered to a state electorate rather than to a governor or to the President, controlled an independent litigation docket, and could act against conduct that federal agencies had declined to pursue. Attorneys general also sued as parens patriae, a form of action in which the state asserted a quasi-sovereign interest in the welfare of residents and sought relief on behalf of natural persons residing in the state. Congress endorsed that device in federal antitrust law in Section 4C of the Clayton Act, enacted in 1976, and state statutes supplied comparable authority under state law. Parens patriae standing allowed a single public plaintiff to aggregate harms that were individually small and widely dispersed, the pattern typical of a deceptive fee, an unauthorized charge, or a servicing error repeated across thousands of accounts.

The multistate action converted those individual offices into a national institution. Attorneys general coordinated through the National Association of Attorneys General and through standing working groups on subjects such as consumer protection and financial services, shared investigative material under common interest arrangements, designated lead states to negotiate for the group, and settled on terms that participating states adopted in parallel consent judgments. Pooling mattered for capacity: a small state office gained the benefit of investigators and litigators assembled by larger states, while a national firm faced a single negotiation rather than fifty separate proceedings. The template reached its largest scale in the Tobacco Master Settlement Agreement, entered on November 23, 1998, between the attorneys general of forty-six states and the four largest cigarette manufacturers, with payments of at least 206 billion dollars over the first twenty-five years and restrictions on marketing. Tobacco concerned Medicaid reimbursement rather than consumer deception alone, but the settlement demonstrated that coordinated state litigation could produce nationwide conduct rules without a federal statute directing the result.

Mortgage servicing after the financial crisis of 2007 to 2009 supplied the clearest consumer protection example. On February 9, 2012, the federal government and the attorneys general of forty-nine states announced the National Mortgage Settlement with the five largest mortgage servicers, Bank of America, JPMorgan Chase, Wells Fargo, Citigroup, and Ally Financial. The settlement was valued at approximately twenty-five billion dollars, composed of about twenty billion dollars in financial relief for homeowners and five billion dollars in payments to state and federal governments, and Oklahoma reached a separate agreement. Consent judgments filed in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia imposed national servicing standards covering single points of contact, communication with borrowers, and standards for executing foreclosure documents, with compliance tested by an independent monitor. A later joint settlement followed the same pattern: on December 19, 2013, Ocwen Financial Corporation agreed with forty-nine states, the District of Columbia, and the Bureau to a settlement valued at 2.1 billion dollars, including two billion dollars in first lien principal reduction and 125 million dollars in cash payments to borrowers, to resolve allegations of servicing misconduct and unlawful foreclosures. On May 12, 2015, the attorneys general of all fifty states and the District of Columbia, together with the Bureau and the Federal Communications Commission, announced settlements with Sprint and Verizon Wireless totaling 158 million dollars, with Sprint paying 68 million dollars and Verizon paying 90 million dollars, to resolve allegations of mobile cramming, meaning the placement of unauthorized third party charges on consumer telephone bills.


### Can state attorneys general enforce federal consumer protection law?

Yes, within limits set by Congress. A state attorney general may bring a civil action to enforce Title X of the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 and regulations issued under it, after giving notice to the Bureau. Against national banks, however, the action may enforce Bureau regulations only.

The provision behind that answer, Section 1042 of the Dodd-Frank Act, codified at 12 U.S.C. 5552, created a second federal enforcement channel outside the federal agencies. Subsection (a)(1) authorized a state attorney general to bring a civil action in the name of the state, in federal or state court, to enforce Title X or regulations issued under Title X and to secure remedies provided by that title or otherwise provided by law. A parallel grant allowed a state regulator to proceed against an entity chartered, incorporated, licensed, or otherwise authorized to do business under state law. The design meant that a failure of federal enforcement did not end the federal claim: the same prohibition could be invoked by a state plaintiff with a different election cycle, budget, and assessment of the evidence. The background to that grant is traced in [the account of the Bureau's creation and powers](/2013/12/01/cfpb-creation-and-powers/), and Section 5552 reads most clearly against that background, as a decision to place enforcement capacity in more than one set of public hands.

Congress hedged the grant at the point where state enforcement met the federal banking charter. Under 12 U.S.C. 5552(a)(2), a state attorney general could not bring an action against a national bank or a federal savings association to enforce a provision of Title X itself, except as the statute permitted. The permission in Section 5552(a)(2)(B) ran to enforcement of a regulation prescribed by the Bureau under Title X, together with remedies under Title X or other law. For national banks and federal savings associations, therefore, the operative federal standard had to arrive in the form of a Bureau rule, and the free standing prohibition on unfair, deceptive, or abusive conduct in Title X was not directly available to a state plaintiff. Procedure supplied a second hedge. Under Section 5552(b), before initiating an action against a covered person, a state attorney general or state regulator had to provide timely notice describing the action, together with a copy of the complete complaint to be filed, to the Bureau and to the prudential regulator, if any; where prior notice was not practicable, notice followed immediately upon filing. Notice was not consent, and the statute gave the Bureau defined rights after notice: intervention as a party, removal of an intervened action to federal district court, a hearing on all matters arising in the action, and appeal to the same extent as any other party. Section 5552(a)(3) added that Title X did not supersede state authority under an enumerated consumer law to enforce that federal law.

Preservation of state law stood beside the enforcement grant. Section 1041 of the Dodd-Frank Act, codified at 12 U.S.C. 5551, provided that Title X, apart from specified sections, could not be construed to annul, alter, affect, or exempt any person from complying with state statutes, regulations, orders, or interpretations, except to the extent of an inconsistency with Title X, and then only to the extent of that inconsistency. Section 5551 also stated that a state provision was not inconsistent with Title X if the protection afforded to consumers was greater than the protection provided under Title X. A separate section treats preemption doctrine at length, and for enforcement purposes the point is narrower: Section 5551 kept state UDAP claims available in parallel, while Section 5552 added federal claims to the state docket. A state plaintiff after July 21, 2011, the designated transfer date under Title X, could therefore plead state law, federal law, or both.

The practical consequences began with redundancy as a design feature. Federal and state enforcers could investigate the same conduct, and the joint settlements of 2012, 2013, and 2015 showed redundancy operating as cooperation rather than duplication. Redundancy also operated as insurance against inaction. When Richard Cordray resigned as Director of the Bureau on November 24, 2017, and Mick Mulvaney became acting director on appointment by the President, the change of leadership at a single director agency changed federal enforcement priorities by design of the office. State authority under Section 5552 and under state UDAP statutes did not depend on that appointment, and state offices retained the capacity to open investigations that the Bureau declined. The cost side was equally structural: a national firm faced overlapping document demands, inconsistent settlement postures among jurisdictions, and exposure to successive proceedings arising from one course of conduct.

Forum choice gave the redundancy tactical content. A state attorney general enforcing state law could file in state court, subject to removal on federal grounds available to a defendant, while a state attorney general enforcing Title X under Section 5552 could file in federal district court or in state court, subject to the Bureau's intervention and removal rights after notice. Settlement leverage differed by forum and by plaintiff, because a multistate coalition could offer a defendant peace across most jurisdictions in a single instrument, while a single state plaintiff could offer only a partial release and left the defendant exposed elsewhere. Defendants accordingly negotiated for breadth of participation as a settlement term, and attorneys general negotiated for leadership roles and allocations of relief among states. The choice of plaintiff and forum, made before any court reached the merits, often determined the remedies realistically on the table.

The politics of the model followed from the selection method and the settlement device, and evaluation of that politics divided along institutional lines. The U.S. Chamber of Commerce, through the Institute for Legal Reform, proposed a code of conduct for attorneys general in a document presented on October 24, 2007, and Lisa A. Rickard, president of the Institute for Legal Reform, stated that some state attorneys general were increasingly using civil litigation to reap power, publicity, and political advantage. The Chamber's proposals included limiting multistate activity to conduct significantly affecting the attorney general's own state, giving reasonable notice before suit, restraining prejudicial public communications, and barring contingency fee arrangements with outside counsel in cases involving the police power of a state. The National Consumer Law Center took the opposing institutional position in analysis of the Dodd-Frank Act and the role of the states, emphasizing that the limitations on state enforcement of Title X were few and defined, that attorneys general could enforce Bureau rules against national banks and federal thrifts, and that notice to the Bureau, rather than federal permission, was the governing condition. State officials defending multistate settlements pointed to the relief figures in the settlements of 2012, 2013, and 2015 and to conduct standards that a single state proceeding could not have imposed nationally. The dispute concerned accountability: an elected state officer claimed a democratic mandate for national settlements, and a national business organization claimed that such settlements set national policy without a legislature or a federal rulemaking record.

For the axis of enforcers, neither system displaced the other by February 1, 2019. Federal enforcement offered uniform interpretation, nationwide resources, and, after 2011, a specialist Bureau for financial products, offset by sectoral carve-outs and the dependence of a single director agency on one officeholder. State enforcement offered fifty potential plaintiffs, electoral independence, parens patriae aggregation of small harms, and a multistate mechanism that repeatedly produced national settlements, offset by uneven office capacity, divergent state statutes, and persistent objection from the U.S. Chamber of Commerce to policy made by settlement. Section 5552 fused the two systems by making state officials enforcers of federal law under federal notice and intervention rules, and Section 5551 preserved the state law claims that made state enforcement independent in the first place. The enforcer question in a consumer case was thus a genuine threshold question, because the answer selected the claims, the forum, the available remedies, and often the settlement that ended the case.

## Axis Five: Arbitration as the Overriding Factor

The first four axes of comparison between federal and state consumer protection law concern the substance of legal rules: which sovereign wrote the prohibition, which sovereign set the remedy, and which sovereign defined the conduct that counts as unfair or deceptive. The fifth axis concerns a question that precedes all of those questions and, in a large share of consumer disputes, renders them academic. That question is whether the consumer signed a contract containing an arbitration clause paired with a waiver of class proceedings. A consumer who signed such a contract may hold a claim under a generous state statute, a federal statute, or both, and the relative generosity of the two bodies of law may never matter, because the claim will be heard, if heard at all, by a private arbitrator sitting alone with a single claimant. Arbitration decides more than preemption. Preemption doctrine allocates power between Congress and the state legislatures. Arbitration doctrine, as the Supreme Court constructed that doctrine between 2011 and 2018, allocates power between public courts and private contract terms, and the allocation usually happens before any court reaches the merits. The contract, rather than the sovereign, selects the forum, the procedure, and, in practical terms, the likelihood that a small claim will be pursued at all.

The statutory foundation is a short provision enacted in 1925. Section 2 of the Federal Arbitration Act, codified at 9 U.S.C. 2, provides that a written provision in a contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce, under which the parties agree to settle a controversy by arbitration, shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract. The saving clause at the end of that sentence preserves the ordinary contract defences that apply to contracts generally, such as fraud, duress, and unconscionability as a general doctrine of contract law. The main clause places arbitration agreements on an equal footing with other contracts and directs courts to enforce such agreements according to their terms. The transformation of Section 2 into the dominant force in consumer enforcement came through a sequence of Supreme Court decisions in the second decade of the twenty first century, each of which closed a route by which state law, or a federal statutory argument, might have limited enforcement of a class waiver.

The first and most consequential of those decisions was AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion, 563 U.S. 333 (2011). Vincent and Liza Concepcion had entered a cellular telephone contract that required arbitration of all disputes and barred classwide proceedings. After AT&T charged sales tax on the full retail value of telephones advertised as free, a charge of $30.22 in the Concepcions' case, the Concepcions sued, and the suit was consolidated with a class action alleging false advertising and fraud. The lower courts, applying the rule of Discover Bank v. Superior Court, a 2005 decision of the California Supreme Court, held the class waiver unconscionable and the arbitration provision unenforceable. The Discover Bank rule treated class waivers as unconscionable where the agreement was a contract of adhesion, where disputes were likely to involve small amounts, and where the party with inferior bargaining power alleged a deliberate scheme to defraud. The Supreme Court, in a majority opinion by Justice Scalia, held that the Federal Arbitration Act preempted the Discover Bank rule because the rule stood as an obstacle to the accomplishment of the purposes and objectives of Congress. The reasoning turned on the character of arbitration itself. Requiring the availability of classwide arbitration, the majority reasoned, interfered with the fundamental attributes of the arbitral forum: class arbitration sacrificed the informality of bilateral arbitration, made the process slower and more costly, and greatly increased the risks borne by defendants, all under a form of review that focuses on arbitrator misconduct rather than arbitrator mistake. A state remained free to apply generally applicable contract defences, but a state rule that conditioned enforcement on the availability of class procedures was, in operation, a rule that disfavored arbitration, and Section 2 displaced it.

Concepcion settled the fate of state unconscionability rules aimed at class waivers. American Express Co. v. Italian Colors Restaurant, 570 U.S. 228 (2013), addressed the parallel federal argument. The plaintiffs were merchants who had accepted American Express cards under agreements requiring individual arbitration and barring class arbitration. The merchants alleged a tying arrangement in violation of the Sherman Act, supported by evidence that the expert analysis required to prove an antitrust violation would cost at least several hundred thousand dollars and could cost as much as $1,000,000, while the maximum recovery for any individual claimant was $12,850, or $38,550 when trebled. The merchants invoked the effective vindication doctrine, an idea drawn from earlier Supreme Court language suggesting that an arbitration agreement might fail if the agreement operated as a prospective waiver of the right to pursue statutory remedies. The Supreme Court held the class waiver enforceable. The fact that proving a claim on an individual basis was not worth the expense, the majority held, did not eliminate the right to pursue that remedy, and the antitrust laws did not guarantee an affordable procedural path for every claim. No contrary congressional command displaced the Arbitration Act. Italian Colors therefore extended the logic of Concepcion beyond state law: a federal statutory claim, no less than a state consumer claim, could be confined to individual arbitration even where the economics of individual proof made the claim irrational to bring.

A third decision confirmed that state courts could not reach the same result by interpretive indirection. In DIRECTV, Inc. v. Imburgia, 577 U.S. 191 (2015), the service agreement required individual arbitration, barred class arbitration, and provided that the entire arbitration provision would be unenforceable if the law of the customer's state made class arbitration waivers unenforceable. California law had done exactly that under Discover Bank when the contracts were signed, and the California Court of Appeal reasoned that the parties had incorporated that state rule, as the rule stood apart from federal preemption, into the contract. The Supreme Court reversed in a six to three decision. The state court's interpretation, the majority held, singled out arbitration agreements for disfavored treatment and was therefore preempted. Imburgia foreclosed a drafting based escape route: after 2015, a state court could neither invalidate a class waiver directly nor construe a contract term to give the invalidated state rule continuing effect against arbitration clauses alone.

The employment parallel arrived in Epic Systems Corp. v. Lewis, decided on May 21, 2018, and reported at 138 S. Ct. 1612. Employees in three consolidated cases had agreed to resolve employment disputes through individual arbitration, and the employees argued that the National Labor Relations Act, which protects the right of employees to engage in concerted activities for mutual aid or protection, rendered class and collective action waivers unlawful. The Supreme Court held that Congress, in the Arbitration Act, had instructed courts to enforce arbitration agreements providing for individualized proceedings, and that neither the saving clause nor the National Labor Relations Act suggested a contrary instruction. In the workplace setting, as the decisions collected in the article on [workplace discrimination court cases](/2015/04/15/workplace-discrimination-court-cases/) illustrate across a longer period, the choice between an individual proceeding and an aggregate proceeding often determines whether a statutory protection produces any recovery, and Epic Systems placed that choice in the contract. A worker alleging unpaid wages under federal law and a consumer alleging a deceptive fee under state law occupied the same procedural position after 2018: each held a substantive right whose practical value depended on a clause drafted by the other party.

The mechanism by which arbitration clauses decide outcomes operates through procedure rather than through any ruling on the merits. A typical consumer contract for a credit card, a bank account, a mobile telephone plan, or a consumer loan contains a predispute arbitration agreement: a clause, accepted before any dispute exists, under which the consumer agrees to resolve future disputes in arbitration and waives the right to participate in a class action in court or a class proceeding in arbitration. When a consumer nevertheless files a class action, the defendant moves to compel arbitration under Sections 3 and 4 of the Arbitration Act, and the court, applying Concepcion and Italian Colors, ordinarily stays or dismisses the court case and orders the named plaintiff to arbitrate on an individual basis. The class allegations do not transfer to the arbitral forum, because the waiver bars class arbitration as well as class litigation. Each consumer in the putative class is remitted to a separate, individual proceeding. At that point the substantive law, federal or state, remains formally intact: the prohibition on the defendant's conduct reads the same, and the statutory damages read the same. What changes is the arithmetic of enforcement. An individual arbitration requires a filing, a presentation of evidence, and, in cases that turn on expert proof or on records held by the defendant, an investment of time and money that bears no proportion to a claim worth thirty dollars, or three hundred dollars, or, in a great many consumer cases, less than one thousand dollars. Rational claimants abandon such claims, and rational lawyers decline to represent such claimants. The clause therefore decides the outcome in the only sense that matters: the federal or state rule is neither vindicated nor rejected, because the case in which the rule would have applied does not proceed.

### Do arbitration clauses defeat consumer protection claims?

In many small value cases, the practical answer is yes. A clause with a class waiver leaves each consumer to arbitrate alone, and the cost of proving a claim often exceeds any individual recovery. Larger claims, public enforcement actions, and statutory exceptions remain outside that result in defined settings.

The empirical record assembled by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau gave that mechanism measurable dimensions. The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 directed the Bureau to study predispute arbitration agreements in consumer financial markets and authorized the Bureau to regulate such agreements by rule if regulation was in the public interest and for the protection of consumers. The Bureau delivered its Arbitration Study to Congress in March 2015. The study reported that very few consumers brought individual actions in any forum. Individual consumer arbitrations averaged in the hundreds per year across the markets studied, and the Bureau counted 1,060 arbitration cases filed between 2010 and 2012 in which arbitrators awarded consumers a combined total of less than $400,000, while arbitrators ordered consumers to pay $2.8 million, largely in disputed debts. Class actions reached a different order of magnitude. The study found that at least 160 million class members were eligible for relief in federal consumer finance class actions over the five year period studied, that settlements totaled $2.7 billion in cash relief, in kind relief, and attorneys' fees and expenses, and that at least $1.1 billion was paid or scheduled to be paid to at least 34 million consumers in cases with available payment data. More than ninety percent of the arbitration agreements studied expressly prohibited class proceedings in arbitration, and three out of four consumers surveyed in the credit card market did not know whether the governing contract contained an arbitration clause at all. The study's authors drew the conclusion that the data supported: arbitration agreements restricted consumer relief principally by limiting class actions, and class actions supplied relief and changes in business conduct at a scale that individual proceedings did not approach.

The Bureau acted on that study in July 2017, and Congress reversed the Bureau within four months. On July 10, 2017, the Bureau issued a final rule, published in the Federal Register on July 19, 2017, at 82 Fed. Reg. 33210, under which a provider could not rely on a predispute arbitration agreement to bar a consumer from filing or participating in a class action in court concerning a covered product or service. The rule did not prohibit arbitration agreements. Individual disputes could be routed to arbitration. The rule instead severed the class waiver from the arbitration clause, required providers to include language reflecting the limitation, and required providers to submit specified records of arbitral and court proceedings to the Bureau. The rule took effect on September 18, 2017, and compliance was required for agreements entered on or after March 19, 2018. The compliance date never governed a contract. Congress proceeded under the Congressional Review Act, which permits Congress to nullify an agency rule by joint resolution and bars the agency from issuing a substantially similar rule without specific congressional authorization. The House of Representatives passed House Joint Resolution 111 on July 25, 2017, by a vote of 231 to 190. The Senate passed the resolution on October 24, 2017, by a vote of 51 to 50. The President signed the resolution on November 1, 2017, and the resolution became Public Law 115-74. By operation of that Act, the Bureau's rule had no force or effect, and the Bureau was barred from reissuing the rule, or a substantially similar rule, unless Congress enacted new authority. The one regulatory intervention that would have restored aggregate litigation in consumer finance came from a federal agency, rested on a federal statute, and was extinguished by the federal legislature before the intervention applied to a single new agreement. State law supplied no substitute, because Concepcion had already held that a state rule accomplishing a comparable result through unconscionability doctrine was preempted.

Against that background, the statutory exceptions to arbitrability stand out precisely because exceptions require Congress to speak directly. Congress has banned mandatory arbitration outright in a small number of consumer settings. A provision of the Dodd-Frank Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. 1639c(e), provides that no residential mortgage loan, and no extension of credit under an open end consumer credit plan secured by the principal dwelling of the consumer, may include terms requiring arbitration or any other nonjudicial procedure as the method for resolving a controversy arising out of the transaction. The same subsection preserves the ability of a consumer and a creditor to agree to arbitration after a dispute has arisen. The prohibition took effect for loans with applications received on or after June 1, 2013. The Military Lending Act supplies a second exception. Under 10 U.S.C. 987(e)(3), a creditor may not extend consumer credit to a covered member of the armed forces, or to a dependent of such a member, on terms under which the creditor requires the borrower to submit to arbitration. Each exception proves the structure of the general rule: where Congress wanted a court forum preserved, Congress said so in statutory text addressed to a defined category of transactions, and where Congress stayed silent, the Arbitration Act, as construed in the four decisions, supplied an answer that favored the clause as written.

The arguments on each side of that structure were made by named institutions, on stated grounds. The efficiency case, advanced by the U.S. Chamber of Commerce in comments to the Bureau and in litigation challenging the Bureau's 2017 rule, held that arbitration delivers a faster, less formal, and less expensive forum for the individual disputes that consumers actually bring, and that class litigation delivers its benefits disproportionately to lawyers. In the Chamber's account, class actions rarely produced material benefits for class members, imposed enormous litigation expenses and settlement pressure on businesses regardless of the merits, and passed those costs to consumers in higher prices and reduced access to credit. The Chamber's challenge argued that the rule departed from the Dodd-Frank Act because the rule was neither in the public interest nor for the protection of consumers, and the Chamber pointed to Bureau data showing that consumers who completed individual arbitrations recovered amounts measured in thousands of dollars, while class members who received payments received amounts measured in tens of dollars. The Concepcion majority supplied the doctrinal form of the same argument. Bilateral arbitration, in the majority's reasoning, is a creature of contract designed for streamlined proceedings; class arbitration transforms the forum into a slow and costly proceeding that resembles class litigation while lacking judicial supervision and appellate review, and a defendant forced into class arbitration faces aggregate liability with only the narrow review of awards that the Arbitration Act permits. On that reasoning, enforcing the clause as written protects the attributes that made arbitration worth choosing, and a rule against class waivers abolishes the form of arbitration to which the parties agreed.

The access to justice case, advanced by the Bureau's study authors, by Public Citizen, and by the National Consumer Law Center, answered the efficiency case at the level of aggregate effects. Public Citizen argued that arbitration clauses paired with class action bans, inserted into nonnegotiable contracts, denied consumers and workers a day in court, deterred the pursuit of claims, kept corporate misconduct outside public view, and limited accountability for conduct that harmed large numbers of people in small amounts. The National Consumer Law Center argued that consumers rarely understood the clauses at the point of signing, that very few consumers meaningfully agreed to surrender court access, and that forced arbitration immunized corporations from liability for widespread practices that no individual claimant could afford to challenge. The Bureau's study supplied the evidentiary version of the argument: the relevant comparison, in the study's framing, was not the average award in a completed arbitration against the average payment in a class settlement, but the total relief delivered across a market, including relief delivered to consumers who filed nothing, and including the changes in business conduct that class settlements required. Justice Breyer's dissent in Concepcion, joined by Justices Ginsburg, Sotomayor, and Kagan, gave the argument judicial form. The Discover Bank rule, the dissent reasoned, applied to class waivers in contracts generally and therefore placed arbitration agreements and litigation agreements on the same footing, as the saving clause contemplated. The dissent pressed the economics that the majority treated as beside the point: a claim worth $30.22 would not attract counsel and would not justify an individual proceeding, so permitting class waivers in small claim adhesion contracts allowed the drafter to insulate the drafter from liability for deliberate schemes that extracted small sums from large numbers of consumers. Justice Kagan's dissent in Italian Colors, joined by Justices Ginsburg and Breyer, carried the point into federal statutory law, arguing that the effective vindication doctrine should have prevented enforcement where the cost of individual arbitration made a federal claim worthless in practice. Each side, at full strength, describes a real feature of the mechanism. Arbitration as designed is genuinely informal and genuinely fast for a dispute that a claimant can afford to bring. Class waivers as enforced genuinely extinguish the only proceeding in which a small, widely shared claim can be proved at a rational cost.

The verdict that the fifth axis supports follows from the sequence of decisions and the single completed regulatory episode. Between 2011 and 2018, the Supreme Court held that a state could not treat a class waiver as unconscionable, that a federal court could not refuse a class waiver because individual arbitration was uneconomic, that a state court could not construe its way around the first holding, and that a federal labor statute did not displace the same rule for employees. Congress preserved court access in mortgages and in lending to servicemembers by express statutory command, and Congress withdrew, before the compliance date arrived, the one agency rule that would have preserved class litigation across consumer finance. A comparison of federal and state consumer protection law that stops at preemption therefore misses the operative decision in most consumer disputes, a decision that sits in the contract file. Where an arbitration clause with a class waiver governs the transaction, the clause determines the forum, the procedure, and, for the small claims that consumer statutes most often address, whether enforcement happens at all. Where no clause governs, or where Congress has forbidden the clause, the federal and state comparison recovers full importance. Arbitration decides more than preemption because arbitration decides whether the preemption question is ever reached.

## The Limits on Both Sides of the Line

Federal law supplies a broad prohibition without a general private right of action under Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, while state statutes supply private actions, fee shifting in many states, and multiplied damages in many states, and the contrast can make state law appear categorically stronger. The record resists that appearance. State statutes differ from one another on scope, proof, procedure, and remedy to a degree that changes outcomes for identically described conduct, and federal law contains sectoral gaps that leave the broadest federal standard unavailable against entire classes of sellers. Protection depended, in the period ending on February 1, 2019, on the particular state, the particular industry, and the particular contract, rather than on a general superiority belonging to one level of government.

### Is state consumer protection law always stronger than federal law?
No. State statutes supplied broader private remedies in many settings before February 2019, yet exemptions, reliance requirements, notice conditions, and damages caps left entire categories of conduct and claimants outside state protection, while federal sectoral statutes and agency enforcement reached conduct that particular state statutes excluded or burdened procedurally.

Variation starts with scope, which decides cases before proof begins. Every state had enacted a statute prohibiting unfair or deceptive acts or practices by 2019, and the shared label conceals divergent texts. Victor E. Schwartz, Cary Silverman, and Christopher E. Appel, surveying state law in a 2007 article in the Washburn Law Journal, reported that about two thirds of state unfair and deceptive practices laws exempted conduct that a state or federal agency had regulated, permitted, approved, or specifically authorized, or conduct that complied with government regulations. An exemption drafted around permission removes a category of conduct from private litigation altogether, while an exemption drafted around regulation alone can remove a category of defendants. Defenders treated deference to specialized oversight as protection against conflicting commands, while the National Consumer Law Center, in treatise treatment, treated broad exemptions as openings through which regulated sellers escaped the general standard. A broad exemption therefore offers less protection than the shared label suggests.

Florida illustrates the exemption structure with unusual clarity. Section 501.212 of the Florida Statutes, the application provision for the Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act, provided that the act did not apply to an act or practice required or specifically permitted by federal or state law, and removed claims for personal injury or death and for damage to property other than the property that formed the subject of the consumer transaction. A separate subsection removed persons and activities regulated under laws administered by the state insurance regulator, regulated banks and savings and loan associations, and activity regulated by the Florida Public Service Commission. The structure therefore combined a permission defense, a subject matter exclusion, and an institutional exclusion. A deceptive practice by an unregulated retailer fell inside the statute, while a parallel practice connected to an exempt regulated activity could fall outside the statute even though the consumer experience looked identical. A remedy has no value in a case removed at the outset.

A different scope limit carves professional services out of the definition of commerce. Section 75-1.1(b) of the North Carolina General Statutes defined commerce to include all business activities, however denominated, while excluding professional services rendered by a member of a learned profession. An opinion issued under Attorney General Rufus L. Edmisten explained that the exclusion clarified an intent to reach commercial activities rather than strictly professional activities, and University of North Carolina School of Government guidance identified attorneys, architects, and health care providers as examples of learned professionals. North Carolina authority distinguished professional services from commercial conduct such as advertising, following Bates v. State Bar of Arizona in 1977. A billing practice or a piece of legal or medical advice characterized as a professional service therefore sat outside the statute, while a retail sale sat inside the statute. Treatment of a doctor, a lawyer, or an accountant under a general consumer statute therefore depended on the state of the transaction, because other states adopted comparable limits in varied forms or omitted such limits.

Proof requirements create a second layer of variation for private plaintiffs. Pennsylvania supplies the most fully litigated example. The private action provision of the Pennsylvania Unfair Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Law, at Title 73, Section 201-9.2 of the Pennsylvania Statutes, authorized a purchaser or lessee for personal, family, or household purposes who suffered an ascertainable loss as a result of a prohibited act to bring an action. In Weinberg v. Sun Company, decided in 2001 and reported at 777 A.2d 442, the Supreme Court of Pennsylvania reversed an order that had allowed class certification with a reduced showing of reliance in a false advertising case. Practitioner analysis summarized the holding in a consistent formula: the statute was not intended to do away with the traditional common law elements of reliance and causation. The court confirmed the point in Yocca v. Pittsburgh Steelers Sports in 2004 and reiterated in Toy v. Metropolitan Life Insurance in 2007 that justifiable reliance formed an element of claims under the statute. A Pennsylvania consumer therefore carried an individualized burden that Commission enforcement under Section 5 never carried, complicating class treatment because reliance could differ across purchasers.

Florida law marks the opposite pole on the same element. Practitioner summaries of the Florida statute described the Florida threshold as an objective test, asking whether a practice was likely to deceive a consumer acting reasonably under the circumstances, without a requirement of individual reliance. A Pennsylvania claim built on the same advertisement, by contrast, faced dismissal or denial of class certification if individual reliance could not be shown. Defenders of a reliance requirement, including the Pennsylvania Supreme Court in Weinberg, anchored the requirement in text authorizing recovery for a loss suffered as a result of a prohibited act, while critics of the requirement answered that mass advertising rarely permits clean proof of individual reliance. Two states applying statutes with the same general prohibition imposed materially different proof burdens, and a claim viable under state law in one jurisdiction could fail under state law in another jurisdiction on an element that federal agency enforcement did not require at all.

Remedy rules add a third layer, because several states conditioned multiplied damages on a heightened showing about the mind of the defendant. Massachusetts Chapter 93A, Section 9(3), directed a court to award up to three times, and not less than two times, the amount of actual damages if the court found that the act or practice was a willful or knowing violation of Section 2, or that a refusal to grant relief upon the statutory demand was made in bad faith with knowledge or reason to know that the act or practice violated Section 2. Actual damages, attorney fees, and costs could therefore rest on a lesser showing, while double or treble recovery depended on willfulness, knowledge, or a bad faith refusal after demand. Texas law drew a parallel separation in the Deceptive Trade Practices Consumer Protection Act. Under Section 17.50 of the Texas Business and Commerce Code, a prevailing consumer recovered economic damages as the baseline, while knowing conduct supported mental anguish and up to three times economic damages, and intentional conduct supported up to three times the combined total of economic and mental anguish damages. Multiplication was therefore a conditional remedy, available only after a plaintiff proved an additional fact about the defendant.

Procedure imposes a fourth layer, through pre-suit demand and notice requirements with no counterpart in a Commission complaint and no uniform counterpart across states. Massachusetts Chapter 93A, Section 9(3), provided that at least thirty days prior to the filing of an action, a written demand for relief, identifying the claimant and reasonably describing the unfair or deceptive act or practice relied upon and the injury suffered, had to be mailed or delivered to a prospective respondent. Federal decisions applying York v. Sullivan, decided in Massachusetts in 1975, treated satisfaction of the thirty day requirement as a matter that a plaintiff had to allege and prove. The demand also shaped value, because a claimant who rejected a written tender that a court later found reasonable in relation to the injury actually suffered faced a limit on recovery tied to the tendered relief. Texas imposed a longer sixty day notice interval through Section 17.505 of the Texas Business and Commerce Code. Florida practitioner summaries, by contrast, listed no demand letter requirement before filing suit. A mandatory waiting period therefore shaped leverage and timing in Massachusetts and in Texas, while a consumer in another state could file at once.

Caps complete the remedial variation, converting an apparently uniform multiplier into a bounded sum. Washington law authorized a court, in a private action under the Washington Consumer Protection Act, to increase an award up to an amount not exceeding three times the actual damages sustained, with a proviso in Revised Code of Washington Section 19.86.090 that an increased damage award for a violation of Section 19.86.020 could not exceed twenty five thousand dollars. Trebling was discretionary rather than automatic, and the increase stopped at that fixed sum regardless of the scale of actual damages. Washington law also required the elements articulated in Hangman Ridge Training Stables v. Safeco Title Insurance, decided in 1986: an unfair or deceptive act or practice, occurrence in trade or commerce, public interest impact, injury to business or property, and causation. The public interest element gave Washington courts a screening function that statutes without such an element did not supply. Discretion, a ceiling, and that screen determine whether the headline remedy arrives in a given case.

The federal system contains gaps of a different design, beginning in the text of Section 5 itself. Section 5(a), codified at Title 15, Section 45(a)(2) of the United States Code, empowered the Commission to prevent unfair or deceptive acts or practices, with express exceptions removing banks, savings and loan institutions described in Section 57a(f)(3), federal credit unions described in Section 57a(f)(4), common carriers, air carriers and foreign air carriers subject to Part A of Subtitle VII of Title 49, and persons subject to the Packers and Stockyards Act of 1921, apart from a stated exception. A bank depositor, a credit union borrower, and an air carrier passenger therefore faced a seller outside direct Section 5 authority. After the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and sector regulators supplied substitute authority in portions of that territory. The broadest federal standard therefore did not reach every seller directly through February 1, 2019, and substitute authority differed in procedure, priorities, and remedies.

Insurance presents the federal gap as a deliberate congressional reservation. The McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945, enacted after the Supreme Court held in United States v. South-Eastern Underwriters Association in 1944 that insurance conducted across state lines formed interstate commerce, declared a policy favoring state regulation of the business of insurance. Section 2(b) of the McCarran-Ferguson Act, codified at Title 15, Section 1012(b) of the United States Code, provided that the Sherman Act, the Clayton Act, and the Federal Trade Commission Act applied to the business of insurance to the extent that such business was not regulated by state law. In insurance, a weakness in a particular state regime therefore translated directly into weak protection, with no general federal backstop of the kind available in unreserved markets. Defenders of the reservation, including the National Association of Insurance Commissioners in 1945, argued that state regulators possessed proximity to local conditions, while antitrust commentators who later urged narrowing argued that fragmented oversight invited uneven enforcement. In insurance, the ranking question misstates the arrangement of 1945, because Congress assigned primacy to the states and limited the federal statutes by reference to state regulation itself.

Federal gaps extended into the patchwork character of sectoral law as a whole. Federal consumer protection in the period ending in 2019 layered specific statutes governing credit, debt collection, telemarketing, and other defined practices over the general standard, each statute carrying defined terms, defendants, and remedies. A practice outside a specific statute and inside a Section 5 exception could fall between regimes. State law often filled portions of that space, yet unevenly, for the reasons already described. A Florida consumer complaining about an exempt regulated activity encountered a state exclusion mirroring federal deference to regulated industries, so the two systems compounded a gap rather than offsetting a gap.

State gaps beyond variation require separate attention. Preemption supplies the clearest example, and a brief statement suffices in a section that does not own the detail. The Fair Credit Reporting Act, through the relation to state laws provision codified at Title 15, Section 1681t(b)(1) of the United States Code, barred states from imposing requirements or prohibitions with respect to enumerated subject matter regulated under specified provisions, including the responsibilities of persons who furnished information to consumer reporting agencies under Section 1681s-2. In Purcell v. Bank of America in 2011 and Macpherson v. JPMorgan Chase Bank in 2011, federal courts of appeals held that the furnisher preemption reached state statutory and common law claims concerning furnisher reporting. A state legislature therefore lacked authority to impose a stronger standard in the preempted field, regardless of the generosity of the state general statute. Credit reporting affected access to credit, housing, insurance, and employment, so the unavailable field was not marginal, and no improvement in a state demand rule, multiplier, or fee provision could cure the limit.

Small value claims expose a second state gap, grounded in economics. Deceptive practices frequently impose losses that are real in aggregate and small for each purchaser, as a misleading fee or a deceptive renewal charge extracts modest sums from large numbers of buyers. A private action for actual damages alone rarely supports representation, because attorney time, filing costs, and demand steps consume the value of an individual recovery. Two mechanisms decide whether such claims proceed at all: class aggregation, which pools small losses into a controversy large enough to litigate, and fee shifting, which transfers representation costs to a losing defendant. The availability of each varied by state and by claim. A state with generous multiplied damages could leave small claims stranded if class certification failed on individualized reliance, as Weinberg demonstrated, or if a public interest screen fragmented the claimant pool. A state with modest multipliers could support small claims more effectively if fee shifting was mandatory and courts certified classes on an objective standard.

Public enforcement capacity forms a third state gap, resisting reduction to statutory text. State statutes typically authorized the attorney general to investigate, seek injunctions, obtain restitution, and pursue civil penalties, and the formal grants look similar across state codes. The offices exercising those grants differed sharply before 2019 in budget, staffing, dedicated divisions, and competing criminal, civil rights, environmental, and antitrust dockets. Two states with identical statutory language therefore fielded enforcement programs of very different scale. Multistate investigations coordinated through the National Association of Attorneys General partially compensated for thin resources, producing national settlements in areas such as mortgage servicing after the financial crisis of 2007 to 2009. Pooling also concentrated attention on national defendants, leaving local sellers dependent on a single office. Federal enforcement faced parallel resource limits, yet at a single national scale rather than at fifty separate scales of unequal depth. In a state with a small staff, state law therefore functioned as a complaint process rather than as an enforcement system.

The evidence supports a jurisdictional rather than a directional conclusion. A directional conclusion asserts that one level of government protects consumers more strongly in general, without regard to state, industry, or contract. The record defeats that form of claim. Federal law supplied a uniform prohibition with agency enforcement and no general private action, then withdrew direct Commission authority over the sectors named in Section 5 and over insurance to the extent of state regulation, and displaced state law in fields such as furnisher responsibilities in credit reporting. State law supplied private actions with fees and multipliers, then withdrew coverage through exemptions, imposed reliance and ascertainable loss proof in Pennsylvania, imposed demand intervals in Massachusetts and in Texas, gated multiplication behind willful, knowing, or intentional findings, capped the enhancement in Washington, screened Washington claims through a public interest element, and depended on offices of sharply unequal capacity. A flat assertion that state law was simply stronger fails on Florida exempt activity, Pennsylvania reliance, Washington caps, and credit reporting preemption, while a flat assertion that federal law was simply stronger fails on the absent private action, the Section 5 exceptions, and the insurance reservation of 1945.

Three questions therefore replace the ranking question. The first question asks which state governed, because the state determines exemptions, reliance proof, demand procedure, multiplication conditions, caps, fee rules, and enforcement capacity. The second question asks which industry sold the product or service, because the industry determines Commission authority, substitute federal authority, McCarran-Ferguson primacy, preemption, and state regulatory exemption. The third question asks which contract governed, because the contract determines forum, procedure, and the practical ability to aggregate claims, a channeling that can matter as much as the substantive standard. The verdict must therefore weigh preemption, which allocates authority between sovereigns, together with the contract driven allocation of disputes between individual and aggregate resolution. The range of defensible verdicts is therefore conditional in form. A conditional verdict can state that a specified body of law protects a specified class of consumers against a specified class of sellers under specified conditions more effectively than an alternative, and the evidence supports many verdicts of that kind. An unconditional verdict contradicts the exemption lists, the proof rules, the caps, the preemption provisions, and the enforcement budgets on the record for the period ending on February 1, 2019.
## The Five Axes Side by Side

The five axes separate questions that a single ranking would collapse into confusion. The private right axis asks who could bring a claim in court, the preemption axis asks whether state law remained available in a regulated field, the remedies axis asks what a successful claim paid, the enforcers axis asks which public officials could act, and the arbitration axis asks whether a contract removed the dispute from court altogether. Read together, the axes show a pattern of divided strength, with state law supplying the ordinary individual claim, federal law supplying national public enforcement and sectoral private claims, and contract terms controlling access to either system in a large share of small disputes.

| Axis | Federal law | State law |
|---|---|---|
| Private right of action | No private action under Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act; express private actions in sectoral statutes such as credit reporting and debt collection law | Private action granted by most unfair and deceptive practices statutes for a person injured by a prohibited practice |
| Preemption posture | Floor in lending, collection, and credit discrimination law through savings clauses; ceiling in credit reporting under Section 1681t(b) | State law preserved where protection was greater in floor fields; barred in enumerated credit reporting subjects |
| Remedies | Agency restitution and penalties; sectoral statutory damages, punitive damages in defined settings, and fee awards | Actual damages, statutory minimums, multiplied damages for knowing or willful conduct, injunctions, and fee shifting in many states |
| Enforcers | Federal Trade Commission, Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, and sector regulators, subject to statutory carve outs | State attorneys general, state regulators, and private plaintiffs, with multistate coordination |
| Arbitration exposure | Federal Arbitration Act enforced class waivers under Concepcion and Italian Colors, subject to express congressional exceptions | State rules against class waivers preempted; state claims diverted to individual arbitration on the same terms as federal claims |

## The Verdict

On the five axes, state law is the operative protection for an individual consumer claim because it alone supplies a private right with multiplied damages and fee shifting. That sentence carries the central finding of the comparison, and each element of the sentence matters. A private right converted a prohibition into a claim that an injured consumer could file without persuading an agency to act. Multiplied damages converted a small loss into exposure that a defendant had reason to settle. Fee shifting converted a claim that no lawyer could afford to carry into a case that counsel could accept on the merits. Federal law, at the general standard level, supplied none of those three elements under Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act. The Commission could investigate, adjudicate, and seek redress under defined conditions, and sectoral federal statutes supplied private rights in credit reporting, debt collection, and other defined markets, but the ordinary deceptive sale, repair, or service transaction reached a courtroom, when a courtroom was reached at all, through a state unfair and deceptive practices statute.

The deciding factor for whether either body of law can be used at all is arbitration exposure, whether the consumer's contract carries an arbitration clause with a class waiver. That factor outranks the others in sequence, because a ruling on forum precedes every ruling on substance. Arbitration decides more than preemption. Preemption decides whether a state rule survives beside a federal rule, a question between legislatures and courts about the allocation of public authority. Arbitration decides whether a public court hears the dispute at all, a question settled by a contract term drafted before any dispute arose. A state statute could survive preemption analysis in full, grant a private action in express terms, treble damages for knowing conduct, and shift fees to a prevailing consumer, and a class waiver could render each of those provisions inoperative for the claims that most needed aggregation. The comparison therefore assigns the greatest practical weight to the fifth axis, not because substance lacks importance, but because forum determines whether substance gets applied.

The reasoning behind that ordering rests on the economics of the characteristic consumer claim. Deception in consumer markets characteristically extracted modest sums from many buyers rather than a large sum from one buyer. A claim worth a few hundred dollars could not support individual litigation or individual arbitration at a rational cost, even with fee shifting, because filing, proof, and time consumed the recovery before judgment. Aggregation solved that arithmetic problem. A class action pooled small losses into a controversy large enough to justify expert proof, document discovery, and contested motion practice, and a public enforcement action pooled the same losses through an attorney general or a federal agency. A class waiver disabled the first solution while leaving the second solution dependent on agency priorities, budgets, and elections. Between 2011 and 2018, the Supreme Court decisions in Concepcion, Italian Colors, Imburgia, and Epic Systems closed the principal judicial routes around such waivers, and the disapproval of the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau arbitration rule in 2017 closed the principal regulatory route. The combined effect was a legal order in which the strongest substantive statute could be present in the code and absent from the outcome.

Preemption retains real force inside the territory that arbitration leaves open. In credit reporting, Section 1681t(b) of Title 15 removed enumerated subjects from state legislation altogether, and no drafting skill at the state level could restore authority over furnisher duties, reporting periods, or prescreening rules that Congress had placed beyond state addition. In lending, collection, and credit discrimination, savings clauses preserved state law that protected consumers more than federal law, and the Dodd-Frank Act settlement of 2010 preserved that floor while narrowing national bank preemption to the Barnett Bank standard. A litigant who ignored preemption risked dismissal of the very claim that state remedies made attractive. The verdict therefore does not demote preemption to insignificance. Preemption governed the validity of state law in regulated fields. Arbitration governed the usability of valid law in contracted transactions. Validity without usability produced no recovery, and usability without validity produced no claim, and the cases that failed on usability outnumbered the cases that failed on validity in the ordinary run of small consumer disputes through 2018.

Remedies and enforcers explain why the state advantage, where a forum remained available, was more than formal. Massachusetts Chapter 93A paired a private action with a statutory floor, mandatory multiplication for willful or knowing violations, and fees. Texas paired economic damages with mental anguish and multiplication for knowing or intentional conduct. California paired equitable restitution under one statute with damages and punitive relief under another. Federal sectoral law matched parts of that pattern, notably in credit reporting and debt collection, where statutory damages and fee awards supported a substantial private docket. Public enforcement added a second channel that private economics could not close entirely, because an attorney general or a federal agency could aggregate harm without a class certification fight and could obtain conduct relief that an individual judgment could not impose. The multistate settlements of 2012, 2013, and 2015 demonstrated that channel at national scale. Against that record, a verdict favoring federal law for the individual claim would have to explain away the absent private right under Section 5, and a verdict favoring state law without qualification would have to explain away exemptions, reliance elements, demand requirements, caps, and uneven enforcement capacity documented in the sections on variation. Neither explanation survives the record.

A further consideration strengthens the ordering without changing the identity of the operative private law. Public enforcement, however vigorous in a given period, operated through selection. Agencies chose cases for market significance, evidentiary strength, and institutional priority, and attorneys general chose cases under electoral and budgetary constraints that differed across states and across years. A consumer whose dispute fell outside the selection criteria of every available public enforcer held only the private claim, and the private claim existed, at the general standard level, only under state law. Selection therefore magnified the importance of the state private right in ordinary disputes and magnified the damage done by a class waiver, because the waiver removed the one enforcement channel that did not depend on an official agenda. Where public enforcement filled the gap, relief often arrived as a settlement negotiated for a group, with terms shaped by the enforcer rather than by the individual claimant, and the difference between a negotiated group settlement and a judgment on a pleaded claim mattered for conduct standards, for release scope, and for the distribution of money among injured consumers.

The conditional form of the verdict is therefore deliberate. For a consumer with a substantial individual loss, no arbitration clause, and a transaction inside a strong state statute, state law supplied the most complete private remedy in the comparison. For a consumer harmed by a furnisher of credit information or by conduct inside a federally occupied subject, federal sectoral law or federal agency enforcement supplied the only available route, whatever the state statute promised in general terms. For a consumer bound by an arbitration clause with a class waiver and holding a small claim, neither body of law supplied an effective private route, and relief depended on public enforcement or on an express congressional exception such as the mortgage and military lending prohibitions. The deciding factor formulation captures that distribution in one test that a reader can apply to a new dispute: examine the contract for an arbitration clause and a class waiver before weighing preemption, remedies, or the identity of the enforcer, because the clause, where present and enforceable, settles the practical question that the other axes only prepare to answer.

## Choosing a Starting Point by Reader Type

A student beginning the subject gains the clearest entry through state law, because a state unfair and deceptive practices statute presents the complete anatomy of a consumer claim in one text: a prohibition, a private plaintiff, defined remedies, fee shifting, and the defenses and exemptions that limit each element. Study of a single statute, such as Massachusetts Chapter 93A or the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act, exposes the questions that recur across the field, and comparison with a second state then teaches the lesson that the shared label conceals divergent law. Federal law enters the student path at the second stage, through Section 5 doctrine as interpretive background and through the sectoral statutes as examples of Congress granting the private right that the general statute withheld. A structured route through the primary sources, organized in the order of the five axes, appears in [the US legislation study guide](/2022/06/15/us-legislation-study-guide/), and regular review through [practice and revise US government and civics material on ReportMedic](https://reportmedic.org/tools/us-government-civics-study.html) reinforces the institutional vocabulary on which the comparison depends.

A staffer drafting or evaluating legislation gains the most from beginning with preemption and arbitration, the two axes that determine whether a bill operates after enactment. A state staffer who drafts a stronger prohibition without checking Section 1681t(b) in credit reporting, or the savings clauses in lending and collection law, risks producing a statute that a court sets aside in the first contested case. A federal staffer faces the mirror problem: a savings clause preserves state experimentation, an express preemption clause imposes uniformity, and silence on arbitration leaves the class waiver regime of Concepcion and Italian Colors in control of private enforcement regardless of the remedies written into the bill. For the staffer, the operative skill is drafting with the displacement doctrines in view, stating expressly whether state law survives, whether a private right exists, and whether predispute arbitration terms remain enforceable for the claims created.

A researcher gains the most from beginning with enforcers and remedies, because the gap between law in text and law in operation lies in those two axes. Enforcement records, settlement instruments, and fee awards reveal which prohibitions agencies selected for action, which private claims counsel accepted, and which statutory multipliers influenced settlement value. A research design that codes cases by state exemption, reliance element, demand requirement, and damages cap can test whether variation in text predicts variation in outcome, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau arbitration study of 2015 supplies a model of empirical work that changed a regulatory outcome and then a congressional response. For the researcher, federal and state law form one dataset with two levels of variation, and the arbitration clause operates as a variable that censors observation of both levels.

Sequencing matters for each reader type because the axes build on one another in a fixed order of dependence. A prohibition means little without a plaintiff, a plaintiff means little without a remedy that supports representation, a remedy means little in a preempted field, and every element means little when a contract diverts the dispute to individual arbitration. A curriculum, a drafting checklist, a research design, and a reporting plan that respect that order of dependence produce fewer false starts than a plan organized by sovereign alone. The recommended sequences differ in entry point because professional tasks begin at different points in the chain, yet each sequence ends by covering all five axes, since omission of any axis leaves a known class of surprise in the analysis.

A journalist covering a consumer dispute gains the most from beginning with the contract and the complaint file. The contract establishes whether a court action was ever realistically available, whether a class waiver confined the dispute to individual arbitration, and whether a statutory exception restored a court forum. The complaint file, whether held by a state attorney general, the Federal Trade Commission, or the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, establishes whether public enforcers had notice of a pattern and how a single grievance related to aggregate harm. A journalist who asks about forum, aggregation, and agency notice before asking which side a statute favors produces an account that explains an outcome rather than merely announcing an outcome. For each reader type, the recommended starting point differs because the task differs, and the five axes supply a common map on which student, staffer, researcher, and journalist can locate the same dispute from different professional positions.

## Closing Assessment

The comparison ends with a division of labor that neither level of government designed as a single plan. Congress built a broad prohibition and assigned enforcement to agencies, then added sectoral statutes with private rights in markets where agency enforcement alone appeared insufficient. State legislatures borrowed the broad prohibition and attached the private action that the federal model omitted, then limited that action through exemptions, proof rules, procedure, and caps that reflected local judgments about regulated industries and litigation volume. Courts then reshaped both systems through preemption doctrine, which policed the boundary between the legislatures, and through arbitration doctrine, which moved a large share of disputes outside the courts that both legislatures had assumed would hear the disputes. The resulting structure, as the law stood on February 1, 2019, rewarded a reader who asked sequential questions in the order of the axes and penalized a reader who began with a preference for one sovereign.

Durability is the strongest claim that can be made for the dual structure. Redundancy between federal and state enforcers meant that a change in leadership at one agency, a budget contraction in one legislature, or an exemption in one statute rarely eliminated every route to relief. A practice that escaped a state exemption could meet a federal sectoral claim, a practice that no private plaintiff could afford to pursue could meet a multistate investigation, and a federal enforcement pause could leave state authority intact under the Dodd-Frank Act preservation provisions. Fragility is the corresponding weakness. The same redundancy produced overlapping demands on defendants, inconsistent obligations across state lines in fields without a federal ceiling, and a dependence on contract doctrine that left the most common small claims without an aggregate forum after the arbitration decisions and the 2017 disapproval resolution. A durable structure with a fragile point of access describes the record more faithfully than a ranking of sovereigns.

Measured against the problem that produced both systems, the record supports qualified confidence in the combined structure and skepticism toward any proposal that treats one level of government as a complete substitute for the other.

For study purposes, the field rewards organized retention of the moving parts, because a change in one element, a preemption amendment, a new damages cap, a revised demand rule, or a fresh arbitration decision, alters the balance without altering the vocabulary. A reader who can [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html) holds an advantage over a reader who encounters each dispute as an isolated controversy, since the five axes recur and the decided cases supply the content of each axis over time. The federal and state systems remained, at the close of the period examined, two answers to one problem: deception in consumer transactions imposed losses too small for ordinary litigation and too widespread for sporadic agency action. State law answered with a private claim built to be filed. Federal law answered with agencies built to act at scale and with sectoral claims in defined markets. Arbitration doctrine answered for the contract drafter, and that answer governed whenever a signed form carried a class waiver into the dispute.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: Do states have their own consumer protection laws?

Every state enacted a consumer protection statute prohibiting unfair or deceptive acts or practices, and the statutes are commonly called little FTC Acts because many borrowed language from Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act. Each statute has a distinct text, scope, set of exemptions, and remedial scheme, so the shared label describes a family of laws rather than a single uniform code. A state statute typically authorizes enforcement by the state attorney general and, in most states, grants an injured consumer a private right of action with damages and attorney fees. The content of protection therefore depends on the particular state statute governing the transaction, including reliance rules, demand requirements, and damages caps that vary considerably across jurisdictions.

### Q: Which federal consumer protection law preempts state law the most?

The Fair Credit Reporting Act preempted state law most broadly among the major federal consumer statutes in the period examined. Section 1681t(b) of Title 15 barred states from imposing requirements or prohibitions with respect to enumerated subjects, including prescreening, dispute timing, adverse action duties, information contained in consumer reports, furnisher responsibilities, and affiliate information sharing. Unlike neighboring statutes, the credit reporting provision contained no general qualification preserving state law that protected consumers more. Amendments in 2003 made the preemptions permanent by removing a scheduled sunset. Neighboring statutes such as the Truth in Lending Act and the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act adopted the opposite posture, preserving state law except to the extent of an inconsistency and treating greater protection as consistent.

### Q: Can you sue under state consumer protection law but not federal?

A consumer could sue under a state consumer protection statute in many situations where no federal private claim existed, because the general federal statute withheld a private right while most state statutes granted one expressly. Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act assigned enforcement to the Commission, and Holloway v. Bristol-Myers Corporation held in 1973 that consumers held no private action under that Act. A deceptive repair, sale, or service transaction therefore proceeded in court, if at all, under a state unfair and deceptive practices statute. Federal sectoral statutes supplied exceptions to the pattern, since credit reporting and debt collection law authorized private suits with statutory damages and fee awards for defined violations in defined markets.

### Q: What is a little FTC Act in consumer protection?

A little FTC Act is a state statute modeled on Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, prohibiting unfair or deceptive acts or practices in trade or commerce. State legislatures enacted such statutes mainly during the 1960s and 1970s, drawing on federal language, on the Uniform Deceptive Trade Practices Act, or on the Federal Trade Commission model law circulated through the Council of State Governments. The nickname records the borrowing of the prohibition, while the operative provisions usually diverged from the federal model in the respect that mattered most to consumers: most state statutes added a private right of action, damages provisions, and attorney fee awards that the federal Act omitted. Scope, exemptions, and remedies nevertheless differed substantially from state to state.

### Q: Can state attorneys general enforce federal consumer protection law?

State attorneys general could enforce specified federal consumer protection law within limits set by Congress. Section 1042 of the Dodd-Frank Act, codified at 12 U.S.C. 5552, authorized a state attorney general to bring a civil action to enforce Title X of that Act and regulations issued under Title X, after giving notice to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. Against a national bank or a federal savings association, the action could enforce a Bureau regulation, while the freestanding statutory prohibition on unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts was not directly available to a state plaintiff against such an institution. The Bureau held rights to intervene, to remove an intervened action to federal court, and to appeal after receiving the required notice.

### Q: Do arbitration clauses defeat consumer protection claims?

An arbitration clause paired with a class waiver defeated many small value consumer protection claims in practice, even though the underlying statute remained valid. After AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion in 2011 and American Express Company v. Italian Colors Restaurant in 2013, a consumer bound by such a clause was ordinarily confined to individual arbitration, where the cost of proof often exceeded the amount in dispute. Rational claimants abandoned small claims and counsel declined representation, so the prohibition in the statute produced no proceeding. Larger individual claims, public enforcement actions by agencies or attorneys general, and transactions covered by express congressional prohibitions on mandatory arbitration stood outside that practical result in defined settings.

### Q: Did Congress repeal a consumer protection arbitration rule?

Congress disapproved the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau arbitration rule under the Congressional Review Act. The Bureau had issued the rule in July 2017, published at 82 Fed. Reg. 33210, to prevent providers from using a predispute arbitration agreement to bar a consumer from filing or participating in a class action in court. The House of Representatives passed House Joint Resolution 111 on July 25, 2017, the Senate passed the resolution on October 24, 2017, and the President signed the resolution on November 1, 2017, as Public Law 115-74. By operation of that law, the rule had no force or effect, and the Bureau was barred from issuing a substantially similar rule without new congressional authorization.

### Q: Which should a student study first, federal or state consumer protection?

A student benefits from studying state consumer protection law first, because a state unfair and deceptive practices statute contains the complete anatomy of a claim in a single text: a prohibition, a private plaintiff, damages, fee shifting, exemptions, and procedural conditions. Mastery of one statute, followed by comparison with a second state, teaches both the shared structure and the variation that decides cases. Federal law then supplies essential second stage material, through Section 5 doctrine as interpretive background for state courts, through sectoral statutes that grant private rights in defined markets, and through preemption and arbitration doctrine that limit the state claim. The sequence follows the path of an ordinary individual dispute, which typically begins as a state claim.

### Q: Can a consumer bring federal and state consumer protection claims in the same lawsuit?

A consumer could commonly plead federal sectoral claims and state consumer protection claims in the same lawsuit, provided each claim rested on an available right of action. A complaint might join a Fair Credit Reporting Act claim or a Fair Debt Collection Practices Act claim with a state unfair and deceptive practices claim arising from the same transaction, and a federal court could exercise supplemental jurisdiction over the state claim. No private claim under Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act could be added, because that statute granted enforcement to the Commission alone. Preemption, exemptions, and arbitration clauses could nevertheless remove individual claims from the combined pleading, so joinder depended on the statutes and the contract involved.

### Q: Are banks and insurance companies covered by state consumer protection laws?

Coverage of banks and insurance companies under state consumer protection law varied by state and by activity. Many state statutes contained exemptions for regulated industries, for conduct permitted under other law, or for persons regulated by a banking or insurance authority, and such an exemption could remove a claim that a parallel claim against an unregulated retailer would sustain. Other states applied the general prohibition to financial and insurance conduct subject to sector specific rules. On the federal side, Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act excepted banks and related institutions from Commission authority, and the McCarran-Ferguson Act limited federal application to the business of insurance that state law regulated. The governing exemption text therefore required examination in every such case.

### Q: Why do state consumer protection laws differ so much from one state to another?

State consumer protection laws differ because fifty legislatures made independent choices on scope, proof, procedure, and remedies while borrowing from shared models. One legislature exempted regulated industries broadly, another exempted only conduct specifically permitted by law. One statute required individual justifiable reliance, another applied an objective deception standard. Demand periods ranged from no requirement to thirty days in Massachusetts and sixty days in Texas. Multiplication of damages depended in some states on willful or knowing conduct and in Washington faced a fixed ceiling of twenty five thousand dollars on the increased award. Federal models supplied common vocabulary, and local legislative judgments about litigation, industry, and enforcement capacity supplied the divergent operative rules.

### Q: Can a state enact a consumer protection law stronger than federal law?

A state could enact consumer protection law stronger than federal law in fields where Congress adopted a floor rather than a ceiling. Savings clauses in the Truth in Lending Act, the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, and the Equal Credit Opportunity Act preserved state law except to the extent of an inconsistency, and each framework treated greater consumer protection as consistent with federal purpose. Title X of the Dodd-Frank Act stated the same rule for state consumer financial laws. The Fair Credit Reporting Act supplied the principal counterexample, because Section 1681t(b) barred state requirements in enumerated subjects even where a proposed state rule would have protected consumers more. The answer therefore turned on the preemption text of the governing federal statute.

### Q: What is a multistate consumer protection settlement?

A multistate consumer protection settlement is an agreement resolving investigations or litigation brought jointly by attorneys general from multiple states against a defendant whose conduct affected consumers across state lines. Participating states coordinated investigation through the National Association of Attorneys General, designated lead states for negotiation, and adopted parallel consent judgments containing payments, restitution or other relief, and conduct standards. The National Mortgage Settlement of February 9, 2012, joined the federal government and forty nine state attorneys general with five large mortgage servicers in a settlement valued at approximately twenty five billion dollars. Pooling allowed small offices to share litigation capacity and allowed a defendant to obtain broad peace in a single negotiation rather than fifty separate proceedings.

### Q: Did the Dodd-Frank Act change the balance between federal and state consumer protection law?

The Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 changed the balance in favor of concurrent authority rather than displacement. Title X created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau with authority over unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts in consumer finance, preserved state consumer financial laws that protected consumers more than federal law, narrowed national bank preemption to the Barnett Bank standard, and authorized state attorneys general to enforce Title X and Bureau regulations under Section 1042. The Act also left statute specific preemption provisions in older laws in operation, including the credit reporting ceiling in Section 1681t. The settlement therefore combined a new federal enforcer, a floor as the default rule for the new framework, and continued state enforcement capacity on two independent legal bases.

### Q: Are arbitration clauses banned in any consumer contracts?

Congress banned mandatory predispute arbitration in a small number of defined consumer settings. A provision of the Dodd-Frank Act, codified at 15 U.S.C. 1639c(e), prohibited a residential mortgage loan, and an extension of credit under an open end consumer credit plan secured by a principal dwelling, from including terms requiring arbitration or another nonjudicial procedure to resolve a controversy arising from the transaction, while preserving an agreement to arbitrate made after a dispute arose. The Military Lending Act, at 10 U.S.C. 987(e)(3), prohibited a creditor from requiring a covered servicemember or dependent to submit to arbitration as a term of consumer credit. Outside such express prohibitions, the Federal Arbitration Act generally required enforcement of arbitration agreements as written.

### Q: What damages can a consumer recover under a state consumer protection statute?

Damages under a state consumer protection statute typically began with actual damages, and many statutes added components designed to make small claims viable. Massachusetts Chapter 93A provided actual damages or twenty five dollars, whichever was greater, with an award of up to three times but not less than two times actual damages for a willful or knowing violation, plus attorney fees and costs. Texas law provided economic damages, mental anguish for knowing conduct, and additional damages up to defined multiples, plus fees. Washington law allowed discretionary trebling subject to a ceiling of twenty five thousand dollars on the increased award. The precise combination depended on the state statute, on proof of culpability, and on compliance with demand or notice conditions.

### Q: Does the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau enforce state consumer protection law?

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau enforced federal consumer financial law, and state law entered Bureau activity mainly through preservation and coordination rather than through direct Bureau enforcement of state statutes. Title X of the Dodd-Frank Act preserved state consumer financial laws, authorized state attorneys general and state regulators to enforce Title X and Bureau regulations, and authorized state officials to enforce enumerated consumer laws under state authority preserved by the Act. Bureau supervision and enforcement proceeded under federal standards, including the prohibition on unfair, deceptive, or abusive acts or practices. Joint settlements, such as the Ocwen settlement of December 19, 2013, with forty nine states and the District of Columbia, showed the Bureau and state officials acting together under respective authorities on shared facts.

### Q: Which consumer protection law governs an online purchase from another state?

An online purchase from another state could implicate the consumer protection law of more than one jurisdiction, together with federal law governing the market involved. The state of the consumer, the state of the seller, and the choice of law and forum terms in the transaction documents each influenced which state statute a court applied, and state statutes differed on territorial scope and on exemptions. Federal law applied according to the activity, so a credit, reporting, collection, or product safety statute could supply a claim or a preemption defense regardless of the states involved. An arbitration clause in the online terms could also determine the forum before a court selected the governing substantive law, making the contract a threshold document in the analysis.

### Q: Can a business be sued under a state consumer protection law for conduct federal law permits?

A business could face a state consumer protection claim for conduct that federal law permitted, where the governing federal statute adopted a floor and the state statute prohibited more than federal law required. Savings clauses in lending, collection, and credit discrimination law preserved state rules that protected consumers more, and a state unfair and deceptive practices statute could prohibit a practice that no federal prohibition reached. Defenses remained available where a federal statute preempted the state rule, as in enumerated credit reporting subjects, where a state exemption covered conduct specifically permitted by law, or where federal law conferred an affirmative right that the state rule obstructed. Permission under federal law therefore functioned as a defense only in defined circumstances, not as general immunity.

### Q: How does a class action waiver change a consumer protection case?

A class action waiver changed a consumer protection case by confining each claimant to an individual proceeding, whether in court where permitted or in arbitration where a clause so provided. Aggregation had allowed small losses shared by many consumers to support one action with common proof, shared costs, and aggregate exposure proportioned to the scale of a practice. Without aggregation, each consumer faced filing, proof, and time costs measured against an individual recovery that was often a few hundred dollars or less. The waiver therefore altered settlement value, representation decisions, and deterrence, even though the substantive prohibition and the damages formula in the statute remained unchanged. Public enforcement by an agency or an attorney general remained available as a separate aggregation channel.
