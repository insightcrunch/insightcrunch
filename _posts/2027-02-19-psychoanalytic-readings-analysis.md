## Psychoanalytic Readings and the Verdict They Earn

Psychoanalytic readings of Romeo and Juliet begin from a refusal: the refusal to take the most famous love in English drama at face value. Where the popular account sees two young people struck by a feeling too large for their city, the psychoanalytic school sees a pattern that was assembled long before the feeling arrived, inside the two households, inside the structure of the family, inside the buried traffic between love and death that the play keeps announcing in its own words. The Prologue calls the love "death-marked" before either lover has spoken (Prologue, line 9). Juliet, at the moment of first sight, answers the discovery of Romeo's name with a sentence that puts her grave in her wedding bed: "If he be married, / My grave is like to be my wedding bed" (1.5.134-135). Romeo, waking in Mantua, reports a dream in which his lady finds him dead and kisses him back into life (5.1.6-9). The school's founding observation is that a play which fuses desire and death this systematically, at this density, in speech after speech, is not decorating a romance with morbid colouring. It is exhibiting a structure, and structures of that kind are what psychoanalysis exists to describe.

![Psychoanalytic readings of the lovers and the tomb - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

The verdict argued through this article can be stated at the outset, because the school deserves the compliment of a clear test. Psychoanalytic reading gives the strongest available account of the passion's peculiar physics: why it is total rather than partial, instant rather than gradual, fed rather than thwarted by its obstacle, and aimed from its first hour at the tomb. No rival school explains all four of those properties with one mechanism, and the mechanism, the unconscious patterning of desire by the family and by the drives, is testable against scene after scene of quoted text. The same school gives the weakest available account of the play as a made object: it cannot date a line, source a scene, edit a crux, or recover what the tragedy meant in the playhouse that first staged it, and its central concept was assembled three centuries after the play from materials the play helped to create. The anachronism charge, examined in full below, therefore lands against the school's historical claims and fails against its declared use as an instrument. Read the lovers with Freud's tools openly borrowed, and the play's intensities become intelligible as no other method makes them. Mistake the borrowing for a recovery of Shakespeare's own meanings, and the method becomes the thing its enemies describe: a Viennese family romance imposed on a Veronese novella. The stations of the school, its genuine discoveries and its documented limits, are set out in that order.

One discipline governs everything that follows. The brief for this article requires the approach to be treated clearly and without jargon, and the school's texts to be paraphrased rather than quoted at length. Each technical term is therefore introduced with its plain meaning attached, and each reading is held against the quoted lines of the play, cited by act, scene, and line from the Folger Shakespeare text edited by Barbara A. Mowat and Paul Werstine. Where a famous psychoanalytic claim about this play turns out not to exist, the article says so, starting with the largest absence of all.

## The Founder Who Never Wrote on This Play

The psychoanalytic Romeo and Juliet rests on an odd foundation: Sigmund Freud published no reading of the play. His Shakespeare was another set of texts. The Oedipus reading that made his reputation as a literary interpreter belongs to Hamlet, proposed in a letter to Wilhelm Fliess in 1897 and printed in The Interpretation of Dreams (1900), where the Prince's paralysis is explained by the buried wish the tragedy shares with Sophocles. "The Theme of the Three Caskets" (1913) works on The Merchant of Venice and King Lear. "Some Character-Types Met with in Psycho-Analytic Work" (1916) takes Lady Macbeth and Richard III. The Leonardo study (1910) applies the method to a painter's childhood memory. Romeo and Juliet passes through this corpus without receiving an essay, a case, or a sustained paragraph. The school that reads the play psychoanalytically is therefore not transmitting a founder's verdict. It is building with a founder's instruments, and the distinction matters for every claim that follows: the instruments can be inspected in Freud's own pages, dated and defined, while their application to Verona is the work of later hands, answerable to the text in a way an inherited verdict would not be.

The instruments themselves are specific, and the school's history is the history of their successive arrival. From The Interpretation of Dreams (1900) came the dream-work: the doctrine that a dream is a disguised fulfilment of a wish, assembled by condensation and displacement out of the day's residues and the dreamer's buried life. From the short paper "Family Romances" (1909) came the child's fantasy of exchanging its actual parents for grander ones, the first sketch of the family as a theatre of desire rather than a fact of birth. From the three Contributions to the Psychology of Love (1910 to 1917) came the school's account of how adult desire is split and priced: the second of them, "On the Universal Tendency to Debasement in the Sphere of Love" (1912), argues that the affectionate and sensual currents of love habitually fail to fuse, and contains the sentence on which this play's whole obstacle economy turns, examined in its own section below. "On Narcissism" (1914) supplied the beloved as mirror. "Mourning and Melancholia" (1917) supplied a mechanism for suicide. "The Uncanny" (1919) supplied the double and the return of what was buried. Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920) supplied the repetition compulsion and the death drive. Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego (1921) supplied the overvaluation of the loved object, the suspension of criticism that being in love performs. A school that can date its tools this precisely has no excuse for vagueness about which tool is doing which work, and the readings below are assigned to their instruments accordingly.

### What does the school mean when it calls a motive unconscious?

A motive is called unconscious when the person acting on it cannot state it, would disown it if it were stated, and yet organises conduct around it with perfect consistency. The school's claim is never that a hidden thought exists somewhere like a letter in a drawer.

The claim is that the pattern of choices, repetitions, and intensities in a life, or in a character's speeches, is intelligible only if a wish the speaker cannot avow is steering them, and that the steering shows in slips, dreams, jokes, and the disproportion between an occasion and the feeling it releases.

That disproportion is the school's point of entry into this play, and it answers a documented complaint from outside the school. Marjorie Cox, opening her clinical reading of the play in the Psychoanalytic Review in 1976, records the standing objection that Romeo and Juliet fails as tragedy because its outcome flows not from the characters' faults but from "fortuitous happenings," a missed letter, a mistimed waking, a plague-quarantined messenger. Cox's answer, which the whole school inherits, is that the accidents are the surface and the psychologic is underneath: read the lovers' choices with clinical attention and the catastrophe acquires an inner logic that the calendar of accidents conceals. Readers who prefer the accidents left uncaused, as the play's true subject, have their own full account in the tragedy read through [fortune, chance, and accident](/2024/03/22/fortune-chance-accident-analysis/), and the quarrel between that reading and this one is the quarrel over whether anything in Verona is accidental at all. The psychoanalytic wager is that nothing is: that a young man who falls totally in love twice, both times with daughters of the house his house hates, both times within the economy of prohibition, is not experiencing coincidences but repeating a pattern, and that repetition is the school's home ground.

The wager has a price, which the school's critics name precisely and which this article keeps in view. A dramatic character has no childhood except the one a reader constructs, no free association except the speeches a playwright wrote for other purposes, and no resistance that is not also a plot requirement. The clinical readings that follow are therefore strongest exactly where their evidence is the play's language rather than an invented biography, and weakest where they supply the biography and then discover it. That line, between the text's pattern and the reader's construction, is where the verdicts below are drawn.

## The Play Thinks in Dreams Before the Theory Arrives

No play of its decade gives a theory of dreaming more stage time than this one, and the psychoanalytic reading begins where the play does: with the proposition that dreams are legible. Mercutio's Queen Mab speech (1.4.53-94) is usually read as a decorative set piece, a fairy miniature painted to delay the walk to the Capulets' feast. Attend to its structure and it is a catalogue with a thesis. Mab, "the fairies' midwife," gallops nightly through sleepers' brains and delivers each sleeper the dream of his own appetite: courtiers dream "of curtsies straight," lawyers "of fees," a lady "of kisses," a soldier "of ambuscadoes, Spanish blades... and then drums in his ear." The speech assumes, three centuries before The Interpretation of Dreams, that a dream is not a random phantasm but a wish wearing a costume, sorted by the dreamer's station and hunger. Freud's dream-book argues that the dream is the disguised fulfilment of a repressed wish, its manifest nonsense produced by a censorship that the dream-work evades. Mercutio's catalogue argues the daylight version of the same doctrine: dreams betray what the sleeper wants, and a skilled reader can sort the wanting by trade. The play even supplies its own sceptic inside the speech's frame, since Mercutio ends by conceding that dreams "are the children of an idle brain, / Begot of nothing but vain fantasy" (1.4.96-97), and Romeo stops the performance with a clinician's rebuke: "Thou talk'st of nothing" (1.4.95). The debate the school would later stage between interpretation and dismissal is already staged here, in the street, before the lovers meet.

Romeo's own dreams carry the heavier charge, because the play gives him two and makes the second one prophetic in reverse. In 1.4 he refuses the feast on the strength of an unnamed foreboding, "my mind misgives / Some consequence yet hanging in the stars / Shall bitterly begin his fearful date / With this night's revels and expire the term / Of a despised life closed in my breast / By some vile forfeit of untimely death" (1.4.106-111). The school fastens on the economy of that speech: the fear is framed as astrology, but its content is a wish's negative, a life already experienced as "despised" and enclosed, awaiting an occasion to expire. In Mantua the dream returns with its sign reversed. "I dreamt my lady came and found me dead, / Strange dream that gives a dead man leave to think! / And breathed such life with kisses in my lips / That I revived and was an emperor" (5.1.6-9). As wish-fulfilment the dream is transparent to the point of embarrassment: it grants Romeo the two things his waking life cannot hold together, union with Juliet and death, and it grants them in the order that makes death the doorway rather than the price. He is dead, she comes to him, her kisses resurrect him into an emperor. The dream's logic is the tomb scene's logic rehearsed in advance, with the ending's despair edited out. When Balthasar rides in moments later with the report that Juliet is dead, the school's point is not that the dream predicted the news. The point is that Romeo receives the news into a mind the dream has already furnished: the possibility of joining her in death has been dreamed as bliss before it is proposed as fact, and his response, "Is it e'en so? Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1.24), has the speed of a decision taken earlier, in sleep.

### What does a dream do in this play that a soliloquy cannot?

A soliloquy tells the audience what a character thinks; a dream shows what a character wants without the character's permission or knowledge. In this play the distinction is load-bearing, because the lovers' most consequential admissions are the ones they never make awake: Romeo's Mantua dream confesses, in code, the wish his waking speeches call a fear.

Romeo can say, in soliloquy, that he fears the night's consequences; only the dream can admit that the feared consequence, to be found dead by his lady and kissed, is also the desired one. The dream is the one speech in the tragedy that nobody in the play interprets, and that the audience is left to interpret alone.

Juliet's corresponding text is not a dream but a waking rehearsal of one, the potion soliloquy of 4.3, and the school reads it as the return of everything the Friar's plan required her to leave unthought. Alone with the vial, she enumerates the fears in ascending order: that the mixture will not work at all, that it is poison, that she will wake before Romeo comes "to redeem me," that she will suffocate in the vault "where bloody Tybalt, yet but green in earth, / Lies festering in his shroud," that she will go mad among "the horrible conceit of death and night" and dash out her brains with a kinsman's bone. Each fear is a thought the plan's success depends on her not having, arriving in the forty lines before she drinks. The school's term for this is the return of the repressed, and the scene is its cleanest exhibit in the play, because the text marks the mechanism itself: she masters the cascade not by answering it but by converting it back into desire, raising the vial to an absent person: "Romeo, Romeo, Romeo! Here's drink. I drink to thee" (4.3.57-58). The toast is the wish reasserting command over the fear. Between Mercutio's catalogue, Romeo's two dreams, and Juliet's waking nightmare, the play distributes a complete psychology of the night across three speakers, and the psychoanalytic reading's first claim is simply that it did not invent this material. It inherited a text already fluent in the language the school would later formalise, which is why the anachronism question, when it comes, cannot be settled by pointing out that the play came first. The play's priority is the school's problem and its evidence at once.

## The Family Is the First Text the Lovers Read

Every psychoanalytic reading of the play starts one scene earlier than the romance does, in the households. Before Romeo and Juliet are lovers they are children: a son whose parents discuss his melancholy with his cousin because they cannot approach him themselves (1.1), and a daughter whose mother must summon a nurse into the room before she can open the subject of marriage (1.3). The school's second founding observation is that the play's desires are all routed through this prior structure. Nobody in Verona wants anything except as a member of a house, and the two protagonists meet as the feud's children before they meet as persons. When Juliet learns the identity of the young man she has kissed, her sentence fuses the two registers in a single breath: "My only love sprung from my only hate! / Too early seen unknown, and known too late!" (1.5.137-138). The love is hers; the hate is inherited, absorbed with the name before she had any say in either. Psychoanalysis calls that prior absorption the ground on which all later desire is built, and the play, unusually, dramatises the absorption itself.

Freud's "Family Romances" (1909) describes the child's fantasy life about its parents: the growing child, disappointed in the father and mother it has, imagines itself the offspring of grander people, exalting the father in memory and replacing both parents in daydreams. The fantasy's function is emancipation: it loosens the parents' authority by making their parenthood doubtful. The lovers of Verona run the romance in reverse, and the reversal is the school's favourite exhibit in the play. Romeo and Juliet do not fantasise grander parents. They fantasise no parents at all, or rather no names: "Deny thy father and refuse thy name; / Or, if thou wilt not, be but sworn my love, / And I'll no longer be a Capulet" (2.2.34-36). Juliet's offer is the family romance turned negative, emancipation not by upgrading the father but by resigning from him. Romeo accepts the terms at once: "Call me but love, and I'll be new baptized; / Henceforth I never will be Romeo" (2.2.50-51). The school reads the orchard scene as a joint attempt to exit the family system by an act of renaming, and reads the rest of the play as the system's answer. The names cannot be resigned from, because the names are not labels on the lovers but the medium they live in: the invitation list that admits Romeo to the feast is a Capulet document, the law that banishes him speaks to a Montague, and the letter that might have saved him is addressed through the feud's geography. The fantasy of the name shed is the lovers' family romance; its failure is their tragedy restated in the school's grammar.

### What is a family romance, and how do the lovers reverse it?

In Freud's 1909 paper, the family romance is the child's daydream of being born to grander parents, a fantasy that loosens the real parents' authority by making their claim doubtful. The lovers reverse the direction. Neither imagines a nobler father; each imagines no father, no house, no name at all.

Their emancipation fantasy is subtraction rather than promotion, and the play tests it to destruction: the world of Verona recognises persons only by their houses, so a lover without a name is a lover without a world to live in.

The parents the play actually stages support the reading at every point where it can be checked. Capulet disposes of his daughter in the grammar of property transfer, and says so to her face when she refuses the match he has made: "An you be mine, I'll give you to my friend; / An you be not, hang, beg, starve, die in the streets" (3.5.191-192). The conditional is the whole paternal system in two lines: daughterhood is a tenure, held on condition of obedience, revocable at the father's word. Lady Capulet, in 1.3, performs the complementary office, presenting Paris to her daughter as a book to be read for its cover and its value: "Read o'er the volume of young Paris' face," a text whose worth lies in "all that he doth possess." The mother here is the father's broker, and the school notes the datum that makes her one: she was herself married at Juliet's age, "I was your mother much upon these years / That you are now a maid" (1.3.74-75). Lady Capulet is not an individual villain of the piece but a graduate of the system she administers, handing her daughter the identical transaction she once underwent. That repetition, a mother reproducing on her daughter the disposal that was practised on her, is the kind of pattern the school exists to catch: nobody chooses it, everybody transmits it, and its cause is nowhere stated because it is nowhere remembered as a choice.

Against the two households the lovers assemble a substitute family, and its composition is exact. Juliet's effective mother is the Nurse, who suckled her, whose own daughter Susan died in infancy, whose memory of Juliet's childhood is bodily and calendrical down to the earthquake and the weaning: the play gives the nursing, the substituted child, and the mourning in a single speech (1.3). Romeo's effective father is the Friar, who hears his confessions, chides his inconstancy, marries him, shelters him, and devises the plot of the feigned death. The school reads the Nurse and the Friar as parental images split off from the prohibiting parents: the indulgent mother who connives at the desire and the permissive father who solemnises it. The substitutes serve the wish rather than the law, which is why the lovers can proceed, and why the proceeding ends as it does. At the crisis each substitute fails in character. The Nurse, confronted with the father's rage, advises the bigamous surrender she had helped make unnecessary: "I think it best you married with the county" (3.5.217). The Friar, confronted with the tomb's evidence, confesses his fear and flees: "I dare no longer stay" (5.3.159). The substitute family enables the desire and abandons it, and the lovers die, in the school's account, of the same deprivation that organised their love: the parents they were given forbade it, and the parents they chose could not protect it.

### Why does the feud's forgotten cause matter so much to this school?

Because a hatred without a remembered cause is the social form of repression. The Prologue calls the feud an "ancient grudge" and no character in five acts ever states what it was about; the houses repeat the enmity the way a neurotic repeats a symptom, with full conviction and no memory of its origin.

For the school, that blank at the centre of Verona's public life is the datum that licenses the whole reading: what a city cannot remember, it re-enacts, and the lovers inherit the re-enactment as the element their desire must swim in.

Coppélia Kahn's "Coming of Age in Verona," first published in Modern Language Studies in the winter of 1977-78 and expanded in Man's Estate (1981), is the point where this family reading and psychoanalytic method fuse most influentially, and it is treated here at the length its ownership allows, since her article in this series carries her argument whole. Kahn reads the feud as the fathers' institution and the play as a rite of passage that goes wrong: Verona's sons are inducted into manhood through violence against the rival house, and Romeo's refusal of that induction, his effeminacy in the eyes of the code, is what the catastrophe punishes and completes. The clinical surveys of the school summarise her contribution as phallic violence impelled by a patriarchal social order. Her reading's psychoanalytic machinery, its account of what the fathers' swords mean to the sons who must take them up, gives the family section of this article its bridge to the feminist criticism of the play; its strain, conceded in her own article's hearing, is the point where the psychoanalytic flourish outruns the documentary text. What matters for the survey is her demonstration that the school's family grammar could organise evidence no earlier reading had organised: the fathers' near absence from their children's emotional lives, the mothers' brokerage, the sons' compulsory violence, all fall into one pattern under her hand.

## The Clinic Takes the Play: Adolescence, Metaphor, and the Depressive Romeo

Between Freud's instruments and the play stands the clinical literature proper, the readings produced by analysts and analytically trained critics who treated the lovers as case material. Its map, as the school's own surveys draw it, has four principal stations, and the disagreements among them are more instructive than their agreements. The first station is adolescence. Marjorie Cox's "Adolescent Process in Romeo and Juliet" (Psychoanalytic Review, 1976) argues that the play dramatises the adolescent task itself: the young person's need to find a love object outside the family, non-incestuous, neither parent nor sibling nor any figure the household supplies. Cox's word for the lovers' attempt is that it miscarries, and the miscarriage is structural. Each adolescent reaches outside the family and grasps, of all the persons in Verona, the one person the family's law most forbids, so that the exit from the household is attempted through the household's own defining enemy. Desire and defiance become indistinguishable, and every step out of the family is simultaneously a step against it, which is why the love cannot slow down: delay would give the family time to reassert the claim the love exists to escape. Katherine Dalsimer's chapter on the play in Female Adolescence: Psychoanalytic Reflections on Literature (1986) carries the station to Juliet, reading her story as the record of middle adolescence compressed into five days: the girl who answers her mother's marriage proposal with "It is an honour that I dream not of" (1.3.66) is, within the week, conducting a marriage, defying a father, and planning a feigned death, and Dalsimer's subject is the velocity of that passage rather than its romance. The full territory of Juliet's wanting, its language and its objects, is mapped in the series' study of [female desire in Romeo and Juliet](/2024/03/01/female-desire-romeo-and-juliet/); the clinic's contribution is to name the developmental clock the wanting runs on.

The second station is the sound of the language itself. Albert Rothenberg, writing in 1973, located the play's psychoanalytic evidence below the level of plot, in its figures: when Shakespeare wants to express wishes, fears, and anxieties that derive from the early stages of mental life, Rothenberg observed, he characteristically turns to the use of metaphor. The exhibit is Romeo's first great speech, the cascade of oxymora that answers the street brawl: "O brawling love! O loving hate! / O anything, of nothing first create! / O heavy lightness, serious vanity, / Misshapen chaos of well-seeming forms, / Feather of lead, bright smoke, cold fire, sick health, / Still-waking sleep, that is not what it is!" (1.1.176-181). Rothenberg reads the cascade as pre-oedipal in structure: feeling before the opposites have been separated, love and hate, lightness and weight, health and sickness held in single phrases because the mind producing them has not yet sorted them into stable objects. Against this stands Dalsimer's cooler verdict on the same lines, delivered from inside the same school: the oxymora are the Petrarchan convention doing its ordinary work, and "Shakespeare here is smiling at Romeo." The disagreement is genuine and this article adjudicates it. Dalsimer is right about the figures' availability: any sonneteer of the decade could mint them, and did. Rothenberg is right about their placement: Shakespeare puts the cascade in the mouth of a young man who has just passed a brawl between the two houses, at the exact moment when love and hate are about to become his biography, and the speech ends by reverting to the rejecting Rosaline, "This love feel I, that feel no love in this" (1.1.182). A convention explains the words; it does not explain the timing. The verdict is that the figures are borrowed and the charge is real, a combination the school meets again in the lovers' shared sonnet, and one that Belsey's neighbouring school would later make its own.

The third station is diagnosis. A clinical portrait published by Shapiro in 1964 reads Romeo as a depressive character structure: the melancholy of the opening scenes, the conviction of untimely death already fully formed in the "despised life closed in my breast" of 1.4, and the suicidal readiness that precedes any occasion for it, so that when the false report arrives in 5.1 the death it proposes has been rehearsed for four acts. Otto Kernberg's contribution, as the surveys transmit it, reads Romeo's naivety, his incapacity for aggression against Tybalt in 3.1, as neurotic inhibition: the aggression is not absent but barred, and it discharges all at once when Mercutio falls, in the killing Romeo immediately disowns as fortune's work. The fourth station is the method's limit, and it is documented by the school's own more recent practice. A clinical essay in PsyArt, the journal for the psychological study of the arts, constructs for Romeo a full childhood trauma out of his opening speeches, a repressed early rejection re-enacted in his choice of unavailable women, and argues the construction openly, conceding that the childhood must be built from the language since the text never mentions it. The construction is ingenious and unfalsifiable in equal measure, and it marks the boundary this article has drawn from the start: where the school reads the pattern of the speeches, it is answerable to the text; where it writes the biography the speeches are then said to confirm, it is answerable to nothing. Cox's adolescence, Dalsimer's Juliet, Rothenberg's metaphors, and Shapiro's depressive all stay, in their different degrees, on the evidence side of that line. The trauma construction steps over it, and the school's critics are entitled to the concession.

## Idealization: Rosaline, Juliet, and the Obstacle That Feeds the Flame

The fact about Romeo that every account of the play must somehow digest is the speed of the transfer. In 1.1 he is dying by inches for Rosaline, in a rhetoric of siege and starvation; in 1.5 he sees Juliet across a crowded room and the entire structure of feeling is dismantled and rebuilt inside a dozen lines: "Did my heart love till now? Forswear it, sight! / For I ne'er saw true beauty till this night" (1.5.52-53). Romantic accounts call this the recognition of the true beloved. The psychoanalytic school calls it the single most diagnostic event in the play, because a love that can change its object totally, instantly, and without residue is a love whose energy was never primarily attached to the first object. The Friar states the school's thesis in the play's own idiom, two scenes before any critic needed to: Romeo's love lies "not truly in their hearts, but in their eyes," and what he felt for Rosaline was "doting, not for loving" (2.3). The school's vocabulary for the Friar's observation is idealization and the fungibility of the object, and its documents are two of Freud's most careful papers.

Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego (1921) describes being in love as a state in which the object is overvalued: criticism of the beloved is suspended, the object's faults become invisible or lovable, and the beloved is installed in the place of the ego-ideal, the standard against which the lover measures and finds himself wanting. "On Narcissism" (1914) adds the mechanism of the choice itself: one of the paths to an object runs through the self, loving what one is, what one was, or what one would like to be. Read together, the two papers produce the school's Romeo. His beloved is whoever occupies the position of the ideal at a given moment, and the occupation is what matters, not the occupant: Rosaline held the post while she was unattainable, and lost it the moment a new candidate appeared whose attainability was framed by an even grander prohibition. The lovers' first exchange then becomes the school's set piece, since the shared sonnet of 1.5 is a mirror structure in verse: two speakers completing one fourteen-line form, each supplying the rhyme the other has set up, each describing the other in the vocabulary of pilgrims and saints until Juliet caps the exchange with the judgment "You kiss by th' book" (1.5.109). The narcissistic reading does not deny the scene's beauty. It explains the beauty's specific quality, the sense of two people recognising themselves in each other with unnatural completeness, as the signature of a love in which each partner is the other's idealised double. That is why the play's love poetry is so full of exchanged identities, of names offered up, eyes exchanged, and deaths anticipated in common: the couple is written, from its first sonnet, as a single system with two mouths.

The 1912 debasement paper sharpens the account at its most counterintuitive point. Freud's subject there is the habitual split in civilised love between the affectionate current, directed at respected and therefore untouchable objects, and the sensual current, which can flow only toward objects who do not command respect. Rosaline is the split made visible: worshipped precisely because she has "forsworn to love," wrapped in Diana's armour, she can be adored without desire ever reaching her, and Romeo's misery is the split experienced as weather. Juliet is the anomaly the paper predicts will be rare and overwhelming: an object in whom the two currents fuse, respectable and desired at once, a Capulet daughter and a willing bride. The school's claim is that the fusion is possible only because a third term holds the split at bay, and the third term is the feud. Juliet can be both idealised and possessed because possessing her is a transgression: the prohibition does the work that debasement does in Freud's case histories, keeping the sensual current licensed by danger while the affectionate current is licensed by her rank and innocence. Remove the feud from the play and, on this reading, the fusion collapses into one of its components, either a decorous courtship of the Paris kind or a liaison without the sacred charge. The play's own counterfactual is built into 1.3, where the approved match is presented first, in the approved way, and generates no poetry at all.

Then the obstacle itself. In the same 1912 paper Freud pauses over a general law of erotic economy, and the sentence deserves the prominence the school has given it: "An obstacle is required in order to heighten libido; and where natural resistances to satisfaction have not been sufficient men have at all times erected conventional ones so as to be able to enjoy love." Verona is that sentence built as a city. The feud is a conventional obstacle in Freud's exact sense, an artificial resistance maintained by general consent across generations, whose manifest function is honour and whose latent function, on the school's reading, is the pricing of desire. Every meeting of the lovers is a trespass with a tariff: the orchard wall, the enemy's house, the rope ladder, the banishment, the tomb's monument of Capulets. The school notes what the romantic account cannot easily explain, that the lovers never once seek the available remedy of patience. Friar Laurence's counsel, "Wisely and slow. They stumble that run fast" (2.3.94), is the reality principle speaking in proverbs, and the play's haste, its four days from meeting to tomb, is the pleasure principle declining the advice. Haste in this reading is not a character flaw stapled to two teenagers. It is the desire's own logic: a love constituted by its obstacle experiences delay not as prudence but as extinction, because delay is the condition in which obstacles do their ordinary work of cooling what they were erected to heat.

## Eros Bound to Thanatos: The Death Drive at Work in the Tomb

If the obstacle explains the passion's intensity, the death drive explains its direction, and the school's most consequential claim is that the two explanations are one. The evidence is distributed through the play with a regularity that no reading has ever denied, only interpreted differently. The Prologue marks the love as death's property before it exists. Juliet's first speech about Romeo, on learning his name, reaches for the grave as the marriage's alternative and equal: "If he be married, / My grave is like to be my wedding bed" (1.5.134-135). The Friar, marrying them, speaks the fusion as a warning: "These violent delights have violent ends / And in their triumph die, like fire and powder, / Which, as they kiss, consume" (2.6.9-11). Capulet, over his daughter's feigned corpse, names the substitution outright: "Death is my son-in-law, Death is my heir" (4.5.38). Romeo, in the tomb, imagines his rival for Juliet as Death itself, keeping her "in dark to be his paramour" (5.3.103-105), and answers the rivalry by joining her in the rival's element: "Here, here will I remain / With worms that are thy chambermaids" (5.3.108-109). A concordance of the play's love language is a concordance of its death language, and the school's question is what kind of desire speaks this way from its first hour, in delight and not only in despair.

Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920) is the instrument the school brings to that question. Freud's late speculation proposes that beneath the pleasure principle, which seeks discharge and satisfaction, lies a deeper tendency: the drives' pressure toward the restoration of an earlier state, ultimately the stillness of the inorganic, together with the repetition compulsion, the mind's return to unmastered experiences in the attempt to bind them. The school's application to Verona runs on both tracks. The feud is the repetition compulsion at civic scale, a city re-enacting an injury it cannot remember in the hope, never fulfilled, of mastering it. The love is the death drive wearing the mask of its opposite: a desire whose fulfilment is imagined, from the balcony scene onward, as a state beyond disturbance, beyond names, houses, mornings, and separations, a stillness the lovers' language keeps calling rest, sleep, and grave because those are the words the culture provides for the end of wanting. Norman Rabkin's Shakespeare and the Common Understanding (1967) had already made a version of this argument in English criticism before the vocabulary settled: Rabkin treats Romeo's oxymora not as rhetorical furniture but as "a definition of [Romeo's] life," a life in which love and death are not two themes in dialogue but one appetite with two names. The school's later readers, armed with the 1920 paper, recognised Rabkin's definition as the death-drive reading in embryo, and the oxymoron cascade of 1.1, re-read in this light, is the play's thesis stated by its least reliable speaker at the moment he least understands it.

### Why do the lovers keep naming death inside their love speeches?

Because, on this reading, the naming is not decoration or premonition but accuracy. The lovers' desire aims at a union that Verona's world has no room for: unobserved, unnamed, unseparated, and permanent. Only one state in the play satisfies every term of that description, and the lovers' language keeps finding it, in the orchard as faithfully as in the tomb.

Juliet wishes Romeo cut into little stars; Romeo swears by the inconstant moon and is corrected; both reach, again and again, for images of a love removed from time, change, and witness. The death vocabulary is the sound of the desire describing its own destination in the only terms available to it.

"Mourning and Melancholia" (1917) supplies the tomb scene's mechanism, and it is the school's most precise piece of fitting. Freud's melancholic, unable to surrender a lost object, withdraws the object into the ego and turns upon himself the reproaches and hatred that belong to the loss; the paper's terrible conclusion is that the ego can kill itself only when it can treat itself as an object, directing against itself the hostility intended for the abandoning beloved. Romeo's suicide is the mechanism staged with an exactness that seems designed for it. He dies for an object that is not lost: Juliet lies before him alive, breathing, her colour returning, and he reads the evidence of life as death's mockery, "Why art thou yet so fair? Shall I believe / That unsubstantial death is amorous" (5.3.101-103). The melancholic structure is literalised. What Romeo cannot surrender is not Juliet but the image of Juliet dead, the version of the story in which his failure to arrive in time is complete and punishable, and he executes the punishment on the self that has absorbed the blame. "Thus with a kiss I die" (5.3.120) is the identification completed: the kiss that the Mantua dream imagined as resurrection is performed as the seal of the sentence. Juliet wakes into the mechanism's second half. Finding the object actually lost now, she refuses the world's offer of a future without it, declines the Friar's rescue, and makes her own body the object's resting place: "O happy dagger! / This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die" (5.3.169-170). The school's word for the tomb scene's genre is the liebestod, love-death, the consummation the world forbade achieved in the only venue the feud cannot police. The play's treatment of Death as a person, a bridegroom and heir who courts where Paris failed, is traced in full in the series' account of [Death personified as a character in the tragedy](/2024/05/24/death-personified-romeo-and-juliet/); the psychoanalytic contribution is to say why the personification persuades: Death is the one suitor whose suit the lovers' own desire has been seconding from the first scene.

## The Name, the Father, and the Letter That Never Arrives

The French succession to Freud reorganised the school around language, and its Shakespeare must be introduced with the same honesty applied to the founder: Jacques Lacan's sustained reading of a Shakespeare play is of Hamlet, in the seminar of 1958-59 published as Desire and Its Interpretation. For Romeo and Juliet the Lacanian work was done by his readers, and its centre of gravity is the scene the whole tradition keeps returning to, the orchard exchange of 2.2, now read as a seminar on the signifier. Juliet's question is grammatical before it is romantic: "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" asks not where he is but why he is called what he is called, and her argument that follows is a small treatise on the arbitrariness of the sign. "What's in a name? That which we call a rose / By any other word would smell as sweet" (2.2.43-44). The name Montague is "nor hand, nor foot, / Nor arm, nor face, nor any other part / Belonging to a man" (2.2.41-42). Her proposal is that the lovers step out of the naming system altogether: he will refuse the name, she will no longer be a Capulet, and desire will operate on persons rather than on the words that file them. Lacan's vocabulary gives the proposal its structural description. The name is the father's bequest, the point where the law enters language; to be named is to be assigned a place in a system of kinship, alliance, and prohibition that precedes any wish of one's own. The lovers' project, in this grammar, is a project to love outside the law of the name, and the play is the demonstration of the project's impossibility, conducted with an exactness that makes the scene the station's founding text.

Catherine Belsey's essay "The Name of the Rose in Romeo and Juliet," published in The Yearbook of English Studies in 1993, is the reading that carried this station into the mainstream of the play's criticism, and it is engaged here in the single passage its ownership in this series prescribes, since her article carries the argument whole. Belsey's verdict on the orchard fantasy is austere: the name is not at the disposal of the subject who bears it. A name is not a coat to be doffed, as Romeo imagines when he offers to have the word cut from his body; it is the position from which he is intelligible to everyone else in Verona, including the woman asking him to surrender it, who found him, desired him, and located him in the dark by means of it. Her reading's force for the survey is that it converts the orchard scene from the play's most romantic passage into its most rigorous one: the lovers state the theory of the name's arbitrariness perfectly, and the plot then refutes them with instruments that are all made of names, the guest list that admits Romeo under a mask, the proclamation that banishes him by name to Mantua, the marriage register that makes Juliet a wife while the world arranges her as a bride.

The letter completes the station. Lacan's seminar on Poe's "The Purloined Letter," delivered in 1956 and placed at the head of the Écrits, made the itinerary of a letter the emblem of how a signifier determines the subjects it passes among, and its maxim, that a letter always arrives at its destination, became the school's shorthand for the signifier's reliability. Verona supplies the counterexample that Lacanian readers of the play cannot leave alone. Friar John's account of the undelivered letter (5.2) is a chain of pure contingency: a companion sought, a plague suspected, doors sealed by the searchers of the town, the letter returned unopened to the Friar's cell. Against Lacan's maxim the play sets a letter that does not arrive, and a destination reached instead by the wrong communication, Balthasar's eyewitness report, accurate in every detail except the one that matters. The station's reading of the ending follows from that substitution. The plot's machinery of catastrophe is linguistic before it is physical: what kills the lovers is not poison or steel, which are only the instruments, but a message system in which the true letter miscarries and the false report travels at speed. Desire in this play is repeatedly entrusted to carriers, the Nurse with her fan and her delays, Friar John with his sealed doors, Balthasar with his post horses, and every carrier bends the message. The Lacanian station's claim is that this is not a flaw in Shakespeare's plotting, to be apologised for by editors, but the tragedy's subject stated in its mechanics: between the wish and its object there always stands a medium, and the medium is where the law lives.

### Why does a failed letter, on this reading, matter more than the potion?

The potion is a device of the body, and it works perfectly: Juliet sleeps her forty-two hours and wakes on schedule. The letter is a device of language, and it alone fails. For the school of Lacan, that distribution of failure is the tragedy's thesis in miniature.

Bodies in this play do what they are told; signs do not. The lovers are not defeated by the physics of poison and steel but by the postal system of the symbolic order, the network of names, messages, and proclamations that decides who may know what, and when, and at whose door the knowledge stops.

## Kristeva and the Economy of Love-Hatred

Julia Kristeva's Tales of Love, published in French in 1983 and in English in 1987, contains the school's most ambitious single chapter on the play, "Romeo and Juliet: Love-Hatred in the Couple," and its argument reorganises everything the earlier stations had separated. The book's declared programme is an inquiry into narcissism and idealization in the formation of the love object, conducted under the sign of a term Kristeva coins for the purpose: love/hate, the fusion she argues is not a pathology of loving but its constitution. Her reading of the play begins from the datum the feud articles in this series establish from the historical side, that the lovers' passion and the houses' hatred are contemporaneous, proportionate, and inseparable, and proposes the mechanism that joins them. The hatred between the houses is not merely the love's obstacle, in the manner of the 1912 paper's erected resistance. It is the love's drainage system. A couple's intimacy generates its own ambivalence, its own quota of aggression toward the beloved who frustrates, delays, and differs; the feud gives that aggression a licensed external address. Romeo and Juliet can keep their image of each other immaculate because Verona supplies a sanctioned object for every dark feeling the passion produces: whatever is hateful in the situation belongs to the other house, to Tybalt, to the fathers, to the stars. Kristeva's summary of the mechanism, as the school's surveys transmit it, is that intrapsychic hatred is dissipated by the feud, and the dissipation enhances the passion. The purer the hatred outside, the purer the love inside; the couple's famous absoluteness is an artefact of the feud's bookkeeping.

The reading explains, as the obstacle model alone does not, the datum of the double object-choice that opened the idealization section. Romeo's two loves are both Capulets: Rosaline, the unseen niece of the house, and Juliet, its daughter. On Kristeva's account the repetition is the signature of the mechanism rather than a coincidence of the marriage market. A desire formed inside the feud's economy does not merely tolerate the enemy's house as its object's address; it requires the address, because the beloved must be someone across the line for the hatred to have somewhere to go and the love to stay clean. "My only love sprung from my only hate" is, in her reading, not the heroine's paradox but her diagnosis, the one moment where the system states itself in the first person. The oxymoron cascade of 1.1 receives its third interpretation of this article and its most economical: brawling love and loving hate are not symptoms of Romeo's immaturity or definitions of his life in the abstract, but the precise verbal form of an affect that has not yet found the feud's drainage and so sloshes, undischarged, between its poles. Once the feud provides the channels, the same affects run clear, and the poetry of the balcony scene is famously lucid where the street speech was turbid. The school's internal history is visible in that progression: Freud's obstacle prices the desire, Rabkin's definition names its fusion, Kristeva's mechanism explains the fusion's plumbing.

The maternal stratum of Kristeva's reading is carried by the chapter's place in her book, where it stands beside "Stabat Mater," her meditation on the mother, and by the play's own nursing economy, which the family section above established: a heroine suckled by a servant whose own child died, mothered at a distance by a graduate of the marriage system, and buried, at the last, in a vault of her father's house among its dead. Rothenberg's pre-oedipal stratum and Kristeva's converge here from different directions: beneath the oedipal drama of fathers and names lies an earlier economy of feeding, substitution, and loss, in which Juliet is a replacement child before she is a bride, and the Nurse's grief for Susan is the play's buried first tragedy of love and death. The adjudication this article owes the school is therefore not a choice between its stations but an ordering of them. Cox explains the lovers' ages, Freud's papers explain their intensity, Lacan's readers explain their names, and Kristeva explains their purity, the feature of the passion that every reader notices and no other mechanism reaches: its freedom from the ambivalence that marks every other attachment in the play, including the Nurse's, the Friar's, and the parents'. A love that clean, in a world that dirty, is either a miracle or an arrangement. Kristeva shows the arrangement, and showing it is the school's strongest single performance on this play.

## The Uncanny Vault: Apparent Death, Doubles, and the Living Corpse

One further instrument from the founder's shelf earns its place before the rival and the prosecution arrive, because it governs the play's final movement. "The Uncanny" (1919) defines its subject as that species of the frightening which leads back to what was once familiar and has been repressed: the double, the repetition of the same features or fates, the inanimate thing that may be alive, the living thing that may be a corpse. The essay's own inventory reads like a plot summary of the tragedy's second half. A woman is laid in a tomb who is not dead. A man dreams that his lady finds his corpse and kisses it alive, then rides to make the dream's first half true. Two houses, indistinguishable in dignity, mirror each other's hatreds, funerals, and losses until the Prince can punish them only in the plural. A statue of gold is promised to each dead child by the other's father, doubling the bodies in metal. Freud, following Otto Rank's study of the double, argues that the double begins as a defence against death, an assurance of survival, and becomes in time its harbinger, the thing whose appearance announces that the self is to be extinguished. The Capulet vault is the uncanny's architecture: a familiar place, the family's own burial chamber, made strange by containing a living sleeper among the festering dead, and Juliet's terror in 4.3 is precisely the terror of the category error, of waking "in the horrible conceit of death and night" among ancestors who may not stay inanimate. The school's point about the ending's peculiar power, its capacity to disturb beyond the pathos of two deaths, rests here: the tomb scene violates the boundary between the living and the dead in both directions at once, a corpse who breathes and a lover who elects to be a corpse, and the audience's response is the uncanny response the 1919 essay anatomises, a shudder older than any interpretation. The instrument also disciplines the school's other claims. What is repressed in Verona returns, the essay's law states, in the form in which it was buried: the feud's forgotten grievance returns as compulsory re-enactment, the lovers' buried death-wish returns as a dream of resurrection, and Juliet's unspoken fears return as a soliloquy in the hour before she drinks. The uncanny is the school's theory of the play's form, the reason its meanings arrive doubled, reversed, and too late.

## The Rival at the Gate: Girard's Triangle Against Freud's Drive

The psychoanalytic school does not hold the field of desire theory alone, and its most serious rival was built partly in opposition to it. René Girard's mimetic theory, set out in Deceit, Desire, and the Novel (1961) and applied to Shakespeare in A Theatre of Envy (1991), denies the school's founding premise. For Freud, desire is a drive: it originates in the subject, fastens on objects by the logic of the subject's buried history, and the object, however exalted, is in principle replaceable, as Romeo's transfer from Rosaline to Juliet seems to prove. For Girard, desire is triangular: it originates in imitation, the subject desiring what a model or mediator desires, and the object is precious because the mediator's wanting has marked it. Girard's campaign against Freud runs through his work from the first book onward, where the Freudian object is dismissed as a screen for the mediator's prestige. The feud, on his account, is the triangle institutionalised: two houses desiring against each other until the desire has no object left but the rivalry itself. The full account of that reading, and the documentary oddity that Girard's projected essay on this play was omitted from A Theatre of Envy, leaving only his verdict that the play is full of mimetic satire, belongs to [René Girard's account of mimetic desire and the feud](/2027/01/15/rene-girard-mimetic-desire-feud/); what this article owes is the adjudication between the two theories of desire, since they cannot both be right about the lovers, and the play provides the evidence to judge them.

On the feud itself, Girard wins, and the psychoanalytic school should concede the territory cleanly. The forgotten cause, which the family section above read as repression, fits the mimetic account with less machinery: the houses need no buried grievance because the rivalry is self-feeding, each side's violence certifying the other's, and the doubling the uncanny section traced is, for Girard, not a symptom but the mechanism itself. On the transfer from Rosaline to Juliet, the evidence splits, and the split is instructive. The Freudian account predicts the transfer's totality and speed: the drive's energy withdraws from one object and invests another entire, "Did my heart love till now?" Girard's account predicts the objects' addresses: both women are Capulets, marked by the enemy house's prohibition, which functions in his system as the supreme mediator's mark, the obstacle that certifies value. The play's chronology, however, contains a datum the mimetic account handles awkwardly. Romeo's transfer is instantaneous and occurs at a distance, across a torchlit room, in a single look, before any mediator can intervene between the look and the investment: Tybalt's recognition and rage follow the transfer and cannot have caused it. The Freudian account, for which the object's identity is secondary to the position it occupies, absorbs the instant without strain. The verdict on the switch therefore goes to Freud, by the margin of one crowded scene. On the tomb, the verdict also goes to Freud, and by more. Girard's sacrificial mechanism explains what the lovers' deaths accomplish for Verona, the reconciliation of the doubles over the bodies, with unmatched economy. It says nothing about what the deaths are for the lovers, the experienced consummation that the liebestod section documented speech by speech, the kiss that seals, the sheath that receives the dagger. A theory of desire that cannot enter the tomb's interior, where the play's meaning concentrates, is a theory of the feud with the lovers left outside it. The school's final ordering is therefore a partition rather than a victory: Girard owns the city, Freud owns the couple, and the play is the record of what happens when the couple's economy and the city's economy are run, fatally, on the same streets.

## The Case Against, Part One: The Play Is Not a Patient

The objections to the school begin before Freud, in the resistance to the habit of mind psychoanalysis inherited. L. C. Knights's essay "How Many Children Had Lady Macbeth?" (1933) mocked the character criticism of the Bradleyan era for treating dramatic persons as acquaintances with offstage lives, biographies to be completed and motives to be diagnosed, when the plays supply nothing but words arranged for theatrical effect. The mockery transfers to the psychoanalytic school with interest, because the school's method is the character method with a clinic attached: it completes the biography in the one place the text is most silent, the childhood, and then reads the speeches as the adult patient's associations. The objection is not that the readings are fanciful. It is that they answer a question the object cannot pose. Romeo has no unconscious, the objection runs, because Romeo has no mind; he has lines, and the lines were written to be spoken in a playhouse by a boy apprentice, in a fiction adapted from an Italian novella through an English poem, for effects their author calculated in public. To ask what Romeo repressed is to commit the category error of asking what a chess knight is thinking. The school's moderate practitioners have always half-conceded the point, which is why the strongest of them, Rothenberg on the metaphors, Cox on the adolescent process, Kristeva on the couple's economy, read structures of language and relation rather than a patient's interior. The weakest, the constructed childhoods, concede nothing and prove the objection's case.

The second objection is epistemic, and its most formidable modern prosecutor is Frederick Crews, whose The Memory Wars (1995) and Freud: The Making of an Illusion (2017) argue that psychoanalysis fails as a science because its confirmations are manufactured by its method: the patient's agreement is evidence, the patient's disagreement is resistance, and resistance is also evidence. Applied to literary study, the charge sharpens. A reading that discovers repression in Juliet's silence and repression in her eloquence, the death drive in the lovers' haste and the death drive in their delays, has insured itself against the text, and an insured reading can no longer lose, which means it can no longer win either. The school's defence, where it is honest, is Dalsimer's: to accept falsifying instances inside the work itself, the oxymora that are convention rather than symptom, the haste that is plot rather than drive, and to let the text vote against the theory in particular cases. This article has followed that discipline deliberately, staging the school's defeats alongside its victories, the letter that contradicts Lacan's maxim, the transfer that Girard's triangle cannot time. A psychoanalytic reading that cannot lose a scene is worth nothing; the readings that survive in this survey are the ones that can.

The third objection is the one the brief for this article singles out, the charge of anachronism, and it deserves its own hearing because it is the objection with a documented champion, a documented concession, and an editor's evidence to call.

## The Case Against, Part Two: The Anachronism Charge on Trial

Stephen Greenblatt's essay "Psychoanalysis and Renaissance Culture," published in 1986 and reprinted in Learning to Curse (1990), is the anachronism charge in its most formidable form, and it is engaged here at the length of a method essay rather than a reading of the play, a distinction the record requires, since the essay's subject is the school's fitness for an entire period. Greenblatt's verdicts are quotable because they were meant to be: applied to Renaissance culture, he argues, psychoanalysis can only be "belated," "anachronistic," "crippled," and "marginal," and Freud's own engagements with the period's art are "suggestive but deeply inadequate." His reasoning is structural. Psychoanalysis presupposes a subject who possesses an interior, continuous and self-owned, whose depths explain its surface; the Renaissance, on his account, produced identity differently, in networks of family, patronage, law, and confession, so that the analytic instrument measures a kind of self the period had not finished inventing. His summary sentence is the charge's canonical form: psychoanalysis is "the fulfillment and effacement of specifically Renaissance insights: psychoanalysis is, in more than one sense, the end of the Renaissance." An instrument that is the period's end cannot be the period's key; to read Verona through Vienna is to mistake the descendant for the ancestor.

Then the essay turns, and the turn is why it remains the charge's best form rather than merely its loudest. Greenblatt's central exhibit is the Martin Guerre case, the sixteenth-century imposture in which a man lived another man's life, with his wife and lands, for years, and he draws the conclusion his own argument has made necessary: if psychoanalysis was made possible by, among other things, the legal and literary proceedings of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, then its interpretive practice is not irrelevant to those proceedings, nor is it an anachronism. The concession is exact and limited. It does not license the clinic's free movement through the period's texts. It establishes a genealogy: the discourse that would become psychoanalysis descends in part from the period's own proceedings, its trials, confessions, and fictions of identity, among which the drama stands high. A descendant is not a native, but neither is a descendant a foreigner. The adjudication this article reaches on the anachronism charge follows Greenblatt's own structure. Against the school's historical pretensions, the charge stands: no psychoanalytic reading recovers what the play meant in 1595, because the meanings available in 1595 did not include an unconscious of Freud's design, and the lovers' inwardness was built from the period's own materials, humours, rhetoric, conscience, and the soul's anatomy, which the historicist school describes better. Against the school's instrumental use, the charge fails by its author's own concession: where a modern reader declares the instrument modern and asks what it discloses about the play's patterns for minds formed as ours are, the genealogy Greenblatt traces makes the instrument a legitimate heir cross-examining an ancestor's estate.

### Does this reading claim that Shakespeare anticipated Freud?

No, and the school is at its worst when its admirers say so. The claim inverts the documented direction of descent. Shakespeare's culture supplied materials, the divided self of the soliloquy, the legible dream, the confessed secret, out of which later centuries built the idea of an interior life that psychoanalysis would claim as its discovery.

The play did not anticipate the theory; the theory is one of the things the play's tradition eventually produced. That is why the anachronism question dissolves when the direction is kept straight: an heir is not an anachronism in the ancestor's house, provided the heir does not forge the will.

Harold Bloom supplies the literary form of the resistance, and his case has the peculiar strength that he cannot be dismissed as an enemy of Freud. Bloom's own masterwork of theory, The Anxiety of Influence (1973), is an Oedipal engine, poets struggling with precursor fathers in a family romance of literature; his Freud is the great modern theorist of the imagination's agon. Yet in Shakespeare: The Invention of the Human (1998), whose chapter on this play stands early in the volume, Bloom's verdict on the lovers refuses the clinic entirely: the play, he writes, is "the largest and most persuasive celebration of romantic love in Western literature." A celebration is not a case history. Bloom's objection is that the school's vocabulary converts the lovers' achievement into their affliction, reading as symptom what the play offers as the invention of a kind of personhood, mutual, articulate, and self-delighting, that Western literature had not possessed before. The adjudication splits the difference on a line Bloom himself draws. His objection is decisive against the school's tone whenever the tone is pity, the lovers as patients to be diagnosed from a height. It does not touch the school's mechanisms, since a celebration can still have an economy, and Bloom's own theory of poetry is proof that one can hold the Oedipal machinery and the celebration together, provided the machinery is made to serve the poem rather than the poem the machinery.

The editor's evidence closes the trial, because editors are the profession obliged to say what the text is before anyone says what it means. Edward Dowden, who edited Romeo and Juliet for the first Arden Shakespeare in 1900, is the apt witness, since he stands on both sides of the line the school would later draw. Dowden's Shakspere: A Critical Study of His Mind and Art (1875) had founded the biographical-psychological approach to the canon, dividing the career into periods of a mind's growth, in the workshop, in the world, out of the depths, on the heights: the author psychologised a generation before the characters were. Yet Dowden the editor works by collation, source, and stage history, establishing what Shakespeare read in Brooke and Painter, what the quartos print, where the text is corrupt. The two Dowdens are the trial in one career. The psychological impulse is old, honourable, and productive; the editorial discipline is what keeps it answerable, and the modern editions' introductions enact the settlement, treating the lovers' inwardness as a made thing, of rhetoric, convention, and theatrical occasion, into which depth-psychological vocabularies may be admitted as guests but not as landlords. That settlement is, in substance, the verdict this article has been approaching from the evidence side, and the primer that follows is built to honour it: every station in the table carries its objection in the same row as its claim.

## Haste, and the Unconscious That Takes No Account of Time

One instrument remains, and it answers the brief's standing question about the play's most obvious peculiarity, its speed. In the metapsychological paper "The Unconscious" (1915), Freud states as a clinical finding that the processes of the unconscious take no account of time: they are not ordered temporally, are not altered by the passage of time, and carry no date. Wishes in the unconscious do not age, queue, or learn sequence; a desire of childhood presses for discharge in the adult with its original force and no date stamp. The school reads the tragedy's famous compression, five days from first meeting to double suicide, as the dramatic form of that indifference to time. Verona above ground runs on clocks, and the play is full of them: the nine of the morning in 1.1, the count of Juliet's years to the day and hour, the forty-two hours of the potion measured like a prescription, the wedding moved from Thursday to Wednesday, the watch arriving at the tomb on schedule. The lovers run on the other time. Juliet's great speech of 3.2, "Gallop apace, you fiery-footed steeds," is a demand that the sun's horses hurry the night in, that time itself be whipped; her impatience is not a young person's ordinary eagerness but a refusal of interval as such, of the delay in which consequences live. Romeo matches her from the other side: banished in 3.3, he experiences the sentence as an eternity precisely because it is an interval, "More validity in the world's report than death" being no consolation to a desire that recognises only now and never. The school's formulation is that the lovers inhabit dream-time while the city inhabits clock-time, and the catastrophe is the collision of the two systems at the tomb, where the potion's forty-two hours, the letter's three days, and the watch's rounds all arrive correctly by the clock and wrongly by the wish. Accident, in this final refinement of Cox's answer, is what that indifference to time looks like when it is forced to move through a timed world: every mistiming in the plot is a point where the lovers' refusal of sequence meets a sequence that will not refuse them back. No other school makes the haste intelligible as a metaphysic rather than a character note, and the reading earns its place in the primer on that ground alone.

## Verdict: What the Couch Can and Cannot See

The evidence is in, and the verdict can be given in the parts the primer's rows suggest. On the passion's physics, the psychoanalytic school stands first among the play's interpreters, and the margin is not narrow. It alone explains, with mechanisms that can be checked scene by scene, why the love is total (the object installed as ego-ideal, criticism suspended), why it is instant (the position precedes the occupant, so the transfer costs nothing), why it feeds on its obstacle (the erected resistance of the 1912 law), why it refuses delay (the unconscious's refusal of sequence at war with Verona's clocks), and why it aims at the tomb (the death drive's signature in the play's own vocabulary from the Prologue forward). Kristeva's drainage mechanism completes the system by explaining the passion's purity, the one property the other mechanisms leave untouched. A reading that accounts for five properties with one connected theory has earned its place at the head of the table, and the rival partition confirms the award: Girard takes the city and leaves the couple, the historians take the occasion and leave the intensity, the formalists take the verse and leave the compulsion. Only the school attempts the whole phenomenon, and on the phenomenon it is right more often than it is wrong.

On the play as an object in history, the school stands last, and the concession must be as clean as the award. It cannot establish a text, date a composition, identify a source's pressure on a scene, or hear the play in the ears of its first audience, and where it forgets those limits it produces the constructed childhoods and insured interpretations that Knights mocked and Crews prosecuted. Its Freud is real but its Freud on this play is an honourable fiction of application, since the founder wrote on Hamlet and Lear and left Verona alone, and its Lacan is a readers' Lacan, since the seminar's Shakespeare was again Hamlet. The school also has a standing moral hazard the verdict should record: its vocabulary converts joy into symptom with suspicious ease, and Bloom's resistance, the insistence that the play is a celebration before it is a case, is the correct permanent check on that conversion. The lovers are not improved by being pitied, and a reading that leaves the reader sorrier for Romeo than Romeo is for himself has mistaken the direction of the play's energy.

The settlement the evidence supports is therefore jurisdictional. Within the jurisdiction of the passion, its structure, its economics, its strange purity and its deathward aim, psychoanalytic reading is the law of this play's criticism, subject to the falsifying discipline Dalsimer practised and this article has enforced. Outside that jurisdiction, in the territories of text, date, source, and first meaning, its writ does not run, and the editors and historians hold the ground. Used inside its borders with its anachronism confessed, the school does what the series thesis for this article promised: it restores to the famous passion the unconscious patterns that the face-value reading, the greeting-card reading, the balcony postcard, all conspire to hide. The lovers of Verona are not two innocents struck by weather. They are the heirs of two households' buried hatred, the graduates of a family system that disposed of children as assets, the dreamers of dreams that told the truth in code, and the pursuers of a union their world had priced at exactly one tomb. The school saw that first, and seeing it is no small thing. It is the difference between a love story and a tragedy.

## The Confessional Before the Couch: The Play's Own Institution of Interior Speech

The anachronism trial closed with a genealogy, and the genealogy has a scene. Greenblatt's concession was that psychoanalysis descends in part from the legal and literary proceedings of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries; the play contains, in working order, the period's own institution for the excavation of interior life, and the school's readers have not failed to notice that the lovers spend the tragedy's decisive hours inside it. Romeo goes to Friar Laurence's cell to confess, to be interpreted, and to be prescribed for, and he goes at every crisis: after the Rosaline disappointment, after the marriage, after the killing of Tybalt, when he lies on the cell floor in a collapse the Friar treats as a case, naming its parts like a diagnostician, "Art thou a man? Thy form cries out thou art; / Thy tears are womanish" (3.3.109-110). Juliet comes to the same cell in 4.1 with a knife and a suicidal resolve, and receives from the same practitioner the boldest prescription in the play, a simulated death and a staged resurrection, a cure by theatre. The confessional and the consulting room are not the same institution, and the school falsifies its own genealogy when it confuses them: the Friar absolves, where the analyst interprets; the Friar acts on the plot, where the analyst is forbidden the patient's life. But the structural rhyme is the reason the play receives psychoanalytic reading with so little friction compared with, say, a chronicle history. Its world already routes its deepest truths through a private speech spoken to a bound listener under a seal, already believes that what is said in the cell governs what happens in the street, and already stages the failure of that speech, the Friar's remedies all sound and all too slow, as the tragedy's mechanism. When the school's critics describe the play as proto-analytic they usually mean its dreams or its desires. The stronger claim is institutional: Shakespeare's Verona has a couch, upholstered as a confessional, and the lovers are on it from the second act. The anachronism, on this showing, is a matter of furniture rather than of soul.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the InsightCrunch psychoanalytic primer, and what pattern does it expose?

The InsightCrunch psychoanalytic primer is the table in this article that sets fifteen stations of psychoanalytic reading in single rows, each with its central claim, the scene that gives the claim its strongest evidence, and the objection standing against it. The pattern the rows expose, read in order, is the school's whole biography in miniature: a founder who left instruments but no reading of this play, a clinical generation that applied the instruments to adolescence, metaphor, and diagnosis, a linguistic generation that moved the inquiry into names and letters, a rival theory that partitions the field, and a standing trial for anachronism under which every claim must be filed with its objection attached. The primer's discipline is the pattern's point: no station in it is allowed to appear stronger than its best scene and its best answer to its critics permit, which is the form in which this article argues the school deserves to survive.

### Q: Did Sigmund Freud ever publish a reading of Romeo and Juliet?

No. Freud published no essay, case, or sustained discussion of Romeo and Juliet, and the psychoanalytic tradition on this play is the work of his successors using his instruments. His Shakespearean criticism is documented elsewhere: the Oedipus reading of Hamlet proposed in 1897 and printed in The Interpretation of Dreams in 1900, "The Theme of the Three Caskets" in 1913 on The Merchant of Venice and King Lear, the Leonardo study of 1910, and the character-types paper of 1916 on Lady Macbeth and Richard III. The absence is not a technicality. It means the school's claims about Verona cannot borrow the founder's authority and must be settled scene by scene against the text, which is how this article has treated them: the tools are Freud's, dated and inspectable in his papers from the dream-book of 1900 to Beyond the Pleasure Principle in 1920, and the applications are later work, answerable for itself.

### Q: How does Marjorie Cox read the lovers' adolescence?

Marjorie Cox reads the play, in "Adolescent Process in Romeo and Juliet" in the Psychoanalytic Review in 1976, as the drama of the adolescent task itself: the young person's developmental need to find a love object outside the family, neither a parent nor any figure the household supplies. Her verdict is that the lovers' attempt miscarries structurally, because each reaches outside the family and grasps the one person the family's law most completely forbids, a child of the enemy house. Exit and defiance become the same act, so the love cannot afford delay: every pause would let the household reassert the claim the escape exists to defeat. Cox opens her essay by answering the standing complaint that the tragedy runs on fortuitous happenings, arguing that the unconscious logic of the adolescent process supplies the necessity the accidents seem to lack.

### Q: What does Katherine Dalsimer find in Juliet's adolescence?

Katherine Dalsimer finds, in her chapter on the play in Female Adolescence: Psychoanalytic Reflections on Literature in 1986, the record of middle adolescence traversed at tragic speed: the girl who tells her mother that marriage is "an honour that I dream not of" is conducting a secret wedding, defying her father, and planning a feigned death within five days. Her subject is the velocity of that passage, the developmental distance covered between the obedient daughter of 1.3 and the solitary actor of the potion soliloquy. Dalsimer is also the school's internal sceptic in this survey: confronted with Romeo's cascade of oxymora, she declines to read the figures as symptoms and concludes that they are the Petrarchan convention at its ordinary work, adding that Shakespeare here is smiling at Romeo. Her caution is the discipline this article adopts, since a school that cannot tell convention from symptom will find symptoms everywhere.

### Q: Why does Freud's sentence about obstacles matter to the feud?

Freud's sentence, from "On the Universal Tendency to Debasement in the Sphere of Love" in 1912, states that an obstacle is required in order to heighten libido, and that where natural resistances have not been sufficient, human beings have erected conventional ones so as to be able to enjoy love. The sentence matters to the feud because it converts the feud from the love's misfortune into one of its conditions. Verona's ancient grudge is a conventional obstacle in Freud's exact sense, an artificial resistance maintained by general consent across generations, and the lovers' meetings are all trespasses priced by it: the orchard wall, the enemy's feast, the rope ladder, the banishment. On this reading the obstacle does not merely oppose the desire from outside. It is part of the desire's economy, which is why the lovers never pursue the available remedy of patience and why Friar Laurence's counsel of slowness is the one piece of advice the play's passion is structurally unable to take.

### Q: What is the death drive, and where does the reading find it in the tomb?

The death drive is the tendency Freud proposed in Beyond the Pleasure Principle in 1920, a pressure beneath the pleasure principle toward the restoration of an earlier, undisturbed state, visible in the compulsion to repeat unmastered experience and, at its limit, in the organism's aim at the stillness from which life departed. The reading finds it in the tomb as the scene's organising logic rather than its decoration. Romeo enters the vault imagining Death as a rival lover keeping Juliet "in dark to be his paramour," sets up his "everlasting rest" beside her, and performs the kiss of union as the act of dying. Juliet wakes and completes the identification, making her body the dagger's sheath. The tomb is the one place in Verona where the lovers are unobserved, unnamed, and unseparated, and the death-drive reading holds that this is not the tragedy's irony but its destination, announced in the Prologue's "death-marked love" and in Juliet's first-sight sentence about her grave and her wedding bed.

### Q: How does Freud's Mourning and Melancholia explain the lovers' suicides?

"Mourning and Melancholia," published in 1917, explains suicide as the ego's attack on itself: unable to surrender a lost object, the melancholic withdraws the object into the self and directs against the self the reproaches and hatred belonging to the loss, so that self-killing becomes possible only when the ego can treat itself as an object. The paper fits Romeo's death with uncommon precision. He dies for an object that is not lost, since Juliet lies before him alive, and what he cannot surrender is not the woman but the image of her dead and of himself as the failure who arrived too late; he executes that absorbed defendant with the poison, sealing the identification with a kiss. Juliet's waking reverses the mechanism into its completion: the object now is lost in fact, the world offers a future she declines, and the dagger makes her body the lost object's resting place, the sheath speech stating the melancholic logic in a single image.

### Q: What does idealization mean in the transfer from Rosaline to Juliet?

Idealization means the overvaluation of the loved object described in Freud's Group Psychology of 1921: criticism of the beloved is suspended, faults become invisible or endearing, and the object is installed in the place of the ego-ideal, the standard by which the lover judges and condemns himself. In the transfer from Rosaline to Juliet, idealization is what makes the transfer's totality intelligible. Romeo's feeling does not diminish and reattach; it evacuates one object entire and invests another entire within a single scene, "Did my heart love till now? Forswear it, sight!" The school's inference is that the energy was never the first object's property: it belongs to the position of the idealized beloved, which Rosaline occupied while unattainable and Juliet occupies from the moment of the feast. The Friar's diagnosis inside the play says the same in period language, that young men's love lies in their eyes rather than in their hearts, and that Romeo's first passion was doting rather than loving.

### Q: Why do Lacanian readers dwell on the name and the father in the orchard scene?

Lacanian readers dwell there because the orchard scene is a treatise on their central doctrine spoken by the characters themselves. Juliet's "wherefore art thou Romeo" asks why he bears the name he bears; her rose argument asserts that the name is no part of the man; her proposal, "Deny thy father and refuse thy name," identifies the name as the father's bequest, the point where law and kinship enter language. In the vocabulary of Lacan's school, a name assigns its bearer a place in a system of alliance and prohibition that precedes any wish, so the lovers' project of loving outside their names is a project of loving outside the law that makes them legible to Verona. The plot then refutes the project with instruments made of names: a guest list, a banishment proclamation, a marriage register. Catherine Belsey's 1993 essay on the rose speech gives the station its verdict, that the name is not at the disposal of the subject who bears it.

### Q: How does Julia Kristeva connect the lovers' passion to the feud's hatred?

Julia Kristeva connects them, in the chapter "Romeo and Juliet: Love-Hatred in the Couple" in Tales of Love, published in French in 1983 and in English in 1987, by treating the feud as the couple's emotional drainage system. Her coinage for the book's argument is love/hate: intimacy generates its own ambivalence, its own aggression toward the beloved who frustrates and differs, and a couple requires somewhere for that aggression to go. Verona supplies the address. Every dark feeling the passion produces can be discharged onto the sanctioned object, the other house, so the beloved's image stays immaculate and the love stays absolute. The school's surveys summarise her mechanism as intrapsychic hatred dissipated by the feud, thus enhancing passion. Her textual warrant is Juliet's own sentence, "My only love sprung from my only hate," and the datum that both of Romeo's loves are Capulets: the desire does not cross the line despite the hatred but by means of it.

### Q: What is the anachronism objection to psychoanalytic criticism of this play?

The anachronism objection holds that psychoanalytic reading imports into the play a model of the self that the play's period had not produced, and therefore cannot recover anything the play meant. Its strongest form is Stephen Greenblatt's essay "Psychoanalysis and Renaissance Culture" of 1986, which calls the method, applied to the period, belated, anachronistic, crippled, and marginal, and Freud's own attempts on Renaissance art "suggestive but deeply inadequate." The reasoning is that psychoanalysis presupposes a continuous, self-possessing interior whose depths explain conduct, while the Renaissance distributed identity across family, law, patronage, and confession. Supporting objections add that dramatic characters have no childhoods to excavate and that the method is insured against refutation, since disagreement can always be reclassified as resistance. This article's adjudication accepts the charge against the school's historical claims and rejects it against the school's declared instrumental use, on grounds Greenblatt's own essay supplies.

### Q: How does Stephen Greenblatt both press and withdraw the anachronism charge?

Greenblatt presses the charge in his 1986 essay by arguing that psychoanalysis is the fulfilment and effacement of specifically Renaissance insights, in more than one sense the end of the Renaissance, so that an instrument born at the period's end cannot serve as the period's key. He withdraws it, partially and precisely, at the essay's centre, in the discussion of the Martin Guerre imposture. If psychoanalysis was made possible by, among other things, the legal and literary proceedings of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, he concludes, then its interpretive practice is not irrelevant to those proceedings, nor is it an anachronism. The withdrawal does not acquit the school; it establishes a genealogy. A discourse descended partly from the period's own trials, confessions, and fictions of identity is a legitimate heir examining an ancestor's estate, provided it does not forge the will by claiming to recover original meanings. This article's verdict on the charge follows his structure exactly.

### Q: Why does Harold Bloom refuse to treat the lovers as case studies?

Harold Bloom refuses because his reading of the play, in Shakespeare: The Invention of the Human in 1998, takes it as a celebration rather than a clinic: he calls it the largest and most persuasive celebration of romantic love in Western literature. To treat the lovers as case studies is, on his account, to convert an achievement into an affliction, reading as symptoms the speeches in which Shakespeare invents a mutual, articulate personhood that Western literature had not previously possessed. The refusal is the more pointed because Bloom is no enemy of Freud: his own Anxiety of Influence of 1973 is an Oedipal theory of poetry, a family romance of strong poets and their precursors. His objection is therefore to the school's tone and jurisdiction, not to its machinery. This article's adjudication accepts his check on the school's pity, the habit of sorrowing over the lovers from a clinical height, while holding that a celebration can still possess an economy, which the school's mechanisms describe.

### Q: How do Girard and Freud differ on why Romeo desires?

Girard and Freud differ at the origin of desire. For Freud, desire is a drive arising in the subject, shaped by the subject's buried history, and fastened on objects that are in principle replaceable, which is why Romeo's total transfer from Rosaline to Juliet, accomplished in one look across a room, is the Freudian account's home ground. For René Girard, in Deceit, Desire, and the Novel and A Theatre of Envy, desire is triangular and imitated: the subject wants what a model or mediator wants, and the object's value is the mediator's mark upon it, which is why both of Romeo's loves being Capulets, certified by the enemy house's prohibition, is the mimetic account's home ground. This article's adjudication partitions the play between them: Girard explains the feud, its causelessness and its doubling, better than the school does, while Freud explains the couple, the transfer's speed and the tomb's interior, better than Girard can. The instant of the feast, where no mediator intervenes between the look and the investment, is the deciding evidence.

### Q: What does Albert Rothenberg hear in Romeo's metaphors?

Albert Rothenberg hears, in his reading of 1973, the sound of the earliest stratum of mental life. His general observation, as the school's surveys transmit it, is that when Shakespeare wants to express wishes, fears, and anxieties deriving from the early stages of mental life, he characteristically turns to metaphor. Romeo's cascade after the street brawl, brawling love and loving hate, heavy lightness, cold fire, sick health, is his exhibit: a rush of fused opposites in which love and hate, weight and lightness, health and sickness have not yet been sorted into separate objects, the verbal form of a pre-oedipal economy. The rival hearing inside the school is Katherine Dalsimer's, for whom the same figures are Petrarchan convention and Shakespeare is smiling at his hero. This article adjudicates for Rothenberg on the question of placement: a convention explains the words' availability, not their arrival at the exact moment when a brawl between the houses has shown Romeo his own biography in miniature.

### Q: Why does Norman Rabkin treat the oxymora as a definition of a life?

Norman Rabkin treats them so, in Shakespeare and the Common Understanding in 1967, because he reads Romeo's figures not as the period's rhetorical furniture but as the character's constitution stated in advance. The cascade of 1.1 yokes every term to its opposite, love to hate, creation to nothing, health to sickness, waking to sleep, and Rabkin's claim is that this is not how Romeo talks about his life but what his life is: a single appetite experienced as its own contradiction, in which the love and the deathward pull are not successive moods but one structure. The school's later readers, equipped with Beyond the Pleasure Principle, recognised the definition as the death-drive reading in embryo, a decade and more before the vocabulary became common critical coin. Rabkin's importance to this survey is that he demonstrates the school's central perception arising independently inside English criticism, from the text outward rather than from Vienna inward.

### Q: What can psychoanalytic reading not explain about this play?

Psychoanalytic reading cannot explain the play as a made object in history. It cannot establish the text between the early quartos, date the composition, trace what Shakespeare took from Brooke's poem and Painter's novella and what he refused, reconstruct the playing conditions of the first performances, or recover what the tragedy meant to the audience that first heard it, since the meanings of 1595 did not include an unconscious of Freud's design. It cannot, at its weakest, distinguish its discoveries from its inventions, as the constructed childhoods of the clinical literature show. And it has a standing failure of tone before the lovers' joy, which its vocabulary keeps converting into symptom, the conversion Harold Bloom's resistance exists to check. This article's verdict is jurisdictional for exactly these reasons: the school's writ runs over the passion's structure and economics, where it is unrivalled, and does not run over the territories of text, date, source, and first meaning, where editors and historians hold the ground.

### Q: How do psychoanalytic readers treat Romeo's Mantua dream as a wish?

Psychoanalytic readers treat it as a wish fulfilled in the dream's own despite the dreamer's waking dread. Romeo reports in 5.1 that his lady came and found him dead, breathed such life with kisses in his lips that he revived and was an emperor. Read by the dream-work's rules, the dream grants in one image the two satisfactions his waking situation forbids him to hold together: union with Juliet and death, with death repositioned as the doorway to the union rather than its price, and with resurrection added as the wish's flourish. The school's point is sequential. The dream is dreamed before Balthasar's report arrives, so when the false news comes it enters a mind already furnished with the fantasy of joining Juliet in death as bliss, and Romeo's instant response, "Then I defy you, stars," has the speed of a decision taken in sleep. The dream does not predict the catastrophe. It prepares the dreamer to choose it.

### Q: What role do the Nurse and the Friar play as substitute parents in this reading?

In this reading they are the parental images split off from the prohibiting parents, the indulgent mother and the permissive father the lovers assemble when the given parents refuse them. The Nurse is Juliet's effective mother by the play's own evidence: she suckled her, mourned her own dead daughter Susan into the substitution, and carries Juliet's childhood in her body and her memory. The Friar is Romeo's effective father: confessor, interpreter of his motives, solemniser of his marriage, and deviser of the plot that is meant to save it. The substitutes serve the wish rather than the law, which is what allows the love to proceed, and they fail at the crisis in character: the Nurse counsels the bigamous surrender to Paris, and the Friar flees the tomb he dare not stay in. The school's conclusion is that the lovers die of the deprivation that organised their love, forbidden by the parents they were given and unprotected by the parents they chose.
