## A Price Quoted to One Person

A personalized price is set by estimating what one identified buyer will pay for an item and charging close to that estimate, rather than displaying one price to every buyer who views the item. Two shoppers can open the same product page within the same hour, from homes a few streets apart, and see different amounts, and neither shopper can see the price shown on the other screen. Willingness to pay, the highest amount a particular buyer will accept before walking away, is the hinge concept, because the entire mechanism exists to locate that amount and price against it.

![A price tag split into many different prices for the same product - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-44.webp)

A personalized price is not a market price with a name attached; it is an estimate of one buyer converted into a number, and its power comes less from the accuracy of the model than from the buyer being unable to see the prices quoted to anyone else. A market price gains strength from common visibility, because a seller who quotes a high figure in public risks an immediate comparison with a rival figure quoted in the same public space. A price delivered inside a private account session faces no such test at the moment of decision. The buyer can accept, refuse, or delay, but the buyer cannot place an identical offer made to a neighbor alongside the offer on the screen and measure the gap between the two.

The estimate at the center of the process rests on inference rather than declaration. A buyer rarely states a maximum in plain terms, and a seller rarely asks for one directly, because a stated maximum would invite strategic answers and end most negotiations before a sale. Instead, the seller assembles traces of past behavior, such as purchases completed, purchases abandoned, searches repeated, and discounts accepted or ignored, and treats those traces as evidence about a limit that was never announced. Each trace is weak in isolation. A completed purchase at a given price proves only that the price was acceptable on one occasion, under one set of pressures, for one need. Combined across hundreds of transactions, however, the traces form a pattern from which a pricing system draws a probability that a given amount will be accepted.

That probability is then converted into an amount through a rule set by the seller, and the rule matters as much as the prediction. A cautious rule prices slightly below the estimated maximum and protects the sale. A bolder rule prices at the estimated maximum and risks refusal in exchange for a larger margin on each completed transaction. The difference between the two rules can exceed the difference between two competing models, because a model supplies a guess about a person while a rule decides how aggressively the seller will act on that guess. Personalized pricing, understood mechanically, is therefore a chain with three links, namely observation of a buyer, estimation of a limit, and a decision about how much of that limit to claim as price.

Opacity completes the mechanism and explains why the practice attracts a kind of suspicion that a public price rise does not. When a supermarket raises the posted price of coffee, every shopper faces the same increase and any shopper can verify the fact by reading the shelf label. When a price is generated for one account, verification requires access to another account, another device, or another household, and even then a difference might reflect timing, stock, or a test rather than a personal estimate. The seller holds the records that could settle the question. The buyer holds a single number and a suspicion. That asymmetry of evidence, more than any single calculation, defines the subject and shapes every dispute about fairness, legality, and proof that follows from it.

## The Posted Price and What It Protected

The posted price was an institution before it was a habit, and its terms were plain. One amount was displayed with the goods, the amount applied to every buyer who presented payment, and the amount could be checked by any person who walked into the shop and read the label. Retail tradition credits John Wanamaker, the Philadelphia merchant whose store opened in 1876, with championing the fixed price tag, and the same practice is also credited to Quaker retail custom, in which a single fair price for all customers carried a moral as well as a commercial meaning. Under either account, the innovation was not the tag itself but the commitment behind the tag, namely that the figure would not change when a different face appeared at the counter.

That commitment protected three things at once, and each protection is easier to see once the commitment weakens. Comparability came first, because a displayed figure allowed a buyer to compare one shop against another without opening a negotiation in either place. Freedom from haggling cost came second, because a fixed figure removed the time, skill, and social confidence that bargaining demands, and a buyer with a weak bargaining position paid the same amount as a buyer with a strong one. Trust came third, because a seller bound by a public figure could be caught breaking the commitment, and the risk of exposure disciplined the seller between transactions. Anonymity ran through all three protections. The posted price asked what was being bought, in what quantity, and at what moment, and it asked nothing about the identity of the person buying.

A commodity market offers the clearest large scale version of the same impersonality, and the contrast shows what a price formed for one buyer abandons. Crude oil changes hands at figures produced by many buyers and sellers reacting to supply, demand, storage, and transport, and [the way oil prices are set](/2026/10/09/how-oil-prices-are-set/) reflects that crowd of competing judgments rather than an assessment of any single purchaser. No refinery receives a secret figure calculated from its browsing habits, and no trader receives a discount because a model judged that trader unlikely to pay more. The figure is impersonal because the process that forms the figure is public in structure, even when individual trades occur quickly and screens display the result for seconds at a time.

A personalized price reverses each element of the arrangement in sequence. Display gives way to delivery, because the figure appears inside an account rather than on a shelf. Universality gives way to differentiation, because the figure is built from a record attached to one buyer. Anonymity gives way to identification, because the record has value only when the seller can connect a returning visitor to past visits. None of these reversals requires a dramatic technology. A loyalty card from the nineteen eighties already linked purchases to a named household, and a mail order catalogue already varied offers by customer segment. Digital commerce changed the scale and the speed, allowing a record to be assembled during a single visit and a figure to be calculated between one page request and the next.

The posted price therefore survives as a standard of comparison even in settings where few prices remain posted in the older sense. A complaint about a personalized price is rarely a complaint about arithmetic. The complaint is that a figure presented as an ordinary market fact may in truth be a private assessment, offered without the protections that a public figure carries, and checked against no rival figure that the buyer is permitted to see.

## Willingness to Pay, the Number Nobody States

Willingness to pay is the highest amount a buyer will give for a specific item, in a specific quantity, under specific conditions, before preferring to keep the money or spend the money elsewhere. Economists also call the same limit a reservation price, because the buyer in effect reserves a boundary beyond which a transaction stops making sense. The boundary is real in the sense that behavior respects it, since buyers do refuse offers and do abandon purchases at amounts they judge too high. The boundary is elusive in every other sense. It shifts with income, urgency, available substitutes, the quality of information about alternatives, and even the way an offer is framed, so a single buyer carries not one stable number but a range that narrows or widens as circumstances change.

Consumer surplus gives the concept its mechanical importance. When a buyer values an item at an amount above the price charged, the difference between the valuation and the price is surplus retained by the buyer, a gain from trade that the buyer keeps in the form of money unspent. When a seller charges exactly the valuation, the entire gain passes to the seller and the buyer retains nothing beyond the item itself. A uniform price set below the valuations of many buyers therefore leaves surplus scattered across a whole customer base, in amounts that vary with each valuation. A seller who could observe each valuation directly would have a clear incentive to claim that scattered surplus piece by piece, charging keen buyers more and reluctant buyers less, and converting a shared gain into a captured one.

Direct observation is precisely what a market denies the seller, and the denial is structural rather than accidental. A buyer who reveals a high maximum gains nothing from the revelation and risks paying the revealed amount in every later negotiation, so rational buyers conceal the number even from themselves in any precise form. Most buyers, asked for an exact maximum for an ordinary purchase, would offer an approximation shaped by the price already seen, which means the stated figure follows the seller figure with the same frequency that the seller figure follows it. The gap between valuation and price is thus unobservable at the moment when knowledge of the gap would be most valuable, and every technique of personalized pricing is an attempt to infer an unobservable quantity from observable behavior surrounding it.

Inference proceeds by substitution, replacing the missing number with correlates that can be measured. A past purchase at a high price suggests tolerance for high prices, though the purchase might reflect urgency rather than tolerance. Repeated visits to a product page without a purchase suggest interest paired with resistance, though the resistance might reflect distraction rather than price. Acceptance of a small discount suggests sensitivity at the margin, while indifference to a large discount suggests either strong need or weak attention. Each correlate is ambiguous in isolation, and a pricing model earns its keep by weighing ambiguous signals in combination, across a population large enough for the errors in individual cases to average toward a usable pattern.

The result is an estimate with an error range, not a discovered fact about a person, and the error range changes the moral and commercial character of the practice. A seller acting on a precise reading of a stated maximum would be extracting a declared limit. A seller acting on a statistical guess is placing a bet, and bets fail in both directions. A guess set too high loses the sale entirely. A guess set too low completes the sale and leaves surplus with the buyer that a better guess would have captured. Willingness to pay remains the number nobody states, and personalized pricing is the industrial effort to price as though the number had been stated after all.

## Pigou and the Three Degrees

Arthur Cecil Pigou gave price discrimination its durable vocabulary in The Economics of Welfare, published in 1920, and the vocabulary endures because it sorts practices by the information a seller holds rather than by the technology a seller uses. Pigou described three degrees. First degree discrimination charges each buyer the maximum that buyer will pay for each unit, leaving the buyer with no surplus at all. Second degree discrimination offers a schedule of versions, quantities, or conditions and allows buyers to sort themselves by choosing among the options on the schedule. Third degree discrimination sets different prices for different groups, with membership in a group observable before the sale and used as the basis for the figure charged.

The distinctions reward slow attention, because practices that look similar at a checkout belong to different degrees and raise different questions. A bulk discount belongs to the second degree when any buyer can obtain the lower unit price by buying the larger quantity, since the seller has set a schedule and the buyer has performed the sorting. A student discount belongs to the third degree when eligibility depends on verified student status, since the seller has priced a group rather than a quantity. A price generated from an individual purchase record belongs to the first degree in ambition, whatever its accuracy in practice, since the target is a single person maximum rather than a group average or a self selected schedule. Pigou supplied the ambition with a name a full century before the data existed to pursue the ambition at scale.

Joan Robinson carried the analysis further in The Economics of Imperfect Competition, published in 1933, and added the question that still complicates any simple verdict on discrimination. Robinson examined whether dividing a market and charging different prices in each part raises or lowers total output, and the answer depended on the shape of demand in the parts being divided. Where discrimination opens a market that a single uniform monopoly price would leave unserved, because buyers with low valuations were priced out entirely, output can expand and buyers who gained access can benefit even while other buyers pay more. Where discrimination merely rearranges sales among buyers who would have been served anyway, the gains concentrate with the seller. The framework therefore refuses a blanket judgment, and any serious account of personalized pricing inherits that refusal.

For most of the twentieth century, first degree discrimination remained a thought experiment, useful for clarifying the limits of seller power and impractical as a description of ordinary commerce. The obstacle was information in three forms. A seller lacked a record of what an individual buyer had paid or refused in the past. A seller lacked a reliable way to identify a returning buyer who preferred to remain anonymous. A seller lacked a cheap way to calculate and present a distinct figure for each buyer at the moment of sale without slowing the transaction and advertising the practice to every other buyer in the shop. Cash payment, crowded stores, and printed price lists each reinforced the obstacle, and the uniform price persisted partly because no alternative could be operated at a tolerable cost.

Digital records removed the three obstacles in sequence rather than at once. Accounts identified the returning buyer. Stored histories preserved what that buyer had done. Automated calculation produced a distinct figure within the time taken to load a page. None of the removals changed the underlying economics that Pigou and Robinson described, and the older framework still predicts the central effects, namely a transfer of surplus toward the seller when estimation is accurate, a possible expansion of sales to buyers priced below a uniform figure, and a loss of sales when estimation overshoots. The technology changed the feasibility of the first degree. The theory of what the first degree does, once feasible, was written before the feasibility arrived.

## The Data That Enters a Price

### What data about a buyer can a price actually use?

A price can use any recorded signal attached to an identified buyer, including location, device, browsing history, past purchases, and patterns of hesitation, provided a seller connects the signal to a prediction about acceptance. Breadth of collection matters less than linkage to a pricing decision for that buyer.

The catalogue begins with the record a buyer knowingly creates. Account history holds past orders, returns, subscription status, and the prices accepted in earlier transactions. Loyalty records add the same information from physical stores when a card or a telephone number joins an in store purchase to an online account. Payment history, held in token form rather than as full card details, signals reliability and sometimes spending capacity. None of these records states a willingness to pay, and each acquires pricing value only when a model treats a pattern in the record, such as repeated full price purchases in a category, as evidence about the price a future offer can carry.

A second layer arrives without deliberate creation by the buyer. Device and operating system data identify the hardware and software presenting a request, and sellers have long treated premium hardware as a rough correlate of spending capacity, though the correlation is coarse and contested. Location enters at several resolutions at once, from country and region down to a postal district and, where permission has been granted, a precise position, and each resolution supports a different inference about income, competition, and urgency. Time patterns add a further signal, since a purchase attempted late at night, in a hurry before travel, or after a long interval since a previous order can each be read by a model as evidence about the strength of need behind the request.

A third layer consists of behavior inside the selling environment itself. Browsing history records pages viewed, searches repeated, filters applied, and items compared. Basket contents record what a buyer gathered and then removed, saved for later, or left unpurchased at checkout. Dwell time and pointer behavior record hesitation in a form a machine can count. The interim staff findings published by the Federal Trade Commission in January 2025, following Section 6(b) orders issued in July 2024 to eight intermediary firms, described location, browsing history, and mouse movements among the inputs that firms said their tools could feed into pricing. The attribution matters, because the findings catalogued capabilities that firms described, and the findings made no determination that a specific retailer charged a specific consumer a personalized price.

A fourth layer is inferred rather than observed, and inference is where a catalogue becomes a profile. Estimated income, household composition, price sensitivity, brand loyalty, and likelihood of comparing rival offers can each be modeled from the observed layers, and the model outputs then enter a pricing calculation as though the outputs were facts about the buyer. Errors compound at that step, since an inferred attribute built on a coarse correlate, such as a postal district used as a stand in for income, can misdescribe an individual household entirely while remaining accurate on average across a district. A pricing system optimized for average accuracy has no internal reason to correct an individual misdescription, and a buyer quoted a figure built on a false inference has no access to the inference and no route to contest the figure that the inference produced. The data that enters a price is therefore best understood as a chain of decreasing certainty, from records a buyer created, through signals a system observed, to attributes a model guessed.

## From a Person to a Record

A person becomes priceable at the point where separate traces can be joined into a single record, and the joining process has a technical name and a commercial history. Identity resolution is the practice of matching identifiers that belong to the same individual or household across devices, sessions, and channels, so that a purchase made on a telephone, a search made on a laptop, and a store visit paid for with a loyalty card all accumulate in one profile. The identifiers doing the joining include account credentials, cookies stored in a browser, device identifiers assigned by an operating system, payment tokens reused across transactions, email addresses supplied for receipts or delivery updates, and loyalty numbers presented at a till. Each identifier covers part of a life, and the resolution process stitches the parts into a continuity that no single identifier provides alone.

The logged in state and the guest state are two different information worlds, and the difference explains much of the practical shape of personalized pricing. A signed in account gives a seller a stable identity, a long history, and a delivery and payment record, all available at the moment a price is requested. A guest session gives a seller a device, a location, a referring page, and whatever behavior unfolds during that session, plus any match that a cookie or a device identifier can establish with an earlier visit. A price built for a signed in account can draw on years of transactions. A price built for a guest rests on thinner evidence and leans harder on coarse correlates such as geography and hardware. Sellers encourage account creation with discounts and faster checkout because an account converts an anonymous visitor into a resolvable record.

Between the seller and the raw traces stands an industry of intermediaries, and the Federal Trade Commission study that produced interim staff findings in January 2025 was directed at that layer, through Section 6(b) orders issued in July 2024 to eight intermediary firms. Intermediaries collect, enrich, and resell data, appending attributes from outside sources to a record a seller already holds, in a process generally called enrichment. A seller holding only an email address and a purchase history can buy an appended estimate of income band or household size from a broker. Enrichment widens the profile without any new interaction with the person described, and a figure quoted to that person can therefore rest partly on data the person never supplied to the seller quoting the figure.

Resolution also fails, and the failures are instructive about what a profile actually is. Shared devices merge the behavior of several people into one record. A new device or a cleared browser store splits one person into several records. A household account blends a careful bargain hunter with an impulsive buyer of the same goods, and a model reading the blended record estimates a maximum that belongs to neither person. The profile is thus a statistical construction with a name and an address attached, accurate enough for aggregate prediction and wrong in particulars that matter to the individual priced by the profile. Personalized pricing prices the record, and the record approximates the person with an error that the person cannot inspect.

Consent and permission govern parts of the pipeline unevenly, which adds a jurisdictional patchwork to the technical account. A location permission granted for delivery routing can supply a signal that a pricing model later uses for a different purpose. A cookie accepted for site functionality can persist as an identifier long after the visit that justified the cookie. Rules in the European Union and in several American states attach disclosure and deletion rights to parts of the record, while other parts move under older and weaker constraints. The mechanism operates across all of these regimes with the same basic steps, and legality depends on place, purpose, and data category.

## The Model That Guesses a Maximum

A pricing model begins with features, the measurable attributes drawn from a buyer record and from the circumstances of a request, and the choice of features decides what kind of guess the model can make. Features might include the number of past purchases in a category, the average amount paid, the interval since the last purchase, the device in use, the district of delivery, the depth of any discount accepted previously, and the presence or absence of a competing tab or a comparison search in the same session. A feature is a variable that has carried predictive weight in past data, for reasons that can be causal, incidental, or spurious.

Training gives the features their weights, and training depends on a record of past encounters between prices and buyers. A training set assembles earlier requests, the prices shown, and the outcomes that followed, whether a purchase, a refusal, an abandonment, or a purchase after a delay or a discount. From those encounters a model learns the statistical relationship between features and outcomes across a population. The central difficulty is built into the training data itself, because the record shows what happened at prices that were actually charged and stays silent about prices that were never offered. A buyer who paid a given amount might have paid more, and a buyer who refused might have accepted a slightly lower amount, and neither counterfactual appears in any log. The model learns from a history that the seller pricing policy itself created.

Two predictions are commonly confused, and the confusion matters because only one of the predictions supports personalized pricing in the strict sense. Predicting whether a buyer will buy at a stated price is a classification task, useful for ranking visitors and allocating attention. Predicting the highest price at which a buyer will still buy is a harder task, requiring an estimate of how acceptance changes as price moves, for a segment or for an individual. That relationship is usually expressed through elasticity, the responsiveness of demand to a change in price. A buyer or a segment with low elasticity accepts a rise with little loss of sales, while a buyer or a segment with high elasticity defects quickly. A model that estimates individual elasticity from sparse individual data borrows strength from similar buyers, which means the individual figure is partly a segment figure wearing individual clothing.

Price testing feeds the model the variation that passive observation cannot supply, and the standard instrument is an experiment called A/B testing. Different visitors receive different prices or different discount depths for comparable items, outcomes are compared across the groups, and the comparison reveals how acceptance moves with price in a way that historical records, generated under a single policy, cannot reveal. Testing solves an information problem for the seller and creates a detection problem for everyone else, because a genuine test and a personalized price produce the same surface appearance, namely different amounts for the same item. The Amazon DVD price test of 2000 entered public debate through exactly that ambiguity, when customers comparing notes in an online DVD forum found different prices for the same discs, and the company apologized, described the variation as a random price test unrelated to customer demographics, and refunded the difference to buyers who had paid more.

Model quality is therefore judged by a standard that outsiders rarely see, namely predictive performance on transactions the model did not train on, measured across a whole population rather than in any individual case. A model can be profitable while mispricing a large minority of buyers, provided the gains on correctly read buyers exceed the losses on buyers who were overestimated and walked away. A buyer who produces a plainly absurd price, generated from a misread record, has identified a genuine individual error and has not dented the commercial case for the system, which never depended on being right about any particular person.

## The Price Rule That Turns a Guess Into a Number

An estimate becomes a price only after passing through a rule, and the rule is a policy decision made by people inside the selling organization, even when software executes the decision thousands of times per hour. The rule specifies how close to the estimated maximum a quoted figure may sit, and the specification reflects appetite for risk as much as belief in the model. A seller confident in a category and tolerant of lost sales sets the figure near the estimate. A seller protecting volume, repeat business, or a reputation for fairness sets the figure well below the estimate and treats the unclaimed surplus as the cost of trust. Two sellers running identical models on identical data will quote different prices if the rules differ, which is why an account of personalized pricing that stops at the algorithm has stopped before the commercially decisive step.

Guardrails surround the rule on every side and reveal the anxieties of the organization setting the price. Floors protect margin, preventing a discount generated for a price sensitive buyer from falling below a level that covers cost and a minimum return. Ceilings protect against absurdity and legal exposure, capping how far above a reference figure any individual quote may rise. Discount depth rules limit the distance between the highest and lowest figures available for the same item, so that a gap discovered by buyers comparing screens stays within a range the seller is prepared to defend. Rate limits control how often a figure can change for the same buyer, preventing a price from rising visibly during a single deliberation. Each guardrail is an admission that unconstrained optimization would produce figures the seller would rather not explain.

Margin targets and campaign objectives bend the rule further, in directions that have nothing to do with any individual buyer. A quarter end push for revenue can raise the fraction of the estimated maximum that quotes claim. A growth target can lower the fraction in order to convert hesitant buyers and enlarge the customer base whose records will feed later estimates. A clearance objective can invert the usual logic entirely, pricing to move stock before storage costs or obsolescence erase the margin that a higher figure was protecting. The individual quote that reaches a screen is thus a compound of an estimate about a person, a standing policy about risk, and a temporary objective about the business, and a buyer attempting to read a personal judgment into a single figure is reading a compound as though the compound were a verdict.

Incidence, the question of who gains and who loses, follows from the accuracy of the guess and the aggressiveness of the rule in combination. When the guess is accurate and the rule is bold, surplus moves from buyer to seller on that transaction, and the buyer retains the item and little else. When the guess overshoots, the sale is lost, the seller gains nothing, and the buyer keeps money that might have been spent at a lower figure, so an aggressive error can leave both sides worse off than a modest uniform price would have left them. When the guess undershoots, the sale completes, the buyer retains surplus, and the seller has spent data and computation to arrive below a figure the buyer would have accepted. When the guess is accurate, the rule is cautious, and the resulting figure sits below a uniform monopoly price that would have excluded the buyer altogether, the transaction serves a buyer whom uniform pricing failed, which is the efficiency case in miniature. The rule turns a guess into a number, and the number distributes gains according to how the guess and the rule interact, not according to the intention announced in any corporate description of the system.

## Four Practices That Are Not the Same Thing

Four practices dominate complaints about unfair prices, and confusion among the practices produces most of the heat in public argument, because a remedy suited to one practice misses the mechanism of another entirely. The comparison in the table settles which of the practices a shopper meets actually set a price for one person, and which practices change a price for everyone, for a moment, or for a group, while leaving the individual buyer unexamined.

| Pricing method | What the price responds to | What differs between two buyers | Named example |
| Uniform pricing | The product and the posted offer | Nothing, both buyers see the same price | A shelf price in a supermarket |
| Dynamic pricing | Demand, inventory and time | The moment of purchase, not the person | An airline fare rising as seats sell |
| Group price discrimination | Membership of a priced group | The group each buyer belongs to | A student discount with identification |
| Personalized pricing | An estimate of one buyer | The estimate attached to each buyer | A price generated from a shopper profile |
| Algorithmic pay setting | An estimate of one worker | The offer made to each worker | A delivery payment offered per driver |

Uniform pricing and dynamic pricing both preserve a form of impersonality, though of different kinds. A uniform price ignores the buyer and the moment alike within the period the price stands. A dynamic price responds to the moment, to remaining stock, and to the pace of demand, and two buyers quoted different amounts under dynamic pricing have usually been quoted at different moments or against different remaining inventory, so the same figure would have been offered to any buyer arriving in the same position. The test is substitution. If swapping the identities of two buyers while holding timing and stock constant leaves the figures unchanged, the practice is dynamic rather than personalized, however sharply the figures move.

Group discrimination and personalized pricing both examine the buyer, and the difference lies in the resolution of the examination. A group price reads a single verified attribute, such as student status, age band, or membership of a loyalty scheme, and applies a figure shared by every member of the group. A personalized price reads a profile and produces a figure that may be unique to one buyer, shared with no group whose membership the buyer could state. Algorithmic pay setting applies the personalized mechanism on the other side of a market, estimating what an individual worker will accept rather than what an individual buyer will pay, and the symmetry matters because the estimation errors, the opacity, and the guardrails operate in the same way whether the number is a price demanded or a payment offered.

A fifth practice stands apart from all four rows and causes a large share of mistaken accusations. Steering changes which products a buyer is shown, in which order, and with which options emphasised, while leaving the price of any given item unchanged for every buyer. A shopper steered toward expensive options can spend far more than a shopper shown cheaper options first, and can reasonably feel that the shop was arranged against that shopper, without any item having carried a personal price. Steering is a practice of assortment and ranking rather than a practice of pricing, and separating the two is the first step in judging any reported case, because evidence that proves steering, such as different search results on different machines, does not prove that an identical item was priced differently at all.

## Second Degree: Making Buyers Sort Themselves

Second degree discrimination solves a problem that first degree discrimination creates for the seller. A seller rarely knows the highest amount a particular buyer will accept, and direct questioning produces strategic answers rather than reliable information. The menu replaces the question. Arthur Cecil Pigou described the structure in The Economics of Welfare in 1920 as a schedule of offers in which each buyer selects a combination of quantity, quality and price, and the selection itself reveals information that the seller could not extract by asking. The mechanism has three steps. The seller designs options that differ in a dimension buyers value unevenly, attaches a price pattern that rewards a costly or inconvenient choice, and then watches which option each buyer takes. Sorting happens without identification, without a personal file and without a separate negotiation.

A coupon performs the sorting through time. Two buyers face the same shelf price for the same packaged food or detergent, yet the buyer who searches for a coupon, saves the coupon, carries the coupon and presents the coupon at the register pays less than the buyer who values an uninterrupted routine more highly than the discount. The discount is payment for effort, and effort is unevenly distributed in a way that correlates with willingness to pay. A buyer with a flexible schedule and a tight household budget may treat twenty minutes of searching as a fair exchange for several dollars saved. The coupon simply makes the lower price available only through a route that the higher paying segment often declines to travel.

Rebates extend the same logic across a delay. The posted reduction arrives only after a form is completed, a receipt is retained and a deadline is met. Breakage, the share of eligible buyers who fail to complete the process, forms part of the economics. The seller can advertise a lower effective price while paying the reduction to the subset of buyers persistent enough to claim the reduction. 

Versioning applies the mechanism to quality rather than effort. Carl Shapiro and Hal Varian, in Information Rules in 1999, described versioning as the deliberate design of a product line in which buyers choose among editions that differ in features, speed, capacity or convenience. A software publisher may sell a basic edition, a professional edition with added tools and an enterprise edition with administrative controls. The price gaps need not track the cost gaps. A professional edition may cost little more to deliver than a basic edition, yet carry a much higher price because professional buyers derive more value from reliability, support and added functions. 

The damaged goods version carries the logic one step further. A manufacturer may disable a feature, slow a process or remove a convenience from a product that could have been sold intact at little added cost. The inferior version exists to protect the segmentation. If the cheaper version were too attractive, buyers able to pay the premium price would migrate downward and the seller would collect less from the segment with the greatest willingness to pay. Deliberate limitation therefore serves a pricing function rather than a production function. 

Quantity discounts perform a comparable sorting by volume. A lower unit price for a larger package rewards buyers who can store goods, transport goods and commit cash in advance. A higher unit price for a smaller package falls on buyers whose storage space, transport or cash position makes bulk purchase difficult. The seller reads the chosen package size as a signal, though the signal mixes need, capacity and price sensitivity in proportions the seller cannot separate. The options sit in public view, the conditions for each price are stated in advance and any buyer can in principle take the cheaper route by accepting the attached inconvenience, delay or limitation.

## Third Degree: The Group Price and Its Manners

Third degree discrimination divides buyers into groups and charges a different price to each group for the same good or service. Pigou placed group pricing in The Economics of Welfare in 1920 after direct personal pricing and menu pricing, because the seller needs less information than a personal estimate requires and more information than a menu requires. The seller needs a marker that can be observed or verified at low cost, a difference in willingness to pay or demand elasticity between groups, and a barrier that prevents the favored group from reselling to the disfavored group at a price between the two official prices. When any one of those conditions fails, the group price weakens. If the marker is easy to fake, enforcement collapses. If demand differences are small, the gain from separation is small. If resale is easy, arbitrage closes the gap.

Student pricing illustrates the enforcement problem clearly. A cinema, a software firm or a transit agency offers a reduced price to enrolled students because student budgets are constrained on average and student demand responds strongly to price. Enrollment, however, must be checked. A student card, an institutional email address or a verification service stands between the claim and the discount. The check costs money and creates friction, and the seller accepts both costs because an unchecked discount would leak to buyers the seller intended to charge in full. Senior pricing replaces enrollment with age, a marker carried on government identification and verified with little dispute. Geographic pricing replaces personal documents with location, using a shop address, a delivery address or a regional edition to assign the buyer to a market. Occupational pricing uses employment by a school district, a hospital, a military service or a large employer as the marker, often through a negotiated program rather than a public counter offer.

The social legibility of a group price comes from the publicity of the rule. A posted student price names the eligible class, the required proof and the amount of the reduction. A buyer denied the reduction can identify the reason, compare the treatment with the treatment of a classmate and challenge an error through a recognizable procedure. The rule may be resented, disputed or regarded as crude, because age and enrollment are rough proxies for income and need. A wealthy student receives the discount and a poor worker of the same age pays in full. The roughness is visible, though, and visible roughness can be debated in public language. 

An individual price lacks that public grammar. When two neighbors receive different prices with no stated rule, neither neighbor can tell whether the difference came from a coupon, a loyalty balance, a data error or an estimate of personal willingness to pay. The grievance has no category to attach to and no procedure to invoke. Group pricing therefore carries manners that personal pricing does not. The category flatters or offends in the open, and the openness disciplines the seller. 

Arbitrage sets the outer limit on how far group prices can drift apart. If the favored group can buy cheaply and resell to the disfavored group, a secondary market forms and the seller loses control of the final price. A discounted ticket that can be transferred freely will be transferred, so sellers attach names, require identification at entry or restrict transfer windows. The sustainable gap between group prices is roughly the cost of defeating the barrier between the groups. When the barrier is cheap to defeat, the gap stays narrow. When the barrier is strong, the gap can persist. 

## Airlines and the Invention of the Managed Fare

Airlines built the most influential template for modern price variation by learning to treat a seat as inventory that expires. An unsold seat on a departed flight has no salvage value, while a seat sold too cheaply to an early buyer cannot be sold again to a later buyer willing to pay far more. The problem is therefore one of timing, uncertainty and segmentation at once. After deregulation removed federal control over domestic routes and fares in the United States in 1978, carriers gained the legal freedom to experiment with fare structures that matched the perishability of the product. Computers, reservation networks and forecasting methods turned freedom into a daily operating discipline.

American Airlines became the canonical case through a yield management program developed during the 1980s and associated with the DINAMO reservation and inventory system. The program forecast demand by flight, date, route and booking class, then opened and closed fare classes as the departure date approached and as actual bookings arrived. A fare class was not a different seat. Passengers in different classes could sit in adjacent rows, receive the same meal and arrive at the same time, while paying amounts that differed by hundreds of dollars. The difference came from the conditions attached to the ticket and from the moment of purchase, both managed as instruments for separating buyers by flexibility and urgency.

The fences did the discriminating work. An advance purchase requirement separated a traveller able to commit weeks ahead from a traveller whose plans formed late. A Saturday night stay requirement separated a leisure traveller, for whom a weekend away fits the purpose of the trip, from a business traveller whose employer pays for a return before the weekend. Change fees and refund restrictions separated a traveller who values certainty of schedule from a traveller who values freedom to alter the schedule. Each fence imposed a cost that fell unevenly. A family visiting relatives could accept an advance purchase and a Saturday stay with little loss. A consultant summoned to a client site on Tuesday and expected home on Friday faced a different calculation, because the employer needed presence on particular days and treated schedule failure as more costly than the fare.

Forecasting turned the fences into a dynamic allocation problem. For each flight, forecasters estimated how many late booking, high fare passengers would materialize and protected seats for that demand by closing lower fare classes early. If protection was set too low, early discount buyers occupied seats that later buyers would have taken at higher prices. The system learned from each departure, comparing forecast bookings with actual bookings and adjusting the pattern for later flights on the same route.

The historical line matters for the distinction between pricing scarcity and pricing a person. Classic revenue management priced the circumstances of purchase. The fare depended on route, date, time remaining before departure, remaining capacity, refundability and stay pattern. Two strangers who bought under identical conditions through the same channel received the same fare, even though the airline had designed the conditions to correlate with willingness to pay. The traveller was sorted by behavior and constraint, much as a coupon sorts by effort, rather than by a dossier assembled from unrelated activity. Identity entered mainly through loyalty programs, corporate contracts and travel agency relationships, channels that offered negotiated terms openly to defined groups.

Competition sharpened the machinery. Revenue management promised a third path, filling seats that would otherwise expire while protecting inventory for high value demand. Resentment rises when conditions appear arbitrary, when a fare changes during a single purchase session or when the traveller suspects that personal characteristics rather than trip characteristics set the price. That suspicion marks the boundary the industry historically stayed behind. Managed fares discriminated aggressively among purchase situations. Personalized fares would discriminate among persons, using identity as the fence itself.

## Is a Surge a Person? Dynamic Pricing Under Pressure

### Is personalized pricing the same as surge pricing?

No. A surge price responds to demand and supply at a moment and applies to everyone facing those conditions. A personalized price responds to an estimate of one buyer and can differ between two buyers facing identical conditions at the same moment in the same market.

Dynamic pricing changes a price as conditions change. A ridehail platform raises a multiplier when requests in a district exceed available drivers, a road authority raises a toll when traffic approaches capacity and an electricity market raises the wholesale price when generation runs short on a hot afternoon. The signal is public in structure even when the exact formula is proprietary. Scarcity has increased, the price rises to ration the scarce supply and the higher price also calls forth added supply where supply can respond. Drivers move toward the district with the multiplier, travellers shift a journey to a cheaper hour and large electricity users cut discretionary load. The price speaks to a market condition rather than to a biography.

Personalized pricing changes the unit of analysis. Two riders requesting the same route at the same minute from the same district can receive different prices if the system estimates different willingness to pay from account history, device, location patterns or past responses to offers. The market condition is held constant and the person becomes the variable. That distinction explains why a surge can be unpopular yet legible, while a personalized difference can be smaller in money and larger in grievance. A personalized difference hides inside accounts, sessions and histories that companions cannot easily reproduce.

Managed fares supply the historical bridge between the two forms, because [the Airline Deregulation Act of 1978](/2016/05/01/airline-deregulation-act-1978-guide/) freed airlines to vary fares by class and condition and thereby taught sellers how much revenue a disciplined schedule of differences could produce. The airline system remained mainly dynamic and conditional, adjusting by inventory and time rather than by personal identity. Digital platforms inherited the machinery and added a layer the airline counter lacked, a persistent account tied to a phone, a payment method and a record of past behavior. The added layer makes blending possible. A system can observe that demand in a district is high and also observe that a particular rider has accepted high prices during past surges, travels from an airport or rarely cancels a request. Each variable can enter a model that sets an upfront amount rather than a transparent multiplier applied to a common base. The output then mixes rationing with estimation, and an outside observer cannot separate the components from the final number alone.

The economic functions also differ under pressure. A pure surge allocates a fixed short term supply and discourages lower value uses during a peak. When supply can expand, the surge rewards providers for entering the market at inconvenient hours or in difficult weather. A personalized increment extracts more from a buyer identified as less sensitive, without necessarily allocating supply more efficiently or calling forth added supply. The same final price can therefore carry different justifications. A higher price charged to one stranded traveller because past behavior signals desperation carries a different moral and political weight, even if the dollar difference is identical. Electricity pricing shows the dynamic form in a defensible public version. 

Tolls make the same point in physical space. A congestion charge varies by time and traffic because the scarce resource is road capacity at a place and hour. Every driver entering the zone during the charged period faces the posted schedule. The vocabulary matters because policy follows vocabulary. Treating a surge as personalization misdirects the first set of questions. Treating personalization as a surge misdirects the second set and leaves the central fact unexamined, that a person rather than a moment set the price.

## The Amazon Test of 2000

The Amazon DVD episode of 2000 endures as a teaching case because the discovery method was simple, social and difficult for a large platform to control. Customers shopping for DVDs noticed that prices for the same discs differed between buyers. The customers compared notes in an online DVD forum, matching titles and amounts in public messages, and a pattern emerged from the comparison that no single shopper could have established alone. Buyers pooling observations create a baseline from fragments, and the fragments in the forum turned suspicion into a documented discrepancy that journalists and the company itself had to address.

The company apologized for the episode and refunded the difference to buyers who had paid more for the discs. Amazon described the variation as a random price test unrelated to customer demographics, a test of consumer response to different price points rather than a system for charging each customer according to a personal profile. The distinction between a random test and demographic personalization matters for accuracy. A random test assigns prices by chance to measure how demand responds across a population. A personalized system assigns prices by prediction, using characteristics of the individual buyer to estimate willingness to pay. The forum evidence established that different buyers saw different prices for the same discs during the test period. The forum evidence alone did not establish why any particular buyer received a particular price, and the company account supplied the explanation that the assignment was random.

Holding both parts together is the discipline the episode teaches. The customers documented the outcome correctly through comparison. The company supplied a mechanism that contradicted the most alarming interpretation of the outcome. A careful account reports the discrepancy, reports the apology, reports the refund of the difference and reports the company description of a random price test unrelated to customer demographics, without converting customer suspicion into a finding about demographic targeting. Allegation and proof occupy different positions in the sequence, and collapsing the sequence into a single claim misstates the record in either direction. Dismissing the discrepancy because the company offered an explanation would erase the documented fact that identical discs carried different prices. Treating the discrepancy as proof of demographic pricing would claim more than the comparison could show.

The reputational sequence moved faster than any regulatory sequence could have moved. The forum comparison created publicity, publicity created pressure and pressure produced an apology and refunds within a news cycle rather than after an investigation, a hearing or a rule. The mechanism of enforcement was communication among buyers. Each buyer held a weak signal, one price on one screen, and communication aggregated the weak signals into a strong collective observation. The episode therefore reveals a structural feature of personalized pricing that remains central. Where buyers can compare, variation becomes visible and must be explained. Where buyers cannot compare, variation can persist without ever becoming an event.

Price tests themselves are ordinary commercial practice in many settings. The test measures response and guides a later common price or a later segmentation. A test that once varied by catalogue edition or store region can vary by session and account, with results recorded automatically and analyzed in detail. 

The refund decision also carries a mechanical lesson. Refunding the difference to buyers who paid more removed the financial injury that the test had created for those buyers, while preserving the information the test had generated about demand at different price points. The company thus separated the data value of the experiment from the transfer imposed on the higher paying participants. Early detection through buyer comparison made a clean remedy possible. The episode set a template for later disputes. A discrepancy surfaces through screenshots, forums or coordinated measurement. The firm explains the mechanism in terms that may narrow the claim, from personalization to testing, from testing to an error or from an error to a supplier feed. 

## The Vending Machine That Read the Weather

A vending machine appears to be the least personalized selling device in ordinary commerce. The machine stands in a public place, displays a price, accepts payment from any passerby and dispenses the same can to a student, a tourist or an office worker. The episode involving Coca-Cola in 1999 disturbed that image by attaching the price of a soft drink to the weather outside the machine. Douglas Ivester, chairman of Coca-Cola, remarked in 1999 that vending machines could raise a soft drink price when the temperature rose, because a cold drink becomes more desirable during hot weather and technology could allow a machine to respond to the condition. The remark described a condition based adjustment rather than a personal file, yet public reaction treated the proposal as a breach of an understood bargain, and the idea was dropped after the reaction gathered force.

The speed of the retreat teaches a lesson about legibility. The causal story fits in one sentence and invites an immediate fairness judgment. A hidden adjustment buried inside a private account history offers no such story. The buyer cannot tell whether weather, scarcity, personal tolerance or a dozen other variables produced the number on the screen. Visibility therefore does not merely inform the buyer. Visibility determines whether a fairness argument can form at all, because an argument needs a stated or observable reason to answer.

Daniel Kahneman, Jack Knetsch and Richard Thaler supplied the classic framework for such judgments in Fairness as a Constraint on Profit Seeking, published in the American Economic Review in 1986. The framework holds that buyers judge a price increase by reference to the reason offered for the increase and to a reference transaction that defines fair dealing between firm and customer. A hardware store raising the price of a snow shovel after a blizzard served as the canonical example in the research tradition associated with the framework, because the increase appears to exploit a sudden need created by weather rather than to reflect a rise in the store cost. Respondents in fairness surveys tend to accept a price increase that passes along a cost increase and reject an increase that captures a windfall from misfortune or urgent need, even when the market clearing effect of the two increases is similar.

The vending machine proposal collided with the reference transaction for a familiar branded drink. A can of soda carries a reference price formed by repetition across machines, shops and advertisements. Buyers expect the reference price to hold across ordinary variation in the day, and a hot afternoon is an ordinary variation rather than a new product. Raising the price because thirst intensifies converts a predictable feature of summer into a surcharge, and the conversion feels like a change in the terms of dealing rather than a response to scarcity in the machine. 

The episode also shows the power of a single executive sentence. No national rollout had to occur for the fairness judgment to form. A remark by Douglas Ivester in 1999 supplied the hypothetical in concrete form, with a named company, a familiar product and a clear trigger variable. A vending machine on a hot pavement can be pictured by anyone who has bought a cold drink, and the picture carried the argument faster than any technical description of demand elasticity could have carried a defense. Secrecy and fairness therefore stand in an uneasy relation. A pricing method that buyers can see may be constrained by fairness norms even when the method is lawful. Coca-Cola paid a small instalment of that debt in 1999 for a machine that existed mainly as a remark.

## Steering Is Not Pricing, and the Difference Matters

Steering changes what a buyer sees. Pricing changes what a buyer pays for a chosen item. The distinction sounds technical, yet the Orbitz episode of 2012 shows how a seller can move spending sharply without altering the price of any identical product. Dana Mattioli reported in the Wall Street Journal in 2012 that Orbitz had found that users of Apple Mac computers spent more on hotels than users of Windows computers, and that Orbitz steered Mac users toward pricier hotels in search results. Mac users spent up to 30 percent more per night on hotels in the pattern reported. The same room cost the same for everyone, a fact that defines the episode as steering rather than a different price for an identical item.

The mechanism begins with prediction from a device signal. A computer operating system correlates with income and with past spending in travel categories, so the platform used the signal to order results rather than to set a room rate. Ordering is powerful because buyers choose from the top of a list far more often than from the bottom, because screen space is limited and because comparison across dozens of properties imposes time and attention costs. A traveller presented first with polished, expensive properties may book a polished, expensive property even when a cheaper property with similar location and quality sits lower in the ranking. The platform collects a larger booking value and often a larger commission, the hotel collects a higher room revenue and the traveller pays more in total without ever facing a discriminatory rate for the selected room.

Search ranking is the quiet instrument in many digital markets because ranking feels like assistance rather than persuasion. 

Pricing rules mostly miss steering because pricing rules ask whether two buyers were charged different amounts for the same item. In the Orbitz pattern, the answer is negative. The identical room carried the identical price, the tax treatment was the same and the booking terms were the same. The difference lay in the choice architecture surrounding the transaction, the order of presentation, the default sort and the prominence assigned to properties at different price levels. A rule that audits only final prices records no difference, while the spending difference of up to 30 percent more per night remains in the data as a difference in choices freely made. The transfer produced by steering resembles the transfer produced by discrimination in effect, though the legal and evidentiary routes differ. A traveller who books a pricier hotel because the ranking elevated pricier hotels spends more and consumes a different bundle, perhaps a larger room, a better view or a more central location. In steering, the buyer receives a different item and pays more, so the analysis must ask whether the altered choice matched the buyer preference or manufactured the preference through prominence.

Measurement can still expose the pattern. Aniko Hannak and co-authors, in a 2014 study measuring discrimination and steering across 16 e-commerce sites, showed how controlled accounts and repeated searches can reveal systematic differences in prices and in result ordering. The unseen part of the list leaves no trace in personal experience. Device signals also illustrate how a proxy can enter through product design rather than through an explicit pricing rule. The Orbitz disclosure mattered because the signal was easy to grasp and the company practice could be stated plainly. A platform sorted travellers by computer type and showed different travellers a different commercial landscape. Once stated, the practice raised a question that final price audits cannot answer, whether a marketplace owes each buyer the same landscape or merely the same prices inside whatever landscape the marketplace chooses to display. Personalized pricing charges one buyer more for the same thing. Steering shows one buyer a more expensive world and lets the buyer choose inside the world on display.

## The ZIP Code as a Stand-In

A ZIP code looks like a neutral administrative fact. The code routes mail, defines a delivery area and places a household inside a geography that a pricing system can read in milliseconds. The Princeton Review episode shows how such a code can carry far more than geography into a price. Julia Angwin and Surya Mattu reported for ProPublica in 2015 that prices for an online SAT preparation course from the Princeton Review varied by ZIP code, that the price in some places reached nearly double the price charged elsewhere and that residents of ZIP codes with large Asian populations were more likely to face the higher price. The Princeton Review said its prices reflected market rates by geographic area. Both the reported pattern and the company account belong in any accurate description, because the pattern documents what buyers in different places were charged and the account states the basis the company gave for the variation.

The mechanism begins with regional pricing, a familiar practice with ordinary justifications. A pricing table keyed to ZIP code converts a regional strategy into thousands of local prices, each assigned automatically when a buyer enters an address or when a system infers location from a connection.

A proxy enters when a variable chosen for one purpose correlates with another characteristic that the seller may not name, may not measure directly and may not intend to use. ZIP codes in the United States are geographically small enough in dense areas to sort residents by income, housing cost, school district and ethnic composition with considerable precision. A code selected as a stand in for market conditions can therefore function simultaneously as a stand in for household resources and for demographic composition. The reported Princeton Review pattern, in which residents of ZIP codes with large Asian populations were more likely to face the higher price, illustrates the shadow that a geographic variable can carry. The finding as reported concerns the prices attached to places and the composition of the places, stated exactly in those terms, and the lesson concerns the behavior of proxies rather than any characterization of the residents themselves.

Causation requires care at each step. A price map can correlate with ethnic composition because both correlate with income, with urban density, with the concentration of competing tutoring firms or with demand for test preparation services. The effect on buyers, however, does not depend on the intention behind the map. A buyer in a high price code pays the high price regardless of the variable that placed the code in the high price band. That separation between intention and effect is why proxy pricing presents a distinct analytical problem. ProPublica reporting in 2015 performed a version of that outcome test from outside the company, using prices visible to buyers in different ZIP codes.

The company account deserves the same precision as the reported finding. The Princeton Review said its prices reflected market rates by geographic area, which places the episode inside the long tradition of regional pricing rather than inside a claim that each buyer received an individually estimated price. Geographic pricing is third degree discrimination in the Pigou framework from 1920, a group price in which the group is defined by place. The digital delivery of the course changed the visibility of the group boundaries. A price nearly double in some places for the same online course invites comparison, and comparison across ZIP codes is easy once a buyer suspects that an address is an input. Angwin and Mattu could vary the ZIP code and record the resulting price, turning a suspicion into a map. The map then revealed a pattern no individual buyer could see, because each buyer normally supplies one address and receives one price. The analytical task is not to declare place an illegitimate input in every setting. 

## Insurance and the Price of Inertia

### Can a company charge two people different prices for the same item?

In the United States, no general rule forbids different prices for the same item for most goods, and sellers set varied prices through discounts, coupons and negotiated terms. Specific statutes restrict particular markets and particular grounds, and those exceptions operate sector by sector rather than through one code.

Insurance makes the logic of personalized pricing unusually visible because insurance already charges different buyers different amounts for protection against the same categories of loss. A premium is supposed to price risk. Two drivers buy cover for comparable cars and comparable liability limits, and the insurer charges different premiums because age, driving record, territory, vehicle type and claims history predict different expected losses. That form of differentiation rests on a cost story. The buyer expected to generate higher claims pays more because the product, protection against expected loss, costs more to supply to that buyer. Disputes in insurance pricing usually concern whether a variable predicts risk fairly and accurately, not whether differentiation itself is allowed.

Price optimization breaks the cost story while keeping the machinery of differentiation. Under price optimization as regulators described the practice during the mid 2010s, an insurer analyzes how sensitive a customer is to a price increase and adjusts the renewal premium according to the tolerance the customer has shown or is predicted to show. A customer who has renewed repeatedly without shopping, who pays by automatic transfer and who has ignored past increases receives a larger increase than a customer whose behavior signals a readiness to leave. The premium then prices inertia rather than risk. Two customers with identical risk profiles can receive different renewal prices because one customer appears less likely to resist.

The renewal moment gives inertia its value. Many policyholders postpone the task year after year, and an insurer can model the postponement as a measurable trait. The same file supports both calculations, and the second calculation uses loyalty as evidence that a higher price will be paid.

Regulators objected on grounds that follow directly from the structure of insurance law in the United States. State insurance law generally requires rates to be adequate, not excessive and not unfairly discriminatory, a standard administered by state insurance regulators rather than by a single national price authority. During the mid 2010s, a wave of state prohibitions and regulatory bulletins against price optimization spread through state insurance departments, with regulators stating that charging a policyholder more because the policyholder is unlikely to shop violates the prohibition on unfair discrimination when the difference bears no relation to expected loss or expense. The objection was not to risk based pricing. The objection was to a price component that measured the customer rather than the risk and concealed the measurement inside a premium presented as a risk calculation.

The distinction between risk and tolerance also matters for market discipline. Competition can discipline a risk based premium when a rival insurer estimates the same risk more cheaply and offers the customer a lower price. Competition disciplines an inertia based premium only if the customer shops, and the premium is designed around the expectation that the customer will not shop. The practice therefore weakens the very mechanism that would correct the practice. A customer who discovers a cheaper rival offer can escape the increase, which means the burden concentrates on customers least equipped or least inclined to search, including customers whose loyalty was built over decades of renewals. Insurance therefore supplies a documented case of a personalized price in a traditional industry, set by an explicit analytical practice with a name, a regulatory record and a defined objection. Price optimization estimates the individual, converts the estimate into a premium and relies on the policyholder comparing the renewal figure with past personal bills rather than with the prices offered to identical risks elsewhere.

## The Same Machinery, Pointed at Pay

Algorithmic pay setting turns the personalized pricing mechanism toward the other side of a transaction. A platform that sells a service to a customer also buys labor from a worker, and an estimate can be aimed at either party. For the customer, the profitable estimate is the highest price the customer will accept. For the worker, the profitable estimate is the lowest payment the worker will accept for a defined task. The arithmetic mirrors perfectly. Information about the individual replaces a common posted rate, the model predicts acceptance, the offer arrives as a single number and the recipient cannot see the offers made to others performing the same work at the same time in the same place.

Uber supplies the documented sequence in two stages. Uber introduced rider upfront pricing in 2016, replacing a fare calculated after the trip from time and distance with a price quoted before the trip began. The upfront rider price could incorporate route, demand and other factors into a single figure presented for acceptance or refusal. Uber then expanded upfront offers to drivers in 2022, presenting a driver with the payment and trip details before acceptance. The expansion decoupled driver pay from a fixed share of the rider fare. Under a fixed share system, driver pay rises and falls mechanically with the amount charged to the rider. Under an upfront offer system, the platform can set the rider price and the driver payment through separate estimates, retaining the spread between the amount a rider will pay and the amount a driver will accept.

The decoupling changes the distribution of gains from a match. When a rider values a trip highly because of urgency, weather or scarcity, a fixed share passes a defined portion of the high fare to the driver who supplies the trip under difficult conditions. A separate driver estimate can instead offer the driver an amount calibrated to the driver history and situation, which may be far below a fixed share of the high rider fare. Flexibility is the genuine advantage claimed for the system, because a platform can subsidize one side of a market to build volume or balance supply. 

The driver estimate can draw on signals parallel to the signals used in buyer pricing. Acceptance history shows which payments a driver has taken under which conditions. A model can infer that a driver who accepts most offers quickly will accept a lower payment for a marginal trip, while a driver who declines low paying trips must be offered more to take the same trip. 

Delivery and task platforms display the same pattern in different dress. 

Opacity operates symmetrically and compounds in the middle. A rider cannot see driver pay, a driver cannot see the rider fare in many upfront arrangements and neither party can see the distribution of offers across the market during the same hour. Comparison, the force that exposed the Amazon DVD discrepancy in 2000 when customers pooled observations in a forum, requires workers to pool pay observations across accounts, zones and time windows. The symmetry completes the account of personalized pricing by showing that the customer is not the only person who can be estimated. Any party facing a platform individually, through an account, under time pressure and without access to the offers made to peers can receive a number tuned to personal tolerance. The buyer pays closer to a personal maximum. The worker receives closer to a personal minimum. Surplus that a common price would have left with the buyer or the worker moves to the intermediary that owns the estimate and controls the comparison environment. A posted wage and a posted price share a social property that an individualized offer lacks. An individualized offer fragments that public ground. Each recipient negotiates alone against a system that has observed thousands of similar negotiations and remembers the outcomes.

## Who Pays for a Personal Price

A personal price moves surplus rather than creating it at the moment of sale, and that distinction decides who pays. Surplus is the gap between the most a buyer would have paid and the price actually charged, and it belongs to the buyer when the price sits below that maximum and to the seller when the price climbs toward it. Under a uniform price, every buyer who values an item above the posted figure keeps the difference as a private gain, invisible to the seller and unshared with anyone else. A seller that estimates each buyer separately can reclaim part of that gap person by person, raising the price for a buyer judged eager and holding or lowering it for a buyer judged hesitant. The higher payment funds no extra production, no better materials and no faster delivery, because the item, the warehouse and the transaction remain identical. The transfer travels straight from the eager buyer's pocket into the seller's margin, and the cost is measured in lost surplus rather than in resources consumed.

The burden therefore falls unevenly, and the average buyer is a fiction that hides both groups at once. One group consists of buyers whose signals read as commitment: a repeated purchase, a premium device, an address in an expensive district, a history of paying full price without waiting for a discount. A model treats those signals as evidence of a high maximum and quotes accordingly, so the buyers least likely to resist pay the largest share. The second group consists of buyers whose signals read as fragility: abandoned carts, long comparison sessions, a pattern of buying only during sales. Averaged together, the two groups can look untouched, with a mean price close to the old uniform figure, while the distribution underneath has stretched in both directions. Arthur Cecil Pigou, writing in The Economics of Welfare in 1920, built the three degrees of discrimination on exactly this sorting logic, and the sorting, rather than the average, remains the object worth examining.

The group quoted less raises the hardest part of the incidence question, because a lower personal price can be a genuine gain rather than a share of someone else's loss. A uniform monopoly price excludes every buyer whose maximum sits below it, and exclusion produces no transaction, no surplus for either side and no access to the good. A discriminating seller can serve that excluded buyer at a lower figure while keeping the higher figure for everyone else, and the new transaction creates surplus that did not exist before. In that narrow case, nobody pays for the gain; output expands and the excluded buyer enters a market that a single price had closed. The mechanism matters because it separates partition of existing surplus from enlargement of the market itself.

Competition decides how much of any transfer stays with the seller, and its route is the outside seller's quote. A personal price that climbs too far above a rival's comparable offer gives the buyer a reason to leave, and each departure disciplines the estimate that produced it. Where rivals are plentiful and comparison is easy, a seller that extracts surplus aggressively loses volume, so part of the extracted amount returns to buyers through lower headline prices, richer discounts or better terms elsewhere in the transaction. Where a seller stands alone, through a patent, a platform lock or a local monopoly, no such return route exists and the transfer accumulates as margin. The payer, in the end, is whichever buyer lacks a credible alternative at the moment of quotation, and the payment is largest precisely where the buyer's dependence is deepest.

## The Identification Problem

Proving that a particular price was personalized is harder than suspecting it, because a price difference between two buyers has many innocent causes that produce the same visible result. Inventory falls and a dynamic system raises the remaining units. A seller runs a controlled experiment and assigns visitors to price groups by coin flip rather than by identity. Taxes differ by jurisdiction, shipping differs by distance, and a promotional code applied by one buyer and missed by another changes the final figure without any estimate of willingness to pay entering the calculation. Each cause leaves an identical trace on two screens, a gap, and the gap alone cannot say which cause produced it. The counterfactual at the center of the question, the price the same buyer would have received with a different history, is unobservable by definition, because a transaction happens once and the alternative quotation vanishes with the session that never occurred.

The internal record that could settle the question sits inside the firm, protected as a trade secret and structured for operations rather than for outside inspection. A pricing system may combine hundreds of features into a score, and even the team operating it may be unable to point to a single feature and say that the feature moved a given quote by a given amount. Documentation describes inputs and objectives; it rarely preserves a clean narrative for one buyer's price on one day. An investigator therefore faces a layered problem: first establishing that a gap existed, then excluding inventory, timing, experiment and geography as explanations, then showing that an identity linked feature, rather than a neutral operational variable, carried the effect. Each layer demands evidence of a different kind, and failure at any layer leaves the case at the level of pattern rather than proof.

The Federal Trade Commission's surveillance pricing study shows the institutional shape that such an investigation takes when the evidence is private. In July 2024 the Commission issued Section 6(b) orders to eight intermediary firms, companies positioned between sellers and the pricing tools sellers use, and compelled information about products, inputs and practices that ordinary observation could never reach. The interim staff findings released in January 2025 described capabilities and inputs, including location, browsing history and mouse movements, that firms said could feed pricing decisions. The findings made no determination that a specific retailer charged a specific consumer a personalized price, and that restraint was structural rather than timid. A study power collects the machinery; a determination about one consumer's price would require the transaction level record, the model version and the excluded alternatives for a single case, all held together at once.

Measurement from outside therefore works by elimination and repetition rather than by a single dramatic comparison. A researcher who finds one gap has an anecdote. A researcher who holds the item, the hour and the delivery address constant, varies one profile trait, repeats the comparison hundreds of times and watches the gap follow the trait has evidence that survives the innocent explanations, because inventory and timing were frozen by design and experiment groups would scatter rather than track a trait. Even then, the result identifies an effect without identifying intent, since a model can learn a proxy for a trait that no designer selected. The identification problem is thus permanent in form: visibility is asymmetric, the seller sees the inputs and the output, the buyer sees only the output, and every outside method is an attempt to reconstruct a hidden calculation from its shadows.

## The Efficiency Defense, Given Its Best Form

The strongest case for price discrimination begins with the buyers a uniform price leaves outside the market entirely. A single monopoly price is set for the buyers who value a good most, and everyone below that threshold goes unserved even when serving them would cost the seller very little. Joan Robinson posed the governing question in The Economics of Imperfect Competition in 1933: discrimination changes total welfare only through its effect on output, so the practice earns an efficiency defense when it raises the quantity sold and fails that defense when it merely rearranges payments for the same quantity. Where a lower price for a weaker market brings excluded buyers into transactions they value above the cost of supply, output rises, deadweight loss shrinks and the seller's extra profit is financed by new sales rather than by transfers from existing buyers.

Dirk Bergemann, Benjamin Brooks and Stephen Morris widened that defense in The Limits of Price Discrimination, published in the American Economic Review in 2015, by showing how little the visible data of a market settles the outcome. Working from the same underlying demand information, their analysis found that the division of surplus consistent with that information spans a wide range, running from outcomes in which buyers keep nearly all the surplus to outcomes in which the seller captures nearly all of it, with many divisions between those poles also possible. The result cuts against confident condemnation as firmly as against confident praise. If identical market data can support such different distributions, then the fairness and efficiency of discrimination cannot be read off the existence of discrimination itself; the surrounding structure, the information each side holds and the constraints on the seller decide where inside the range a real market lands. A critic who treats every differentiated price as extraction has skipped the step the 2015 paper insists on, which is locating the actual division rather than assuming the worst one.

Jacques-Francois Thisse and Xavier Vives supplied the competitive version of the defense in the American Economic Review in 1988, and its mechanism runs opposite to the intuition that discrimination always softens rivalry. When two rival sellers can each identify the other's strong market, each gains an incentive to cut the price inside the rival's territory to poach buyers there, while defending its own territory against the same attack. Discrimination turns one broad contest into many local contests, and the local contests can be fiercer than the broad one, because a price cut can be aimed at contested buyers without surrendering margin on every sale. In the model Thisse and Vives constructed, the outcome can be lower prices across both territories than uniform pricing would have produced, a result driven by the targeting itself. The defense therefore does not depend on a benevolent seller.

Taken together, the three arguments give discrimination a serious claim: output can expand for the excluded, the surplus division is structurally open rather than fixed at extraction, and targeted rivalry can push prices down. The thesis of a personalized price as an estimate converted into a number survives those arguments, but narrowed to its defensible core. The arithmetic of estimating a buyer and quoting accordingly is neutral; nothing in the calculation itself dictates whether the result will be access, competition or capture. Everything depends on conditions outside the calculation: whether a rival can quote against the estimate, whether the buyer can see that other prices exist and compare them, and whether an outside option gives refusal real force. Where competition is active, visibility is high and exit is cheap, the efficiency defense describes real outcomes. Where a seller faces no rival, quotes in darkness and sells something a buyer cannot postpone or replace, the same arithmetic serves extraction, and the defense describes a possibility the structure has foreclosed.

## The Law in the United States

United States law contains no general federal ban on charging different consumers different prices for the same goods, and the structure of the statutes explains the gap. Federal price discrimination law grew up around distribution chains rather than around the final buyer. The Robinson-Patman Act of 1936, the statute most often named in this connection, reaches sales of goods to competing resellers, addressing a seller that favors one retailer over a rival retailer and thereby injures competition between them. Its documented scope is the wholesale relationship, and a household buying for personal use stands outside that frame. A consumer charged more than a neighbour for the same item therefore finds no claim in the statute built for grocers and wholesalers, however close the word discrimination makes the two situations appear.

The operative federal theory against a hidden consumer practice runs instead through deception and unfairness under Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act. A price can be lawful while the presentation around it is not: a seller that advertises one price and engineers another through concealed personal inputs invites a deception analysis centerd on what a reasonable consumer was led to believe, not on the mere existence of different prices. Unfairness is a harder route, requiring substantial injury that consumers cannot reasonably avoid and that is not outweighed by countervailing benefits, and a hidden practice is not automatically an unfair one under that test. Enforcement also moves through institutional channels with real limits, a constraint visible in [how the FTC writes consumer rules](/2019/01/15/ftc-consumer-protection-rulemaking/), because the Commission's rulemaking authority is narrower and slower than its power to bring individual cases. The practical consequence is a federal posture built case by case, in which the secrecy, the claim made to the buyer and the availability of an alternative matter more than the price gap alone.

Below the federal level, state law supplies a patchwork rather than a code. State consumer protection statutes in all 50 states prohibit deceptive acts in trade, and a personalized price presented as a standard price can be tested under those general clauses without any statute naming the practice. State privacy statutes add a second layer by governing the collection and use of the personal data that feeds an estimate: California's Consumer Privacy Act, effective in 2020, grants rights over personal information held by covered businesses, and states including Colorado, Connecticut and Virginia adopted comparable statutes in the years that followed. Their effect on a quoted price arrives indirectly, through consent, deletion rights and limits on sensitive data. Sector statutes form a third layer, and insurance is the clearest case, with state regulators in more than a dozen states acting against price optimization practices that raised premiums for policyholders judged unlikely to shop around.

The pattern across all three layers is protection arriving statute by statute and sector by sector, a shape visible across [the history of consumer protection law in the United States](/2019/02/15/us-consumer-protection-law-history/), in which each new harm tended to receive its own instrument rather than a general principle announced in advance. Personalized pricing sits inside that pattern as a practice awaiting a specific instrument. Antidiscrimination statutes supply one further boundary: a price that differs because of race, sex, religion or another protected characteristic can violate the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Fair Housing Act or the Equal Credit Opportunity Act depending on the market, even though a price that differs because of browsing behavior alone violates none of them. The legal question in the United States is therefore rarely whether two prices differed. It is which statute, drafted for which earlier problem, can be made to reach the mechanism that produced the difference, and in many consumer markets the honest answer is that none reaches it directly.

## Disclosure Regimes Beyond the United States

The European Union chose a label where the United States chose study, and the Omnibus Directive, Directive 2019/2161, states the duty in a single mechanical rule. When a trader offers a consumer a price that was personalized on the basis of automated decision making and the profiling of consumer behavior, the trader must inform the consumer of that fact before the consumer is bound by the contract. The duty attaches to the method rather than to the size of any difference: a personalized price one cent above a standard price carries the same disclosure obligation as a price doubled by the same route. Member states transposed the directive into national law, with application from May 2022, and enforcement rests with national consumer authorities. The directive does not prohibit the personalized price, cap it or require the seller to reveal the standard price beside it. Its theory is informational: a buyer told that a figure was generated for that buyer alone can discount the figure's authority, seek a rival quote or refuse, and the market is expected to do the rest of the work.

The General Data Protection Regulation stands adjacent to that duty as the broader frame for the data underneath the price. Its provisions on automated decision making, including Article 22, give a person rights concerning decisions based solely on automated processing that produce legal or similarly significant effects, and its transparency articles require information about the existence of profiling and the logic involved, described in general terms. A price set by a model draws on personal data at every step, from the browsing record that trained the estimate to the location signal read at the moment of quotation, so the regulation governs the inputs even where a single price might fall short of the significant effect threshold. Between the two instruments, a European seller faces a disclosure duty aimed at the price itself and a data regime aimed at the machinery behind it, while retaining the legal ability to personalize. The combination is distinctive: neither a ban nor a free field, but a practice permitted inside a structure of declared processing.

China's algorithmic recommendation rules, effective in March 2022, took the opposite route and addressed the result rather than the label. The Provisions on the Management of Algorithmic Recommendations in Internet Information Services, issued by the Cyberspace Administration of China with other ministries, prohibit providers that hold a dominant position in a transaction from using data and algorithms to impose unreasonable differential treatment in transaction prices and other conditions. Where the European rule asks whether the buyer was told, the Chinese rule asks whether the differential treatment was unreasonable, a substantive standard that requires a regulator to judge the difference itself. The same body of rules requires providers to offer users a way to switch off algorithmic recommendation and to avoid models that induce addiction or excessive spending, placing price treatment inside a general governance of recommendation systems rather than inside consumer contract law.

Three jurisdictions therefore give three different answers to the same mechanism, and the comparison is mechanical rather than rhetorical. The European Union demands a label and leaves the price standing. China prohibits an unreasonable result and leaves the definition of unreasonable to enforcement. The United States, through the Federal Trade Commission's Section 6(b) study begun with orders in July 2024, gathers information about a practice that no general federal consumer statute either labels or bans. Each regime also creates a different evidentiary world for buyers. A disclosure regime generates records of which prices were declared personalized. A prohibition regime generates investigations into differences judged excessive. A study regime generates reports about capability and practice that stop short of case level findings. The price on a screen may be produced by identical software in all three places; the law decides what trace that production must leave behind.

## How Anyone Knows: Audits, Sock Puppets and Scraped Prices

### How do researchers detect personalized pricing from outside a company?

Researchers create controlled accounts that differ in one trait, hold timing and item constant, record every quoted price, and compare the results across many repetitions, so a persistent gap tied to the varied trait, rather than chance or inventory, becomes the evidence that a price responded to identity.

The canonical form of that method arrived in 2014, when Aniko Hannak and co-authors published a measurement study of price discrimination and steering across 16 e-commerce sites. Their design built the controls directly into the accounts. Some profiles were clean, carrying no history a model could read, while others were trained, taken through browsing and purchasing behavior chosen to give a pricing system something to estimate. The researchers then requested prices for identical items from matched profiles and compared the results, treating a difference that followed the profile, under otherwise identical conditions, as the signal worth reporting. The scale mattered as much as the design: a single mismatched quote proves nothing, because experiments, caching errors and inventory effects scatter prices constantly, while a gap that repeats across hundreds of paired requests in the same direction has excluded most of those explanations by accumulation.

Geography receives the same treatment through spoofing, because location is among the cheapest signals for a seller to read and among the easiest for a researcher to vary. A study can route identical requests through addresses in different cities or present different ZIP codes at checkout, holding the account, the item and the hour fixed, so any price movement isolates the location variable. Paired testing borrows its logic from housing and employment audit studies, in which matched testers differing in one characteristic measure how a market treats that characteristic. Scraped prices supply the third instrument: automated collection of the same item's price across many sessions builds a distribution, and a distribution reveals structure that a pair of screenshots cannot, including whether prices cluster by profile type, spread randomly as an experiment would spread them, or move with stock levels in a pattern that points to dynamic pricing instead.

The limits of the method deserve the same precision as the method itself, and the comparison table of pricing methods in the earlier taxonomy of this subject is the right yardstick for reading an audit result. An audit can establish that a difference existed, tied to a trait, under controlled conditions. It can often narrow the likely reason, distinguishing steering toward different products from a different price for an identical product, a distinction the Hannak study was designed to keep separate. It cannot, from outside, establish intent, because a model that learned a proxy for income from browsing patterns produces the same observable gap as a model instructed to charge by income. Nor can an audit see the prices never quoted, the buyers deterred before a session began, or the internal objective the system was optimizing. Replication across sites and years strengthens the inference further, because a trait linked gap that appears under different inventories, designs and market conditions is harder to explain as an artifact of one shop's operations, while a gap confined to a single test remains a finding about that test alone. The strongest honest claim an outside study can make is therefore a claim about behavior under test conditions, stated with its sample and its controls, and the gap between that claim and a verdict about a firm's practice is the identification problem wearing a lab coat.

## The Seller's Own Limits

Sellers restrain personalized pricing for reasons that begin inside the model itself, because an estimate of willingness to pay is a probability statement dressed as a number. A buyer who lingered on a page may have been comparison shopping, reading reviews for a gift or simply distracted, and each interpretation points to a different price. A model that reads hesitation as eagerness raises the quote, loses the sale and records the loss as weak demand rather than as its own error. Pricing teams know that conversion, the share of visitors who buy, punishes prices that feel wrong with unusual speed, and most commercial pricing work is organized around conversion and revenue per session rather than around extracting the last unit of surplus from each visitor. A system tuned to keep conversion healthy will sacrifice theoretical extraction whenever the two conflict, because a buyer retained at a moderate price is worth more than a buyer alienated at a perfect one.

Discovery carries a second cost, and the Amazon DVD price test of 2000 remains the standing illustration of its size. Customers who compared prices in an online DVD forum found different figures for the same discs, the company apologized, described the episode as a random price test unrelated to customer demographics and refunded the difference to buyers who had paid more. The financial cost of the refunds was minor beside the reputational cost of the lesson the episode taught every observer: a price difference, once compared in public, becomes a story about fairness rather than a story about method. Secrecy is a load bearing part of the mechanism, since the estimate works best when the buyer treats the quote as the price rather than as an opening position, and discovery removes that support in a single news cycle. A firm weighing personalization must therefore price in the probability that two buyers will eventually compare screens, a probability that rises with every forum, screenshot and audit study in existence.

Legal exposure adds a third restraint, uneven across markets but never absent. A price pattern that correlates with a protected characteristic can attract antidiscrimination scrutiny even when no protected data entered the model, because a proxy learned from behavior can reproduce the pattern the law forbids selecting directly. Privacy statutes in California and the European Union add compliance cost to the data collection the estimate requires, and disclosure duties convert a silent practice into a declared one wherever they apply. Ariel Ezrachi and Maurice Stucke mapped the wider terrain in Virtual Competition in 2016, setting out a taxonomy of algorithmic arrangements that runs from messenger systems carrying out human decisions to self learning systems whose behavior their operators predict only loosely. Their taxonomy matters for restraint because responsibility blurs along that scale: a firm may deploy a tool whose pricing behavior it cannot fully explain, and the inability to explain becomes a liability the moment a regulator, a court or a journalist asks for the reason behind one buyer's price.

None of these restraints amounts to reassurance, and the distinction matters. Model error restrains overreach only where the error is visible in lost sales; a monopolist can misprice for years without a signal strong enough to correct the model. Reputational cost restrains only firms whose buyers can leave and whose name is worth protecting. Legal exposure restrains only the patterns a statute reaches. The limits are real, they explain why observed practice has stayed narrower than technical capability, and they operate as friction rather than as principle. A market in which the friction weakens, through greater lock in, weaker comparison habits or a model that finally reads buyers well, would test restraints that were never designed as safeguards at all.

## What Would Settle the Argument

The argument about personalized pricing persists because the decisive evidence is a specific kind of record that sits inside firms and almost never reaches public view. Settlement would require firm level records of who was quoted what, at which moment, from which model version, with which features active, together with the standard price or the price quoted to a matched buyer in the same window. With those records, every major question in the debate becomes answerable by arithmetic. The distribution of prices for an identical item could be measured rather than sampled. The features carrying the largest effect could be ranked. The output question posed by Robinson in 1933 could be answered by counting transactions that a uniform price would have excluded. Without those records, public debate recycles a small set of episodes, the Amazon test of 2000, the Orbitz steering reported in 2012, the Princeton Review ZIP code pattern reported in 2015, and generalises from episodes that were never designed to measure prevalence.

Regulators reach for compulsory process precisely because voluntary disclosure cannot produce that record, and the Section 6(b) study power of the Federal Trade Commission is the mechanism built for the gap. Section 6(b) of the Federal Trade Commission Act authorizes the Commission to require reports and answers from companies about their organization, practices and management, without alleging any violation and without opening an enforcement case. The July 2024 orders to eight intermediary firms used that power to obtain information about surveillance pricing products, their data inputs and their customers, material that no audit study could compel and no firm had reason to publish. Compulsory process has limits of its own: the material arrives under confidentiality protections, the public receives findings at the level of practice and capability, and the January 2025 interim findings accordingly described inputs such as location and browsing history without naming a consumer level case. The power settles questions for the regulator first and for the public only in summary.

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development stated the resulting position plainly in its 2018 report Personalised Pricing in the Digital Era, which found that evidence of widespread true personalization, a different price for an identical item set from an individual estimate, remained thin relative to the scale of concern the practice attracted. The report separated personalization from the dynamic and segmented pricing that surrounds it, and much of the apparent evidence dissolved under that separation into practices that were older, broader and less individual than the label suggested. A reader keeping notes on rival prices across sellers has [a companion reference notebook](https://vaultbook.net) available. The thinness of the record cuts in both directions, and honest treatment keeps both edges visible. Absence of public evidence is compatible with a practice that is rare and with a practice that is common and well hidden, and the visibility asymmetry at the heart of the mechanism means the public record will understate the practice by an unknown amount in either case.

Settlement, if it arrives, will therefore arrive as infrastructure rather than as a single revelation. Disclosure duties generate declared cases that can be counted. Audit methods generate controlled comparisons that improve with repetition and scale. Compelled studies generate regulator held records that can anchor findings about inputs and objectives. Combined over years, those three streams could narrow the open range that Bergemann, Brooks and Morris identified in 2015, locating real markets between full consumer benefit and full extraction with evidence instead of temperament. Until such a record accumulates, the defensible position is disciplined uncertainty: the mechanism is fully understood, several episodes are documented in detail, the law in three major jurisdictions has taken three different postures toward it, and the prevalence that would decide the argument remains the one quantity the available evidence cannot supply.

## Reading a Price as a Message About Its Maker

A quoted price can be read, and reading it begins with classification. Four practices produce the figures on a screen, and each sends a different message. A uniform price says the seller treats the market as one body and accepts the exclusions that follow. A dynamic price says the figure answers conditions, time, stock and demand shared by everyone in the same window, and the message concerns the market rather than the buyer. A group price says the seller has sorted buyers by a visible trait, a student status, a member card, a postal code, and accepts that the sorting can be seen and judged. A personalized price says something more specific: the seller has formed an estimate of one buyer, converted that estimate into a number and presented the number as though it were simply the price. Where your screen shows a figure that a friend's screen contradicts for the identical item at the identical hour, classification does the first work, separating a market signal from a judgment about a person.

Restated without its original phrasing, the central claim is that a price built for one buyer draws its strength from the missing comparison. A seller's model can be crude, trained on thin signals and wrong about a large share of buyers, and still profit from quotation in private, because a buyer who cannot see the neighbouring prices cannot tell a fair figure from an extraction and usually treats the number on the screen as a fact about the item rather than as a guess about personal resolve. Visibility, not accuracy, is the load bearing condition. That is why disclosure duties, audit studies and price comparison habits matter out of proportion to their modest appearance: each restores a fragment of the publicity that the posted price once supplied automatically, when one figure hung in a window and every passer by could read the same number.

The leverage available against an estimate follows from its construction. An estimate is built from signals, and signals change: a comparison across sellers forces the estimate to compete with a real alternative, delay lets urgency signals decay and stock conditions shift, a clean profile with no stored history presents the model with a stranger it must price cautiously, and a rival seller with a posted price restores the outside option that disciplines any quote. None of that leverage touches the seller's underlying capability, and none of it should be mistaken for a remedy for a market with one seller. Where you face a monopolist, the honest reading is structural: the quote reflects the absence of alternatives more faithfully than it reflects the item's cost or worth, and no browsing habit repairs a missing competitor.

The deepest change is institutional rather than financial. The posted price was a public institution, a single declaration made to everyone at once, open to inspection, comparison and argument, and its publicity disciplined sellers before any regulator arrived. A regime of private prices moves information along with money: the seller learns more about the buyer with every transaction, while the buyer learns less about the market, seeing a sample of one drawn from a distribution the seller alone can inspect. Judged by totals, the sums transferred may stay modest for years. Judged by structure, the shift is large, because a market in which each participant sees a different price is a market in which the shared reference point that made prices legible has been withdrawn. Reading a price carefully, asking which practice produced it and what its maker believed, is the habit that keeps that reference point alive in the one place a seller cannot reach, the judgment of the person quoted.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is personalized pricing in simple terms?

Personalized pricing means a seller sets a figure for one buyer rather than for a whole market. The seller gathers signals about that buyer, such as past purchases, location, device and browsing behavior, and feeds those signals into a model that estimates the highest amount the buyer is likely to accept. The estimate becomes the quote. Two buyers can therefore receive different figures for an identical item at the same hour, with neither buyer shown the other's figure. The practice differs from a shopkeeper haggling in one important respect: the calculation is automated, repeatable and hidden, and the buyer usually has no way to tell that the figure on the screen was built for one person instead of posted for everyone.

### Q: How is personalized pricing different from dynamic pricing?

Dynamic pricing changes a figure in response to conditions that apply to every buyer in the same window: remaining stock, time of day, season, weather or a surge in demand from a crowd. An airline seat grows more expensive as a flight fills, and the increase confronts each shopper who arrives late, regardless of identity. Personalized pricing changes a figure in response to the buyer, so two shoppers arriving in the same minute, facing the same stock level, can still be quoted differently because their histories differ. The test that separates the practices is simple to state and hard to apply from outside: hold the moment and the item fixed, vary only the person, and see whether the figure moves. Movement tied to conditions is dynamic; movement tied to identity is personal.

### Q: What is the difference between price discrimination and personalized pricing?

Price discrimination is the wider category, described by Arthur Cecil Pigou in The Economics of Welfare in 1920 through three degrees. The third degree sorts buyers into visible groups, students, members or residents of a region, and charges each group its own figure. The second degree lets buyers sort themselves through quantity discounts and version choices. The first degree, an ideal rather than a routine practice in Pigou's account, charges each buyer the maximum that buyer will bear. Personalized pricing is the technological attempt at that first degree: instead of assigning a person to a group with a shared figure, a model estimates the individual directly from personal signals. Every personalized price is discrimination, but most discrimination, including ordinary group discounts, is far coarser than personalization.

### Q: Why would a company charge different prices to different people?

A single posted figure leaves money uncollected in two directions. Buyers who would happily have paid more receive the standard figure and keep the difference, while buyers priced out by that figure make no purchase at all. Charging differently promises to repair both leaks at once: a higher quote captures more from the committed buyer, and a lower quote rescues a sale that the standard figure would have lost. Different figures also serve quieter purposes. A seller can reward loyalty, fill capacity that would otherwise sit empty, test how demand responds to a figure before changing a public one, or match a rival's offer for a wavering customer without lowering the figure for everyone. Each motive rests on the same belief, that one figure wastes information the seller already holds about the people arriving to buy.

### Q: What is willingness to pay and why does it matter for pricing?

Willingness to pay is the highest amount a particular buyer will exchange for a particular item under particular circumstances, and it varies with income, urgency, taste, available substitutes and the buyer's knowledge of alternatives. It matters because it marks the ceiling of any transaction: above that ceiling no sale occurs, and below it the buyer keeps the gap between the ceiling and the figure charged as surplus. A uniform figure guesses at an average ceiling and inevitably sits too high for some buyers and too low for others. Personalized pricing tries to measure the individual ceiling instead, using behavior as evidence, since buyers reveal fragments of the ceiling through past purchases, abandoned carts, search patterns and responses to earlier discounts. The entire mechanism stands or falls on the quality of that measurement.

### Q: How do websites know what a shopper might be willing to pay?

A website rarely knows a ceiling directly; it assembles clues and lets a model weigh them. Account history shows what the shopper bought before and at which figures sales succeeded or failed. Browsing behavior adds signals about urgency and comparison, including repeated visits to one item, time spent on premium options and the use of discount searches. Device, operating system, location and connection details supply context that correlates, imperfectly, with income and alternatives. The Federal Trade Commission's interim staff findings of January 2025 described inputs of this kind, including location, browsing history and mouse movements, that firms said could feed pricing. A model trained on millions of earlier sessions converts the assembled clues into a probability that a given figure will be accepted, and that probability, not certainty, drives the quote.

### Q: Are coupons a form of price discrimination?

Coupons are a classic form of price discrimination, usually classed with the second degree in the scheme Arthur Cecil Pigou published in 1920, because buyers sort themselves. A shopper willing to spend time finding, saving and redeeming a coupon reveals a lower willingness to pay and receives a lower figure, while a shopper who values time more highly pays the shelf figure for the identical item. The seller obtains two figures without estimating anyone, since the coupon itself performs the sorting at almost no cost. Coupons feel acceptable to most shoppers for reasons that expose how fairness judgments work: the discount route is open to everyone, the rules are visible, and the extra effort explains the extra saving. Personalized pricing removes each of those features, replacing an open route with a hidden estimate that the buyer cannot inspect, contest or choose to join.

### Q: Why do airlines charge so many different fares for the same flight?

Airlines learned, after deregulation freed fare setting in the United States in 1978, that a seat is several products wearing one label. A seat sold months ahead to a traveller with flexible dates holds a different value from the same seat sold on the final day to a traveller whose meeting cannot move, and an empty seat at departure is worth nothing at all. Revenue management systems divide the cabin into fare classes with rules about refunds, changes and advance purchase, then open and close those classes as bookings arrive and forecasts change. Most fare differences on a flight come from that machinery, which responds to timing and demand conditions rather than to a passenger's identity. Personal data can refine the offers wrapped around a fare, such as bundles and upgrades, but the sprawling fare ladder itself is chiefly dynamic and group based discrimination refined over decades.

### Q: Did Amazon really charge different customers different prices?

In 2000, Amazon customers comparing notes in an online DVD forum discovered that the same discs carried different figures for different buyers, and the episode became the best documented early case of online price variation reaching public view. Amazon apologized for the confusion, described the variation as a random price test unrelated to customer demographics, and refunded the difference to buyers who had paid more. The company's account placed the episode in the category of price experimentation, a standard retail practice of testing figures to measure demand response, rather than in the category of estimating individual buyers. The distinction matters for evidence: the forum comparison proved that figures differed, while the reason for the difference rested on the company's explanation. The case endures in pricing debates less for what it proved than for the reaction it caused, which showed how quickly a discovered difference becomes a question about fairness.

### Q: What happened when Coca-Cola suggested weather-based soda prices?

In 1999, Douglas Ivester, chairman of Coca-Cola, remarked in a magazine interview that vending machines equipped with temperature sensors could raise the price of a soft drink when the weather grew hot, on the logic that demand rises with heat and a chilled drink is worth more on a hot afternoon. The reaction from the public and the press was swift and hostile, competitors signalled that their machines would hold prices steady, and the company dropped the idea without testing it in the market. The episode is a study in the difference between a mechanism and its acceptance. Weather based pricing is dynamic, responding to a condition rather than to a person, and similar logic governs accepted practices from seasonal resort rates to electricity tariffs. The vending machine proposal failed because the condition was visible, the thirst was personal and the increase arrived at the exact moment of greatest need, a combination that made an ordinary demand argument feel like exploitation.

### Q: Is it legal to charge two customers different prices for the same product?

In the United States, no general federal law forbids a retailer from charging two consumers different figures for the same product, and the main federal price discrimination statute, the Robinson-Patman Act of 1936, addresses sales to competing resellers rather than final consumers. Legality changes with the reason for the difference and the market involved. A difference produced by deception, such as advertising one figure while engineering another, can be challenged under Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act and under state consumer protection statutes. A difference that tracks race, sex or another protected characteristic can violate civil rights and lending laws in the markets those laws cover. European law adds a disclosure duty for personalized prices rather than a ban. The position therefore depends on jurisdiction, sector and mechanism, and a general answer describes documented law without deciding any individual case.

### Q: What is surge pricing and is it based on personal data?

Surge pricing raises a figure for everyone in a defined place and window when demand overwhelms supply, as when ride requests in a district multiply beyond the drivers available there. The multiplier responds to the imbalance, not to the identity of any rider, and its stated purpose is allocative: a higher figure rations scarce rides toward the riders who value them most and draws more drivers toward the shortage. On that definition, surge pricing is dynamic pricing in a sharp form and is not based on personal data. Confusion arises because the same platforms also set individual figures through other systems, and because a surge figure feels personal when it arrives during an emergency or a storm. The evidentiary question stays constant: a figure that rises for every requester in the zone is surge, while a figure that differs between two requesters in the same zone at the same minute requires a different explanation.

### Q: How does personalized pricing affect gig workers and their pay?

The same estimation machinery that sets a customer's price can set a worker's pay offer, with the direction of inference reversed. A platform estimating a rider asks how much that rider will bear; a platform estimating a driver or courier asks how little that worker will accept for a given trip or delivery, given location, history, past acceptance patterns and the alternatives visible to the platform. Uber introduced upfront pricing for riders in 2016, and its expanded upfront offers to drivers in 2022 decoupled driver pay from a fixed share of the rider fare, so the amount a passenger paid and the amount a driver received became separate calculations rather than two shares of one figure. Opacity carries the same consequence in both directions: a worker shown a single offer, with no view of offers made to others for identical work, cannot judge whether the figure reflects the task or an estimate of personal necessity.

### Q: What is price optimization in insurance?

Price optimization in insurance is the practice of adjusting a premium using factors other than the policyholder's expected claims cost and the insurer's expenses, most controversially an estimate of how much of an increase a policyholder will tolerate before shopping for a rival insurer. Traditional ratemaking builds a premium from risk: the probability and size of claims for a class of drivers or homeowners, plus administrative cost and a margin. Optimization layers a behavioral estimate over that base, so two policyholders with near identical risk profiles can face different increases because one has a long, inert history with the insurer and the other has switched providers before. State insurance regulators in the United States objected that the practice breaks the link between premium and risk that insurance law is built around, and regulators in more than a dozen states moved during the 2010s to restrict or prohibit its use in personal lines.

### Q: Can a ZIP code change the price a person is offered?

A ZIP code can change an offered figure, because location is a cheap, available signal that correlates with income, competition and cost, and pricing systems have used it as a sorting device. Reporting by Julia Angwin and Surya Mattu for ProPublica in 2015 found that the Princeton Review's online SAT preparation courses carried different prices by ZIP code, nearly double in some places, and that residents of ZIP codes with large Asian populations were more likely to face the higher figure. The company said its prices reflected market rates by geographic area. The case shows why a geographic variable deserves scrutiny beyond its stated purpose: a ZIP code sorts by place on its surface, while underneath it can carry the composition of the population living there, so a rule written about geography can produce a pattern that follows a demographic line the seller may never have named.

### Q: How do economists judge whether price discrimination is fair or efficient?

Economists separate two judgments that public argument often merges. Efficiency is judged chiefly by output, following the question Joan Robinson posed in 1933: discrimination improves total welfare when it expands the quantity traded, serving buyers a uniform figure would exclude, and merely redistributes welfare when the same quantity changes hands at re-sorted figures. Fairness is judged by distribution, by asking who gains and who loses, and by procedural tests such as whether the rule behind a figure is visible and open to all comers. Dirk Bergemann, Benjamin Brooks and Stephen Morris showed in the American Economic Review in 2015 that the same market information can support divisions of surplus ranging from buyers keeping nearly everything to a seller capturing nearly everything, which means the verdict in any real market depends on structure: the strength of rivals, the visibility of other figures and the quality of each buyer's outside option.

### Q: What did the Federal Trade Commission find in its surveillance pricing study?

The Federal Trade Commission opened its surveillance pricing study with Section 6(b) orders in July 2024, directed to eight intermediary firms that supply pricing products and services built on consumer data. The interim staff findings published in January 2025 described what those firms said their tools could use and do, including the use of location, browsing history and mouse movements as signals that could feed pricing, and described a market in which granular consumer information was gathered and sold for commercial targeting. The findings were careful about the boundary of the evidence: a description of capability and practice is not a determination that a specific retailer charged a specific consumer a personalized price, and the study made no such determination. A study of that kind maps machinery and inputs first, because the transaction level records needed for a case level finding sit inside sellers and are examined, if at all, through separate processes.

### Q: Does European law require sellers to disclose personalized prices?

European law requires disclosure in a defined situation rather than across all pricing. The Omnibus Directive, Directive 2019/2161, obliges a trader to inform a consumer when a price presented to that consumer was personalized on the basis of automated decision making and the profiling of consumer behavior, and the information must arrive before the consumer is bound by the contract. The duty does not forbid the personalized figure, does not cap the difference from any standard figure and does not require the trader to state what another buyer was charged. Its mechanism is transparency: a consumer told that a figure was generated for one person can treat the figure as negotiable information rather than as a market fact, compare rival offers or walk away. Member states carry the duty in national law, and national consumer authorities enforce it alongside the broader data protection frame of the General Data Protection Regulation.

### Q: How can researchers tell if a price was personalized?

Researchers build the comparison that ordinary shopping never provides. Controlled accounts are created in pairs or groups that differ in a single trait, such as browsing history, device or stated location, while the item, the hour and the delivery details are held constant, and identical price requests are issued from each account many times. A gap that follows the varied trait across repetitions, rather than scattering randomly or tracking stock levels, points toward a price responding to identity. Aniko Hannak and co-authors used a version of that design in 2014 across 16 e-commerce sites, combining clean accounts with trained accounts that carried manufactured histories. The method has firm limits: a controlled study can demonstrate a difference and narrow its likely source, but intent sits inside the firm, and a model that learned a proxy for a trait produces the same observable pattern as a model instructed to use the trait itself.

### Q: Why do companies keep personalized pricing hidden if it is legal?

Secrecy protects the mechanism in several ways at once. A buyer who treats a quoted figure as the standard market figure compares it against a budget, while a buyer who knows the figure was built from personal signals compares it against fairness, against the figure a neighbour received and against the seller's motives, and each of those comparisons weakens acceptance. Disclosure also invites consequences that legality alone does not prevent: rivals can advertise against the practice, regulators can ask how the estimates are built, journalists and researchers can design audits around a declared method, and buyers can change the signals fed to the model. The Amazon episode of 2000 showed the reputational arithmetic clearly, since refunds were cheap beside the public reaction. A practice can therefore be lawful, profitable and still dependent on darkness, because its profitability rests partly on buyers mistaking an individual estimate for a public price.
