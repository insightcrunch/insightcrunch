The privy purse abolition of 1971 began with a defeat. On 5 September 1970, the Rajya Sabha refused the special majority that a constitutional amendment demands, and the Constitution amendment bill through which Indira Gandhi's government proposed to end the privy purses of the former rulers of the princely states fell short by a fraction of a vote. The cabinet met the same evening. Before that night ended, President V. V. Giri, encamped at Hyderabad, had signed a set of identical one-paragraph orders under Article 366(22) of the Constitution, each directing that a named ruler cease to be recognised as the ruler of his former state. The orders, dated 6 September 1970, stopped every privy purse in India at a stroke and stripped every former ruler of official standing. Fifteen months later, the same government carried the same object through the front door of the Constitution: the Constitution (Twenty-Sixth Amendment) Act, assented to on 28 December 1971, omitted Articles 291 and 362, inserted Article 363A, and abolished the privy purse in law.

Between those two instruments sits the question this article answers. The standard account of the abolition treats it as the removal of an anachronism: hereditary payments to former princes offended a democratic republic, the payments ended, and the republic moved closer to its own egalitarian professions. That account is correct about the substance and silent about everything that decides the history. It cannot explain why a government that believed abolition to be a matter of principle reached first for an executive shortcut that its own law officers should have known the Constitution forbade. It cannot explain why the amendment, once the Supreme Court had mapped the only lawful route, waited for a general election rather than returning to Parliament within weeks. And it cannot explain why the amendment's sponsors presented a measure worth roughly Rs 4 crore a year as a centrepiece of a national campaign. The reading defended here can explain all three facts. The privy purse abolition was Move 3 in Indira Gandhi's coordinated anti-syndicate campaign: the Congress split of November 1969 supplied the vehicle, the bank nationalisation of July 1969 supplied the programme, and the abolition of 1970 to 1971 supplied the demonstration, a public proof, conducted through the most demanding procedure the Constitution contains, that the Prime Minister commanded her legislators, could reverse a Supreme Court judgment by lawful means, and owned the populist ground on which the 1971 election had been fought. Its fiscal value was small. Its political value was the function.

## The Standard Reading: An Anachronism Removed

The standard reading has the merit of beginning from a genuine incongruity. The privy purses were annual payments, charged on the Consolidated Fund of India and free of income tax, made to men and women whose sole qualification was descent from rulers who had surrendered sovereignty between 1947 and 1949. In a republic whose Constitution opens with a promise of equality of status and of opportunity, and whose Article 18 abolishes titles of honour, a hereditary pension roll for former royalty sat awkwardly. Indira Gandhi stated the incongruity in the sentence that sponsors of abolition repeated throughout the debates of 1970 and 1971: the concept of rulership, with privy purses and special privileges unrelated to any current functions and social purposes, is incompatible with an egalitarian social order. The sentence is quoted in the parliamentary record of the period and captures the principled case in its strongest form. Payments attached to birth, carrying no duties, defensible only as the price of a settlement made under the pressure of 1947, looked to a generation of legislators elected on universal franchise like a survival from the durbar inside the republic.

Textbook treatments, and the encyclopaedic summary that most readers meet first, arrange the events as a tidy procedural sequence: a bill defeated in 1970, a presidential order struck down by the Supreme Court in Madhavrao Scindia v Union of India, and a constitutional amendment in 1971 that completed the work. In that arrangement the politics dissolves into procedure. Each step appears as the mechanical correction of the step before, and the abolition reads as an idea whose time had come, delayed briefly by institutional friction. V. P. Menon's integration history of 1956 is then mined for the background of the settlement, Granville Austin's Working a Democratic Constitution of 1999 for the constitutional mechanics, and the story closes with the princely order's curtain.

The sequence is accurate and the reading built on it is incomplete, in ways that the dates alone expose. The government of India had possessed the principled argument since 1967, when the Congress Working Committee wrote abolition into its ten-point programme, and it had spent three years in negotiation with the rulers before moving a bill. The argument did not change between September 1970 and December 1971. What changed was the government's parliamentary position, its appetite for constitutional confrontation, and its need for a demonstration. A reading that ranks the principle first and the strategy second must treat the shortcut order of 6 September 1970 as an aberration. The order was no aberration. It was the strategy, visible without its later constitutional clothing: take the object by the fastest available instrument, and let the instrument be judged afterwards. The Supreme Court's judgment of 15 December 1970 closed that instrument, and the government's response was not retreat but escalation to the heaviest instrument of all, a constitutional amendment carried on the back of a general election. Timing and method are the evidence. This article reads the abolition through them.

### How much money did the privy purses actually cost India each year?

The White Paper on Indian States of 1950 put the aggregate of all privy purses at roughly Rs 5.8 crore a year at the settlement, and reductions on successions brought the total down to about Rs 4 crore a year by 1970, a small fraction of one per cent of Union government expenditure.

## The Integration Bargain: What the Purse Was Buying

The privy purse was not a gift, and the men who designed it did not describe it as one. Between August 1947 and the commencement of the Constitution on 26 January 1950, the States Ministry under Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, with V. P. Menon as its secretary and chief negotiator, absorbed more than five hundred princely states into the Indian Union through Instruments of Accession and merger covenants. The rulers surrendered defence, external affairs and communications first, and then, in the merger agreements of 1948 and 1949, surrendered sovereignty itself: their territories, revenues, armies and administrations passed to the Union or to the new provinces, and their states ceased to exist as political units. The consideration moving the other way had three parts. The rulers retained their personal property. They kept titles, gun salutes and ceremonial precedence as dignities recognised by the President. And they received the privy purse, an annual sum scaled to the revenue of the surrendered state, guaranteed first by covenant and then by the Constitution itself.

Menon's account, The Story of the Integration of the Indian States, published in 1956, states the logic without embarrassment. The purse was the price of a transfer of sovereignty accomplished almost entirely without war. The sums were calibrated, not lavish: the States Ministry applied a tapering scale to state revenues, imposed a ceiling that only eleven rulers exceeded, and fixed the largest purse, that of the Nizam of Hyderabad, at Rs 43 lakh a year in Indian currency, against the surrender of a state the size of a European kingdom with revenues to match. At the other end of the roll, the ruler of Katodia in Saurashtra received Rs 192 a year. The great majority of the 278 rulers on the purse roll received under Rs 50,000 annually. Menon's rough arithmetic of the alternative, a subcontinent in which even a fraction of the larger states had contested integration by force or by foreign patronage, made the aggregate of roughly Rs 5.8 crore a year read as the cheapest sovereignty ever purchased.

The Constituent Assembly converted the bargain into constitutional text on 12 October 1949, and Patel himself defended the conversion on the floor. His speech of that date, recorded in the Constituent Assembly Debates, asked the Assembly to weigh the purse settlements as consideration for the surrender by the rulers of all their ruling powers and for the dissolution of their states as separate units, and he named the alternative plainly: failure to honour the guarantee would be a breach of faith and would prejudice the stabilisation of the new order. The Assembly accepted the argument. Article 291 charged the privy purse sums on the Consolidated Fund of India, placing them beyond annual parliamentary vote, and declared them free of income tax. Article 362 directed Parliament and the state legislatures, when legislating for the rulers, to have due regard to the guarantees given under the covenants respecting their personal rights, privileges and dignities. Article 366(22) defined the Ruler as the prince, chief or other person recognised by the President as the ruler of an Indian state, the hinge on which recognition, and therefore the purse, turned. Article 363 completed the architecture by barring the courts from disputes arising out of the covenants themselves, a bar the Supreme Court would later hold did not license the executive to extinguish the purse by redefining the payee out of existence.

The bargain's consensual character had three celebrated exceptions, and the exceptions explain why the general settlement took the form it did. Junagadh's Nawab acceded to Pakistan against his subjects' geography and wishes, and a plebiscite of February 1948 reversed the accession. Hyderabad's Nizam held out for independence until the Indian Army's police action of September 1948 ended the standoff. Kashmir's Maharaja signed under invasion in October 1947, on terms that produced a separate constitutional track. Against those three fires, the remaining accessions and mergers, covering the great majority of the princely map, were negotiated settlements signed without a shot, and the purse was the consideration that made signature rational for rulers who retained armies, treasuries and, in several cases, plausible foreign patrons. Patel and Menon's wager was that a generation of hereditary payments cost less than one contested frontier. The wager's terms also explain the ferocity of 1970's breach-of-faith charge: the men who had signed away kingdoms in reliance on a guaranteed income included houses whose signatures had been genuinely free, and their case in the Supreme Court was argued as the case of promisees against a promisor, not of pensioners against a paymaster.

The instruments beneath the text deserve their own clarity, since the litigation of 1970 turned on which of them a court could read. The Instruments of Accession of 1947, executed under the Indian Independence Act, ceded defence, external affairs and communications and no more. The standstill agreements froze administrative arrangements while terms were negotiated. The merger agreements and covenants of 1948 and 1949, by which rulers dissolved their states into provinces or into unions of states such as Saurashtra, Madhya Bharat and PEPSU, carried the privy purse clauses, fixing each sum and the guarantees of property, privilege and succession. It was these covenants that Article 363 withdrew from judicial cognisance and that Articles 291 and 362 translated into constitutional obligation, a double character, diplomatic in origin and constitutional in operation, on which both sides in 1970 built their cases: the rulers pleading the covenant's sanctity, the Union pleading its political nature, and the Court threading between them by enforcing the constitutional translation rather than the instrument.

Two features of this architecture matter for everything that followed. The first is that the purse was deliberately placed beyond ordinary politics. Charged expenditure and a constitutional guarantee meant that no annual budget debate could trim it and no simple-majority statute could end it; only a constitutional amendment, or a judicially sustainable reinterpretation, could touch it. The second is that recognition under Article 366(22) was the purse's single point of failure. The definition made the President the identifier of the Ruler for constitutional purposes, and it was through that definitional door, rather than through Article 291 itself, that the government of 1970 attempted to walk. Understanding the abolition therefore requires holding the bargain and the door in view together: a settlement drafted to be amendment-proof, attacked through its one administrative seam, defended by the Supreme Court at that seam, and finally dismantled by the amendment its drafters had always known would be required.

## The Anatomy of the Purse Roll: Who Received What, and on What Terms

The purse roll that the republic inherited, and that the States Ministry published in the White Paper on Indian States of 1950, was steeply graded. At its head stood the Nizam of Hyderabad with Rs 43 lakh a year in Indian currency, followed by Baroda at about Rs 26.5 lakh, Mysore at about Rs 26 lakh and Gwalior at Rs 25 lakh, with Jaipur, Jodhpur, Bikaner, Travancore, Patiala, Indore and Bhopal completing the eleven houses whose purses exceeded Rs 10 lakh. Below them the roll descended through ninety-one rulers drawing between Rs 1 lakh and Rs 10 lakh, and then through the long tail of petty chiefs whose purses ran to a few hundred rupees, ending at Katodia's Rs 192. The grading followed the merger covenants' arithmetic: a taper applied to the surrendered state's average revenues, softened by regard for the ruler's standing and the size of the territory absorbed. What the grading produced, politically, was a class with a common grievance and wildly unequal stakes. The eleven great houses stood to lose incomes that sustained palaces, stables, retinues and the remaining theatre of rulership; the tail stood to lose sums that would not have kept a district officer. When the crisis came in 1970, the litigation was accordingly financed and led from the head of the roll: the lead petitioner was the Maharaja of Gwalior, and the arguments were framed by the houses for whom the purse remained a material estate.

The terms attached to the purse matter as much as its size. The sums under Article 291 were charged on the Consolidated Fund, which in the Constitution's scheme means they were not submitted to the annual vote of Parliament; they were free of income tax; and they descended, under the covenants and the practice of recognition, to the successor whom the President recognised under Article 366(22) on the previous ruler's death. Recognition of a successor was in form an act of state and in practice routine, and it was this routine hinge that made the purse hereditary in operation. Around the purse clustered the privileges that Article 362 protected in general words: titles and styles, salutes, precedence at official functions, exemption from customs duties on personal effects, immunity from certain processes, the dignities of a court that no longer ruled. The settlement's defenders, in 1949 and again in 1970, insisted that the whole apparatus was contractual consideration and that each element stood or fell with the covenants. The settlement's critics replied that a republic may not contract out of its own egalitarian premises in perpetuity, and that the covenants' consideration had been rendered in full by twenty years of payment on a tapering scale that the negotiators of 1948 and 1949 had designed to diminish as successions intervened. The aggregate's fall from roughly Rs 5.8 crore toward about Rs 4 crore across two decades records that design at work: the settlement was built to wither, and the argument of 1970 and 1971 was, at bottom, an argument about whether the withering should be allowed to run its slow course or be completed at a stroke by the state that had granted the terms.

One further entry in the anatomy deserves a place, because it shaped the politics of the crisis. The purse was paid to the ruler personally, not to the former state or its people, and several recipients were among the wealthiest men in India on any reckoning: the Nizam's personal fortune, held in trusts and securities that V. P. Menon's narrative describes in detail, ran to sums that dwarfed his purse. Abolition's opponents drew from this the conclusion that the purse was a token, a matter of honour rather than maintenance, and that tokens given in exchange for kingdoms are the last obligations a state should repudiate. Abolition's supporters drew the opposite conclusion from the same fact: a token is a symbol, and a state is entitled, indeed obliged, to withdraw its symbols from hereditary privilege when the symbol contradicts the state's founding text. Both conclusions concede the premise on which this article's adjudication rests. By 1970 the privy purse was a political symbol before it was anything else, and symbols are the currency in which campaigns are conducted.

One office in the settlement outlived its usefulness faster than the purse and took part of the princely order's dignity with it. Under the Constitution as commenced, the larger unions of states and the great Part B states were headed by Rajpramukhs, former rulers installed as constitutional governors of the territories they had surrendered: the Maharaja of Mysore, the Nizam of Hyderabad, the Maharaja of Travancore, the Maharaja of Gwalior in Madhya Bharat, the Maharaja of Patiala in PEPSU and their counterparts in Rajasthan, Saurashtra and Vindhya Pradesh drew allowances as officeholders, distinct from their purses, and performed the ceremonial and constitutional functions of a governor. The States Reorganisation of 1956 abolished the Rajpramukh office along with the Part B states, and the former rulers who held it reverted to purely private eminence, sustained thereafter by the purse, the title and the privileges alone. The reorganisation's lesson was available to any attentive observer of the period: the republic had already retired one layer of the princely settlement by ordinary constitutional housekeeping, without crisis, because the Rajpramukhs' office carried functions the new map no longer needed and no coalition had organised to defend it. The purse survived 1956 precisely because it was money and status rather than office, and because its defence could be organised around the sanctity of covenants. The fifteen years between the two extinctions measure the difference between a provision that dies of redundancy and a provision that must be killed in public.

## The Settlement Under Stress: Two Decades of Erosion

Between 1950 and 1967 the purse survived on the strength of the founding generation's sense of obligation, and the terms of its survival foretell the manner of its death. Jawaharlal Nehru's government made no move against the purses, although the Congress's socialist rhetoric of the 1950s sat uneasily beside them; the official position, repeated in parliamentary answers across the decade, was that the settlements were a national commitment into which the republic had entered with its eyes open. Private members periodically moved resolutions of abolition and were talked out of them or defeated. The States Ministry's successor department in the Home Ministry administered recognition and succession as routine business, and the purse roll quietly shortened as rulers died and the recognition of successors applied the covenants' reductions. Erosion, in this period, was administrative and silent, and it gave the settlement's critics no theatre in which to perform.

The elections of 1967 changed the settlement's political weather in two ways at once. The Congress lost its majority in eight states, and the victorious coalitions of the opposition included, in Rajasthan, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh and Orissa, parties in which princely influence was an organising asset: Rajputana's former ruling houses delivered blocs of rural votes to the Swatantra Party and the Jana Sangh, and the Congress learned to speak of a princes' interest allied against it. In the same season, the Congress's own left, the Young Turks led in the Lok Sabha by Chandra Shekhar, pressed the leadership to honour the party's egalitarian professions in deed, and the ten-point programme of May 1967 was the leadership's answer, a list of radical commitments produced under pressure of defeat. The purses thus acquired, in a single year, both a partisan address and a party-political sponsor. They were no longer an antique embarrassment shared by the whole political class. They were a marker dividing the Congress's insurgent left and its leader from the opposition's princely constituency and from the Congress's own old guard, which remained attached to the settlement its founding saints had signed. The seventeen years of administrative quiet had preserved the purse by keeping it boring. The politics of 1967 made it interesting, and in the politics of the Indian republic after 1967, interesting questions of privilege were settled in public, by the loudest available procedure.

## The Long Fuse: From the Ten-Point Programme of 1967 to the Bill of 1970

Abolition entered the Congress programme formally in May 1967, when the Congress Working Committee adopted a ten-point programme that included the abolition of the privy purses and privileges of the former rulers. The date deserves emphasis, because it disciplines both readings of 1971. The principled case was party policy three and a half years before the amendment, under a Congress still led organisationally by the men Indira Gandhi would later fight, and it was party policy in a year when the Congress had just lost power in half the states and was searching for a programme of renewal. Abolition was not invented as a weapon in 1970. It was an agreed commitment waiting for a sponsor willing to spend political capital on it, and for most of the intervening period no sponsor appeared.

The government's first course was negotiation. Y. B. Chavan, the Home Minister, conducted discussions with representatives of the rulers between 1967 and 1969 aimed at a voluntary surrender of the purses, or their commutation on agreed terms. The negotiations failed on the structure of the bargain itself. The rulers, organised and advised, treated the covenants as contracts and the purses as property; a voluntary surrender required either compensation the government would not offer or unanimity the rulers would not give. Chavan reported the deadlock, and the government's options narrowed to the two the Constitution left open: amendment by special majority, or nothing. In his statement of objects and reasons dated 14 May 1970, attached to the amendment bill of that year, Chavan recorded the failure in the dry language of the form: the government had decided to terminate the privy purses and privileges of the rulers of the former Indian States, and hence the bill.

The rulers were not, by 1970, politically weightless antiques, and the geography of their support sharpened the question's partisan edge. Former rulers sat in both Houses of Parliament and in state legislatures, and they sat disproportionately in the opposition. Gayatri Devi of Jaipur had won the Jaipur Lok Sabha seat in 1962 as a Swatantra Party candidate with a margin that entered the record books. Vijaya Raje Scindia of Gwalior, mother of the future petitioner Madhavrao Scindia, anchored the Jana Sangh in Madhya Pradesh. The Swatantra Party and the Jana Sangh both defended the covenants on the floor of Parliament, and after November 1969 the Congress (O), the organisation of the syndicate bosses whom Indira Gandhi had defeated for control of the party, inherited the defence as part of its general brief against her. The consequence is visible in the parliamentary arithmetic of the period. To defend the purse was, by 1970, to stand with the coalition of parties and factions arrayed against the Prime Minister; to abolish it was to strike at a symbol that coalition had adopted. That alignment does not taint the principled case, which rested on the text of the Preamble and needed no partisan help. It does mean that by the time abolition moved from programme to bill, its function in the factional war was already loaded, and no participant in the debates of 1970 and 1971 can have been innocent of the fact.

The coupling of the purses to the larger campaign was performed in public at the Bangalore session of the All India Congress Committee in July 1969, the conclave at which the syndicate's majority on the Congress Parliamentary Board nominated Neelam Sanjiva Reddy for the presidency against Indira Gandhi's wishes. Her written note to the Working Committee of 9 July 1969, circulated through Fakhruddin Ali Ahmed and remembered as her stray thoughts, pressed the party toward the radical programme the Young Turks around Chandra Shekhar were demanding. Within ten days she had taken the finance portfolio from Morarji Desai on 16 July 1969, announced the nationalisation of fourteen banks by ordinance on 19 July 1969, and announced, in the same breath of public measures, the government's intention to withdraw the special privileges and purses of the princes. Bipan Chandra's narrative of the period in India Since Independence records the pairing exactly: the banks and the princes were announced together, received together by the left and the press, and understood together by the syndicate as the programme of a rival power inside the party. The privy purse question thus entered the factional war not in 1970, when the bill was drafted, but in the campaign's first week, as the second-named item of the July 1969 offensive. The August 1969 presidential election completed the alignment: V. V. Giri, standing as an independent with the support of the Communist parties and Indira Gandhi's call for a conscience vote, defeated the official Congress candidate Reddy, and the President who owed his office to that defiance was the President who, thirteen months later, signed the derecognition orders at Hyderabad without recorded hesitation.

The bill Chavan introduced in 1970 proposed the surgical version of abolition: omit the privy purse provisions, end recognition, and let the settlement close. Its parliamentary journey is the hinge of the whole episode, and it belongs to the next section. What the long fuse establishes is narrower. Abolition was old policy, honestly held, blocked for three years by the absence of the special majority the Constitution required. The events of 1969, the split and the bank nationalisation, did not create the policy. They created the politician who needed it, and the manner of its eventual enactment bears her signature rather than the committee's.

## September 1970: One Vote Short in the Rajya Sabha, and the Hyderabad Orders

The Constitution amendment bill of 1970 was voted on in the Lok Sabha on 2 September 1970 and passed by 332 votes to 154, comfortably beyond the special majority of the total membership and two-thirds of members present and voting that Article 368 prescribes. Three days later, on 5 September 1970, the Rajya Sabha considered the same bill and the motion failed: 149 members voted for it and 75 against, and because the two-thirds requirement is computed on members present and voting, the bill fell short of the requisite majority by a fraction of a single vote. The figures matter and are not in dispute; they are recited in the Supreme Court's own judgment in the litigation that followed. A government commanding the lower House by a wide margin had been stopped in the upper House by the arithmetic of a constitution that distributes the amending power between the two chambers and the states.

The Rajya Sabha's composition explains the bill's death without recourse to mystery. The upper House is elected by the state legislatures in biennial thirds, and in September 1970 it still embodied the Congress of 1967 and 1968, a party in which the syndicate's state bosses commanded loyalties the Prime Minister had spent a year fighting. After the split of November 1969, dozens of upper-House members sat with Congress (O) or with the opposition parties, and the government's whips could not promise two-thirds of those present and voting on a measure that the Swatantra Party, the Jana Sangh and the Congress (O) had combined to resist. The fraction by which the bill failed, a single vote at the margin of the two-thirds computation, has invited two generations of what-if retelling. Its analytical meaning is colder. The amending power in India is deliberately distributed across two differently composed chambers, and in 1970 the distribution worked as designed: a government strong in the popular House and weak in the federal House was refused. The orders of the following night were an attempt to secede from that distribution, and the Supreme Court's judgment was the distribution defending itself.

The cabinet met that evening, and the decision taken in it reveals the government's hierarchy of preferences with unusual clarity. The lawful options after 5 September were patience and re-presentation: build the missing votes, wait for the Rajya Sabha's staggered retirements to alter its composition, or carry the question to the electorate. The cabinet chose none of them. It advised the President to withdraw recognition from every ruler in India en masse, under the definitional clause, Article 366(22), on the theory that a person not recognised as a Ruler could claim no purse under Article 291 and no regard under Article 362. President Giri signed the orders at Hyderabad that night. Each order, dated 6 September 1970 and issued in the name of the President from the Ministry of Home Affairs, was laconic to the point of insolence toward the settlement it cancelled: the President directed that the named ruler, identified by the full durbar style the republic still accorded him, do cease to be recognised as the ruler of his former state. All the orders were notified together in the Gazette of India of 19 September 1970. The purses stopped forthwith, and the privileges, from customs exemptions to gun salutes, stopped with them.

### Why did the 1970 bill fail when Congress commanded the Lok Sabha?

Article 368 requires an amendment to pass each House by a majority of its total membership and two-thirds of members present and voting. The government held the Lok Sabha, passing the bill there by 332 votes to 154 on 2 September 1970, but the Rajya Sabha's 149 votes on 5 September fell a fraction of one vote short of two-thirds.

Read the night of 5 and 6 September 1970 carefully, because the whole argument of this article rests on it. A government whose object was simply the removal of an anachronism had no need of haste; the anachronism had survived since 1950 and would survive a further session. A government whose object included the demonstration of will had every reason for haste. The derecognition orders were signed within hours of the Rajya Sabha vote, before the political meaning of the defeat could settle as the meaning. Defiance was converted into action at a speed that only a prepared instrument permits, and the instrument had plainly been prepared: identical orders for every recognised ruler in India are not drafted between dinner and midnight. The episode also fixed the cast of the legal drama. The rulers' remedy lay in the Supreme Court under Article 32, and their chosen petitioner, whose petition would give the case its name, filed within the week: Madhavrao Scindia of Gwalior, grandson of a maharaja who had been among the settlement's pillars, and son of a Rajmata who led the opposition in his state, presented the first of the writ petitions, numbered 376 to 383 of 1970, on 11 September 1970.

## Madhavrao Scindia v Union of India: The Court Draws the Line

The Supreme Court heard the privy purse petitions before a bench of eleven judges, the largest constitution of the Court assembled since the Golak Nath case of 1967, and delivered judgment on 15 December 1970. The citation is 1971 (1) SCC 85, also reported as AIR 1971 SC 530. By a majority of nine to two, the Court struck the presidential orders down as illegal, ultra vires and inoperative, and declared the petitioners entitled to all their pre-existing rights and privileges, including the right to the privy purses, as if the orders had not been passed. Justices G. K. Mitter and A. N. Ray dissented. The rulers were represented by N. A. Palkhivala and M. C. Setalvad, the two most formidable constitutional advocates of the period; the Union was defended by the Attorney General, Niren De.

Chief Justice M. Hidayatullah's judgment for the majority proceeded from the architecture of the settlement rather than from sympathy for princes. Recognition under Article 366(22), the Court held, was not a discretionary favour that the President could withdraw at pleasure from persons admittedly entitled to it; the clause existed so that the constitutional machinery could identify the Ruler with whom the covenants had been made and to whom Article 291 promised payment. The power to recognise answered the question who the Ruler was. It did not confer a power to declare that there was no Ruler at all, still less to exercise that declaration against every ruler in India in a single night for the admitted purpose of escaping a payment obligation that the Constitution had charged on the Consolidated Fund. An en masse derecognition, adopted because a bill had failed in Parliament, was in the majority's analysis a colourable exercise of power: the form of a definitional act used to achieve an amendment's substance without an amendment's majority. The obligation under Article 291 arose from the covenants and the constitutional text, and Article 363's bar on covenant disputes protected the bargain from litigation; it did not arm the executive with a solvent for the bargain's obligations.

Justice J. C. Shah, concurring, stated the consequence in the sentence that fixed the road to 1971: the President has no power to withdraw the recognition already given to the rulers except by following the procedure prescribed by the Constitution for amendment. The dissent of Justices Mitter and Ray treated recognition as a political act within executive competence and would have left the orders standing; it attracted two votes of eleven. The judgment's technical narrowness deserves the emphasis the brief of history has always given it. The Court did not hold the privy purse eternal. It did not question Parliament's power to abolish the purse by constitutional amendment, and it pointed, in terms, to that remedy. What it forbade was the substitution of executive definition for constitutional amendment. Politically, however, the narrowness cut the other way. The Court had not merely blocked a shortcut; it had certified that abolition required the single most demanding legislative feat in the constitutional repertoire, a feat the government had attempted three months earlier and failed by a fraction of a vote in one House. Any government that then completed the feat would not merely change the law. It would exhibit, before the Court, the opposition and the electorate, a command of the Constitution's hardest procedure that the failed bill of September 1970 had thrown into doubt.

Between the orders and the judgment, the settlement lived in suspension, and the suspension's texture is part of the record the majority recited. From 6 September 1970 the payments stopped forthwith, as the orders directed; the rulers' establishments, maintained in several cases on the purse's expectation, faced a winter of unpaid obligation; and the legal question travelled from the Home Ministry's files to the Supreme Court's board within five days of the Gazette notification, at a speed that tells its own story about the preparedness of both sides. The judgment's operative declaration, that the petitioners were entitled to their rights as if the orders had not been passed, converted the suspended quarter into arrears recoverable in full, so that the government's three months of possession ended in restitution. The episode cost the exchequer nothing in the end and the government nothing it valued: the orders had already yielded their political return in the autumn of 1970, as proof to the campaign's audiences that the Prime Minister struck first and consulted the Constitution's custodians afterwards. A defeated instrument that has already performed its demonstrative work is not, in the arithmetic of campaigns, a defeat at all, and the swiftness of the dissolution that followed suggests the arithmetic was understood in the Prime Minister's house exactly so.

The government's public response to the judgment was acceptance of its letter. The orders were withdrawn in effect, the purses resumed, and no appeal to defiance was made. The strategic response was larger. Nine days after the judgment, on 27 December 1970, Indira Gandhi advised the President to dissolve the Lok Sabha a full year before its term expired, and the country went to a mid-term election in March 1971 fought on the slogan Garibi Hatao, abolish poverty. The privy purse question entered that election not as a line item but as evidence in the campaign's central parable: a Prime Minister of the poor blocked by princes, courts and an upper House, asking the electorate for the numbers to finish the work. The connection between the judgment and the dissolution is the second fact the standard reading cannot digest. Courts block governments in every democracy; governments that respond by dissolving the legislature and staking their survival on the blocked policy are announcing that the policy's value to them is measured in power, not in rupees.

## The Arguments at the Bar: What the Eleven Judges Were Asked to Hold

The pleadings in the Scindia case repay reconstruction, because the government's courtroom theory and its parliamentary theory were the same theory in different dress, and the Court's rejection of the one dictated the form of the other. Setalvad and Palkhivala, for the rulers, argued from the settlement's text. The covenants were agreements; Article 291 translated them into a charged constitutional obligation; Article 362 protected the surrounding rights; and Article 366(22) defined the Ruler for the purpose of identifying the beneficiary, not for the purpose of abolishing the benefit. Recognition, on this argument, was declaratory: it acknowledged a status created by the covenants and by succession under the former state's law and custom. A President who could derecognise at will could repeal Article 291 by signature, and a Constitution that required two-thirds of both Houses to amend a comma could not have lodged that power in a definitional clause. The mass character of the orders completed the proof: a power exercised against every holder simultaneously, on the night a bill failed, for the stated end of stopping payments the Constitution guaranteed, was exercised for a purpose foreign to the clause, and bad faith in the constitutional sense did not require proof of personal malice.

Niren De, for the Union, carried the contrary theory with the strength it possessed. Article 291 promised payment to the Ruler; the Ruler was whomsoever the President recognised; recognition was an act of state of the same character as the recognition of a foreign sovereign, into which courts do not inquire; and a purse whose payee the Constitution defined by recognition necessarily ended when recognition ended. The covenants, he argued, were political compacts between sovereigns, and Article 363 had withdrawn them from judicial cognisance precisely so that questions of this order would be settled in the political forum. There was force in the construction, and it persuaded two judges. What it could not answer was the sequence. The Union had sought the same end from Parliament three days before the orders, under a procedure the Constitution prescribes; the failure of that procedure was the orders' only occasion; and the majority's judgments return to that sequence from several directions, treating the timing not as background colour but as the operative fact that disclosed the orders' purpose. Hidayatullah's opinion also recorded what the orders swept away beyond money: the privileges and dignities of Article 362 fell with recognition, so that the executive's definitional stroke had extinguished a chapter of guaranteed rights without a single vote in either House.

One doctrinal knot in the case deserves separate statement, because the amendment of 1971 untied it permanently. Article 363 barred the jurisdiction of any court in a dispute arising out of a provision of a treaty, agreement, covenant or sanad entered into before the Constitution by a ruler of an Indian state. Read at its widest, the bar would have placed the covenants, and every obligation written in them, beyond judicial enforcement altogether, leaving the rulers with a constitutional promise in Article 291 and no forum in which to plead it. The majority declined the wide reading. The obligation on which the petitioners sued was not, in the Court's analysis, the covenant as a private instrument; it was the duty that Article 291 itself laid on the Union, a duty stated in the Constitution's text and enforceable as such, with the covenants serving as the duty's history rather than its source. The distinction mattered beyond the purse. It marked the boundary at which the founding generation's diplomatic instruments ended and the republic's justiciable text began, and it is one reason the Scindia judgment sits in the constitutional canon although the status it protected survived it by barely a year. The Twenty-Sixth Amendment, by omitting Article 291 and inserting Article 363A, removed the duty from the text; the bar of Article 363 then closed over the covenants undisturbed, which is why the rulers' later challenges had to attack the amendment itself rather than sue upon the bargain.

The bar's personnel carried their own history into the courtroom. Setalvad had been the first Attorney General of India and a framer-adjacent figure of the founding generation; his appearance for the rulers placed the settlement's original legal mind against its dismantlers. Palkhivala had argued the bank nationalisation case, R. C. Cooper v Union of India, decided on 10 February 1970, in which a bench of eleven had struck down the bank ordinance's parent Act on property and compensation grounds; he was, by the autumn of 1970, the standing counsel of every interest the campaign had struck. Niren De held the office Setalvad had first occupied. The privy purse case was therefore argued as a second round of a duel the whole legal world was watching, and its result, nine to two for the rulers, was read in the press of December 1970 as the Court's second successive defeat of the government's chosen instruments. That reading, fair or not, formed part of the political weather in which the decision to dissolve the Lok Sabha was taken nine days later.

## The Election of March 1971: Manufacturing the Amendment Majority

The fifth Lok Sabha election of March 1971 returned Congress (R) with 352 seats, a majority so large that the constitutional arithmetic of Article 368 changed character. In the Lok Sabha, the government could satisfy both limbs of the special majority from its own benches with votes to spare. In the Rajya Sabha, where the 1970 bill had died, the staggered biennial retirements and the Congress (R) victories in the state assemblies were rebuilding the upper House in the government's image. The electorate had not voted on the privy purse as a discrete question; the purse formed one strand of the Garibi Hatao platform, alongside bank nationalisation's defence and the promise of land ceilings, in a campaign the opposition Grand Alliance answered with the slogan Indira Hatao. Yet the mandate's usability for the amendment was exact. Every legislator elected on the Congress (R) ticket in March 1971 owed the ticket to the leader personally in a sense the pre-split Congress had never demanded, because the 1969 split had destroyed the state-level machines that once mediated between the legislator and the leadership. The members who would vote the Twenty-Sixth Amendment through Parliament were, institutionally, the Prime Minister's members. (The companion study of that election and the Bangladesh war that followed it, appearing later in this series, takes up the mandate's wider making; the privy purse amendment is one of its first expenditures.)

The election also settled the question of the Court. The Twenty-Fourth Amendment, which restored Parliament's power to amend the fundamental rights against the Golak Nath ruling of 1967 and obliged the President to assent to amendment bills, received assent on 5 November 1971. The Twenty-Fifth followed in the same season, curtailing the property right and inserting Article 31C. The privy purse bill travelled through Parliament in this company, one element of a legislative offensive whose combined object was to re-establish that the elected Parliament, and not the judiciary, held the Constitution's last word. To read the Twenty-Sixth Amendment in isolation, as the encyclopaedic sequence invites, is to miss the company in which it marched.

The dissolution itself was the campaign's boldest instrument and deserves its own reckoning. No Indian government had previously surrendered a year of its term to seek a fresh mandate on a programme; the Lok Sabha elected in 1967 had until 1972 to run. Indira Gandhi's stated ground was that the old House, elected before the split, no longer represented the country's will, and the opposition's Grand Alliance of Congress (O), the Jana Sangh, the Swatantra Party and the Samyukta Socialist Party obliged her framing by uniting around the single slogan Indira Hatao, remove Indira, to which her campaign replied with the period's most durable counter-slogan: they say Indira Hatao, I say Garibi Hatao; they want to remove me, I want to remove poverty. The exchange reduced the election to a plebiscite on her person, which was its design. In such a plebiscite, every blocked measure of 1970, the purses among them, became an exhibit of the obstruction the voter was asked to clear away. The result, 352 seats for Congress (R) in a House of 518, with the Grand Alliance's components reduced to fragments, was returned in the second week of March 1971. When the new Parliament assembled, the amendment arithmetic that had failed by a fraction of a vote in September 1970 no longer described a risk. It described a formality awaiting its date, and the government chose the date at leisure, in the winter session, in the same weeks that carried India into the Bangladesh war and raised the Prime Minister's authority to its wartime height.

## The Twenty-Sixth Amendment: Text, Debates and December 1971

The Constitution (Twenty-Sixth Amendment) Bill was debated in the Lok Sabha in November 1971 and passed through both Houses with the special majorities the 1970 bill had failed to gather, receiving the assent of the President on 28 December 1971 and commencing the same day; the Act was published in the Gazette of India of 29 December 1971. Its text is short enough to hold entire in the mind, and its drafting repays attention, because the government had learned from the Scindia judgment exactly where the settlement's joints were. The amendment did three things. It omitted Article 291, the charging provision that had promised the purse sums from the Consolidated Fund free of tax. It omitted Article 362, the direction that legislatures have due regard to the covenant guarantees of the rulers' personal rights, privileges and dignities. And it inserted a new Article 363A, headed, in the Gazette's plain words, recognition granted to rulers of Indian states to cease and privy purses to be abolished. Article 363A declared that the recognition granted to the rulers by the President shall cease, and that the privy purse is abolished and all rights, liabilities and obligations in respect of the privy purse are extinguished. A substituted clause (22) of Article 366 then froze the definition of Ruler to the persons recognised before the amendment's commencement, so that no succession could manufacture a new claimant through the definitional door the government itself had tried to use in 1970.

December 1971 gave the amendment a setting no parliamentary manager could have purchased. The Bangladesh war opened on 3 December, and on 16 December the Pakistani forces in the east surrendered at Dhaka; the Prime Minister who announced the surrender to the Lok Sabha stood at the summit of a popularity no Indian leader had commanded since 1947. The Twenty-Sixth Amendment received presidential assent on 28 December 1971, twelve days after the surrender, in the month of the government's greatest triumph. The bill had passed the Lok Sabha in November, before the war, and its upper-House passage belonged to the same December weeks; nothing in the record suggests the war was timed for the amendment or the amendment for the war. The conjunction nonetheless completed the demonstration the campaign had been building since 1969. The executive that had been refused by the Rajya Sabha in September 1970 and corrected by the Supreme Court in December 1970 closed the following December as the victor of a war, the author of a new state's liberation, and the holder of a freshly amended Constitution from which the princely order had been struck. Constitutional amendments are rarely carried on such a tide, and the sponsors' decision to spend the tide on this measure, rather than bank it, is the session's clearest statement of the measure's place in their design.

The debates of November and December 1971 replayed the arguments of 1970 with the power relation between the speakers inverted. The government spoke through the arithmetic of March 1971: abolition was the electorate's instruction, the egalitarian sentence about rulership and social purposes was read again, and the opposition's defence of the covenants was framed as a defence of the defeated coalition's clients. The Swatantra Party and the Jana Sangh held the ground Patel had marked out in 1949, that the purses were consideration under a bargain and their extinction a breach of faith by the republic toward the men whose signatures had made the republic's map. The Congress (O) benches, the syndicate's remnant, defended the settlement their own party's 1967 programme had already condemned, a discomfort the government benches did not allow to pass unremarked. What no speaker on the government side dwelt upon was money. A measure whose entire fiscal yield was about Rs 4 crore a year was carried through the Constitution's heaviest procedure, in the same session as the Twenty-Fourth and Twenty-Fifth Amendments, with the Prime Minister's authority visibly committed to its passage. The disproportion between the yield and the effort is the text's own commentary on where the measure's value lay.

The amendment's drafting choices close two escape routes that the litigation of 1970 had exposed, and both repay notice. The first is the choice of omission over modification. The government did not trim the purse, cap it, tax it or commute it into terminable annuities, devices that a statute-like amendment could have adopted and that would have left a residue of obligation for courts to police. It omitted Articles 291 and 362 entire, so that after 28 December 1971 the Constitution contained no promise capable of being breached, and it framed Article 363A in non-obstante words, notwithstanding anything in the Constitution, so that the cessation of recognition and the extinction of rights, liabilities and obligations in respect of the purse overrode any surviving text. No compensation was provided, and none was debated as a serious possibility: a compensated buyout would have conceded the property character of the purse that R. C. Cooper had vindicated for the banks and that the government was determined the purse should not bear. The second closed route is succession. By substituting a frozen definition of Ruler in Article 366(22), confined to those recognised before the commencement of the amendment, the text prevented the slow reassembly of the purse roll through future recognitions, the administrative revival that a change of government might otherwise have attempted by recognising heirs. A future Parliament wishing to restore the purses would have had to amend the Constitution again and say so. The abolition was drafted, in short, by lawyers who had lost in court once and intended the text to be litigation-proof and successor-proof, and the care they took is itself evidence of how much the government valued the result: negligible measures are not usually armoured.

## Privy Purse Abolition as Move Three: The Coordinated Campaign

The privy purse abolition belongs to a sequence, and the sequence is the argument. Between July 1969 and December 1971, Indira Gandhi fought for control of the Indian state against the coalition that had made her Prime Minister in 1966: the syndicate of Congress organisational bosses, the Morarji Desai wing of the parliamentary party, and, behind them, the propertied and princely interests that the opposition parties represented. The campaign that defeated that coalition proceeded by distinguishable moves, each converting a policy instrument into a demonstration of where authority lay. The reading of the abolition as Move 3 follows [the 1969 split as Move 1](/2019/03/26/1969-congress-split-decisive-moment/), in which her expulsion by the Congress (O) leadership on 12 November 1969 was answered by carrying a majority of the parliamentary party into Congress (R) and winning the ensuing contest for the party's name and symbol; and [the 1969 bank nationalisation as Move 2](/2019/04/01/bank-nationalisation-1969-politics/), in which the ordinance of 19 July 1969 taking fourteen major commercial banks into public ownership supplied the left programme on which the split was fought and won. The table below, the article's findable artifact, sets the three moves side by side with the political function of each named, and is cited in what follows as the Coordinated Campaign Trajectory, 1969 to 1971.

| Move | Date | Action | Political function |

Move 1's legal tail deserves its line in the ledger, because it shows the campaign being fought simultaneously in every forum the republic offered. After the split, both Congress factions claimed the party's name and its election symbol, the pair of bullocks, and the Election Commission, applying the test of majority in the party's organisational and legislative wings, recognised Indira Gandhi's Congress (R) as the Indian National Congress for electoral purposes. The Congress (O) faction carried the question to the courts, and in Sadiq Ali v Election Commission of India the Supreme Court sustained the Commission's majority test, confirming that the campaign for the party had been won in the forum of election law as it had been won in the parliamentary party. The sequence across forums is the campaign's signature. Between July 1969 and December 1971, the same contest was fought in the cabinet room, at the Bangalore conclave, in a presidential election, in the Election Commission, in two general elections' worth of campaigning compressed into one mid-term poll, in the division lobbies of both Houses, and in the Supreme Court, and it was won in all of them except the courtroom, whose single contrary verdict the Twenty-Sixth Amendment then answered. The privy purse question appears in that sequence as the thread that runs through the most forums: announced with the banks in July 1969, billed in 1970, ordered in September 1970, adjudicated in December 1970, campaigned on in March 1971 and amended away in December 1971.

The three moves overlapped in calendar time, nationalisation preceding the split by four months, because a campaign is a logic and not a parade. Read through the trajectory, the abolition performed three functions for its author, each distinct and each documented in the record of 1970 and 1971. The first was the demonstration of legislative command. A constitutional amendment is the one parliamentary act that cannot be managed by whips alone in a divided party; it requires two-thirds of members present and voting in two Houses whose members must attend and hold. Carrying it, eighteen months after the same bill had failed, exhibited a parliamentary party rebuilt as an instrument of the leader, the central fact of [Indira's four-phase office-rebuilding trajectory](/2019/03/21/indira-gandhi-personality-institution/). The second function was the public reversal of the Court. The amendment did not defy the Scindia judgment; it obeyed the judgment's own directions and thereby overcame it, teaching the constitutional system's actors that a judicial veto on this Prime Minister's programme was a delaying instrument, not a deciding one. The third was the completion of the populist account. Bank nationalisation had struck at concentrated private capital; the abolition struck at hereditary status; together they let the Garibi Hatao government present itself as the enemy of both forms of unearned eminence, money and birth, in a single parliamentary season.

### What happened to the former rulers after the purses ended?

The Twenty-Sixth Amendment ended recognition for official purposes, extinguished the privy purses and the privileges of rulership, and froze the definition of Ruler to those recognised before 28 December 1971. The former rulers kept their property and social standing, and several remained in electoral politics as ordinary citizens, but the Indian state ceased to maintain a category of recognised royalty.

## The Fiscal Ledger: Small Money, Large Signal

The adjudication this article owes its readers can be conducted, in the first instance, with a calculator. The privy purses at their settlement aggregate of roughly Rs 5.8 crore a year, as the White Paper on Indian States of 1950 recorded them, and their reduced total of about Rs 4 crore a year in 1970, were charged on the Consolidated Fund of India. The Union government's total expenditure in the year the amendment passed ran to several thousand crores. Abolition therefore saved the exchequer a sum that the government's own accountants would have classified as negligible: enough, in the idiom of the period's budgets, to fund a minor irrigation scheme or a few hundred primary schools, and not enough to register in any fiscal aggregate the Finance Ministry reported. No finance minister defended the amendment as an economy measure, because no finance minister could have kept a straight face doing so. The largest individual purses underline the point from the other side. The Nizam of Hyderabad's Rs 43 lakh, the Maharaja of Baroda's purse of about Rs 26 lakh, the purses of Mysore and Gwalior in the same band: these were fortunes to the recipients' households and rounding errors to the state that paid them.

A fiscal reading of abolition thus fails on its own terms, and it is worth being exact about what replaces it. The purse's value had never been fiscal even at its creation. Patel's Constituent Assembly speech of 12 October 1949 did not commend the settlement as cheap administration; he commended it as the price of a bloodless transfer of sovereignty, a political purchase. The abolition inverted the purchase at the same register. What the state bought in 1971, with a constitutional amendment as the currency, was not Rs 4 crore a year. It was the extinction of the last institutional survival of the princely order inside the republic, executed in a manner that identified the executioner. A quiet commutation, negotiated ruler by ruler over a decade, would have saved the same money and signalled nothing. The amendment signalled everything, and the signal's addressees were the legislators who passed it, the judges whose judgment it answered, and the electorate that had been promised, in the campaign of March 1971, that their Prime Minister finished what princes and courts had interrupted.

## What Abolition Did Not Do: The Princes After 1971

Precision about the abolition's limits sharpens rather than softens the strategic reading. The Twenty-Sixth Amendment took the purse, the recognition and the privileges. It took nothing else. The former rulers kept their personal property entire: the palaces, the jewellery held in trust, the securities and the land that the covenants had reserved to them as private estate. No wealth tax settlement accompanied the amendment, no inventory of princely assets was demanded, and the great houses remained great landlords and, in several cases, powers in their former territories by the ordinary currencies of money, caste, kinship and local memory. Titles lost their official existence and kept their social one; the Indian state stopped printing them in its gazettes, and the drawing rooms of Jaipur, Gwalior and Baroda went on using them, a division between the republic's text and the republic's society that the amendment's sponsors accepted as the price of a clean constitutional act. Some houses converted palaces into hotels and durbar halls into museums across the following decades, trading on the very aura the amendment had derecognised, and the tourist economy of Rajasthan is in part the princely order's afterlife in commercial form.

The political afterlife was livelier still. Deprived of the purse, the former rulers remained in possession of the asset that had made several of them valuable to opposition parties all along: personal followings in their former states that no party machine could manufacture. Gayatri Devi held her Jaipur seat through the Parliament elected in 1971 and remained the Swatantra Party's most recognisable face until the Emergency imprisoned her. Vijaya Raje Scindia continued as a pillar of the Jana Sangh and of the party that succeeded it in Madhya Pradesh. The lead petitioner himself traced the arc's most instructive path: Madhavrao Scindia, who had carried the rulers' cause to the Supreme Court in 1970 and won, entered the Lok Sabha in 1971, migrated across the political spectrum in the years after the Emergency, and served in later decades as a Union minister in Congress governments under Rajiv Gandhi and P. V. Narasimha Rao. The abolition's human consequence was thus absorption rather than ruin: the republic extinguished the rulers as a constitutional category and digested them as politicians. That is the outcome a fiscal or punitive measure would not have produced, and it fits the campaign reading precisely. Move 3 was never aimed at the princes' welfare or their wealth. It was aimed at the coalition that sheltered behind their symbol, and once the symbol was struck from the Constitution, the men themselves were free to be, and were, received as citizens of consequence by the very order that had derecognised them.

## The Complication: Principle, Faith, and the Strongest Counter-Reading

The strongest counter to the political-strategic reading is not the defence of privilege. It is the observation that abolition commanded principled support far beyond Indira Gandhi's faction and far beyond the Congress: the socialist parties had demanded it for a generation, the Communist Party of India supported the amendment, constitutionalists of no partisan attachment regarded hereditary state payments as a standing offence against Article 14's promise, and the Congress itself had adopted abolition in 1967 under leaderships that had no thought of serving her campaign. A measure so widely and honestly supported, the counter-reading runs, requires no strategic explanation; the strategy was decoration on a conviction the political class already held. The breach-of-faith charge pressed by the Swatantra Party and the Jana Sangh adds a second edge: Patel's words of 12 October 1949 were a sovereign assurance, and a republic that amends its constitution to escape its own assurances teaches every future counterparty to discount its word.

This article accepts the counter-reading's facts and disputes only its sufficiency. The abolition's substantive defensibility is real, and nothing in the strategic reading requires it to be otherwise; governments rarely choose indefensible measures as demonstrations, precisely because a demonstration must be applauded to work. The analytical distinction the history requires is between a measure's defensibility, which explains why it could be chosen, and its timing and method, which explain why it was chosen then and in that form. Timing and method are where the strategic reading earns its primacy. The three years of drift after 1967 show conviction without a sponsor accomplishing nothing. The night of 5 and 6 September 1970 shows the sponsor, once engaged, preferring an unconstitutional instrument to a lawful delay. The dissolution of 27 December 1970 shows the blocked measure converted into electoral capital. The November and December of 1971 show the measure carried in the company of the Twenty-Fourth and Twenty-Fifth Amendments, as one panel of a sovereignty offensive. Each of these choices is a datum about function, and the principled reading has no account of any of them beyond accident. Strategic timing of substantively defensible action is a distinct analytical category, and the privy purse abolition is its textbook Indian instance.

### Did any later court restore the privy purses?

No. The former rulers and their successors challenged the Twenty-Sixth Amendment itself, and in Raghunathrao Ganpatrao v Union of India, decided in 1993, the Supreme Court upheld the amendment against the argument that abolition damaged the Constitution's basic structure. The purses were never restored, and no later judgment has disturbed the settlement the amendment enacted in December 1971.

## The Roads Not Taken: Commutation, Taxation and the Slow Taper

The strategic reading gains further support from the alternatives the government examined and discarded, because each discarded road would have achieved the fiscal object at a fraction of the political price, and the government's refusal of all three is intelligible only if the political price was the point of the purchase. The first road was negotiated commutation: a funded settlement under which each ruler surrendered the purse for a capital sum or a terminable annuity, extinguishing the obligation by consent. Chavan's negotiations of 1967 to 1969 had explored the ground and failed on the rulers' terms, but the government of 1971 negotiated from a position the negotiators of 1968 could not imagine, holding a fresh two-thirds majority and a voided-court-victory to trade upon. A commutation purchased in the shadow of the certain amendment would have been cheap, final and unanswerable in court. It was not attempted. Consent produces no demonstration; a bought surrender exhibits the buyer's cheque book, not his command of Parliament.

The second road was fiscal erosion: tax the purses, or subject them to the wealth and expenditure levies the period's budgets were multiplying. Article 291's guarantee that the sums be paid free of income tax barred the road as completely as it barred abolition by statute, and opening it required the same amendment as abolition itself; a government already paying the amendment's full procedural price had no reason to buy the lesser object. The third road was the slow taper the covenants themselves contained: allow the roll to shorten by deaths, contest recognitions at the margin, and let the aggregate sink toward insignificance across a generation. The 1967 programme's sponsors had the taper available at no constitutional cost, and the three years of drift after 1967 amounted to an unplanned trial of it. Its defect, from the standpoint of a leadership fighting for its political life in 1969 and 1970, was that it produced no event. Nothing in the taper could be announced on a platform, carried through a division lobby or printed in a Gazette. A fourth road, grandfathering, was canvassed in the debates and rejected on the floor: confine abolition to future successions, pay the sitting rulers for their lives, and let the roll expire with the generation that had signed the covenants. The proposal had equity on its side, since the sitting rulers were the covenanting parties, and it would have disarmed the breach-of-faith charge at a cost of two more decades of diminishing payments. Its defect was the mirror of the taper's: it converted a constitutional confrontation into a mortality table. The government that had dissolved a Parliament over the blocked programme did not propose to wait for biology to finish it, and the amendment's text, extinguishing the rights of sitting rulers and unborn claimants in a single clause, records the choice.

Each road's rejection therefore converges on the same explanation. The government required an act, dated, voted and gazetted, whose performance was inseparable from its effect. That requirement is the definition of the strategic function this article has attributed to Move 3, and the alternatives' fates are its negative proof.

## Precedent: The Executive-Judicial Confrontation Arc, 1951 to 1976

The privy purse amendment was the third time in the republic's life that a government answered a judicial defence of property or status with a constitutional amendment, and the pattern's genealogy matters. [The executive-amendment pattern as precedent](/2019/01/14/first-constitutional-amendment-1951/) was set in 1951, when the First Amendment replied to the courts' early free-speech and land-reform rulings with Articles 31A and 31B and the Ninth Schedule. What changed between 1951 and 1971 was the scale of the claim being asserted. The First Amendment carved exceptions out of the rights chapter for a legislative programme. The amendments of 1971 asserted, against Golak Nath's holding that Parliament could not amend the fundamental rights at all, that the amending power reached every provision of the Constitution. The Twenty-Sixth Amendment travelled inside that assertion: a government content to abolish a pension would not have needed the Twenty-Fourth Amendment's restoration of the amending power as its escort, and the escort's presence in the same session announces that the abolition formed part of a general settlement of the question who governs the Constitution.

The arc's remaining stations are a matter of record. The Twenty-Fifth Amendment's Article 31C subordinated Articles 14 and 19 to specified directive principles, engaging [the basic-structure doctrine context](/2018/12/23/directive-principles-vs-fundamental-rights/) that the courts had been constructing since Golak Nath. Kesavananda Bharati v State of Kerala, decided on 24 April 1973 by a bench of thirteen judges, upheld the amending power and confined it within the basic structure of the Constitution in a single holding, the confrontation's uneasy armistice. The privy purse abolition survived that armistice untouched, and its 1993 vindication in Raghunathrao Ganpatrao confirmed that the extinction of the rulers' constitutional status damaged no basic feature. The confrontation itself resumed with the supersession of judges in 1973 and reached its extreme in the Forty-Second Amendment of 1976, the Emergency-era attempt to place the amending power beyond judicial review altogether, treated later in this series. Located on this arc, the Twenty-Sixth Amendment is neither the first shot nor the heaviest. It is the confrontation's clearest small exhibit: a judgment read, an election fought, an amendment carried, a status extinguished, with each step performed in constitutional form and aimed at a constitutional moral. The moral, that the executive commanding a two-thirds Parliament need accept no permanent judicial veto, is the premise on which the harder battles of 1973 and 1976 were fought, and its genealogy runs back through [the founding's rights-development tension](/2018/12/01/constituent-assembly-three-tensions-founding-india/), the unresolved founding question of how much of the social revolution the courts would be permitted to slow. Two codas fix the amendment's durability. The Janata government of 1977, which counted among its constituents the Jana Sangh and politicians who had denounced abolition as a breach of faith, made no move to restore the purses, and no subsequent government of any colour has proposed their return: the extinction commanded, in the end, the acquiescence of the very coalition that had resisted it, the surest sign that the measure's substance commanded the assent its method had been chosen to dramatise. And the amendment's grammar, omission of the guaranteeing articles, a non-obstante extinction clause and a frozen definition, became a reusable instrument: the Forty-Fourth Amendment of 1978 deleted the fundamental right to property itself from Articles 19 and 31 in the same syntactic pattern, closing the property question that the First Amendment had opened in 1951. Techniques, like precedents, are a form of legacy, and Move 3's drafting outlasted the campaign that commissioned it.

## Adjudication: Ranking the Two Readings

The named disagreement the evidence must settle is between the popularist-progressive reading, abolition as the principled removal of a colonial anachronism, and the political-strategic reading defended here, abolition as Move 3 of a coordinated campaign. Granville Austin's Working a Democratic Constitution of 1999 supplies the institutional frame in which the strategic reading sits: Austin treats the constitutional politics of the early 1970s as a contest over the Constitution's custody between Parliament and the Supreme Court, in which each amendment of the period did double duty as policy and as assertion. Inder Malhotra's biography Indira Gandhi of 1989 supplies the personal register, reading the years 1969 to 1971 as the deliberate reconstruction of a premiership on plebiscitary foundations, with the populist programme as the reconstruction's material. Francine R. Frankel's India's Political Economy, 1947 to 1977, published in 1978, supplies the structural version: Frankel argued that the radicalisation of 1969 to 1971 compensated for the Congress's organisational decay by substituting direct appeals to the electorate for the mediating machinery the party had lost, an account under which the privy purse abolition figures as inexpensive radicalism, high in symbolic yield and low in administrative cost, chosen by a leadership whose capacity for harder redistributive surgery was limited. Bipan Chandra's treatment in India Since Independence, published in 2008, holds the two registers together, presenting the measures of the period as simultaneously conviction and tactic, populism as a governing style adopted by a leader who believed its content and exploited its effects. The adjudication offered here stands closest to Chandra's dual register, with the ranking he leaves implicit made explicit: where conviction and tactic coincide, the historian's question is which of the two determined the observable choices, and in the privy purse case the observable choices, of instrument, of timing, of drafting and of company, are tactic's fingerprints from first to last. The under-cited primary evidence is the paper trail of the strategy itself: the Mohan Kumaramangalam papers at the Nehru Memorial Museum and Library, including the strategic memoranda of 1970 and 1971 on the constitutional-amendment route, record the amendment being valued in the Prime Minister's circle as a political instrument whose worth rose, rather than fell, when the Scindia judgment made it the only route. Kumaramangalam, the former Communist who entered the Congress and the cabinet in 1971 and stood among the closest counsellors of the period, wrote as a participant; his memoranda are the campaign's internal voice, and they corroborate what the public sequence already shows. Readers handling the digitised texts of the amendment, the Gazette notifications and the judgment, which circulate as scanned official documents, work under the same discipline of source care that guides to [protecting research sources](https://vaultbook.net/blog/2026/06/05/how-journalists-use-vaultbook-to-protect-sources/) describe for reporters and historians alike.

Set the sequence out as a ledger, stripped of interpretation, and ask which reading the items require. Item: abolition is adopted as party policy in May 1967, and no bill follows for three years. Item: abolition is announced to the public in July 1969, coupled with bank nationalisation, at the opening of a factional war. Item: the bill of 1970 fails in the Rajya Sabha on 5 September, and executive orders achieving its object by definition are signed the same night. Item: the Supreme Court voids the orders on 15 December 1970, and the Lok Sabha is dissolved nine days later, a year early, on a platform in which the blocked measures are exhibits. Item: the election of March 1971 manufactures the special majority that September 1970 lacked, in a House whose members owe their tickets to the leader the plebiscite was fought to install. Item: the amendment is carried in the winter session of 1971 alongside the Twenty-Fourth and Twenty-Fifth Amendments, in the weeks of the Bangladesh war, and is drafted to be proof against litigation and against successor governments. No item in this ledger is a fiscal act, and only the first is explicable by principle alone. Every subsequent item is a choice among instruments, timings and audiences, and each choice maximised the measure's demonstrative effect on the three audiences the campaign addressed: the Congress legislator, who learned that the leader could carry the Constitution's hardest procedure; the judge, who learned that his veto repriced rather than prevented; and the voter, who had been told in March that the princes stood between the poor and their government, and who could read December's Gazette as the promise kept.

The verdict follows from the evidence marshalled in the sections above, and it is a ranking rather than a refutation. The two readings are compatible: the abolition was substantively defensible, honestly supported across parties since 1967, and fiscally trivial, and it was also the third move of a coordinated campaign whose timing, method and company were chosen for political effect. The strategic reading ranks first because it explains the facts the principled reading leaves as residue: the three years of inaction after the principle was adopted, the unconstitutional order signed within hours of the 1970 defeat, the dissolution that converted the Court's veto into a campaign asset, and the decision to carry a Rs 4 crore measure in the heaviest available legislative company. A principle that waits for a two-thirds majority manufactured by a mid-term election, and is then executed as the capstone of a sovereignty offensive against the judiciary, is being used as well as believed. The privy purse abolition was Move 3 in Indira's coordinated anti-syndicate campaign; its fiscal value was small, and its political value was the function.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What was the privy purse?

The privy purse was the annual sum paid by the Indian state to the former rulers of the princely states under the settlements of 1947 to 1949. In exchange for acceding to India and then surrendering sovereignty through merger covenants, each ruler received a fixed yearly payment scaled to his state's revenues, retention of personal property, and recognition of his titles and dignities. The Constitution guaranteed the arrangement: Article 291 charged the sums on the Consolidated Fund of India, beyond annual parliamentary vote, and freed them from income tax, while Article 362 protected the rulers' personal rights and privileges. The amounts varied enormously, from Rs 43 lakh a year for the Nizam of Hyderabad to Rs 192 for the ruler of Katodia, and the aggregate stood at roughly Rs 5.8 crore a year at the settlement, falling to about Rs 4 crore by 1970. V. P. Menon's integration history of 1956 presents the purse as the purchase price of a bloodless transfer of sovereignty over more than five hundred states.

### Q: When were the privy purses abolished?

The privy purses were abolished on 28 December 1971, when the Constitution (Twenty-Sixth Amendment) Act received the assent of the President and came into force on the same day. The Act omitted Articles 291 and 362 from the Constitution and inserted Article 363A, which ended the recognition of rulers and extinguished all rights and obligations in respect of the privy purse. The abolition of 1971 was the end of a sequence with three earlier stations. A constitutional amendment bill to end the purses passed the Lok Sabha on 2 September 1970 but failed in the Rajya Sabha on 5 September 1970 by a fraction of a vote. Presidential orders derecognising all rulers followed on 6 September 1970 and stopped the payments, and the Supreme Court struck those orders down on 15 December 1970. Only the fourth step, the amendment carried after the Congress (R) victory in the general election of March 1971, extinguished the purses in law.

### Q: What was the Madhavrao Scindia case?

Madhavrao Scindia v Union of India, reported at 1971 (1) SCC 85 and AIR 1971 SC 530, was the Supreme Court's judgment of 15 December 1970 on the presidential orders that had derecognised every ruler of a former princely state. Madhavrao Scindia, the Maharaja of Gwalior, filed the first of eight writ petitions, numbered 376 to 383 of 1970, under Article 32, five days after the orders of 6 September 1970 stopped the privy purses. An eleven-judge bench heard the case, with N. A. Palkhivala and M. C. Setalvad for the rulers and Attorney General Niren De for the Union. By nine votes to two, Justices G. K. Mitter and A. N. Ray dissenting, the Court held the orders illegal and inoperative: recognition under Article 366(22) identified the ruler entitled under the covenants and Article 291, and the President could not abolish the purse by derecognising its payees en masse. The Court pointed to constitutional amendment as the lawful route, the route taken in 1971.

### Q: What is the Twenty-Sixth Amendment?

The Twenty-Sixth Amendment is the Constitution (Twenty-Sixth Amendment) Act, 1971, the amendment through which the Indian Parliament abolished the privy purses and ended the constitutional recognition of the former rulers of the princely states. Its operative provisions are three. It omitted Article 291, which had charged the privy purse sums on the Consolidated Fund of India and exempted them from income tax. It omitted Article 362, which had directed legislatures to respect the covenant guarantees of the rulers' personal rights, privileges and dignities. And it inserted Article 363A, declaring that recognition granted to the rulers shall cease and that the privy purse is abolished, with all rights, liabilities and obligations in respect of it extinguished. A substituted clause (22) of Article 366 confined the definition of Ruler to persons recognised before the amendment commenced, barring new claimants by succession. The Act received presidential assent on 28 December 1971 and commenced the same day.

### Q: Why did Indira Gandhi abolish privy purses?

Indira Gandhi's government stated a principled reason and acted on a political one, and the evidence supports ranking the political reason first. The stated reason, pressed in Parliament in 1970 and 1971, was that rulership with privy purses and special privileges unrelated to any current functions is incompatible with an egalitarian social order; the Congress had held abolition as policy since its ten-point programme of May 1967. The political reason appears in the timing and method. Abolition was announced with bank nationalisation in July 1969 at the opening of her fight against the Congress syndicate; when the amendment bill failed in the Rajya Sabha in September 1970, her government attempted abolition by executive order the same night; when the Supreme Court voided the orders in December 1970, she dissolved the Lok Sabha and campaigned on the blocked programme; and the amendment was carried in December 1971 as a demonstration of her command over Parliament. The fiscal saving, about Rs 4 crore a year, explains neither the urgency nor the theatre.

### Q: How did the privy purse abolition help Indira politically?

The abolition helped Indira Gandhi in three documented ways. First, it demonstrated legislative command: the Twenty-Sixth Amendment required two-thirds majorities in both Houses, and carrying it in December 1971, fifteen months after the same measure had failed in the Rajya Sabha, exhibited a Congress parliamentary party rebuilt around her leadership after the split of 1969. Second, it answered the Supreme Court. The amendment obeyed the Scindia judgment's own instruction that abolition required constitutional amendment, and thereby overcame the judgment, teaching the judiciary and the opposition that her programme could be delayed by courts but not defeated by them. Third, it completed the populist account opened by bank nationalisation in July 1969: the government that had struck at concentrated banking capital struck next at hereditary status, and the Garibi Hatao platform of the March 1971 election acquired a kept promise in the Gazette of 29 December 1971. The measure's fiscal yield, roughly Rs 4 crore a year, was negligible beside these returns.

### Q: Did the abolition violate the original integration agreements?

The charge that abolition breached the integration agreements was the opposition's strongest argument, and the honest answer divides by forum. As a matter of political morality, the charge has substance: the merger covenants were agreements, Sardar Patel told the Constituent Assembly on 12 October 1949 that the privy purse settlements were consideration for the surrender of ruling powers, and he warned that failure to honour the guarantee would be a breach of faith. The Swatantra Party and the Jana Sangh pressed Patel's words throughout the debates of 1970 and 1971. As a matter of law, the charge fails. The covenants were made under a constitutional order that contained its own amending procedure, and the agreements were translated into Articles 291 and 362 of a Constitution that Parliament could amend under Article 368. The Supreme Court never held the purses unamendable; the Scindia judgment of 1970 insisted only that abolition proceed by amendment, and the Court upheld the Twenty-Sixth Amendment itself against challenge in Raghunathrao Ganpatrao v Union of India in 1993.

### Q: How many rulers received privy purses, and what was the largest purse?

The purse roll published in the White Paper on Indian States of 1950 carried 278 rulers. Their payments were fixed by the merger covenants on a tapering scale of the surrendered states' revenues, and the spread was extreme. The largest purse was the Nizam of Hyderabad's, Rs 43 lakh a year in Indian currency; Baroda drew about Rs 26.5 lakh, Mysore about Rs 26 lakh and Gwalior Rs 25 lakh, and eleven houses in all exceeded Rs 10 lakh. Ninety-one rulers drew between Rs 1 lakh and Rs 10 lakh, and the long tail of the roll, some 396 recipients in the States Ministry's reckoning of all recipients at all grades, drew under Rs 50,000, down to the Rs 192 a year of the ruler of Katodia in Saurashtra. The aggregate at settlement was roughly Rs 5.8 crore a year. Reductions applied at successions under many covenants brought the total payable down to about Rs 4 crore a year by the time of abolition in 1971.

### Q: What did Articles 291 and 362 of the Constitution provide?

Article 291 and Article 362 were the constitutional text of the integration settlement. Article 291 provided that any sum payable as privy purse under a covenant or agreement, as it stood before the Constitution commenced, would be charged on and paid out of the Consolidated Fund of India, and that the sums so paid would be exempt from income tax. Charging the payments on the Consolidated Fund placed them outside the annual voting of supply in Parliament, so no budget debate could reduce or withhold them. Article 362 provided that Parliament and the state legislatures, in legislating on matters within their competence, and the executive authorities, were to have due regard to the guarantees given under the covenants respecting the personal rights, privileges and dignities of the rulers. Read with the definition of Ruler in Article 366(22) and the jurisdictional bar in Article 363, the four provisions formed the settlement's complete legal architecture. The Twenty-Sixth Amendment omitted Articles 291 and 362 and rewrote the rest of the architecture in 1971.

### Q: What is Article 363A, and what does it say?

Article 363A is the provision the Twenty-Sixth Amendment inserted into the Constitution in December 1971 under the heading that recognition granted to rulers of Indian states is to cease and privy purses are to be abolished. It opens with a non-obstante clause, notwithstanding anything in the Constitution or in any law in force, which subordinates every contrary provision to its terms. Its first clause declares that the recognition granted to the rulers of Indian states by the President shall cease, and that recognitions granted before the amendment are deemed to have ceased. Its second clause declares that the privy purse is abolished and that all rights, liabilities and obligations in respect of the privy purse are extinguished. The drafting was designed against the litigation of 1970: where the presidential orders of September 1970 had tried to end the purses by manipulating the definition of Ruler, Article 363A ended recognition and the purse together, by express text, and left no surviving article under which a claim could be framed. The provision remains in the Constitution.

### Q: Why did the government use presidential orders in September 1970 instead of waiting for Parliament?

The presidential orders of 6 September 1970 were issued because the parliamentary route had failed the previous day and the government would not accept the failure's political verdict. The amendment bill had passed the Lok Sabha on 2 September 1970 by 332 votes to 154 and fallen in the Rajya Sabha on 5 September, 149 votes to 75, short of the two-thirds majority Article 368 requires. The cabinet met that evening and advised President V. V. Giri, then at Hyderabad, to withdraw recognition from every ruler under Article 366(22), on the theory that an unrecognised person could claim no purse under Article 291. Speed was the strategy's essence: identical orders for every ruler in India, stopping all purses forthwith, converted a parliamentary defeat into an accomplished fact before the defeat's meaning could consolidate. The Supreme Court's judgment of 15 December 1970 in the Scindia case destroyed the theory, holding mass derecognition a colourable exercise of power, but by then the orders had served three months as a public demonstration of the Prime Minister's will.

### Q: Which parties and leaders defended the privy purses in Parliament?

The defence of the privy purses in the debates of 1970 and 1971 was led by the Swatantra Party and the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, the two parties in which former rulers and their political networks were most heavily represented, and it was joined by the Congress (O), the syndicate's organisation, whose opposition to Indira Gandhi's government extended to the whole Garibi Hatao programme. The defence rested on the sanctity of the covenants: the purses were consideration for the surrender of sovereignty, Sardar Patel had defended them in the Constituent Assembly on 12 October 1949 as the price of a bloodless revolution, and a republic that amended its Constitution to escape its own assurances would teach every future counterparty to discount its word. Rulers sitting as legislators, including Gayatri Devi of Jaipur for the Swatantra Party, embodied the argument. The Communist Party of India supported abolition, as did the socialist parties, which had demanded it for a generation, so the parliamentary division did not follow wealth or property in any simple pattern.

### Q: What role did V. V. Giri play in the abolition sequence?

Varahagiri Venkata Giri, the President of India, appears at two hinges of the sequence. He owed his office to the abolition's political camp: in the presidential election of August 1969 he stood as an independent, supported by the Communist parties and by Indira Gandhi's call for a conscience vote, and defeated the official Congress candidate, Neelam Sanjiva Reddy, whom the syndicate had nominated at Bangalore. Thirteen months later, on the night of 5 and 6 September 1970, it was Giri who signed the orders derecognising every ruler in India, acting on the advice of a cabinet that had met hours after the Rajya Sabha rejected the amendment bill; the orders were signed at Hyderabad, where the President was encamped, and dated 6 September 1970. His signature converted advice into accomplished fact within a day of the parliamentary defeat. When the Twenty-Sixth Amendment had passed both Houses in 1971, the same President's assent, given on 28 December 1971, completed the abolition by the lawful route the Supreme Court had prescribed.

### Q: How did the abolition relate to bank nationalisation and the 1969 Congress split?

The abolition was the third element of a single campaign. The ordinance nationalising fourteen major commercial banks was issued on 19 July 1969, three days after Indira Gandhi took the finance portfolio from Morarji Desai, and in the same public announcement season she declared the government's intention to withdraw the princes' privileges and purses. The Congress split followed in November 1969, when the Congress (O) leadership expelled her and she carried the majority of Congress legislators into Congress (R), winning the subsequent contest for the party's name and symbol that the Supreme Court sustained in Sadiq Ali v Election Commission of India. Bank nationalisation supplied the campaign's programme against concentrated private wealth; the split supplied its vehicle, a parliamentary party owing its tickets to the leader; and the privy purse abolition, completed by the Twenty-Sixth Amendment in December 1971, supplied its demonstration against hereditary status. The three measures are best read as one design: the campaign's logic is set out in the Coordinated Campaign Trajectory table presented in this article.

### Q: Were privy purses paid during the months between the September 1970 orders and the December 1970 judgment?

No payments were made while the presidential orders stood, and the arrears were then restored in full. The orders of 6 September 1970 stopped the privy purses forthwith, as the Supreme Court's judgment in the Scindia case records, and the stoppage held from September until the judgment of 15 December 1970. The rulers' remedy was immediate: Madhavrao Scindia filed the first writ petition on 11 September 1970, and the eight petitions were heard together before the eleven-judge bench. The majority's declaration entitled the petitioners to all their pre-existing rights and privileges, including the right to the privy purses, as if the orders had not been passed. In law, therefore, the orders were treated as void from the start, and the quarter's payments became arrears payable under Article 291 rather than sums forgiven. The purses then flowed under the restored settlement for a further year, until the Twenty-Sixth Amendment extinguished the obligation itself on 28 December 1971, after which no arrears or continuing claims survived the extinction clause of Article 363A.

### Q: Did the rulers challenge the Twenty-Sixth Amendment itself in court?

Yes. Having won against the executive orders in 1970, the former rulers and their successors challenged the amendment that abolished the purses, arguing that the Twenty-Sixth Amendment damaged the basic structure of the Constitution and violated rights of equality and property. The challenge reached the Supreme Court as Raghunathrao Ganpatrao v Union of India, decided in 1993, in which the Court upheld the amendment. The distinction between the two litigations is the constitutional point of the whole episode. In 1970 the rulers won because the government had tried to achieve by executive definition what the Constitution reserved to the amending procedure; the Court defended the procedure, not the purse. In 1993 the rulers lost because the procedure had been followed: a constitutional amendment, passed by the special majorities of Article 368, could extinguish the status and payments the original text had guaranteed, and the extinction of a princely pension roll damaged no basic feature of the Constitution. No restoration of the purses has followed from any court, and no government has legislated one.

### Q: Why did the Twenty-Sixth Amendment omit Articles 291 and 362 instead of amending their terms?

Omission was chosen because any surviving text would have remained a peg for litigation and a residue of obligation. Had the amendment merely reduced the purses, or taxed them, or fixed a terminal date, Article 291 would still have contained a constitutional promise whose breach could be sued upon, and the government of 1971 had already lost one lawsuit to careful pleading on the settlement's texts. By omitting both articles entire, inserting Article 363A in non-obstante form to extinguish all rights, liabilities and obligations in respect of the purse, and freezing the definition of Ruler in Article 366(22) to those recognised before commencement, the drafters left no provision under which a claim could be framed and no door through which recognition of an heir could revive the roll. The technique also carried a message consistent with the amendment's political function: the settlement of 1949 was not being renegotiated but closed, and the constitutional text was made to show a clean absence where the princely order had stood.

### Q: What did Granville Austin argue about the privy purse abolition?

Granville Austin treated the abolition within the larger argument of Working a Democratic Constitution, published in 1999: that the Indian Constitution works through a seamless web of commitments, and that the early 1970s strained the web by converting amendments into instruments of a custody battle between Parliament and the Supreme Court. In Austin's frame, the privy purse amendment was one panel of the 1971 offensive, alongside the Twenty-Fourth Amendment's restoration of the amending power against the Golak Nath ruling and the Twenty-Fifth Amendment's Article 31C, by which the government asserted that an elected Parliament commanding special majorities held the Constitution's last word. The abolition's substance, the end of hereditary payments, fitted the Constitution's egalitarian professions; its method and company illustrated Austin's anxiety that the amending power was being exercised as a weapon of institutional rivalry. The reading defended in this article builds on Austin's frame and adds the campaign dimension his institutional focus understates: the audience for the demonstration included the Congress legislator and the voter, not only the judge.

### Q: How did the end of the Rajpramukh office in 1956 affect the princely settlement?

The Rajpramukh was the constitutional head of the Part B states and the unions of princely states under the original Constitution: former rulers such as the Maharaja of Mysore, the Nizam of Hyderabad, the Maharaja of Travancore and the Maharaja of Patiala served as the ceremonial and constitutional governors of territories they had surrendered, drawing allowances as officeholders in addition to their privy purses. The States Reorganisation Act of 1956 dissolved the Part B states and abolished the Rajpramukh office, retiring that layer of the settlement by administrative housekeeping fifteen years before the purse itself fell. The contrast instructs. The office died quietly because it performed functions the redrawn map no longer needed and no organised coalition defended it. The purse, being money, status and covenant in one, survived to be killed in public, by the Constitution's heaviest procedure, in 1971. The two extinctions, of 1956 and 1971, mark the settlement's two modes of ending: redundancy for its offices, and a demonstrated act of state for its symbols.

### Q: What is the coordinated-campaign reading of the privy purse abolition?

The coordinated-campaign reading holds that the abolition of the privy purses was the third move in Indira Gandhi's campaign against the coalition that had installed her in 1966 and sought to control her thereafter: the syndicate of Congress bosses, the Morarji Desai wing of the party, and the propertied and princely interests represented by the opposition. Move 1 was the Congress split of November 1969, which rebuilt the parliamentary party around her person. Move 2 was the bank nationalisation of July 1969, which supplied the populist programme. Move 3, the abolition of 1970 to 1971, demonstrated that the rebuilt party could carry the Constitution's hardest procedure, reversed a Supreme Court judgment by the Court's own prescribed route, and completed the programme's symbolic work against hereditary privilege. The reading does not deny that abolition was principled and widely supported; the Congress had adopted it in 1967. It ranks the political-strategic function as primary because the principled reading cannot explain the three years of inaction, the unconstitutional orders of September 1970, or the dissolution and election that manufactured the amending majority.
