Few phrases in political conversation carry as much weight on as little shared meaning as cultural Marxism. A reader meets it in a newspaper column, in a speech, in a comment thread, in a conversation with a neighbour, and in almost every case the person using it assumes that its meaning is plain. Yet the phrase is asked to do the work of at least three different expressions, each with its own history, its own users, and its own relationship to the evidence. One of them is a quiet label from scholarship. One of them is shorthand for a real argument among historians of ideas. One of them is the name of a conspiracy narrative whose documented lineage runs through some of the darkest polemics of the twentieth century. An article that treats the phrase as though it had a single meaning will mislead its reader whichever of the three the reader arrived with.

![Cultural Marxism, the history of a term, three usages, three histories - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-01.webp)

This article is a history of a term rather than a verdict on a school of thought. Its method is the one historians of ideas apply to any contested expression: establish the usages, date them, attribute them, and keep them apart while the evidence for each is examined. The claim it defends, which this series calls the three-usage separation, is simple to state and laborious to apply. The phrase names a descriptive label, a genuine scholarly question, and a conspiracy narrative, and almost every argument conducted with the phrase fails at the first step, because one party means the second of these and the other means the third, and the two proceed as though a single thing were under discussion. Establishing which usage is in play is not a pedantic preliminary. It is the necessary first move, without which nothing that follows can be assessed.

A word of fairness belongs at the front of the article rather than in a footnote. Many people who use the phrase use it as ordinary political vocabulary, picked up from the conversation around them, with no conspiratorial picture in mind and no prejudicial intent of any kind. They mean, roughly, that ideas associated with the Marxist tradition have become influential in schools, universities, publishing, and entertainment, and they reach for the nearest available term. That usage deserves to be met on its own ground, which is the ground of the second usage, the scholarly question about Marxism and culture, and it is treated with full seriousness below. Nothing in this history licenses dismissing a speaker before discovering which of the three things the speaker means, and the article returns to that obligation more than once, because the obligation runs in both directions.

## What cultural Marxism means depends on who is using it

### Where does the term cultural Marxism come from?

The phrase appears rarely as a descriptive academic label. Its widely circulated conspiratorial sense was elaborated in the early 1990s in a fringe political magazine and popularised later, and scholarship traces its structure to an earlier term used to attack modernist art and Jewish intellectuals.

That short answer contains the article in miniature, and it already shows why a single origin story cannot be given. A term with three usages has three origins, unfolding in different decades, in different countries, in different kinds of publication. The descriptive label grew inside universities, in books about the history of cultural studies. The scholarly question grew inside Marxism itself, as its historians and partisans argued about where the tradition had placed its attention. The conspiracy narrative was assembled in the publications of a fringe political movement at the start of the 1990s, was carried to a far larger audience by a policy organisation later in that decade, and has circulated since as a self-contained story about a handful of exiled German intellectuals. Asking where the term comes from, without saying which term, is like asking where a river comes from when three rivers share a name on the map.

The confusion is not accidental, and it is not symmetrical. The scholarly question and the conspiracy narrative both concern the Frankfurt School and the place of culture in Marxist thought, which makes them easy to mistake for one another at a distance. Up close they differ in the one respect that matters most to a historian. The scholarly question is a question: it asks whether something happened, invites evidence, and has been answered in opposite ways by serious scholars using archives and texts. The conspiracy narrative is an answer in search of its evidence: it asserts a coordinated intention, treats the absence of documentation for that intention as proof of concealment, and arranges whatever it touches into confirmation. Separating them is therefore not a matter of preferring one politics to another. It is a matter of distinguishing an inquiry from a story.

There is also a third party to most disputes over the phrase, easily overlooked: the descriptive label. When a historian of cultural studies writes about cultural Marxism, the reference is usually to a body of scholarship, the Marxist analysis of literature, media, and everyday life that developed in Britain and elsewhere after the Second World War. No programme is implied, no coordination alleged, no subversion charged. The label functions the way labels for schools and tendencies usually function, as a convenience for grouping texts that share a method. Its rarity matters. Because the descriptive usage is uncommon and largely confined to academic shelves, most public encounters with the phrase involve the other two usages, and most public arguments about it are really arguments about which of those two the speaker intends.

The article that follows is organised to make the separation usable. A first group of sections presents each usage on its own terms, with its own documents and dates. The conspiracy usage is then given its history: the older German expression from which its structure descends, the early 1990s text in which its modern form was assembled, and the channel through which it reached a mass audience. A further section tests the narrative against the record of the Frankfurt School itself, since a claim about named historical people can be checked against what those people wrote, argued about, and did. The strongest objection to this whole procedure is stated at full strength and answered. Later sections turn from the term's past to its handling: how historians document a polemical usage without endorsing it, how a reader can examine an undated and unsourced use of the phrase, and what a writer who wants precision should put in its place.

Throughout, the article observes a discipline that its subject makes necessary. It names no contemporary politician, party, commentator, media organisation, or campaign, and it describes the term's circulation historically and generically, so that the history reads the same whenever it is encountered. It does not characterise the people who use the phrase, most of whom met it as unremarkable vocabulary. It states the findings of scholarship about the narrative's structure as findings, attributed to the scholars who published them, rather than as accusations against anyone. And where the genuine scholarly question is concerned, it gives that question its full standing, because a history of a term that cannot distinguish an argument from an allegation has failed at its only task.

One more distinction prepares the ground. A term's history is not the same as the history of the thing it names. The Frankfurt School existed, wrote books, quarrelled, held jobs, and left a large documentary record, all of which belongs to the school's own history and is treated in the companion guide to the school in this series. The phrase under examination here has a different and much stranger biography. For most of the school's lifetime the phrase did not exist in anything like its circulated sense. It was assembled decades after the school's founding, applied retrospectively to people who never used it of themselves, and loaded with a plot those people would not have recognised. Keeping the biography of the phrase apart from the biography of the institution is the precondition for everything that follows, and the failure to keep them apart is the seed of nearly every error the article corrects.

## The three usages table

A history that insists on keeping three usages apart owes its reader a place where the separation can be inspected at a glance. The three usages table below is that place, and it is the findable artifact around which this article is built. Each row states what the usage asserts, where it originates, who characteristically uses it, what evidence bears on it, and where in this series the underlying subject receives its full treatment. The table is a map rather than an argument. The argument is made in the sections that follow it, where each row is unpacked in turn and the documents behind the entries are examined.

Read across any row and the usage holds together: its assertion, its birthplace, its users, and its evidence belong to one another. Read down the columns and the conflation becomes visible. The third usage borrows the personnel of the second, since both concern the Frankfurt School and its contemporaries, and it borrows the respectability of the first, since a phrase that appears on scholarly title pages sounds like a scholarly phrase. What it cannot borrow is evidence. The first two usages rest on documents that anyone may inspect. The third rests on a plot asserted against documents that anyone may also inspect, and the inspection is fatal to it, as a later section shows in detail.

### What does the phrase assert when it is used descriptively?

Used descriptively, the phrase asserts nothing about a movement or a plan. It groups a body of published work: Marxist analyses of literature, schooling, media, and everyday life, written by identifiable authors in identifiable places. The label is a shelf mark for a library, not the name of an organisation or a campaign.

The table's discipline can be applied to any encounter with the phrase. A reader who meets the term in an argument should be able to assign the argument to a row before answering it. If the claim is that a tradition of cultural analysis exists and mattered, the first row governs, and the reply belongs to the history of scholarship. If the claim is that Marxism changed its centre of gravity, the second row governs, and the reply belongs to an evidence-bearing debate in which both sides can lose. If the claim is that a named group of intellectuals plotted the corruption of a civilisation, the third row governs, and the reply belongs to documentary history, where plots require paper trails and paper trails can be checked. Arguments go wrong, characteristically, when a claim from the third row is defended with evidence from the second, or when a speaker using the second row's question is answered as though the third row's plot had been alleged.

It is worth pausing on the modesty of what the separation claims. The three-usage separation does not say that the phrase should be retired, that its scholarly question is unimportant, or that the influence of Marxist ideas on modern culture is a closed subject. It says that three different assertions travel under one form of words, that they have different origins and different evidential standings, and that no honest argument can proceed until the speaker's assertion has been identified. The separation is a piece of intellectual hygiene, of the kind this series has applied before to phrases that name several things at once, and it is offered in that spirit: as equipment for readers, whatever they conclude about the underlying subjects.

## The first usage: a label on a library shelf

The descriptive usage is the least dramatic of the three and the easiest to document, because it lives where documentation is a professional obligation: in books with publishers, journals with editors, and catalogues with classification schemes. When scholars of British cultural studies needed a heading under which to gather the Marxist contribution to their field, some of them reached for this phrase. It named, for them, the tradition of analysis that ran from the adult education classes and little magazines of the postwar decades into the universities: the study of literature as a social fact, of schooling as a sorting machine, of popular entertainment as a text worth reading closely. The phrase asserted the existence of that tradition. It asserted nothing else.

### Which books use the phrase as a scholar's label?

The clearest case is a scholarly history of the British New Left published in 1997, whose title places the phrase on the cover of a work of archival scholarship. Around it stand scattered uses in writing about cultural studies, where the phrase names an approach to texts and institutions rather than a party or a plot.

The book in question is Dennis Dworkin's history of the New Left and the origins of cultural studies in postwar Britain, published by Duke University Press, a university press whose list is peer reviewed and whose volumes sit in research libraries. Dworkin's subject is a milieu: the historians, literary critics, and adult educators who, from the middle of the 1950s, built a new kind of inquiry around working class life and its representation. His phrase for the intellectual contribution those figures made sits in his title without apology or alarm, because in his usage it carries no charge beyond description. A reader who opens the book finds archives, interviews, and arguments about journals and reading groups. There is no cabal in it, and no whisper of one. The title page alone refutes the assumption that the phrase belongs by nature to polemic: here it belongs to a bibliography.

The milieu Dworkin chronicled explains why a label was needed. British cultural studies began outside the established disciplines, in extra mural departments and workers' education, and its founding figures were formed by Marxism without being contained by any party's version of it. Richard Hoggart's study of working class reading and entertainment appeared in 1957. Raymond Williams published his reconstruction of the culture and society tradition in 1958 and spent a long career arguing that culture is ordinary, a whole way of life rather than a garnish on the economy. E. P. Thompson, the historian of the English working class, quarrelled with orthodoxies of every kind while insisting that class is a relationship made in experience. Stuart Hall, who directed the Birmingham centre where cultural studies acquired an institutional home, spent decades adapting Marxist categories to questions of race, media, and popular consent, revising them openly as he went. A historian surveying this varied company needs a collective noun. For some, the phrase under examination supplied it.

Notice what the label does not do, in these hands. It does not claim that Hoggart, Williams, Thompson, and Hall executed a common strategy; anyone who has read their exchanges knows they disagreed about method, about politics, and about each other, in print and at length. It does not claim that their work was directed from anywhere; the Birmingham centre's funding, staffing, and internal disputes are documented in university records and memoirs. It does not even claim that the label is the best one available. Williams preferred to speak of cultural materialism, Thompson of a moral economy and a socialist humanism, Hall of a Marxism without guarantees. The descriptive phrase survives as a convenience, one heading among several, used by some scholars and ignored by others. That is the normal life of academic labels, which are adopted the way filing systems are adopted: provisionally, and for use rather than for truth.

Beyond the British case, the descriptive usage appears in scholarship on Marxist cultural analysis at large: in surveys of Marxist literary criticism, in histories of the sociology of culture, in accounts of the long argument about base and superstructure that runs from the nineteenth century forward. In such writing the phrase functions as a chapter heading for a set of problems. How does a mode of production shape the stories a society tells itself? What does a novel know that an economic table does not? Why do subordinate groups consent to arrangements that disadvantage them, and what part do schools, churches, and entertainments play in that consent? These are the questions of a research tradition, pursued in monographs rather than manifestos, and the descriptive label points to the tradition the way a signpost points to a town: it asserts that something is there, not what the traveller will find.

Two features of the descriptive usage deserve emphasis, because both will matter when the third usage is examined. The first is that the label is retrospective and loose, applied by historians to work whose authors often used other words for themselves. Retrospective labels are the small change of intellectual history: nobody in the Italian Renaissance called it that either, and the looseness is a known property, managed by careful writers rather than a deception practised by anyone. The second is that the label's users are identifiable and accountable. A scholar who groups texts under a heading can be asked which texts, on what criteria, and can be corrected by colleagues armed with the same library. The descriptive usage lives entirely inside that economy of citation and correction. It is, in the plainest sense, boring: a term of art in a trade that argues by footnote.

The rarity of the usage should be recorded honestly, since exaggerating it would repeat the conflation this article exists to undo. The phrase is not the standard name for Marxist cultural analysis, in scholarship or anywhere else. Specialists more often write of Marxist literary criticism, of cultural materialism, of Western Marxism, of the cultural turn, of the Birmingham school, of critical theory. The descriptive phrase occurs at the margins of that vocabulary, in a title here, a passing grouping there, never as the banner of a self-conscious movement. No department bears its name, no journal flies it, no conference assembles under it. If the phrase vanished from scholarship tomorrow, scholarship would lose a minor convenience and nothing else. That marginality is itself a datum. The phrase's loud public career cannot be explained by its scholarly one, and any history that runs the public career back through the universities has the direction of travel backwards.

## The second usage: a genuine scholarly question

The second usage is not a label but an abbreviation. When a writer uses the phrase to mean the scholarly question, the writer is pointing, in compressed form, at one of the best documented debates in the history of Marxist thought: whether the tradition, whose founding monument is a critique of political economy, shifted its weight across the twentieth century from the analysis of capital toward questions of ideology, inward life, and the arts, and what that shift cost and gained. The question is genuine, the evidence for discussing it is textual and abundant, and serious people have answered it in opposite ways. This section gives the question its full standing. The sustained comparison of the two frameworks it names, classical and critical, belongs to the companion study [critical theory vs classical Marxism](/2015/09/01/critical-theory-vs-classical-marxism/), and is signposted rather than argued here; what follows establishes that the question exists, that it is argued from evidence, and that asking it carries no conspiratorial implication whatsoever.

### What exactly is the scholarly question about Marxism and culture?

The question is whether the Marxist tradition, which began with political economy and the critique of capital, shifted its centre of attention during the twentieth century toward culture, ideology, and the inner life, and if so, why, and with what gains and losses for the tradition's explanatory power.

Begin with the tradition's own starting point, since the question is meaningless without it. Marx's mature work addressed the production and circulation of capital: the factory, the market, the wage, the crisis. His famous architectural metaphor of 1859 placed the economic structure as the foundation on which legal, political, and intellectual forms arise, and his followers inherited both the metaphor and the priority it seemed to assign. Yet the same corpus contains the materials of a cultural theory from the beginning: the analysis of religion as consolation and protest, the account of the commodity's fetish character, in which a social relation dresses itself as a property of things, and the running attention to newspapers, novels, and national mythology. The scholarly question is therefore not whether Marxism discovered culture, as a continent is discovered. It is whether the balance of the tradition's work shifted, over a specific century, in a specific direction, and whether the shift was wisdom or retreat.

### Why did Marxist thinkers turn toward culture at all?

The turn is usually explained by defeat and by evidence. Revolutionary movements in the advanced countries failed after the First World War, while economic prediction alone could not explain why workers consented to the order that defeated them. Thinkers imprisoned, exiled, or disappointed reached for ideology, schooling, and common sense as the missing terms.

The documentary landmarks are not in dispute. In 1923 Georg Lukács published his essays on reification, arguing that the commodity form penetrates consciousness itself, so that the worker's inner life becomes a terrain of the class struggle rather than its backdrop. Karl Korsch, in the same season, restored philosophy to the centre of Marxist inquiry. Antonio Gramsci, writing in a fascist prison between 1929 and 1935, built his notebooks around hegemony: the organised consent through which a social order secures itself in schools, churches, newspapers, and habits, long before it needs policemen. The Institute for Social Research in Frankfurt, founded in 1923 to study the labour movement, redirected itself under Max Horkheimer in the early 1930s toward an interdisciplinary programme joining economics, sociology, psychoanalysis, and philosophy, on the stated ground that the failure of the European working class to act as theory predicted could not be explained from the economy alone. Whatever a historian concludes about these moves, the moves themselves are printed, dated, and argued in the open. They are the opposite of a concealed operation; they are a literature.

The question acquired its sharpest scholarly form in 1976, when Perry Anderson published a set of considerations on Western Marxism that mapped the pattern with a cartographer's calm. Anderson observed that a tradition of theory had arisen in Western Europe, between the wars and after, whose characteristic products were philosophical and aesthetic rather than economic or political, written increasingly in the universities, at a distance from mass practice, and marked by a structural pessimism about the prospects of revolution. He named names, dated texts, and drew the map so that others could contest it, which they promptly did. Anderson's book is the standing proof that the scholarly question can be posed at the highest level of rigour by a writer inside the Marxist tradition itself, with no plot alleged and no villain required: the explanation he offered was historical, turning on defeats, exiles, Stalinism, and the long postwar boom, not on anyone's secret intentions.

The opposing answer is equally documented, and an honest history must give it room. Critics of the turn thesis point out that political economy never left the building. Ernest Mandel's treatise on late capitalism appeared in 1972, a work of economics on the grand scale. The decades of the supposed cultural turn also produced dependency theory, world systems analysis, labour process studies, and a flourishing Marxist historiography of class formation. On this reading, what changed was not the tradition's centre but its division of labour and its audience: cultural analysis grew louder because universities grew larger, while the economic work continued in journals fewer journalists read. Other critics argue that the contrast itself is overdrawn, since the classical texts already contained a cultural theory in embryo and the later cultural work often understood itself as completing the economic analysis rather than replacing it. The debate continues, in journals and monographs, with evidence exchanged and positions revised. That is what a scholarly question looks like from the inside.

What hangs on the answer is real, which is why the question will not die. If the turn happened and was a retreat, then a tradition that set out to explain capitalism ended by annotating its entertainments, and its political impotence is written into its bibliography. If the turn happened and was a maturation, then the tradition learned, at cost, that consent is manufactured in classrooms and cinemas as well as imposed in factories, and that an economics ignoring this explains less than it claims. If the turn is a mirage of periodisation, then the lesson concerns the historians, who mistook a change of venue for a change of mind. Each answer rearranges the evaluation of major figures. Each can be held by a reasonable scholar. None of them requires, or survives translation into, a story about a handful of exiles plotting a civilisation's corruption, because each is a claim about the published work of thousands of writers across a dozen countries, most of whom never met, many of whom detested one another's conclusions.

It should therefore be said without qualification, in the plainest terms this article can command: raising the question of Marxism's turn toward culture is not conspiracism, not prejudice, and not polemic. It is scholarship, and it is also legitimate public argument when conducted with the scholar's tools: named texts, checkable dates, influence traced through citations, appointments, syllabuses, and sales rather than asserted through atmosphere. A citizen who believes that Marxist ideas have shaped universities and cultural institutions, and who argues the case from evidence, is participating in the second usage, and is owed a hearing on that evidence. The whole point of the three-usage separation is to protect exactly that hearing from contamination by the third usage, whose methods would disgrace any argument they touched.

## The third usage: a narrative with a plot

The third usage differs from the first two not in degree but in kind, and the difference can be stated without reproducing its contents. The descriptive label groups books. The scholarly question interrogates a tradition. The third usage tells a story, and the story has the formal properties of the conspiracy narrative as historians of the genre describe it: a hidden intention, attributed to a small and identifiable group, which explains large and otherwise unrelated changes as the execution of a plan. Where the scholar sees a century of arguments, defeats, and accidents, the narrative sees a design. Where the record shows intellectuals disagreeing in public, the narrative sees a division of labour. Where documents are absent, the narrative finds concealment, so that the absence of evidence is absorbed as further evidence. It is this self-sealing structure, rather than any political colouring, that marks the usage and separates it from argument.

### What shape does the third usage give to history?

The third usage gives history the shape of a plot. A small, named group is said to have planned a civilisation's corruption in advance, to have worked toward the plan in concert, and to have succeeded in secret. Events with separate causes are gathered into one intention, and disagreement inside the group is edited away.

The narrative's personnel are the members of the Frankfurt School, the exiled institute whose actual history occupies the companion guide, and its setting is their American exile. In the narrative's telling, ideas that the school's members published as difficult works of philosophy and social research were instead a technology of subversion, designed to loosen a civilisation's attachments from within. The changes that later decades brought to family life, schooling, sexual conduct, popular entertainment, and the authority of institutions are then read backwards as the plan's harvest. Two features of this procedure deserve notice because they recur wherever the narrative travels. The first is retrospective attribution: developments with long and well documented genealogies of their own are reassigned to the plot because the plot needs effects. The second is the conversion of fame into function: because the school's books are genuinely read, their readers' independent choices are treated as the authors' operations, as though influence and command were the same relation.

A reader should also notice what the narrative does not resemble. It does not resemble the scholarly question of the previous section, although it grazes on the same pasture. The scholar asks whether attention shifted and weighs texts. The narrative asserts that a corruption was intended and weighs nothing, since every fact is pre-assigned a role. It does not resemble the descriptive label either, although it wears the label's clothes. The scholar's grouping is loose, revisable, and boring. The narrative's grouping is tight, total, and thrilling. That the two share a phrase is the accident on which the whole confusion rests, and it is why the phrase's history, rather than any single argument, is the necessary remedy: only the history shows that the plot was assembled at a datable moment, by identifiable hands, out of older materials, long after the events it purports to explain.

The sections that follow perform that history. They first recover the older German expression whose structure the modern narrative inherited. They then identify the text in which the modern version was put together, and the channel that carried it to its audience, treating those writers and organisations as historical actors of their period. They then set the narrative beside the documentary record of the Frankfurt School, which is extensive and fatal to it. Throughout, the narrative itself is described by its structure and its transmission, not summarised as a case, because a summary of a plot's claims, laid out in order, functions as the plot's brief regardless of the summariser's intent. This article declines to write that brief. It writes the plot's biography instead.

## An older phrase: cultural Bolshevism

The modern narrative's structure was not invented in the 1990s. Its grammar had been composed sixty years earlier, in the German speaking world, around a term of abuse that performed the same work: Kulturbolschewismus, cultural Bolshevism. The term circulated in the cultural polemics of the Weimar Republic and hardened into an instrument of state under the National Socialist regime. Its function was to redescribe artistic modernism as political warfare. Atonal music, abstract painting, expressionist theatre, the architecture of the Bauhaus, the new objectivity in painting and photography: all of these, in the term's usage, were not styles to be judged but operations to be exposed, the cultural advance guard of a Bolshevik assault on the German nation, conducted by artists who were said to be alien to the culture they worked in.

### Who were the people the older German term was used against?

The targets were the makers of modern culture in Germany: composers of atonal music, abstract painters, architects of the Bauhaus, theatre directors, and the critics who defended them, with Jewish artists and intellectuals singled out as the alleged agents of a foreign corruption working inside the nation's own institutions.

The term's career is documented in the scholarship of Nazi cultural policy, and its milestones are matters of public record. Through the late 1920s, nationalist and völkisch critics applied it to exhibitions, productions, and appointments they opposed, treating every modernist premiere as a provocation and every Jewish conductor, composer, or museum director as a placement. After 1933 the polemic acquired ministries. The Bauhaus was closed under pressure. Modern works were purged from museum walls. In 1937 the regime staged its logic for inspection in Munich, exhibiting confiscated modern art under the rubric of degeneracy, hung crookedly and labelled with derision, while a companion exhibition nearby displayed the approved art of the regime for contrast. The older term and the degeneracy campaign were not identical, but they belonged to one instrument: the conversion of aesthetic dislike into a theory of national subversion, in which the artist's style became evidence of the artist's mission and the artist's origins became the mission's explanation.

Three properties of the older term matter for the history being traced here. The first is that it named a plot where there were only artists. The composers and painters it targeted had no common programme, no coordinating body, and no political design beyond the various designs individual modernists happened to hold; their unity existed only in the accuser's sentence. The second is that it made ancestry do the work of argument. The Jewishness attributed to the modern movement, wildly overstated as demography and irrelevant as criticism, functioned as the plot's connective tissue, the hidden sameness beneath the surface variety of the accused. The third is that it was unfalsifiable in use. A modernist work's popularity proved the corruption's advance; its unpopularity proved the public's healthy resistance, which the corrupters were said to be scheming to overcome. A term that can absorb every outcome explains none of them, and historians treat cultural Bolshevism accordingly: as a specimen of the polemics that prepared and accompanied a cultural purge, not as a contribution to the understanding of modern art.

The connection between the older term and the modern narrative is structural before it is verbal, and stating it precisely is important. The claim made by the scholarship examined below is not that every later user of similar language inherited the older term consciously, nor that the two expressions are interchangeable in every mouth. The claim is that the narrative assembled in the 1990s reproduces the older polemic's architecture: a small group of intellectuals, marked by their Jewish origins, is alleged to have coordinated the subversion of a nation's culture from within its own institutions, with art and ideas as the weapons and the nation's traditions as the target. Architecture of that specificity does not arise by accident as often as it arises by descent, and the scholars who have traced the descent have done so through the publications in which the modern narrative was actually composed, to which the history now turns.

## The modern version takes shape in the early nineteen nineties

A term's modern career can usually be traced to documents, and this one can. The narrative in its circulated form was assembled in a single long article published in the winter of 1992 in Fidelio, a magazine of the Schiller Institute, the cultural arm of the political movement gathered around Lyndon LaRouche. The article, written by Michael Minnicino and presented as an exposure of the Frankfurt School and of political correctness, gathered the school's members into a single intention, read their published disagreements as camouflage, and attached to them responsibility for changes in American culture that its argument required but its evidence could not supply. The venue matters to the history. Fidelio was not a scholarly journal, a newspaper, or a magazine of the political mainstream. It was a movement publication, written for adherents, in a movement whose literature is characterised by exactly the habit this article has been describing: the discovery of hidden coordination behind unrelated events. The narrative was born in the genre it belongs to.

Two later facts about that founding text belong in the record. The first is that its author repudiated it. Minnicino subsequently left the movement and disowned the article he had written inside it, describing his younger self's work in terms no hostile critic would need to sharpen. A founding document whose own author withdraws it is not thereby refuted, since arguments stand on evidence rather than on their authors' biographies, but the withdrawal is a documented part of the term's history and it removes the text's claim to be one man's independent research: by its author's own later account, it was movement work. The second fact is that the article might have remained where it began, in a small circulation magazine, had it not been taken up by a far more effective carrier.

### How did a fringe magazine article travel so far?

It travelled by copying. A policy organisation with a mailing list and a conference circuit took up the story at the end of the 1990s, condensed it into a presentation and a video, and carried it to audiences that a movement magazine could never reach. Citation replaced reading, and the story simplified as it spread.

The carrier was the Free Congress Foundation, a conservative policy organisation in Washington founded by Paul Weyrich, which toward the end of the 1990s made the narrative a set piece of its cultural programme. A presentation by William S. Lind, a military theorist associated with the foundation, condensed the story into a lecture on the origins of political correctness, and the foundation circulated a video treatment of the theme around the turn of the century. The condensation did what condensations do. The qualifications that even the founding article had retained fell away; the cast shrank to a handful of names; the plot acquired the clean lines of a briefing. From the foundation's conferences and mailing lists the story passed into syndicated columns, talk radio, and the early political internet, each relay simplifying the dramatis personae and hardening the intention attributed to them. By the time the phrase reached general circulation, its users were often several removes from any text at all, repeating a story whose documents they had never seen, in a form its transmitters no longer needed to defend because nobody was asking them to cite it.

Scholarship caught up with the narrative once it had become a public fact, and the scholarly literature is where the lineage claims of this article come from. Martin Jay, the historian whose study of the Frankfurt School, published in 1973, remains a standard account, returned to the subject in a 2011 essay in the journal Salmagundi, examining the conspiracy literature as a phenomenon in its own right and setting its portrait of the school against the archival record he had spent a career reading. The Belgian political scientist Jérôme Jamin published analyses of the narrative's structure and diffusion in the following years, treating it as a case study in how a polemical construction moves from fringe publication to common currency. Other scholars of conspiracy rhetoric have contributed parallel examinations. The findings on which this article relies are theirs, and they are findings about structure and transmission: that the narrative's architecture reproduces that of cultural Bolshevism and of older antisemitic conspiracy patterns, in which a small group of Jewish intellectuals is cast as the hidden author of a nation's cultural decline, and that its documentary base does not extend beyond its own chain of repetition. Stating these findings is a report about scholarship, not an accusation against any reader. A person can repeat a story without knowing, or sharing, the design of its architecture, and the scholars in question have been careful to analyse the narrative rather than to indict its audience.

One further document belongs to the record of the term's circulation, and it is entered here in the single sentence its gravity permits and its relevance requires. The narrative also appeared in the manifesto published by the perpetrator of a mass killing in Norway in 2011. Historians record that appearance as a fact about the narrative's transmission and reach. It is not a fact about the millions of ordinary conversations in which the phrase has been used by people with no violent intent of any description, and this article draws no line from the one to the other. What the manifesto's use demonstrates is narrower and, for a history of a term, sufficient: that by 2011 the narrative had hardened into a portable account of the world, available for incorporation into a violent worldview without alteration, precisely because its structure required no evidence and admitted no correction.

## Why the narrative fails as history: the record of a quarrelsome institute

A conspiracy narrative about named historical people has one great vulnerability: the people left records. The Frankfurt School was a funded research institute with a journal, a correspondence, personnel files, published quarrels, and a paper trail running through three countries and two languages. Its members' books were printed by commercial and university publishers, reviewed in newspapers, and argued over in the open for decades. If a coordinated project of cultural subversion had existed, it would have had to be coordinated somewhere, by someone, in writing or in minuted speech, among men who wrote letters the way other men breathe. No such documentation has ever been produced, by the narrative's authors or anyone else, in the decades during which archives have been open and scholars of every persuasion have worked them. What the archives contain instead is the record summarised in this section, and the record contradicts the narrative at every load bearing point. The full institutional history is set out in [The Frankfurt School: A Complete Guide](/2015/06/01/frankfurt-school-complete-guide/); what follows is the portion of it that bears directly on the plot.

### What did the Institute's members actually argue about?

They argued about nearly everything their narrative assigns to a single mind: whether mass culture reconciled or stupefied, whether psychoanalysis belonged inside historical materialism, whether the Soviet Union was a model or a warning, whether reason itself was implicated in domination. Their correspondence records the disputes in detail, unresolved and often unkind.

Consider the disagreements the narrative requires its reader to forget. Erich Fromm, the institute's psychoanalyst, developed a social psychology of authoritarian submission that his colleagues came to reject, and he departed the institute in 1939 after years of friction over money, method, and doctrine, conducting a feud with Adorno in print for years afterward. Walter Benjamin, the institute's most famous stipendiary, never held its membership and chafed under its editorial demands; Adorno's letters criticising the Baudelaire study Benjamin submitted in 1938 are models of courteous demolition, accusing its method of exactly the undialectical materialism the institute existed to correct, while Benjamin complained to friends that his work was being strangled by Frankfurt's caution. Horkheimer and Adorno's wartime book together, the bleakest text the school produced, was not shared by Marcuse, whose postwar writings moved toward an affirmation of liberation that Adorno regarded with open scepticism. In 1969, when students occupied the institute in Frankfurt, Adorno acquiesced in calling the police; Marcuse, writing from California, criticised the decision and defended the students' impulse. A conspiracy requires a command structure or at least a common doctrine. The institute had a director, a journal, and a stipend budget. Its common doctrine is not visible in its own papers, because it did not exist.

Nor does the record show anything resembling a programme for the culture of the West. The institute published a journal, the Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung, whose pages carried econometrics, family sociology, musicology, and philosophy in no discernible order of operations. Its collective projects were empirical studies: of authority and the family, of workers' attitudes, later of prejudice in America. Its members wrote books that contradicted one another and then revised their own. The narrative's handling of this record is instructive. Where the school agreed, the narrative finds the plan. Where it disagreed, the narrative finds camouflage or division of labour. A method that reads both agreement and disagreement as confirmations has exempted itself from evidence, and a reader is entitled to notice the exemption and to decline the method, whatever the reader's politics.

### Did any of them work for the American government?

Several did, in the least clandestine way available: they held salaried posts in the American wartime state. Franz Neumann, Herbert Marcuse, and Otto Kirchheimer worked for the Office of Strategic Services, analysing the enemy they had fled, and Neumann's study of the Nazi regime was published openly in 1942.

The exile itinerary itself resists the plot. The institute left Frankfurt in 1933 ahead of the police, paused in Geneva, and re established itself in New York under the shelter of Columbia University, which gave it a building and academic legitimacy. Its endowment, diminished by the Depression and by the costs of flight, forced economies and job hunting on its members. During the war, several of them took the most explicable employment available to German speaking experts on Germany: Neumann, Marcuse, and Kirchheimer analysed the Nazi state for the American Office of Strategic Services, and Neumann's Behemoth, the standard anatomy of that state, was published for anyone to read in 1942. Marcuse remained in American government service into the early 1950s, working on the Soviet bloc for the State Department. These are not the biographies of men running a secret war against their host country. They are the biographies of refugees who had lost one country to the regime the narrative's own ancestors excused, and who repaid their refuge with the only capital they had, which was knowledge of the enemy.

### What were their tastes in art and music?

Their tastes were austere to the point of severity. Adorno championed Schoenberg and the difficult modernists, treated jazz as a commodity wearing rebellion's clothes, and held that serious art kept faith with suffering by refusing consolation. The school defended a narrow canon of demanding works against the popular culture it is accused of designing.

The cultural record completes the contradiction. The school's best known cultural argument, the culture industry chapter of its wartime book, is an attack on commercial entertainment: on Hollywood plotting, hit song formulae, and the amusement that reconciles its consumers to their world. Adorno's writings on music defend twelve tone composition and the late Beethoven quartets against a listening public he thought was regressing into distraction, and his essays on jazz, unfair as many readers have found them, are the work of a man who heard in popular music the very administered culture he spent his life opposing. Whatever one thinks of these positions, and this series examines them on their merits in [the culture industry explained](/2015/07/15/culture-industry-explained/), they are the aesthetics of cultural conservatives of a peculiarly uncompromising kind: mandarin, pessimistic, and hostile to the commercial mainstream. The narrative asks its audience to believe that the authors of these texts were engineering the triumph of the entertainment they despised. Belief of that order requires the documents to be read as their own opposite, which is not reading at all.

The pessimism deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the narrative's most quietly impossible premise. The school's mature work does not read as a strategy for transforming Western culture. It reads, overwhelmingly, as an explanation of why transformation had failed and would go on failing: enlightenment collapsing into mythology, reason into administration, rebellion into lifestyle. Its authors described their wartime book as written for a future reader who might find it as a castaway's message is found, without any confidence that the finding would change anything. Men who expect their message to wash up unread do not design half centuries of cultural engineering. Pessimism of that depth is a strange engine for a plot, and the narrative has never explained why the plotters bothered, on its own premises, to be so hopeless.

Finally, the phenomena themselves refuse the assignment. The great cultural changes of the postwar decades arrive in the historical record with their own paperwork. The contraceptive pill was a pharmaceutical development with a clinical and commercial history. Rock and roll emerged from the economics of independent record labels and the meeting of Black and white musical traditions in American cities. The civil rights movement grew out of Black churches, labour organising, and a constitutional strategy argued in courts. Second wave feminism published its own books, founded its own organisations, and quarrelled with male Marxists at least as bitterly as with anyone. Television transformed domestic culture because advertisers paid for audiences, a mechanism no exile designed. Historians of each development trace its causes in archives that do not pass through Frankfurt. The narrative's method is to gather all of it, retrospectively, into a single intention located in a handful of difficult German books. Gathered evidence is not found evidence, and the distinction is the whole of the case.

## The strongest objection to this article, stated fairly

An article of this kind owes its reader the best case against itself, stated at the strength its proponents would choose, and the case is not weak. It runs as follows. Whatever the phrase's origins, the dismissal of it as a conspiracy narrative has become, in practice, a technique for ending arguments rather than winning them. A citizen observes that Marxist ideas exercise real influence in universities, in teacher training, in publishing, in the curatorial and programming choices of cultural institutions, and reaches for the vocabulary in common circulation to say so. The reply comes back, not with a denial of the influence, which would require engaging the evidence, but with a genealogy: the phrase is tainted, its ancestry is fringe, a murderer's manifesto contained it, and therefore the observation need not be answered. The citizen is placed, by association, in company the citizen never sought, and the argument about influence, which may have been a good one, is never heard. On this objection, histories of the term function as border guards for acceptable speech, protecting institutions from scrutiny by discrediting the words in which scrutiny arrives. The objector adds that the scrutiny is warranted: that the migration of Marxist categories into the humanities and the professions is documented in plain sight, in course catalogues and citation counts, and that a culture of dismissal has made an ordinary empirical claim unsayable in respectable company. If this article is one more instrument of that dismissal, the objection concludes, then its scholarship is a costume and its fairness a tactic.

The objection deserves a direct answer, beginning with concession. The point about foreclosed argument is conceded. Wherever the phrase's genealogy has been used to refuse a hearing to an evidence bearing argument about intellectual influence, the use has been intellectually disreputable, and the histories that supply the excuse bear part of the blame. A term's ancestry cannot acquit or convict a claim. Arguments stand on their evidence, and the citizen described above has offered the beginnings of evidence: institutions, curricula, citations, appointments. The correct response is to examine it, which this article's second usage exists to make possible. Nothing in the three-usage separation prevents the argument from being made. The separation insists only that it be made as what it is: a claim about the diffusion of ideas, which requires tracing the diffusion, rather than as a claim about a plot, which requires the plotters.

### When is an influence claim a scholarly claim?

An influence claim is scholarly when it can be checked: when it names the text, the reader, the syllabus, the appointment, the citation, and lets the chain be audited link by link. It leaves scholarship when it asks atmosphere to do the work of evidence and intention to do the work of transmission.

The distinction the objection needs is therefore preserved, and sharpened, by the separation rather than threatened by it. Consider the two claims a speaker might make with the phrase. The first: Marxist frameworks shaped whole disciplines in the later twentieth century, through identifiable books, teachers, journals, and appointments, with consequences the speaker deplores and can enumerate. The second: a named group of exiles designed those consequences in advance as a strategy of civilisational subversion, coordinating their execution across generations. The first claim is the scholarly question wearing public dress. It is arguable, evidence bearing, partly true and partly false in ways that research can determine, and nobody's ancestry discredits it. The second claim is the conspiracy narrative. What separates them is not tone, party, or politeness. It is the assertion of coordination and deliberate intent, for which, after decades of open archives, no evidence exists. An objector who holds the first claim loses nothing by abandoning the second. The objector gains a hearing, because the second claim's presence in an argument is precisely what allows lazy opponents to refuse the first claim its hearing. The separation is thus not a favour done to the phrase's critics. It is the strongest available defence of the legitimate argument, cut free from the plot that drags it down.

Two further courtesies follow, and this article has tried to practise both. The first is that users of the phrase are not their phrase's history. A speaker who absorbed the term from ordinary political conversation has not endorsed Fidelio magazine, the LaRouche movement, or any manifesto, and treating the speaker as though the whole transmission chain were a personal confession is the association fallacy in its purest form. The second is that the scholarly question's legitimacy does not depend on the answer going any particular way. A historian may conclude that Marxist influence in the universities was vast, or that it was a fashion of a few departments, or that its effects were mostly rhetorical, and all three conclusions belong to the same legitimate conversation. The conversation is what this article defends. The plot is what it refuses, because the plot is false to the record, and falsehood about dead men who cannot answer is a small thing to renounce at the price of an honest argument.

## Documenting a usage without endorsing it

The work done so far raises a methodological question worth answering in its own right, because readers will face the same problem with other terms: how does a historian document a polemical usage, fully and fairly, without becoming its amplifier? The craft has conventions, developed in the study of propaganda and of conspiracy rhetoric, and they are worth stating as this article has tried to observe them.

### How should a contested term be dated and attributed?

A contested term should be handled the way an editor handles a contested quotation: with its earliest located uses, its venues, its authors, and its changes of meaning recorded in order, and with each use attributed to the writer who made it rather than to the atmosphere of a decade.

The first convention is the primacy of the document. A usage exists where it can be shown: on a title page, in a dated article, in conference proceedings, in a catalogue. Historians therefore build a term's biography from located instances, each with a venue and a date, and they distinguish what the instances prove from what repetition merely suggests. Applied here, the convention yields the shape of the whole article: a scholarly title of 1997 for the label, a literature of debate for the question, a magazine article of 1992 and a foundation's presentations at the decade's end for the narrative. Where a detail cannot be located in a document, the honest historian omits it rather than decorating the account with it, and this article has followed that rule wherever the popular literature offered colour that the record would not certify.

The second convention is attribution without ventriloquism. A polemical usage is reported in the historian's own prose, at the level of its structure and claims about the world, without adopting its vocabulary for the people it describes and without quoting its texts at a length that lets them argue their own case in the historian's pages. The distinction is delicate but real. To say that a narrative casts exiled scholars as secret engineers of decline is to describe the narrative. To reproduce its catalogue of their alleged deeds, item by item, in its own order and rhetoric, is to publish the narrative with a disapproving preface, which most readers will experience as the narrative. This article has consistently chosen the first course, and the choice explains several of its silences, which are deliberate rather than evasive.

The third convention concerns proportion. A history of a term should allocate its space by the term's documentary and intellectual weight, not by the voltage of its most extreme usage. The conspiracy narrative is the loudest of the three usages and the least substantial: one founding article, one foundation's campaign, a scholarly literature of refutation and analysis. The scholarly question, by contrast, is a century of books. An article that inverted those proportions, dwelling lovingly on the plot and glancing at the question, would misinform its reader about the term even if every sentence were accurate, because prominence is itself a claim. This article has therefore kept the narrative's exposition shorter than the treatment of the other two usages combined, and placed its centre of gravity in the correction rather than in the thing corrected.

The fourth convention is the separations of persons from phrases, already practised above and worth naming as method. The historian of a term studies texts and their transmission. The people who repeat a phrase in ordinary life are data about transmission, not defendants in a trial of the phrase's origins, and the historian who forgets this will write contemptuously about audiences while meaning to write carefully about documents. Readers who have encountered the phrase in good faith are the very audience a history of the term is for. It should read, to them, as an offer of equipment rather than as a rebuke, and the test of success is whether such a reader finishes the article better able to say what they mean, not shamed out of meaning anything.

## The literature the label points to

A descriptive label is only as good as the shelf it marks, and the shelf in this case is long, public, and well catalogued. Marxist cultural analysis is a literature with founding documents, classic controversies, and a century of accumulated commentary, and understanding what it contains is the surest way to understand both the descriptive usage and the scholarly question, since the literature is the evidence each of them handles. The full survey belongs to [the companion volume](/2020/06/01/marxist-cultural-theory-guide/); here the outline is enough to establish the kind of thing the label denotes: not an organisation, not a campaign, but a bookshelf, assembled by writers who mostly never met and frequently annoyed one another.

### What does the label gather when scholars use it carefully?

It gathers a literature rather than a movement: essays, histories, and studies in which Marxist categories are applied to novels, newspapers, schools, songs, and screens, by writers who shared a method loosely and an adversary clearly, and who published their disagreements in the same journals that carried their agreements.

The literature's beginnings lie inside the founding texts, in passages their author treated as instruments rather than ornaments. The German Ideology, drafted in 1845 and 1846 and published in full only in the twentieth century, contains the tradition's first theory of culture in a single compressed claim: that the ideas of the ruling class are in every epoch the ruling ideas, since the class that controls material production controls the production and distribution of ideas as well. Marx's journalism practised the theory for decades, in newspaper analyses of theatre, censorship, and the popular press. The Eighteenth Brumaire anatomised the costumes in which a political crisis dresses itself, borrowed from earlier revolutions. None of this was marginal to the work. It was the work's connective tissue, the account of how an economic order becomes a lived world. Later generations who turned toward culture were not importing a foreign substance into Marxism. They were developing a vein the founders had opened and then, under the pressure of other tasks, left comparatively unworked.

The vein was mined hardest where politics had failed. Lukács, whose essays of 1923 supplied the scholarly question with its first great exhibit, moved between philosophy and the novel for the rest of his career, writing on realism, on Balzac and Scott, on the historical novel as the form in which a society narrates its own becoming. His standard was the nineteenth century realist tradition, and he wielded it against modernism with a severity that set him at odds with half the writers this article has occasion to mention. Brecht answered from the theatre, defending experiment against Lukács's realism in a controversy conducted through essays neither man published in his lifetime. Benjamin answered from the arcade and the cinema, arguing that reproducibility had changed what an artwork is. Gramsci answered from a cell, treating folklore, serial novels, and the national popular literature as evidence about the consent of the governed. Adorno answered from the concert hall and the radio studio, defending the most difficult music of his century against the entertainment industry assembling around it. A literature whose canonical figures disagree about whether modernism is a symptom or a hope, whether realism is a standard or a prison, and whether popular art can be trusted at all, is many things. A coordinated instrument is not among them, and the disagreements are not an embarrassment to the descriptive label but its content: the label groups a quarrel.

The British generation gathered by Dworkin's history extended the quarrel into new territory. Williams rebuilt the vocabulary, giving scholarship the durable distinction between dominant, residual, and emergent cultures, and insisting that a whole way of life, not merely its artefacts, was the proper object of study. Thompson fought for the experience of ordinary people against both economic reductionism and the structuralism arriving from France, in a polemic of 1978 whose ferocity his friends and enemies still quote. Hall and his Birmingham colleagues took the method to television, youth subcultures, and the popular press, coining analyses of encoding and decoding that media studies absorbed wholesale. Feminist critics then turned the tradition's tools on the tradition itself, asking why its exemplary subjects were so often men at work, and rebuilt its account of culture around domestic labour, sexuality, and the organisation of private life. Each wave corrected the previous one in public. The shelf kept growing because the argument kept moving, which is what shelves of living scholarship do.

Two consequences follow for the history of the term. The first is that the descriptive label, where scholars use it, points at this: a sprawling, self correcting, internally hostile literature, produced on several continents over more than a century, housed in identifiable institutions, and available for inspection in any research library. Nothing about it is hidden, and its influence, where it has influence, travels by the ordinary vehicles of print, teaching, and fashion. The second consequence concerns the scholarly question. The question whether this literature represents a shift in the tradition's centre is intelligent precisely because the literature is so substantial and so different in texture from the critique of political economy that preceded it. A reader who has seen the shelf understands why historians argue about what its growth signifies. A reader who has only met the phrase in its polemical career may mistake the shelf for a headquarters, which is the single error from which the third usage draws all its strength.

## A parallel case: the prisoner absorbed into the plot

No figure demonstrates the narrative's method more cleanly than the one it conscripts from outside its own cast. Antonio Gramsci was an Italian communist leader who spent his final decade in Mussolini's prisons and died in 1937, leaving notebooks that were smuggled out, preserved by his sister in law, and published after the war in editions arranged by theme. He was never a member of the Frankfurt Institute, never visited it, and conducted no correspondence with its directors that the record preserves. His work belongs to a different country, a different party, and a different intellectual world from the exiles in New York. Yet in the narrative's economy he is indispensable, because his central concept, hegemony, supplies the one thing the Frankfurt materials cannot supply on their own: a theory of how a society's culture is led, contested, and slowly transformed. The narrative detaches the concept from the notebooks, strips it of its author's strategic context, and installs it as the plot's mechanism. The result is a story in which an Italian prisoner who died before the Second World War becomes the theorist of an American operation conducted by German exiles he never met.

### Why does Gramsci appear in a story about a Frankfurt institute?

Because the narrative needs a theory of cultural leadership and finds Gramsci's hegemony ready made. He was not a member of the institute, wrote in an Italian prison, and died in 1937, before the school's American period began. His presence in the story is borrowed prestige: a real concept, detached from its text and reassigned to a plot.

Gramsci's own reception is a large subject with its own literature, treated in this series in [Gramsci and his misreaders](/2015/05/01/gramsci-reception-and-misreadings/), and it rehearses, in a milder key, every operation the narrative performs. The postwar editions of the notebooks, arranged thematically by editors with a party's needs in view, made it easy to read Gramsci as the sponsor of strategies he never discussed. Translators selected, anthologists excerpted, and movements of opposite colours each carried away a portable Gramsci: the democrat, the Leninist, the theorist of civil society, the patron of cultural studies. Some of those appropriations were fruitful and some were fanciful, but they share a structure with the narrative's use of him: a difficult corpus, published posthumously and in fragments, is unusually available for reassignment, because the reader must assemble the author before arguing with him, and the assembly can be rigged. The scholarly remedy in Gramsci's case is well established. Read the notebooks in their critical edition, date the fragments, restore the context of composition, and the portable Gramscis dissolve into a single, more difficult, more interesting thinker. The remedy generalises. Every figure in the narrative's cast has a critical edition, a correspondence, a chronology; the narrative survives only where those are left unopened.

There is a further irony in the conscription, and it is worth recording because it shows how little the plot respects its own materials. Hegemony, in the notebooks, is a concept about consent organised through the institutions of civil society, and its author's strategic writings are concerned with the open contest for that consent: with newspapers, schools, parties, and the slow construction of alliances in public. The concept presupposes visibility. A hegemony that operated in secret would not be hegemony in Gramsci's sense at all, since its whole mechanism is the winning of assent that knows itself, however imperfectly, as assent. To recruit the concept as the name for a hidden manipulation is to reverse its meaning while borrowing its authority. The narrative does this sort of thing habitually, and the habit is diagnostic: a story that must invert its sources to use them is telling its reader, in advance, what its relationship to evidence will be.

## Three usages, three geographies

The three usages did not merely begin in different places. They travelled by different roads, at different speeds, and arrived in different countries in different orders, which is why the phrase's meaning in any given conversation depends so heavily on where the conversation is happening and what its participants have been reading. A history of the term that ignores its transport system will mistake the map for the territory. The fuller account of the phrase's public career, alongside the other terms of Marxist controversy, is given in this series in [Marxism in political discourse](/2022/02/01/marxism-in-political-discourse/); what follows is the portion needed to explain the divergence.

### What carried the phrase across borders?

Print, translation, and polemic carried it, at different speeds in different places. The scholarly usages travelled through universities and journals, slowly and with citations. The narrative travelled through conferences, broadcasts, and copied texts, quickly and without them. Each country received the version its channels carried first, and the versions have been strangers ever since.

The descriptive label travelled the way scholarship travels: through monographs ordered by libraries, conference papers, translations commissioned by university presses, and the slow osmosis of syllabuses. Its natural habitat was the English speaking academy and its extensions, and its movement can be traced in catalogues with unusual completeness, since libraries record what they acquire and when. Where it arrived, it arrived as a minor term of art, filed among its synonyms, and it has remained minor everywhere. No country possesses a public that recognises the descriptive usage first. That is the fate of labels that do no polemical work: they stay where they were made.

The scholarly question travelled further and faster, because it was carried by the prestige and the troubles of Marxism itself. In France, the question was posed through the structuralist controversies of the 1960s and 1970s, when Althusser's school and its critics fought over whether ideology was a system of representations or the very medium of lived experience. In Britain it was posed through the New Left's institutions and the Birmingham centre, and through arguments about whether the working class had been culturally remade by affluence. In the United States it arrived with the translations of the 1970s, when the Frankfurt School's works entered English in a flood and American humanities departments discovered, in the exiles' writings, tools for analysing their own mass culture. In each country the question took the colour of the local academy, and in each it remained a question, debated with texts in hand. The divergence among national versions of the second usage is the ordinary divergence of scholarship, and it is manageable by ordinary means: translation, citation, conference, correction.

The narrative travelled by other roads entirely, and its geography has the shape of its vehicles. Composed in an American movement magazine, condensed by an American policy foundation, it moved outward through the circuits that connect the Anglophone political world: syndicated columns, conference tours, broadcast commentary, and then the copying ecology of the internet, where a text with a plot travels farther than a text with footnotes because the plot survives excerpting and the footnotes do not. In translation it lost what little attribution it retained, arriving in other languages as a free floating explanation for cultural change, detached from the Frankfurt names at one remove and reattached at another. Countries whose publics met the narrative first, through imported polemic, acquired a usage in which the plot is simply what the phrase means, and speakers in those places who reach for the scholarly question find themselves unintelligible to their neighbours until terms are defined. Countries whose universities received the translations of the 1970s first possess a residual scholarly sense that polemic must argue against rather than merely ignore. The divergence is thus not a mystery of national character. It is a shipping record. Whoever's channel arrived first set the default meaning, and defaults, in vocabulary as in machinery, are what most users never think to change.

One further feature of the public career deserves record, because it explains the phrase's peculiar resilience. The narrative version of the term performs a service for its users that neither scholarly usage can match: it converts a diffuse unease about cultural change into a story with agents, and a story with agents can be repeated, taught, and recognised in a sentence. The scholarly question, by contrast, offers a research programme, and the descriptive label offers a filing decision. In an economy of attention, the plot wins the circulation contest regardless of its truth, which is why refutation alone has never slowed it and why this article has been built as a history rather than as a refutation. A refutation addresses a claim. A history changes what a reader sees when the phrase arrives: not a claim to be answered but a fork in the road, three ways, with signposts, dates, and the state of the evidence posted at each turning.

## Telling the usages apart in the wild

The separation defended in this article is useful only if a reader can operate it on texts that arrive unlabelled, which is how texts arrive. This section therefore sets out the marks by which the usages are distinguished in practice, drawn from the record assembled so far. None of the marks concerns the politics of the writer. All of them concern the writer's relationship to evidence, which is the only ground on which the distinction can honestly be drawn.

The first mark is behaviour toward documents. A scholarly use of the phrase, in either of its first two senses, can be followed back to texts: to the title page of 1997, to the notebooks, to the institute's journal, to a controversy conducted in named journals between named antagonists. The trail may be contested, but it exists, and the writer expects the reader to walk it. A polemical use characteristically offers a trail that circles: the narrative is documented by summaries of the narrative, the summaries by the founding article, and the founding article by assertion. When the reader asks for the plan, the minutes, the letter, the instruction, the trail ends in a gesture toward the plot's secrecy. That gesture is the genre's signature, and recognising it requires no agreement with this article about anything else.

The second mark is behaviour toward the named people's own words. Scholarly usage quotes its subjects against itself when the texts require it: it records that the exiles despised the entertainment they are said to have engineered, that the prisoner theorised open consent rather than hidden manipulation, that the supposed movement published its disagreements. Polemical usage treats the texts as a code in which every statement means its opposite or conceals its meaning, so that the subjects' words can never testify in their own defence. A usage that cannot be embarrassed by its subjects' books is not describing those books. It is describing its own requirements, and the reader should price it accordingly.

The third mark is behaviour toward chronology. The scholarly question lives inside dates: it matters that Lukács wrote in 1923 and Anderson in 1976, that the notebooks were composed in a cell between 1929 and 1935, that the institute reached New York in 1934 with its endowment shrinking and its members job hunting. Sequence is the scholar's discipline, because causes must precede effects and influences must have time to travel. The narrative is strikingly indifferent to sequence, gathering effects from decades its alleged causes cannot reach and crediting men with the deeds of movements that began after their deaths. Indifference to chronology is not a stylistic flaw. It is the visible sign that a text's ordering principle is not the order of events but the order of the plot.

### What should a reader ask of an undated use of the term?

Ask three things. Which usage is this: label, question, or plot? What document is offered, and can it be opened? What would the speaker accept as evidence against the claim? A use that cannot answer the first question has not begun, and a claim that cannot answer the third has ended before it started.

These marks convert into a reader's protocol, and the protocol is short enough to carry into any encounter with the phrase. Establish the usage first, before engaging any content, by asking which of the three things is being asserted; most confused arguments dissolve at this step, since the parties discover they were answering different assertions. Ask for the document next, and prefer primary paper: the book, the letter, the journal, the archive, over any summary of them, including this article, whose own claims are offered with their sources named precisely so that they can be checked rather than believed. Ask about falsification last: a claim that no imaginable evidence could dent is a claim about the speaker's commitments, not about the Frankfurt School, and it should be discussed as such, openly and without rancour. The protocol will not make the phrase's users agree. It will make them disagree about the same thing, which is the precondition of every argument worth having and the whole ambition of the three-usage separation.

One more distinction, the subtlest in this article, separates the scholarly question from its polemical imitation. Both concern influence, and both may reach critical conclusions about the place of Marxist ideas in modern institutions. The scholarly version measures influence by transmission: a text read, a teacher appointed, a syllabus set, a concept borrowed with acknowledgment or without it, each link inspectable and each open to the reply that the borrowing was superficial, the appointment accidental, the fashion brief. The imitation measures influence by outcome: because a later development resembles something an earlier writer is alleged to have wanted, the earlier writer is installed as its author. Resemblance is not transmission, and outcome is not intent. The whole of intellectual history is contained in that pair of sentences, and a reader who carries them away from this article will be equipped for a hundred contested terms besides the one dissected here.

## The scholarly question examined more closely

Because the second usage carries this article's centre of gravity, its anatomy deserves a fuller dissection than a summary can give. A reader who understands how the question arose, in what documents, and with what internal divisions, possesses the strongest available inoculation against the third usage, since the plot withers fastest in the presence of the actual debate it plagiarises. This section therefore walks the question through its principal stations, in the order the tradition itself travelled them.

The prehistory lies in the decade after the First World War, when the Marxist movement possessed both a victorious revolution in Russia and a series of defeats in the advanced industrial countries, and had to explain the difference. The explanations on offer divided along a line the century would keep redrawing. One tradition, strong in the parties, held that the defeats were organisational and temporary: the economics were sound, the consciousness would follow the crisis, the task was discipline. Another, arising among intellectuals at the movement's edge, suspected that the missing variable was inside the subject: that the working class's picture of the world, formed by school, press, church, and the commodity form itself, was part of the mechanism of its defeat. Lukács gave this suspicion its philosophical form in 1923. Korsch gave it a history of philosophy. The Comintern's own debates circled the same problem under the heading of ideology. Nothing here was quiet. The suspicion was published, denounced by party authorities as idealist deviation, defended, revised, and carried into exile by its holders, which is to say it behaved exactly as scholarly questions behave when institutions have opinions about them.

### What did the Institute's own programme say it was doing?

Its founding statements say it plainly: an interdisciplinary study of the whole social process, joining economics, sociology, psychology, and philosophy, because the failure of prediction had shown that no single discipline could explain why societies hold together or fall apart. The programme was published, argued over, and revised in the open.

The Frankfurt Institute gave the question its institutional form. Its first director, Carl Grünberg, had run it as an archive and research centre for the history of socialism and the labour movement, empirical and documentary in temper. When Horkheimer succeeded him in 1930, his inaugural lecture announced a programme of interdisciplinary materialism: the study of the connections among economic life, the psychic development of individuals, and the changes in the cultural sphere, pursued by a standing team of economists, sociologists, psychologists, and philosophers working on shared problems. The announcement was a public academic act, printed in the institute's journal, and its reasoning was the scholarly question stated as a research design. The European working class had not behaved as the classical theory led its adherents to expect. Either the theory was wrong, or it was incomplete in a specific way: it lacked an account of the mediations, the family, the school, the authority structure of the psyche, through which an economic order reproduces itself in the minds of those who sustain it. The institute's famous empirical projects, on authority and family in Germany, on the attitudes of workers and employees, were attempts to measure those mediations. A conspiracy does not publish its programme in a journal and invite the republic of letters to check its questionnaires. A research institute does, and did.

The exile transformed the question again. In America the institute's members encountered a mass culture more thoroughly commercialised than Europe's, a working class being reorganised by radio and cinema, and a social science establishment that demanded empirical credentials. Their responses divided, as this article has recorded: the empirical studies of prejudice, the philosophical despair of the wartime book, Fromm's departure into a therapeutic humanism, Marcuse's long American career in government and then in the universities of California. Adorno's collaboration and friction with American empirical sociology, in the Princeton radio research and in the studies of the authoritarian personality, belong to the documented history of social science itself. The point for the term's history is that the question kept being argued by its own protagonists, in prefaces and polemics, with each relocation and each defeat. Its archive is the opposite of a secret: it is a paper trail so abundant that its historians complain of excess.

The postwar decades posed the question in reverse. As European Marxism rebuilt itself, a generation of scholars asked whether the cultural and philosophical emphasis of the exiles had been a detour imposed by catastrophe, and whether the tradition's future lay in a return to political economy. The structural Marxism of Althusser's Paris seminar attempted a scientific restoration, expelling the humanist and cultural readings as ideology. Thompson's assault on that restoration, and the ferocity of the ensuing quarrel, showed the question's persistence: the two camps agreed on the tradition's importance and on nothing else, least of all on whether its centre should be the economy, the psyche, or the lived experience of class. Anderson's mapping of 1976, and his sequel reflections a decade later on the arguments of Western Marxism, gave the debate its enduring geography. When a single phrase is later used to name this entire contested field, the scholarly usage is born: the phrase as a filing label for the question, wherever an author needs a short name for a long argument. That, and nothing more sinister, is the genealogy of usage two.

## Influence, traced the honest way

The objection answered earlier in this article rests on a true premise: the ideas of the Marxist tradition, and of the Frankfurt School within it, have exercised real influence on universities and on the culture those universities serve. The three-usage separation would be worthless if it required denying that premise, and it does not. What it requires is that influence be discussed the way historians discuss it, as a process with vehicles, dates, and receipts. This section performs that discussion in miniature, tracing three documented transmissions, to show what the scholarly usage looks like when it is doing its proper work and how sharply it differs, in method, from the plot that borrows its conclusions.

### How can real influence on universities be shown?

By the audit trail of print and employment: translations commissioned and dated, editions adopted, citations counted, appointments made, journals founded, students taught who taught others in turn. Influence shown this way is often large and always arguable, because every link can be inspected and its weight disputed by anyone with library access.

The first transmission is the translation wave. For three decades after the war, most of the Frankfurt School's work existed for English readers in fragments: a chapter here, an essay there, the wartime book available from 1973 in a full translation that became a campus classic. The 1970s saw the corpus arrive in bulk, through the efforts of identifiable translators and publishers, and the effect on American and British humanities can be followed in the ordinary records of academic life: new courses, new journals, dissertations, citations accumulating in bibliographies. Martin Jay's history of the school, published in 1973 when its author was a young historian at Berkeley, both documented the institute and served as one of its transmission belts, introducing a generation of graduate students to a cast of exiles most had never heard of. Nothing in this story is hidden. Its agents are translators with contracts, editors with lists, professors with syllabuses. A scholar who wishes to minimise the school's influence can contest the weight of each link, and scholars have: the receipts permit the argument, which is precisely what makes the argument scholarship.

The second transmission runs through Birmingham. When Stuart Hall and his colleagues built the study of contemporary culture in Britain, they worked openly from Gramsci's notebooks, in the selections then available, adapting the concept of hegemony to the analysis of postwar Britain in essays that announced their sources on their first pages. The line of descent from a Turin prison cell to a Birmingham research centre is real, documentable, and entirely unmysterious: it runs through published translations, a reading group culture, and the normal ambitions of scholars building a field. It also involved transformation. Hall's Gramsci is not the party leader of 1926 but a theorist of consent refitted for a media age, and Gramsci scholars have argued for decades about what the refitting kept and what it lost. That argument, conducted in journals with the texts open on the table, is what genuine influence looks like from inside. It is slower than the narrative's version, less thrilling, and true.

The third transmission is the most instructive because it is the one the narrative most often cites as its proof: the arrival of critical theory in the American humanities, and the prestige it acquired there from the 1970s onward. The documented vehicles are mundane. Comparative literature departments, seeking theory for a discipline reorganising itself, adopted the exiles' essays alongside French structuralism and post structuralism. Journals were founded to carry the work. A small number of charismatic teachers trained students who dispersed to other universities. The publishing industry discovered that difficult European theory sold to libraries. Each element is checkable, and the whole has been described, celebrated, and deplored in a literature of academic memoir and disciplinary history that anyone may read. What the record does not contain is direction. No committee assigned the theory its departments. No strategy paper survives because none was written. The diffusion followed the incentives of academic life, prestige, novelty, the needs of disciplines in flux, as diffusions of ideas generally do.

Set the three transmissions side by side and the methodological moral is plain. Real influence is lumpy, belated, contested, and full of accidents: a translator's energy, a department's vacancy, a student's enthusiasm, a publisher's hunch. It leaves receipts everywhere and intentions almost nowhere. The narrative's influence is the opposite: smooth, immediate, total, and intentional, leaving no receipts at all. A reader deciding which account to trust is not choosing between two politics. The reader is choosing between two kinds of causation, one of which is how ideas are known to move through the world and one of which is how they move in stories. The scholarly usage, at its best, exists to enforce that choice, and the citizen who deploys it against the narrative is not defending the Frankfurt School. The citizen is defending the difference between history and fiction.

## Why a plot about intellectuals persuades

A history of this term would be incomplete if it explained only the narrative's falsity and not its plausibility, since falsity alone has never handicapped a story that fits its audience's experience. The narrative persuades, where it persuades, by exploiting genuine features of intellectual life, and naming those features is part of the correction, because a reader who sees the trick's machinery is less likely to buy the trick. This section examines the machinery, in general terms that apply to the genre wherever it appears.

The first feature is the real opacity of scholarly influence. Ideas do move from books into classrooms, from classrooms into professions, from professions into the assumptions of a culture, and the movement is genuinely hard to see. No citizen can watch it happen the way one watches a market or a parliament. Where a process is influential and invisible, the imagination supplies a control room. The narrative is, at bottom, a control room drawn around a black box: it takes the undoubted fact that university ideas percolate outward and gives the percolation a board of directors. The scholarly usages offer the truth, which is that the box contains thousands of uncoordinated people pursuing prestige, truth, fashion, and employment in proportions no one has ever measured. Between a board of directors and an unmeasurable crowd, the board is the better story, and stories are what circulate.

The second feature is the unreadability, to most audiences, of the evidence itself. The Frankfurt School's books are difficult, long, and mostly encountered at second hand. A claim about their contents is therefore cheap to make and expensive to check, and in any argument the side whose claims are expensive to check enjoys an advantage among listeners who will not pay the cost. This asymmetry is not unique to the present case; it attends every controversy about technical or forbidding texts, from scripture to statistics. The narrative exploits it systematically, attributing to the books designs that a reader of the books would not find, secure in the knowledge that the attribution will be tested by almost no one. The remedy is not to demand that every citizen read Adorno. It is to insist that claims about books be made by people who show signs of having opened them, and to treat fluency about a text's secret meaning, unaccompanied by quotation, as the warning sign it is.

The third feature is the cast's genuine foreignness, which the narrative converts into alienness. The exiles were German, largely Jewish, formed by a European catastrophe, writing in a translated idiom about a country not their own. Every element of that description is a fact, and the narrative's work consists entirely in the gloss: foreign becomes alien, refugee becomes infiltrator, the analysis of American culture by grateful guests becomes the sabotage of American culture by resentful enemies. Historians of conspiracy rhetoric recognise the conversion as the genre's oldest move, practised on Jesuits in one century, on Freemasons in another, on bankers and Bolsheviks in a third, with the cast rotated and the stage directions unchanged. The antiquity of the move is itself evidence about it. A form of explanation that fits every cast explains the form, not the casts.

The fourth feature is the kernel of accuracy that the narrative wraps around its invention, a technique the analysts of the genre call the truth sandwich's dishonest cousin. It is true that the exiles criticised American mass culture. It is true that their ideas later influenced universities. It is true that Gramsci theorised cultural leadership and that Marcuse was read by student movements. The narrative places its single invention, coordination with intent, between these truths, so that a listener who verifies any of the truths feels the whole construction has been verified. Checking then stops, which is the construction's purpose. The discipline this article has practised, separating what is documented from what is asserted at every joint, is the only reliable countermeasure, because it denies the invention its borrowed credentials.

None of this requires ill will on the audience's part, and the article has been careful throughout not to impute any. Cultural change is real, often bewildering, and frequently experienced as loss by people whose world is being rearranged by forces they did not choose. An explanation that names agents answers a human need that a bibliography cannot. The scholarly usages, honestly handled, can meet part of that need: they can show which ideas actually travelled, who carried them, and what institutions rewarded the carrying, and they can leave the remainder where it belongs, with economics, technology, demography, and accident. What they cannot do, and should not pretend to do, is restore a world in which change has no address. The narrative promises that address. The promise is the product. The history of the term is the inspection report, and the reader now has it.

## The term in the reference room

There is a room in every library where contested terms are supposed to be neutralised: the reference shelf, home of dictionaries, encyclopedias, and handbooks, whose genre conventions were designed for exactly the situation this phrase presents. Watching how the reference genre handles the term, and where its handling has strained, is a useful test of the three-usage separation, because the reference entry is the one literary form obliged to be fair to all usages at once.

### How do reference works handle a term like this one?

By recording rather than ruling. A sound reference entry lists the term's distinguishable senses, attaches dates and sources to each, notes which are contested and by whom, and declines to award the word to any party. Where an entry instead adopts one usage as the meaning, the reference work has joined the argument it should be mapping.

The conventions of the good entry are worth stating because they amount to the three-usage separation wearing professional dress. The lexicographer's first duty is to senses: not what a word ought to mean, nor what the editor wishes it meant, but what its users have used it to do, distinguished carefully where the uses diverge. The second duty is to evidence: each sense carries its citations, so that a reader can see the sense at work in a dated text rather than taking the dictionary's word for it. The third duty is to labelling: registers and warnings are attached where a sense is technical, obsolete, or loaded, so that the entry maps the minefield instead of pretending the field is clear. An entry built on those conventions, applied to the present term, writes itself in the shape of this article: three senses, the first rare and technical, the second a scholarly shorthand, the third identified as a polemical construction with its lineage and its evidential status recorded. The form is not a compromise between the usages. It is the only accurate description of the facts, and the facts are not required to be even handed between a sense that rests on documents and a sense that rests on repetition, any more than a map is required to be even handed between a surveyed road and a rumoured one.

The strain appears where reference works are written by the crowd or captured by a camp, and the present term has exhibited both failure modes in the wild. Entries drafted from the narrative's side present the plot as the term's meaning and the school's history as its proof, converting an encyclopedia into a pamphlet with better typography. Entries drafted in reaction present the phrase as though it were nothing but the plot, erasing the scholarly question and the descriptive label, and thereby convicting every user of the phrase of retailing a conspiracy, which is false and, as the objection section argued, unjust. Both failures are failures of the same duty: the refusal to distinguish senses. A reader consulting reference works on this term should therefore apply the protocol of the previous section to the reference work itself. Does the entry have senses, dates, and sources? Does it record the scholarly question's existence? Does it report the narrative's lineage as scholarship's finding rather than as the entry's own insinuation? An entry that passes those tests can be trusted as a map. An entry that fails them is a participant, and should be read as one reads any other participant: with interest, and with the documents open.

This article does not exempt itself from the standard it has just described. It is a participant too, in the limited sense that it argues for a separation and a verdict on the narrative's truth. What it offers in place of neutrality is auditability: its documents are named, its dates are given, its attributions are attached to the scholars who published the findings, and its strongest opposing argument is stated in its own pages at a strength its proponents would accept. A reference entry aspires to be believed because of where it sits. A history of a term should aspire to be checkable because of what it cites. The reader who checks this one, and finds its citations in order, will have performed the exact operation the article exists to teach.

## A family of labels, and how vocabularies form

The descriptive usage becomes easier to place when it is seen among its relatives, the other labels scholars coined for the same territory. Intellectual fields generate vocabularies the way ports generate slang: by use, contention, and accident, with no ministry in charge. Western Marxism, the label Anderson's generation standardised, names the tradition by its geography and its distance from party discipline. The cultural turn names a movement of attention across the humanities as a whole, Marxist and otherwise. Critical theory names the Frankfurt Institute's own self description, later extended and loosened. Cultural materialism was Williams's banner for his own method. Each label has a coiner, a first use that can be hunted down, a career of adoption and resistance, and a penumbra of meanings its coiner would not have chosen. None of them has ever denoted a membership organisation, and the scholars who use them spend a cheerful amount of print arguing about their boundaries.

The phrase under examination differs from its relatives in one respect only: it escaped. The others stayed in the academy, where labels are house-trained by citation. This one was picked up by a polemical literature that needed a name for its plot, carried into general circulation by the mechanisms described earlier, and there acquired its third sense, which then overshadowed the two it was borrowed from. Escape stories of this kind are a known hazard of scholarly vocabulary. A term coined for analysis can be conscripted for combat precisely because its scholarly ring lends authority to the conscription, and the borrowing is rarely announced. The reader who meets the conscripted term later cannot tell, from the word alone, whether it is on duty in a seminar or in a pamphlet, which is why this article's first advice is always to ask for the usage rather than to judge the word.

The family's history also corrects a misunderstanding about how such vocabularies relate to the people they name. The men and women gathered under these labels did not, in the main, choose them. Gramsci did not call himself a Western Marxist; the category was built over his grave. The Birmingham scholars called their enterprise cultural studies and argued about what the name committed them to. Horkheimer's institute used critical theory as a deliberately modest flag, partly to distinguish its work from the orthodoxies of the parties. Labels in intellectual history are typically retrospective instruments, imposed by later organisers of the material, and their looseness is a known cost of the organisation they make possible. The narrative's procedure depends on its audience not knowing this. It treats a label as though it were a letterhead, evidence of an organisation that issued it, where scholarship treats the same label as a librarian's pencil mark, evidence only that someone, later, shelved the books together.

For the writer, the practical consequence is a rule of thumb with wide application. Whenever a single term is offered as the name of both a literature and an alleged organisation, ask which of the two the speaker can document. Literatures leave books, which can be listed. Organisations leave charters, memberships, minutes, and money, which can be requested. The descriptive usage of the present phrase survives the first request easily and the second not at all, there being no organisation to document. The scholarly question does not need either, being a question. The conspiracy narrative needs the second desperately and has never produced it. Three usages, three evidential fates: the separation, once again, is simply the record of which requests each usage can answer.

## The companion phrase: how the plot found its market

The narrative did not enter an empty world. It arrived at a moment when its intended audience was already asking the question the narrative was built to answer, and the question concerned another phrase. The founding article of 1992 yoked the Frankfurt School to political correctness in its very title, and that yoking, more than any argument in the text, explains the transmission that followed. A history of the present term is therefore incomplete without a glance at its companion, whose career supplied the plot with its market.

The companion phrase had a prior life of its own, smaller and stranger than its later fame. Histories of the expression trace it through twentieth century left wing usage, where it circulated largely as irony: a self mocking label for doctrinal rigidity within the movement itself, the correctness of comrades who policed one another's formulations. A phrase used by insiders to tease insiders carries no external accusation. Its transformation came at the end of the 1980s and the start of the 1990s, in the United States, when arguments about university curricula, speech codes, and the canon of required reading moved from campus newspapers to bestselling books and national magazine covers. In that migration the phrase changed hands and changed meaning, becoming a general charge against a perceived new orthodoxy in education and culture. Whatever the merits of the underlying arguments, and they were argued fiercely on the evidence of actual syllabuses and actual codes, the phrase's new career created a demand for an origin story. A public told that its universities had been captured by an orthodoxy naturally asked who had authored the orthodoxy, and when.

It was into that demand that the Frankfurt plot was launched, first by the movement magazine and then, decisively, by the policy foundation's presentations at the decade's end. The plot's commercial virtues were considerable. It named authors for an otherwise authorless drift. It converted curricular arguments, which are local and checkable, into a civilisation story, which is neither. It explained why the alleged orthodoxy felt foreign to its critics by giving it a foreign birthplace and a refugee's itinerary. And it flattered the detective in every listener, offering the pleasures of an exposed design where the scholarly account offered only the untidy record of translations, appointments, and fashions. The scholarly question about Marxism and culture, which might have supplied a real answer to the public's question, could not compete in that market: its answer was that thousands of people, across decades and continents, had argued their way toward culture for reasons of defeat and evidence, and that the universities had absorbed the argument piecemeal. True, documented, and useless as a slogan.

Two lessons for the term's history follow. The first is that usages have ecologies. The narrative flourished not because its documents were strong but because its niche was empty: a famous phrase in search of an origin. Any account of the term that treats the narrative's spread as evidence of its truth has the causation backwards; the spread measures the demand, not the documentation. The second lesson concerns responsibility in argument. The early 1990s debates about universities contained serious claims, about reading lists, hiring, and the policing of speech, which stood or fell on institutional evidence. The plot's arrival allowed those claims to be retired into mythology by their friends and dismissed as mythology by their enemies, a double loss that the three-usage separation is designed to prevent. Claims about institutions deserve institutional evidence, in that decade and every other. The phrase's history, patiently kept, returns every argument to the evidence proper to it, which is all that this article has ever claimed to do.

## The question's later life

A scholarly question is known by its descendants, and the question at the centre of this article has had a long posterity that the polemical career of the phrase has entirely obscured. Tracing that posterity for a few pages is worthwhile, because it demonstrates the difference between a usage that generates research and a usage that generates only repetition.

In the decades after Anderson's mapping, the question escaped the boundaries of Marxist historiography and became a general problem in the humanities. Historians of every allegiance began to ask whether their own discipline had executed a cultural turn, as the social history of the postwar decades, built on class and demography, gave ground to histories of language, ritual, gender, and representation. Anthropology, literary studies, and sociology conducted parallel audits of themselves. The Marxist case came to be studied as the earliest and most self conscious instance of a general movement, the moment when an entire intellectual culture, dissatisfied with explanations that stopped at production, went looking for the mechanisms by which meanings are made and enforced. Scholars who had never opened the notebooks found themselves citing Gramsci on common sense; scholars who deplored the turn's excesses found themselves conceding that the older economism had lacked a theory of consent. The question had become equipment for thinking, which is the highest honour scholarship bestows.

The wheel then turned again, in the open, in the way the scholarly usage predicts and the narrative cannot accommodate. The financial crisis of 2008 sent readers back to the critique of political economy in numbers no publisher had forecast, and a younger generation of Marxist scholars rebuilt positions in economics, value theory, and the analysis of finance that their teachers' generation had been accused of abandoning. Journals of the cultural decades began carrying articles on supply chains and rent. The tradition's centre of gravity, if the metaphor is permitted at all, proved to be less a point than a pendulum, swinging with the century's events: toward culture when consent needed explaining, toward economy when crisis made production impossible to ignore. A plot cannot swing. A literature can, and did, and the swing is itself the strongest internal evidence that what the scholarly question describes is a living tradition arguing with itself, rather than an operation proceeding to plan.

This later life matters to the history of the term for one further reason. It shows what the phrase's second usage is for. When a scholar writes that a work belongs to the world the phrase names, the claim being made is about position in a documented sequence: the work stands downstream of the turn, or against it, or athwart the pendulum's swing, and the claim can be tested by reading. The phrase functions, in such sentences, the way coordinates function on a chart. Nobody supposes the coordinates are conspiring. The polemical imagination, encountering the same sentence, supposes exactly that, and the distance between the two readings is the distance this article was written to measure.

## The separation at work: three encounters

Method is best shown in use. This section applies the three-usage separation to three specimens of the kind a reader is likely to meet, presented in generic form, and shows what the separation does to each. The specimens are constructed for instruction; the operations performed on them are the article's real content.

The first specimen is a sentence from a work of scholarship: a historian writes that the cultural Marxism of the postwar British moment gave the New Left its distinctive intellectual style. The separation assigns the sentence to the first usage without hesitation. The claim is that a body of work existed and imparted a style; its evidence is the milieu's books and journals; its author can be asked which texts, in which years, through which institutions the style travelled, and can answer. Nothing is alleged about anyone's intentions beyond the intentions visible in print. A reader may dispute the claim, may think the style owed more to adult education or to the war's aftermath than to Marxism, and the dispute will be conducted with the library open. The sentence is the phrase at its most innocent, and recognising its innocence matters, because a public trained to hear only the plot will misread such sentences as confessions.

The second specimen is a sentence from a debate about universities: a speaker argues that the humanities were remade in the later twentieth century by Marxist ideas about culture, and that the remaking explains the character of those institutions now. The separation assigns this to the second usage and treats it with the seriousness the objection section promised. The claim is about influence, and it is to be tested as influence claims are tested: by transmission. Which ideas, in which texts, entered which departments through which appointments and syllabuses, against what resistance, with what dilutions? Tested so, parts of the claim will likely stand and parts fall; the Frankfurt School's documented American career is real, and so is the independent career of French theory, of feminism, of the new social movements, none of which the speaker's sentence should be allowed to swallow. The separation's gift to this speaker is precision, and its gift to the speaker's opponent is a target smaller than a civilisation: the argument becomes winnable and losable, in portions, on evidence. Both parties leave the exchange knowing more than the plot would ever have told them.

The third specimen is a passage of the kind that circulates as a copied text, in which the phrase arrives trailing a chronology of doom: a school founded, a plan formed, a continent corrupted, a civilisation tottering, all within a few hundred words and without a single document the reader is invited to open. The separation assigns it to the third usage and prescribes the protocol. Ask which usage is meant and the passage's author, if present, will usually insist that all three are one, which is the conflation announcing itself. Ask for the document and the passage offers its own circulation as proof: it is everywhere, therefore it is true, a logic that would validate every chain letter ever copied. Ask what evidence would count against it and the answer, if any, is that concealment explains the absence, which is the plot sealing itself. At that point the reader has learned everything the passage has to teach, which concerns the passage's genre, not the Frankfurt School's history. The reader may then turn, with relief, to the correctives assembled elsewhere in this series, including the wider pattern of repair work collected in [myths about Marxism corrected](/2022/06/15/myths-about-marxism-corrected/), where the same discipline is applied to other phrases that suffer the same three way split between a label, a question, and a legend.

The specimens share a moral that the article has approached from several directions and can now state flatly. The phrase is not a thing but a junction. Arguments fail at junctions when the traffic is not sorted, and they succeed, when they succeed, because somebody sorts it. The sorting is not censorship and it is not pedantry. It is the ordinary courtesy that speakers owe one another: to say which claim is being made, so that the other party can answer the claim that was actually advanced. A culture that keeps its junctions sorted can argue about Marxism's cultural turn forever, profitably and without rancour. A culture that does not will go on mistaking labels for plots and plots for labels, and will call the confusion a debate.

## The books themselves, briefly opened

Every claim in this article about the narrative's falsity rests finally on a set of books that any reader may open, and a history of the term should at least indicate what opening them reveals. This section performs that indication for the school's best known works, not as a substitute for the series' full treatments but as a standing invitation to compare the texts with their narrative doubles. The comparison is the most quietly devastating exercise available to a reader of the polemical literature, because the doubles and the originals share nothing but names.

### What does the school's most famous book actually argue?

Its argument is that enlightenment, the West's project of freeing humanity from myth through reason, has turned into its opposite: reason narrowed to calculation administers nature and society alike, and mass entertainment completes the administration by occupying the hours work leaves free. It is a lament for reason, not a manual for subversion.

Dialectic of Enlightenment, composed by Horkheimer and Adorno in Californian exile during the war and published in Amsterdam in 1947, is the text the narrative most often casts as the plot's founding charter. Read, it is something close to the charter's opposite: a work of mourning for the rational civilisation its authors believed was destroying itself. Its chapters move from Odysseus, read as the first bourgeois, bound to the mast of self preservation, through the Marquis de Sade, through the culture industry, to a set of notes on anti Semitism written while the extermination was under way. Its mood is diagnostic and despairing. Its authors trusted neither the masses nor their rulers, neither progress nor its enemies. The reading experience it offers is not the thrill of a revealed plan but the discomfort of a civilisation's autopsy performed by two of its orphans. The full route through the book is mapped in this series in [the Dialectic of Enlightenment guide](/2015/07/01/dialectic-of-enlightenment-guide/); here it is enough to record that the narrative's founding charter, opened, reads as a requiem.

Adorno's Minima Moralia, drafted during the same exile and published in 1951, carries the discrepancy further. It is a book of aphorisms on damaged life: on dwelling, on gifts, on the etiquette of a world its author thought had made genuine experience nearly impossible. Its best known aphorism, holding in paraphrase that no right life can be lived inside the wrong one, is the utterance of a moralist without a programme, a man conserving standards he expected his century to keep violating. Marcuse's major works diverge from both, which is the record's point. Eros and Civilization in 1955 wagered that Freud's theory contained a utopian surplus, and One-Dimensional Man in 1964 argued that advanced industrial society absorbs its opposition by satisfying it, a thesis about containment that student readers promptly converted into a call their author had framed as a diagnosis. The Authoritarian Personality of 1950, the school's great empirical collaboration with American social psychology, measured the dispositions that make citizens available to demagogues, in the hope of strengthening the democracy that had sheltered its authors. A plot that requires these four books to be one book, with one intention, will always have to prevent its audience from reading any of them. Prevention of reading is, in the end, the narrative's only real mechanism, and it is the one this article exists to interrupt.

## An institute with historians

One measure of the narrative's distance from scholarship is the sheer bulk of the scholarship it must ignore. The Frankfurt School is among the most thoroughly documented intellectual formations of the twentieth century. Its archive survived its exiles. Its correspondence has been published in critical editions, letter by letter, quarrel by quarrel. Its members have been biographied, singly and together, by writers of every sympathy and none. Rolf Wiggershaus's history of the school, published in German in 1986 and in English in 1994, runs to hundreds of pages of institutional detail: budgets, appointments, love affairs, editorial disputes, the price of the institute's survival in three countries. Martin Jay's earlier history drew on interviews with the surviving members. The documentary base is so deep that specialists argue about the school at the level of individual memoranda.

This abundance has a consequence that the narrative's authors have never squarely faced. A secret programme, maintained across decades by men who preserved everything, would have to hide inside the best lit archive of any comparable group, in full view of hostile, friendly, and indifferent examiners, for half a century, without leaving a trace that any of them has reported. Historians are not asked to prove a negative in the abstract. They are asked to weigh a claim against a record, and the record here is not silence but speech: thousands of pages in which the alleged plotters discuss their aims exhaustively, in the idiom of men who assumed posterity was listening, and in which the aims they discuss are the ones this article has described: to understand why revolution failed, why anti Semitism flourished, why mass culture reconciled its consumers to their lot, and whether reason could survive its own triumphs. The burden of proof does not float in the air between the two accounts. It sits, immovably, on the account that requires the archive to be a forgery, and that account has never attempted to lift it.

There is a disciplinary point here that reaches beyond the present term. Intellectual history as a practice rests on a hierarchy of evidence in which private documents outrank public performances, dated texts outrank recollections, and the subject's own stated aims, checked against conduct, outrank the intentions imputed by enemies. The narrative inverts every rank in the hierarchy. It privileges imputation over statement, circulation over composition, and the enemy's summary over the subject's text. A reader trained by the scholarly usages will feel the inversion as a kind of seasickness. A reader untrained may not feel it at all, which is why the training, rather than any verdict, is the article's real offering.

## The pattern elsewhere: separated usages as a method

The three-usage separation belongs to a family of operations this series has performed before, and a brief look at the family will clarify what kind of intellectual tool it is. Earlier articles separated the three civil societies that share a name in Gramsci's notebooks, distinguishing the sphere of consent, the apparatus of the state, and the usage of ordinary speech, and showed that arguments about Gramsci's politics fail when the three are traded as one. Another separated the strata gathered under the name of the middle class, whose unity dissolves under the same treatment. In each case the operation was identical: a phrase carrying incompatible meanings across audiences was resolved into its usages, each usage was given its documents and its dates, and the arguments built on the conflation were retired to be rebuilt, if they could be, on a single usage at a time.

The present case is the family's most consequential member, because the stakes of conflation are highest where a scholarly question and a conspiracy narrative share a form of words. When the civil societies are conflated, the cost is a muddled seminar. When the usages of the present phrase are conflated, the costs are the ones the objection section weighed: a legitimate argument about intellectual influence is discredited by its unwanted travelling companion, and a false narrative about dead scholars is dignified by its borrowed one. The separation pays both debts at once. It returns the question to the arguers, with its evidence, and it returns the narrative to the historians, with its documents, which are few, dated, and unflattering to the narrative's pretensions.

It is worth adding, as a matter of craft, that the separation is a conservative operation in the strict sense. It adds nothing to the record and invents nothing. It declines to coin new terms, to legislate usage, or to forbid the phrase to anyone. It simply insists that the record be consulted before the rhetoric, that usages be told apart because they are apart, and that a claim be answered in the form in which it can be true or false. Readers who finish this article and continue to use the phrase in its second sense, armed with documents, are not the article's opponents. They are its success.

## What a writer should use instead

The practical question survives every history: a writer who needs to talk about these subjects tomorrow morning, in a column or a classroom, needs words that will not betray the argument. The rule the record supports is not that the phrase must never be used. It is that the phrase should never be asked to work alone. Each of its legitimate jobs has a more precise instrument available, and the instruments are worth listing in prose, with their uses.

When the subject is the body of scholarship, name the scholarship. Marxist cultural analysis, Marxist literary criticism, and cultural materialism each pick out a literature without implying an organisation, and each can be narrowed further by naming the tradition's actual formations: the New Left, the Birmingham school, Western Marxism. When the subject is the Frankfurt Institute and its members, the language already exists and is exact: critical theory, the Frankfurt School, the Institute for Social Research, followed by the member actually meant, since Adorno, Marcuse, Benjamin, and Fromm differ from one another more than any of them differs from some writers outside the school. When the subject is the historical question, ask it as a question: whether Marxist thought turned from political economy toward culture is a sentence that invites evidence, where the bare phrase invites only recognition. When the subject is influence in institutions, write the transmission: this syllabus, that appointment, this translation, that journal, with dates, so that a reader who disagrees can disagree at a specific joint. And when the subject is the conspiracy narrative itself, as it legitimately is for historians and journalists covering polemic, name it as a narrative, attribute it to its documents and transmitters, and let its structure be described rather than its plot repeated.

The common principle is that precision is not a courtesy but a load bearing structure. Vague umbrella terms allow a writer to insinuate what the writer could not assert, and they allow a reader to hear what the writer did not say. Both failures are avoided by the same discipline the historians use: prefer the name of the text to the name of the tendency, the date to the decade's atmosphere, the documented link to the suggestive juxtaposition. A writer who follows the discipline will find that the phrase under examination becomes nearly unnecessary, surviving only where the subject is the phrase itself, as in this article, or where a speaker's usage is being quoted and examined. That is the natural habitat of a term with three histories: inside quotation marks, under examination, doing no unexamined work.

## The phrase abroad: translation and drift

A term's history is not complete at its home border, and this term has travelled widely enough that its foreign careers form part of the evidence about it. The scholarly usages moved through the ordinary channels of international scholarship, in translations of the underlying books and in the migration of students and teachers, and they arrived abroad with their citations attached, which kept them corrigible. The narrative moved through the channels of polemic, in copied texts and broadcast summaries, and arrived with its attributions worn smooth, which made it more portable and less checkable with every border it crossed. Jamin's studies of the narrative's diffusion documented the process in Europe: the story reappearing in new languages, its dramatis personae shrinking to the two or three most portable names, its already thin documentation thinning further, until in some receptions the phrase functioned as a pure signal, a way of indicating that cultural change had an author, without any author's works being specified at all.

Translation compounds the drift in ways a historian must record. The phrase renders easily into the major European languages, word for word, and easy renderings smuggle false friends: a compound that sounds technical in one language sounds conspiratorial in another, depending on which usage arrived first and set the default. Scholarly communities in several countries have responded with the tools of their trade, publishing terminological notes and review essays that perform, locally, the same separation this article performs. Those responses belong to the term's history as evidence of its reception, and their pattern is instructive. Where the separation has been published and taught, the phrase's public use becomes more careful, not because polemic has been forbidden but because readers have acquired the habit of asking which usage is meant. Where the narrative arrived unaccompanied, the habit is absent and the plot is simply the meaning. Vocabulary, like water, takes the shape of the channels dug for it, and the digging is done by whoever publishes first and teaches longest.

## A note for the working journalist

Of the phrase's professional handlers, journalists face the hardest version of the problem, because they must report speech they did not choose, on deadlines that punish hesitation, for audiences who arrive with every possible usage in their heads. The standards that the term's history suggests are few and practicable, and they are gathered here in the belief that the newsroom, more than the seminar, is where the phrase does its daily work. The fuller treatment of Marxist vocabulary in the press appears in this series in [Marxism for journalists](/2022/05/15/marxism-for-journalists/).

The first standard is attribution before definition. When a public figure uses the phrase, the reportable fact is the use: who said it, where, in what argument. A report that silently adopts the phrase as the newspaper's own description of the world has taken a side in the argument the phrase carries, usually without noticing. The second standard is the clarifying question, asked of the speaker where possible and of the text where not: which of the documented usages does this use resemble, the label, the question, or the plot? A single sentence of context, recording that the phrase has a contested history and that scholarship distinguishes its senses, discharges most of the duty. The third standard is restraint with the narrative's furniture. Reporting the plot's claims in a speaker's mouth is legitimate; repeating them in the reporter's own voice, or illustrating a story with the narrative's cast of villains as though the cast were established, converts coverage into transmission. The fourth standard is the refusal of the lazy symmetry: the phrase's scholarly question and its conspiracy narrative are not two sides of one debate, to be balanced against each other, because one is a question with evidence and the other is an assertion without it, and balance between a question and an assertion is a category error wearing fairness as a costume.

None of these standards requires the journalist to share this article's verdicts. They require only the older professional virtues: attribute, define, date, and check. A press that practised them on this term would find the term's temperature falling of its own accord, since most of its heat is generated by the friction of usages grinding against each other in the dark. Light, in this case, is not a metaphor for virtue. It is a description of what documentation does to a phrase that thrives on being unexamined.

## Keeping the separation in one's own notes

The operations this article performs are not reserved for print. They are, at bottom, habits of note taking, and a reader who practises them on the next contested term will not need an article of this length again. The habit has three parts. Record the usage before the claim: when a striking phrase appears in reading, note which of its senses the writer is exercising, with the page and the date, before recording what the writer asserts. Record the document before the summary: keep the citation attached to the idea, so that a later self, or a later reader, can reopen the evidence rather than trusting the note. And record the objection before the verdict: write down the strongest form of the case against one's own reading while it is still strong, because the reading that survives that entry is worth keeping and the one that cannot is not.

Readers who keep their study notes in a structured companion will find the discipline easier to sustain than readers who scatter them, since a separation is only as durable as the filing system that holds it. The study template at [VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) is arranged for exactly this kind of work, with the term, its usages, its documents, and its open questions kept in distinct places, so that the sorting performed once is not lost and reperformed at every encounter. The point is not the tool but the table it enforces: three rows, three histories, and no argument admitted until its row is known.

## The field argues about its own name

The descriptive usage has a documentary sub history worth recording, because it shows a scholarly community doing openly everything the narrative imagines being done in secret: founding institutions, naming a field, fighting over the name, and revising the foundation story in print. British cultural studies possessed, from its institutional beginnings, an unusual self consciousness about its own origins. The Birmingham centre issued stencilled working papers, held seminars on its own definition, and published periodic accounts of what it thought it was doing. When Stuart Hall surveyed the field in 1980, he organised its past into two paradigms, a culturalism descending from Hoggart, Williams, and Thompson, centred on experience and the whole way of life, and a structuralism descending from Althusser and Lévi-Strauss, centred on the systems that produce experience behind its back. The essay is a map drawn by a participant, and it was immediately argued with by other participants, who disputed the border, the cast, and the cartographer's own position on the terrain.

The naming quarrels continued for decades, in the open literature of the field. Was the enterprise Marxist, post Marxist, or a general method that Marxism had merely sponsored at birth? Did its centre of gravity lie in the analysis of texts, in ethnographies of audiences, in the political economy of media industries, or in the theory of ideology? Successive anthologies answered differently, and the answers are preserved in their introductions, which double as the field's autobiography. It is within this self documenting tradition that the descriptive phrase does its modest work. A historian surveying the quarrels needs collective nouns for the combatants and the phases, and the phrase is one of the nouns in the box, alongside culturalism, cultural materialism, and the rest. Its use in such writing asserts the existence of a Marxist strain inside the field's parentage, a claim its rivals could and did contest with counter genealogies emphasising adult education, Leavisite criticism, or the sociology of deviance. Nobody in these exchanges alleged that the strain was a plot, because the exchanges were conducted by people who shared libraries, conferences, and in some cases offices with the strain's representatives.

The episode carries a lesson about labels that generalises beyond this field. A scholarly label's meaning is fixed by the arguments in which it is used, and those arguments are on the record, dated and signed. Any reader with a library card can reconstruct what the label meant to its users by reading the exchanges in which they fought over it. The descriptive usage of the present phrase is thin because the exchanges in which it figures are few; the label was a bit player in its own field's naming quarrels, never the trophy. That thinness is precisely what made the phrase available for conscription. A term with a dense public scholarly meaning, fought over in famous books, resists capture, because too many readers know what its owners used it for. A term with a sparse record can be carried off and given a new meaning wholesale, and its original owners, being few and mostly retired from the quarrel, may not notice the theft for years. The phrase's scholarly marginality, recorded honestly at the outset of this article, is thus not a caveat to its history. It is one of the history's causes.

## The American decade, in documents

The narrative's chosen stage is the school's American exile, and the exile is therefore worth one more inspection at ground level, because its documented texture is the plot's most complete refutation. What the record shows is not a directorate executing a design but a small, anxious, frequently insolvent research institute negotiating its survival among universities, foundations, and government agencies, and quarrelling with itself throughout.

The institute arrived in New York in 1934 with an affiliation to Columbia University, a building on West 117th Street, and an endowment already damaged by the Depression and destined to shrink further as the costs of exile mounted. Horkheimer's correspondence of those years, published and available, is preoccupied with money: with stipends that might have to be cut, with members who needed placing in American posts, with the search for project funding. Benjamin in Paris depended on a monthly stipend whose continuance was debated in letters that survive. The institute's American projects were bids for relevance and revenue as much as scholarship: a study of authority, proposals on prejudice, the collaboration with Paul Lazarsfeld's radio research bureau at Princeton, where Adorno's discomfort with audience measurement became part of the folklore of American sociology precisely because it was so unconcealed. Fromm's departure, the arguments over the journal's contents, the internal assessments of one another's manuscripts, all of it was written down, much of it at the time, by men who assumed that institutes keep archives.

The war years deepened the paper trail in the direction least compatible with the plot. The Studies in Prejudice series, sponsored by the American Jewish Committee and published after the war, put the institute's remnant to work measuring American anti Semitism and authoritarian disposition, with The Authoritarian Personality appearing in 1950 under four names, Adorno's among them, from a commercial publisher. Whatever criticisms later social science levelled at its methods, and they were extensive, the work's existence and sponsorship are matters of title pages. Meanwhile Neumann was anatomising the Nazi state for the OSS, Marcuse was analysing German morale and then Soviet society for American agencies, and Horkheimer and Adorno were in Los Angeles drafting the bleak manuscript that would become their wartime book, in circumstances of financial stringency that their letters record month by month. When the war ended, the institute's centre of gravity moved home: Horkheimer and Adorno returned to Frankfurt, rebuilt the institute with German public support, and accepted the honours of the republic whose predecessor had expelled them. Marcuse remained in American government service and then in American universities. The exile's personnel dispersed into the ordinary institutions of two countries, publishing, teaching, and ageing in public.

Against this record the narrative's demands can be stated precisely, perhaps for the first time in the reader's experience of it. The narrative requires that a plan of cultural subversion be located somewhere in this archive: in a memorandum, a letter, a set of instructions, a minuted decision, of the kind that every other documented programme of the century possesses in embarrassing abundance. The institute's genuine programmes, its inaugural lectures, funding applications, and project designs, discuss studying the society it lived in, not corrupting it, and they discuss even that ambition in the language of men who expected to be misunderstood. The narrative's inability to produce a single page of the required kind, from an archive mined for decades by scholars including scholars hostile to the school, is not a gap in the evidence. It is the evidence, of the only kind history ever finally offers: the dog that did not bark, in a house full of paper, where every other dog is barking at once.

## The narrative in the seminar room

There is a final destination in the term's history that its transmitters did not intend: the narrative has become an object of study in its own right, shelved in the growing academic literature on conspiracy rhetoric, where it is dissected as a specimen. The development is worth recording, because it completes the documentary circle. A construction assembled in a movement magazine, in defiance of the scholarly record, is now preserved in that record as evidence about the construction's makers and audiences, in the way that a pathogen is preserved in a laboratory: not honoured, but studied, and rendered less dangerous by the study.

The analysts of the genre place the narrative in a recognised family. Its form, in which a named minority of intellectuals is credited with a civilisation's decline through the secret direction of its culture, recurs across the modern history of polemic with different casts and identical staging, and the scholarship on that family, from historians of earlier anti Semitic and anti Masonic panics onward, supplies the comparative frame in which the structural finding of this article sits. Jamin's work traced the narrative's migration across languages and media, showing how a text loses attribution as it gains reach. Jay's essay approached the case from the archive outward, measuring the portrait against the institute's record and finding the portrait's features assembled from the record's negations: a unified school invented from a quarrelsome one, a programme invented from its absence, an optimism about cultural power invented from a body of work famous for denying that culture could be steered at all. Other scholars have used the case in teaching, because it compresses the genre's whole anatomy into a single phrase: the borrowed scholarly label, the conscripted concept, the elderly plot in new dress, the audience of ordinary users who inherit the phrase without the plot and are then misdescribed by everyone.

For the historian of the term, the seminar room's verdict settles the third usage's classification, and classification was the task. The narrative is a document of the polemical culture of the 1990s and after, descended from the cultural polemics of the 1930s, and its value to scholarship is the value of such documents generally: it reveals the anxieties and the argumentative economy of the worlds that produced and consumed it. It reveals nothing about the Frankfurt School except what a funhouse mirror reveals about a face, namely the mirror's own curvature. Readers who want the face have the archive, and the archive, unlike the mirror, can be visited.

## Four errors the separation prevents

The separation can also be taught backwards, from the wreckage of the arguments it would have prevented. Four failures recur wherever the phrase is used unsorted, and each maps to one of the junctions this article has marked.

The first error is the borrowed refutation. A speaker raises the scholarly question, about the tradition's turn toward culture, and is answered with the narrative's refutation, as though the speaker had alleged a plot. The answer is accurate about the narrative and useless about the question, and the speaker leaves believing, with some justice, that evidence is not what the other side trades in. The second error is the borrowed pedigree, the mirror image: a speaker retails the narrative and, when challenged, retreats to the scholarly question's respectability, claiming to have meant only that Marxism influenced the universities. The retreat is possible only because the phrase covers both claims, and it leaves the narrative's furniture, the plot, the cast, the intention, standing in the room unexamined, ready for the next use. The third error is the symmetrical insult, in which every user of the phrase is treated as the narrative's author. This error converts a vocabulary problem into a census of suspects, and it is refuted by the simplest observation in this article: most users inherited the phrase from the conversation around them and could not name its documents if asked, a condition they share with the users of most political vocabulary most of the time. The fourth error is the conspiratorial compliment, paid by the narrative's transmitters to its targets: the representation of a few dozen exiled scholars as the secret authors of a civilisation's transformation flatters the dead with an omnipotence their own writings explicitly disclaimed, and flatters the transmitters with an enemy worth their alarm. Scholarship that declines the compliment on the dead men's behalf is not defending them. It is declining a genre.

Each error has the same shape, and the shape is the article in miniature: two usages traded as one, in the middle of an argument, at the moment when the trade is profitable. Sorting the usages does not make the arguments easy. It makes them honest, and it restores to each party the burden that party actually undertook: the scholar's burden of documents, the debater's burden of transmission, the narrator's burden of the archive, from which there is no exemption and behind which there is no hiding place.

## The baseline: what the classical tradition actually said

The scholarly question presupposes a before, a classical Marxism centred on political economy, against which the later turn can be measured. The presupposition is broadly sound, but its details matter, because the baseline was never the crude economism that both the turn's celebrants and the narrative's authors find convenient. Recovering the baseline accurately is therefore part of this article's work: a turn measured from a caricature will be exaggerated by exactly the caricature's error.

The canonical statement of the classical position is the preface of 1859, where Marx summarised the theory he had arrived at: the mode of production conditions the social, political, and intellectual life process in general, and social being determines consciousness. Two generations of followers read the preface as a licence to treat culture as a shadow cast by the economy, interesting only as evidence of the substance throwing it. But the same tradition contained, from the beginning, its own correctives. Engels, in letters of the 1890s that became canonical in their turn, insisted that the economic was decisive only in the last instance, that the elements of the superstructure exercised their own effects and reacted upon the base, and that he and Marx had overstated the economic side against their adversaries and left the formal side of ideology underworked. The letters were published, debated, and absorbed into the tradition's self understanding long before the Frankfurt Institute existed. Georgi Plekhanov, the founder of Russian Marxism, built a five storey model of social life in which art and literature occupied the topmost floor with a logic of their own, and wrote sociological studies of French drama and painting. Karl Kautsky, the pope of the Second International, wrote on everything from Thomas More to the materialist conception's literary implications. The classical tradition's centre was indeed the economy. Its margins, where culture lived, were inhabited, theorised, and contested from the start.

The consequence for the scholarly question is a refinement rather than a rebuttal. What changed in the twentieth century was not the discovery of culture but its promotion: from the topmost floor to the engine room, from a dependent variable to a principal mechanism by which orders reproduce themselves. Gramsci's hegemony, the institute's interdisciplinary programme, and the British school's whole way of life all assert, in different idioms, that consent and meaning are not decorations on power but constituents of it. That promotion is the turn, stated precisely, and it is a thesis about causal weight that evidence can engage: one can ask, of any historical case, whether the cultural mechanisms did the work attributed to them. The narrative, by contrast, requires the baseline to be a conspiracy free zone and the turn to be a coup executed inside the tradition, and it therefore needs the classical tradition to be simpler than it was. Accuracy about the baseline is thus not pedantry. It is the same operation as the three-usage separation, performed one level down: refuse the caricature, restore the record, and let the argument proceed on documents.

## Hegemony, correctly stated

Because the narrative's engine is a borrowed concept, the concept deserves a compact statement in its owner's terms, so that the borrowing can be measured. Hegemony, as Gramsci developed it across the prison notebooks, names the way a social group's leadership is exercised through the institutions of civil society: the schools, churches, newspapers, associations, and professions through which a population learns what is normal, admirable, and possible. A group is hegemonic, in this vocabulary, when its worldview becomes the common sense of the society, so that its rule is sustained less by coercion than by the consent of the governed, who experience the order as the natural grain of the world. The concept's companions in the notebooks are its correctives. Consent must continually be organised and renewed; it can crack, in what Gramsci called organic crises, when the ruling ideas lose their grip and the old order's authority drains away faster than a new one can form. And hegemony is always contested terrain, since subordinate groups produce their own intellectuals, their own common sense in embryo, and their own bids for leadership.

Three properties of the concept, visible in the notebooks themselves, disqualify it from the use the narrative makes of it. It is a concept about open processes: consent organised in daylight, through institutions whose operations are public and whose personnel are known. It is a concept without a headquarters: hegemony in Gramsci belongs to classes and their alliances, not to committees, and it is achieved, where it is achieved, as the unintended sediment of a thousand public activities as much as by anyone's design. And it is a concept that cuts against its borrower: a situation genuinely describable in Gramsci's terms would be one in which a worldview had won consent through open contest, which is the opposite of a situation in which a hidden directorate had arranged the outcome. When the narrative installs hegemony as the name of its plot's secret mechanism, it is not applying the concept. It is confiscating the word and reversing its polarity, a procedure that the notebooks' critical edition makes easy to detect and impossible to defend.

The concept's genuine career belongs to the scholarly question, and it has been sketched already: the journey from a prison cell to the Birmingham seminar room, the adaptations, the quarrels about fidelity, the slow absorption into the common equipment of historians and sociologists who may hold no Marxist commitment at all. That career is what influence looks like when it is real. It also explains, incidentally, why the narrative needed the concept so badly. A plot about culture requires a mechanism connecting books to societies, and hegemony is the most distinguished mechanism the tradition produced. The theft is a backhanded tribute, and it is recorded here as such: the narrative could not do without the scholarly tradition it traduces, and every use it makes of the stolen concept reopens the question its theft was meant to close, namely what the concept's author actually wrote.

## The one real intersection: Marcuse and the students

Every durable legend has a photograph it can point to, and the narrative's photograph is real: in the later 1960s, Herbert Marcuse, by then a professor at the University of California in San Diego, was celebrated by student movements on several continents, his portrait carried at demonstrations, his books assigned the status of manuals. The intersection of the Frankfurt School with a living movement happened exactly once, in full view, and its documented character is the legend's undoing, because what the documents show is reception, not command.

The facts are these. Marcuse's One-Dimensional Man, published in 1964, argued that advanced industrial society had learned to absorb opposition by administering satisfactions, and that the system's very tolerance functioned as a technique of rule. Student readers in the United States and Europe took the book as a description of their own experience of affluent unfreedom and made its author, to his evident surprise, a celebrity of the movement press. He lectured to packed halls in Berlin in 1967. He corresponded with student leaders and answered their questions in interviews. He also did what no director of a movement does: he criticised his own reception, in print, repeatedly. He warned against the movement's anti intellectual temptations, distanced himself from its violent fringes, quarrelled with its orthodoxies, and insisted that his concepts be read in their difficulty rather than chanted in their abbreviation. His relations with the other exiles during those years complete the picture of a school that did not exist as a command. Adorno, besieged by his own students in Frankfurt, regarded the American apotheosis of his old colleague with dismay, and the correspondence between the two men in 1969, as movements occupied both their institutes' worlds, records two elderly philosophers disagreeing about what the young were doing and what, if anything, their books had licensed.

The intersection's lesson for the term's history is precise. When a Frankfurt thinker's ideas genuinely entered a movement, the entry was public, dated, reviewable, and contested by the thinker himself, and it left a paper trail of lectures, interviews, letters, and rebuttals dense enough to fill editions. Influence of the kind the narrative alleges, by contrast, is claimed for decades and institutions where no comparable trail exists, where the alleged agents were dead, dispersed, or on record against the uses made of them. The contrast is the method in its purest form. Demand of every influence claim the standard of documentation that the one documented case supplies, and the narrative's claims fail not on ideology but on paperwork, which is where historical claims are supposed to fail when they are false.

## The pessimism dossier

The record assembled against the narrative can be closed with a dossier that no advocate of the plot has ever explained away: the school's later writings, in which its surviving leaders surveyed the civilisation they were allegedly engineering and recorded, with increasing bleakness, their expectation that nothing they valued would survive it. Pessimism is not a proof of innocence in itself; conspirators can despair. But the specific pessimism of these texts is about the impossibility of the very project attributed to their authors, and it is stated in the idiom of men auditing their own defeat.

Horkheimer's trajectory is the dossier's first exhibit. The director who had announced an interdisciplinary science of society in 1930 spent his last decades arguing that the administered world had absorbed the critical impulse itself, that the longing for justice survived chiefly as a theological residue, and that the thinker's remaining task was conservation of a heritage the age did not want. His late interviews return obsessively to the fear that the tradition he served would be remembered, if at all, as a period style. Adorno's late essay on resignation, delivered as a radio address in 1969, defended the thinker who refuses to convert thought into action's servant, and defined his own practice as a holding action on behalf of a possibility he did not expect to witness. The wartime book's bleakest figure, the message entrusted to the sea in a bottle, was not retired in their old age. It was confirmed.

Set the dossier beside the narrative's requirements and the mismatch becomes arithmetical. The plot needs its agents to have worked, successfully and in concert, across the middle of the century, bending education, entertainment, and manners to a design. The agents' own archive records men who believed they had failed to bend even their own institutes, their own students, their own reception, and who said so in print while the alleged bending was supposedly at its height. A reader is not obliged to take historical actors at their own valuation in general; actors lie, posture, and misjudge. But a narrative that discards the actors' valuation must replace it with evidence of the contrary reality, and on this point, after decades, the replacement has not been attempted, because the documents that would constitute it do not exist. The dossier is therefore allowed the last word in this section, in paraphrase of its governing thought: the school expected to be forgotten, and arranged its papers for a posterity it doubted would come. That posterity came, in the form of editions, biographies, and this article's sources. The plot arrived later, by another road, and it is the plot, not the school, that the papers were never meant for.

## The word culture itself

Part of the scholarly question's difficulty, and part of the phrase's slipperiness in every usage, is lodged in its middle word. Raymond Williams, whose Keywords of 1976 remains the standard tour of the contested vocabulary, called culture one of the two or three most complicated words in the English language, and his entry traces its travel from the tending of crops, through the cultivation of minds, to the name for a whole way of life and for the arts that way of life produces. A phrase built on so mobile a foundation inherits the mobility. When a speaker says that Marxism turned toward culture, the sentence can mean that it turned toward the arts, toward the anthropology of everyday life, toward the media of mass communication, or toward the inner formation of persons, and the four meanings support four different arguments with four different evidence bases.

The scholarly usage survives this mobility because scholarship's conventions police it: writers specify which culture they mean, in which texts, in which decades, and reviewers punish the ones who slide. The Frankfurt materials illustrate the policing at work. The institute's members used the word in its several senses with deliberate distinctions among them, reserving their sharpest coinage, the culture industry, for the industrial production of entertainment, and treating high culture, folk culture, and mass culture as separate problems with separate histories. The narrative, by contrast, depends on the slide. Its culture is a single substance, the spirit of a civilisation, which can be poisoned at its source and whose every symptom, from a syllabus to a song, testifies to the same poisoning. A substance that unitary exists only in the plot's requirements. In the record, cultures are plural, layered, and stubbornly resistant to single authorship, which is why the tradition that studied them needed so many books and reached so few conclusions it could share.

The moral returns the article to its beginning. Phrases fail in proportion to the vagueness they are allowed to carry, and they are repaired in proportion to the distinctions someone is willing to draw and defend. The three-usage separation is one such repair, performed on a phrase whose vagueness had become a public nuisance. Williams performed the same repair on the word culture itself, by history rather than by verdict, and his example is the standing model for this article's method: when a word causes arguments it should not cause, write its biography, date its senses, and give the arguers back their distinctions. The arguments that remain after the repair are the real ones, and they are worth having at full length, on the evidence, in the open.

## What changed in the universities, honestly assessed

Since the legitimate form of the public argument concerns the universities, the article owes that argument a fair summary of the documented terrain, neither inflated nor minimised. The humanities in the later twentieth century did change, profoundly, and Marxist ideas were among the agents of change. They were not the only agents, and in most places they were not the largest, and precision about the proportions is what separates the scholarly usage from both the plot and the complacent dismissal.

The documented changes can be grouped by their vehicles. Curricula widened: literatures beyond the European canon, histories of women, of enslaved and colonised peoples, of labour and everyday life, entered syllabuses that had previously confined themselves to statesmen and masterpieces. Method changed: the close reading of texts was joined, and sometimes displaced, by approaches that asked who produced a text, for which audience, under what institutional constraints, questions the Marxist tradition had done much to make unavoidable. Hiring changed: new fields created new posts, and the posts were filled by scholars trained in the decades when Marxist frameworks carried prestige in the relevant disciplines. Each of these changes is real, each is documented in the catalogues and journals of the period, and each had multiple parentage. Feminism rebuilt whole fields from its own texts and organisations, often against Marxist resistance. Postcolonial studies descended from anticolonial thought and from literary scholars of the formerly colonised world, and quarrelled with orthodox Marxism as readily as it borrowed from it. The Frankfurt School's own footprint, traced in the transmission sections above, was genuine but specific: strongest in literary and cultural theory, in the sociology of culture, and in the self understanding of media studies, weakest or absent in the many departments where economics, philology, or analytic philosophy set the tone.

An honest assessment therefore cuts against two satisfactions at once. Against the plot, it shows a diffusion with many sources and no director. Against the dismissal, it shows that the influence was not imaginary: a student could pass through a humanities degree in those decades and absorb, from respected teachers, a picture of culture as a field of power and consent whose lineaments were substantially Gramscian and Frankfurtian, whether or not the names were spoken. That absorption is a legitimate subject of public argument. Citizens may celebrate it as an emancipation of the curriculum or deplore it as a narrowing of the mind, and both judgements are political in the honourable sense, to be fought out with the evidence of syllabuses and outcomes. What neither judgement needs, and what both are weakened by, is the fiction that the absorption was a plot executed by the dead. The fiction insults the living teachers who chose their materials, the students who argued back, and the institutions whose records are open. Argument conducted on the documented terrain is harder than the plot and better than the dismissal, and it is the only kind this article has ever recommended.

## A footnote that matters: the money

Conspiracies run on money, and the institute's money is documented well enough to serve as a final control on the narrative. The Institute for Social Research existed because Felix Weil, the son of a grain merchant whose fortune had been made in the Argentine trade, chose to endow the study of Marxism as a young man's act of conviction, and persuaded his father to fund it. The endowment was a known sum, held and invested under the institute's own arrangements, and its history is a history of attrition: inflation, the Depression, the costs of flight and resettlement, the shrinking stipends and anxious economies that fill the correspondence of the exile years. The institute's directors spent a measurable share of their lives as fundraisers and budget cutters. Its American projects were shaped by the patrons who would pay for them, a foundation here, a committee there, each with its own purposes that the institute had to accommodate to survive.

The footnote matters because it reverses the narrative's physics. The plot imagines an organisation with the resources and the discipline to bend a civilisation's culture across half a century. The record shows an organisation that could not always bend its own budget across a single year, dependent on a dwindling endowment, on a university's hospitality, on the wartime state's salaries, and on the goodwill of the very liberal institutions the plot casts as its victims. Dependence of that order is not a refutation by itself; history contains impecunious plotters. But it fixes the scale of the claims that the evidence must then support, and it joins the quarrels, the pessimism, the government service, and the conservative tastes in a single coherent portrait: a small, brilliant, divided, frequently frightened research institute, of a kind the twentieth century produced in some numbers, whose afterlife in legend says more about the legend's market than about the institute's life. A reader who has absorbed the footnote will find the legend's proportions impossible to unsee thereafter, which is the most that any single fact can do.

## The slogan examined: a long march that began in a lecture hall

One phrase does more work in the narrative's economy than any other, and it repays the article's method in miniature. The long march through the institutions is quoted, in the polemical literature, as though it were the Frankfurt School's own name for its project, the confession around which the plot assembles. Its documentary history is short, dated, and fatal to that use. The phrase was coined by Rudi Dutschke, the best known leader of the West German student movement, in the later 1960s, adapting the memory of the Chinese communists' Long March to the strategy he urged on his own generation: to work within the institutions of the Federal Republic, its universities, media, and professions, and transform them from inside over a generation. Dutschke was a student politician describing his own movement's intentions, in his own name, in his own time. He was not an exile of the 1930s, not a member of the institute, and not the executor of anyone's instructions. He had read Marcuse, among many others, and said so.

Everything the article has taught can be read off this small case. A genuine document exists: a phrase with an author, a date, and a meaning about a specific movement's strategy. The phrase is then detached from its author and reassigned to men who never used it, some of whom were dead before it was coined, because the plot requires a strategy and the slogan supplies one with a pedigree. The reassignment is never announced; the slogan simply begins appearing, in the polemical literature, as the school's own. Readers who meet it there absorb a false memory of the school's archive. Readers who check the phrase, as this article has checked its larger subject, find a student leader of 1967 and a metaphor about perseverance, separated from the exiles by a generation, a language of activism the exiles mostly distrusted, and in Adorno's case by open conflict with the very students who marched under it.

The slogan's career also clarifies the one real relation it gestures at. The student generation did read the exiles, selectively and often against the exiles' own protests, and the reading changed the students, as the Marcuse section recorded. Influence ran from the books to the movement. The slogan's reassignment reverses the current, making the movement's strategy into the books' intention, and then reads the reversed current as proof of direction. It is the narrative's master error in its smallest compass: reception mistaken for command. A reader who can catch it here, in a case small enough to hold in one hand, has acquired the reflex the whole article exists to build, and will catch it again wherever a phrase arrives carrying a plot in its luggage.

## The name the school gave itself

A last documentary control is available, and it is the simplest in the article. Formations that exist leave names for themselves, in their own hand, on their own documents. The institute's members, when they needed a collective description, had one: critical theory, the phrase Horkheimer set out in his 1937 essay distinguishing traditional from critical theory, where it named a method, an account of how thought is implicated in the society it examines, rather than a membership or a mission. The phrase under examination in this article appears nowhere as the school's self description, in no programme, letterhead, lecture, or memoir of its members. They did not call themselves by it, did not organise under it, and in the main died before its modern sense existed. The history of ideas notices such absences, because they are the negative space in which a term's real biography becomes visible: a label applied from outside, decades later, by writers with a plot to furnish.

The contrast between the two names is the whole subject in epitome. Critical theory is a self description with a dated founding text, a stated meaning, and a career that scholars can follow through the school's journals and its students. The examined phrase is a retrospective construction with a dated founding text of its own, in a movement magazine of 1992, and the two texts, set side by side, teach the difference between a tradition naming itself and a polemic naming its target. Everything else in this article is commentary on that difference. The reader who remembers nothing else should remember the direction of the naming: schools name themselves in the open and argue about the name forever; plots are named by their enemies, in stories, after the fact. Between those two directions lies the entire distance between the history of ideas and the literature of suspicion, and a term is known, finally, by the direction from which its name arrived.

## Company the turn kept

The scholarly question gains its final calibration from a comparison the narrative never makes. The turn toward culture was not a Marxist peculiarity. Across the same decades, disciplines with no Marxist allegiance executed turns of their own, for their own reasons, and the parallels discipline any account that would make the Marxist case a plot or a pathology. Philosophy underwent its linguistic turn, relocating its problems from the world to the language in which the world is described. Anthropology, in the interpretive work associated with Clifford Geertz, whose essays of 1973 remain its charter, redefined culture itself as a web of significance to be read rather than a mechanism to be modelled. History absorbed the linguistic and cultural turns in its own sequence. Sociology fought its own wars between structure and meaning. The twentieth century's intellectual centre of gravity shifted toward signs, texts, and interpretations almost everywhere at once, under pressures that included two world wars, the crisis of positivism, the expansion of mass media, and the universities' own growth.

The parallels matter because they supply the control case the argument needs. If a turn toward culture occurred in fields the Frankfurt School never touched, under scholars who never read it, then the turn as such cannot be the school's invention or any tradition's conspiracy; it was a general movement of the century's thought, in which Marxism participated with its own accent. The scholarly question survives the comparison in refined form: not why Marxism alone turned, but what was distinctive in its version of the general turn, its insistence that meanings are made under material constraint and that consent is a political fact. That refined question is the one the comparison article in this series pursues, and it is a better question than the one the polemical phrase can even formulate, since the phrase knows only one culture, one turn, and one address to blame for both.

## What the separation is not

Four misreadings of this article's own procedure are common enough to be worth refusing in advance. The separation is not a ban on the phrase. Writers remain free to use it in any of its senses; the article asks only that the sense be identifiable and the evidence appropriate to it. The separation is not a defence of the Frankfurt School's ideas. Those ideas stand or fall in the articles devoted to them, and a reader may finish this history persuaded that critical theory was a calamity for the humanities, provided the persuasion rests on the school's books and their documented reception rather than on a plot the books refute. The separation is not a verdict on the scholarly question. Whether the tradition's turn toward culture was wisdom, retreat, or mirage remains open, and this article has deliberately equipped both sides. And the separation is not a claim that words are innocent or that vigilance about them is a substitute for argument. It is a claim about order: first establish what is being said, then argue about whether it is true. Most public quarrels about this phrase invert the order and pay for the inversion with their substance. The article's whole ambition is to restore the sequence, for this term, and to leave the reader with a habit that will serve for the next one.

## Keeping three histories apart

The history is now assembled, and it can be carried away in a paragraph. A phrase exists that scholars occasionally use as a loose label for Marxist cultural analysis, a body of published work with a century of argument in it. The same phrase abbreviates a real and unresolved scholarly question, whether that tradition turned from political economy toward culture, and at what gain and cost, a question argued from texts by participants who include the tradition's own historians. And the same phrase names a conspiracy narrative assembled in a fringe magazine in 1992 out of the materials of an older German polemic, carried to its audience by a policy foundation at the decade's end, and contradicted at every load bearing point by the archive of the school it accuses. Three usages, three histories, three evidential fates. The reader who keeps them apart has not been given an opinion about any of the underlying subjects. The reader has been given the prior thing, without which opinions are only noises: a map of what the words in the argument mean.

What the map makes possible, it is worth saying once more, is argument itself. The scholarly question can now be debated without the plot's shadow falling across it, and the citizens who care about the influence of ideas in universities can make their case with the receipts the case deserves. The narrative can now be studied as what it is, a document in the history of polemic, instructive about its transmitters and its times. And the dead can be left to their books, which are difficult, divided, pessimistic, and interesting, everything their legend is not. Terms will go on escaping the academy and going feral in the wild; new phrases will gather incompatible meanings and demand the same patient sorting. The sorting is the skill. This article has practised it on one term, at length, so that the practice, rather than the verdict, is what remains when the details fade.

## For students and exam candidates

Examinations reward the candidate who can sort a contested term before discussing it, and this term is a gift to the prepared. Open with the separation itself: three usages, a descriptive label, a scholarly question, and a conspiracy narrative, each with its dates. Attach the label to its scholarship, the question to its evidence, naming Anderson's mapping of Western Marxism and the debates it codified, and the narrative to its documents, the fringe magazine article of 1992 and the foundation that popularised it. Then answer the question actually set, in the usage the question intends, and state explicitly which usage that is. Candidates lose marks by treating the narrative's claims as the school's positions, and by treating every use of the phrase as the narrative. Candidates gain them by showing that the Frankfurt School's members disagreed, that their cultural criticism attacked the mass culture they are accused of engineering, and that influence, where alleged, must be traced through texts and appointments. Above all, define before you judge: the definition is the answer's foundation, and here it is also most of its content.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

This term makes an unusually efficient teaching instrument, because a single seminar can hold its whole method. Set the three usages side by side on the board, with the table from this article as the handout, and give the students one task: assign specimens to rows and defend the assignment. A page of a scholarly history, a debating claim about universities, a copied polemical text: the room will disagree, productively, and the disagreement will teach the difference between a label, a question, and a plot more durably than any lecture on method. Two disciplines are worth enforcing throughout. Require documents for every assignment, so that a claim about the school must survive contact with the school's books, and require the strongest form of every position, so that the objection about foreclosed argument is heard at its best before it is answered. Students who leave able to ask which usage is meant, and what would count as evidence, have learned the transferable skill. The term itself will fade from their syllabuses; the habit of asking will not.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this term is documentary, and it remains open in both directions. The narrative's transmission history, established in outline by Jay and Jamin, invites completion: fuller bibliographies of its editions and translations, reception studies in the countries it reached, and archival work on the organisations that carried it, whose papers will settle questions that inference cannot. The scholarly question likewise continues to generate work, as new editions of the school's correspondence and the notebooks refine what the tradition's turn consisted in. Researchers approaching either frontier should observe the protocols this article has modelled. Date every usage from a located document. Attribute every finding to the scholar who published it. Distinguish the term's history from the school's at every step, and resist the temptation, fatal in both camps, to let the phrase's polemical voltage set the research agenda. Journalists joining the subject late will find the primary literature more accessible than its reputation suggests, and the secondary literature, from Wiggershaus onward, unusually willing to show its working. The archive is open. The invitation stands.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What does cultural Marxism mean?

It depends on which of three usages a speaker is exercising, and a responsible answer begins by asking. In one, found rarely in scholarship, the words are a label for a shelf of books: Marxist writing about literature, schooling, broadcasting, and ordinary life. In another, they compress a scholarly dispute about the tradition's own development, the argument over whether Marxist thought moved its weight from the critique of capital toward questions of ideology and inward experience, and whether that movement was gain or loss. In the third and most publicly familiar, they name a story in which exiled German scholars are alleged to have conspired to corrupt the West through its culture. The shelf can be inspected, the dispute can be argued from dated texts, and the story collapses when its documents are requested. Sorting a given use into its proper row, before replying to it, is the skill this history exists to teach.

### Q: Where does the term cultural Marxism come from?

There is no single birthplace, because there is no single term, only a shared spelling. The label was coined in the ordinary way of academic vocabulary, inside research on cultural studies and the postwar New Left, surfacing in print as a heading for Marxist writing about culture and reaching its most visible form on the cover of a university press history issued in 1997. The scholarly shorthand grew out of the tradition's internal historiography, as writers charted the movement of Marxist attention across the century. The conspiratorial sense, by contrast, can be dated almost to the month: it took shape in an article carried by a movement magazine in the winter of 1992, drew its architecture from an interwar German insult aimed at modern art and Jewish artists, and reached its wide audience through a Washington policy foundation's lectures and recordings in the decade's closing years. Origins in this subject are plural, and any answer offering one source for all three usages has already made the article's central mistake.

### Q: Is cultural Marxism an academic term or a political one?

The honest reply is that it leads a double life, with the two careers feeding on each other. Inside scholarship the expression is peripheral: the field's working vocabulary runs to other names, among them Marxist literary criticism, cultural materialism, Western Marxism, and critical theory, and the phrase heads no department, journal, or research programme. In political argument it has enjoyed the opposite fate, becoming common currency, with the conspiratorial sense dominant in most public settings. Polemic gained from the arrangement, since a term with a scholarly title page to its credit sounds like learning; scholarship lost, since its minor label was carried off and given a meaning its users never intended. Neither career can be inferred from the other. The reader's task in any encounter is to determine, from the setting, the citations offered, and the treatment of contrary evidence, which of the two lives the phrase is living on that occasion.

### Q: What was cultural Bolshevism?

It was the Weimar Republic's most poisonous piece of cultural vocabulary, later adopted as an instrument of Nazi cultural policy. The German compound Kulturbolschewismus performed a single manoeuvre: it translated dislike of modern art into a charge of political conspiracy. The Bauhaus's buildings, atonal composition, abstract canvases, and expressionist stages were presented not as works inviting judgement but as manoeuvres in a Bolshevik campaign against the nation, executed by insiders who were said to be foreigners in spirit. Jewish artists, conductors, critics, and museum officials were cast as the campaign's local agents, their ancestry offered as the explanation that their art could not supply. Once the regime held power the insult became administration: institutions closed, collections purged, livelihoods ended, and the campaign's logic displayed to the public in the Munich exhibition of 1937, where confiscated modern works were hung as specimens of degeneracy. Scholars of the period therefore file the term under the machinery of persecution, and later analysts have traced the modern Frankfurt plot's skeleton to its design.

### Q: Did the Frankfurt School have a plan to subvert Western culture?

Nothing of the kind has ever been documented, and the archive, which is unusually complete, points the other way at every junction. The institute published its intentions in its own journal: a cooperative research programme joining economics, sociology, psychology, and philosophy to explain why the predicted revolution had failed and why authority commanded consent. Its internal life was a record of dissension conducted in letters that survive: a psychoanalyst who quarrelled his way out by 1939, a brilliant associate in Paris who fought the directors over every draft, wartime co authors who diverged after the war into incompatible positions, an elder statesman of the group who sided with students against his own closest colleague in 1969. Its exiles served the American state that sheltered them, anatomising the Nazi regime for its intelligence service. Its cultural doctrine exalted austere modernism and despised the commercial amusement it supposedly plotted to install. A coordinated design would have required orders, minutes, and money trails. The papers show budgets, quarrels, and job applications instead.

### Q: Why do scholars describe the conspiracy version as antisemitic?

Because of its blueprint, which specialists in conspiracy rhetoric have compared, document by document, with the older polemics it descends from. In essays and studies published from 2011 onward, the historian Martin Jay and the political scientist Jérôme Jamin, among others, set out the finding: the narrative assigns the cultural decline of whole nations to the secret agency of a handful of intellectuals, most of them Jewish, working through a society's own schools and studios, which is the load bearing structure of classic antisemitic conspiracy literature, and its line of descent runs through the interwar German campaign against cultural Bolshevism, where Jewish artists were cast in the identical role. The Frankfurt exiles' actual Jewish origins are made to do the work that evidence does in honest argument, standing in for the coordination that no document attests. The scholars who publish this analysis direct it at a text and its transmission. They do not direct it at the phrase's ordinary users, who typically inherited the story as folklore and cannot be answerable for an architecture they never inspected.

### Q: Is there a real question about Marxism and culture?

Emphatically yes, and it is among the most thoroughly evidenced arguments in the tradition's historiography. Set the founding corpus, centred on capital, labour, and crisis, beside the work the next century produced: a Hungarian philosopher theorising reification and the novel in 1923, an Italian prisoner filling notebooks with the mechanics of consent, a Frankfurt programme wedding political economy to psychoanalysis, a British movement rebuilding the study of ordinary life. Whether those facts amount to a migration of the tradition's centre, and whether the migration deserves praise as maturation or blame as retreat, has been disputed at the highest level, with Perry Anderson's 1976 mapping of Western Marxism as the landmark statement and a generation of replies contesting its geography. The disputants cite chapter and verse and expose themselves to refutation, which is the signature of a real question. It becomes unreal only when a speaker exchanges the argument about published work for an allegation about hidden intentions, at which point the scholarly question has been abandoned, not answered.

### Q: Who popularised the modern conspiracy version?

Two relays did the work, and both are documented. The story was first assembled in the winter of 1992, when the Schiller Institute's magazine Fidelio carried a long article by Michael Minnicino presenting the Frankfurt exiles as the engineers of political correctness; its author, having left the LaRouche movement that published it, afterwards withdrew the piece as movement work. The decisive relay came at the decade's end, when the Free Congress Foundation in Washington, the policy organisation built by Paul Weyrich, adopted the tale as a set piece: a lecture by its associate William S. Lind compressed it into an origin story for political correctness, and a foundation video carried the compression further. From that circuit of conferences, mailing lists, syndicated columns, and broadcasts the phrase passed into general circulation, growing simpler and more certain with each copying. Scholars of the narrative's diffusion stress the pattern, because it is the reverse of scholarship's: there, claims gain footnotes as they travel, while this one gained an audience by losing them.

### Q: Do academics ever use the phrase descriptively?

They do, from time to time, and the usage is easy to recognise by the company it keeps. Its showcase instance sits on the cover of a work of archival scholarship: the historian Dennis Dworkin titled his 1997 study of the British New Left and the birth of cultural studies with the phrase, using it as a plain heading for the Marxist strand in that milieu, and the book that follows is footnotes, interviews, and institutional history. Similar descriptive appearances are scattered through writing on Marxist cultural analysis, where the words sort a literature into a grouping and allege nothing about the grouped writers beyond a shared method. Context certifies the sense: where the phrase appears among citations, dates, and named texts, and where its author can specify which works are meant, it is functioning as a label. The usage is nonetheless uncommon, peripheral to the field's own preferred names for itself, and that very rarity left the phrase undefended when polemic requisitioned it for other work.

### Q: What should a writer use instead of the phrase?

Name the actual subject, in its own vocabulary, and the sentence will repair itself. Where a literature is meant, call it one: Marxist cultural analysis, Marxist literary criticism, cultural materialism, or the specific formation in view, whether the New Left, the Birmingham centre, or the Western Marxist tradition. Where the Frankfurt Institute is meant, use the name its members used, critical theory, or the school's own, and then specify the thinker, because the members diverge from one another at least as sharply as any of them diverges from writers beyond the school. Where the historical development is meant, phrase it as the open question it is, a shift of attention from political economy toward culture, which readers can test against the record. Where institutional influence is meant, exhibit the mechanism: a translation and its date, a chair filled, a syllabus adopted, a journal founded. And where the polemical story itself is the subject, label it a narrative and credit it to its documents. The umbrella term survives only where the topic is the phrase itself.

### Q: How does the three-usage separation keep the term's history honest?

It works the way double entry bookkeeping works, by refusing transfers between accounts. Under the separation, each usage must pay its own way: the label with a list of the books it groups, the question with dated texts and an argument that opponents can join, the narrative with whatever documents it can produce, which after three decades amounts to its own founding article and the record of its copying. Dishonesty enters the term's history precisely at the transfer points, when the narrative draws respectability from the label's scholarly title pages, or draws its cast and its plausibility from the question's genuine evidence, or when a critic of the narrative spends the refutation on a citizen who was only ever raising the question. Bar the transfers and every party is left with its own assets: the scholar with a literature, the debater with an arguable thesis about influence, the storyteller with a tale whose papers are in order for any reader to inspect and find wanting.

### Q: What should a reader check when the term appears without a date or a source?

Three enquiries, in order, will sort nearly every case. Begin with the claim's shape: is the speaker pointing to a shelf of writings, proposing that a tradition changed its mind, or unveiling a hidden design? The first two can be discussed; the third must be evidenced, and confusion among them is the usual source of cross purposes, with each side rebutting a claim the other never made. Continue with the paperwork: what is actually cited, and does the citation lead to a publication that can be opened, or does it lead, as the narrative's citations do, to summaries of summaries resting on one movement article from 1992? End with the exit condition: ask what finding would persuade the speaker to drop the claim. Scholarship always has an answer, since its claims are wagers on evidence. A construction that counts missing documents as proof of concealment has no exit, and recognising that fact, calmly and without accusing the speaker of anything worse than repeating a story, ends the matter.

### Q: Why did the usages diverge in different decades and countries?

The divergence follows the transport. Scholarly senses ride scholarly vehicles, journal articles, monographs, translations commissioned by presses, teachers moving between universities, and they travel slowly, footnoted, arriving in each country dressed in that country's academic debates: structuralist in Paris, Gramscian in Birmingham, Frankfurtian in the American humanities of the translation years. The label stayed minor everywhere because its vehicle fleet was small. The narrative rode the vehicles of polemic, movement magazines, foundation lecture circuits, syndication, talk formats, and then the internet's copying machines, and it travelled fast, lightened of its citations, so that in many places it arrived first and installed itself as the phrase's plain meaning before any scholarly sense was heard of. Translation widened the gap, since the compound renders word for word into several languages while its senses do not. A nation's default meaning for the phrase is thus a record of which convoy reached it earliest, which is why the same words can be a librarian's heading in one conversation and a plot summary in another, with both speakers honestly unaware of the other's cargo.

### Q: How do historians document a polemical usage without endorsing it?

The craft has settled conventions. Everything begins with located instances: the historian builds the usage out of items that can be produced, a magazine article with a date, a pamphlet with a publisher, a broadcast with a transcript, and leaves unlocated colour out of the account entirely. The usage is then rendered in the historian's own sentences, as an account of its design and its claims about the world, while its targets are described in neutral terms and its own texts are quoted sparingly, because setting out the narrative's assertions in sequence hands the reader the narrative itself in a more respectable binding. Space follows weight rather than excitement, so a usage carried by a single article and a single foundation receives less of the history than the century of scholarship it parasitises. Persons, finally, are kept apart from phrases: those who repeat a term in good faith belong to its transmission story, not to a trial of its origins, and the finished history should read to them as equipment offered, not as a verdict delivered.

### Q: What separates a scholarly usage from a polemical one in the record?

In the record the difference is visible as a set of working habits. The scholarly usage leaves a trail that a stranger can retrace: editions, dates, reviews, replies, an archive open to consultation. It lets its subjects speak in their own defence when the evidence requires, so that a history of the school reports the members' loathing for the commercial entertainment they supposedly served alongside its influence. It keeps its causes before its effects and its influences within travelling distance of their dates. Above all it is corrigible in public. The polemical usage inverts each habit. Its citations circle back to its own retellings. Its subjects' writings are treated as ciphers whose plain sense is always the opposite of the hidden one. Its effects are gathered from decades its causes cannot reach. And its central assertion is armoured against the archive, since every empty shelf is entered as proof of the concealment it alleges. Two kinds of writing, two kinds of fate in the record: one is absorbed into knowledge, the other into the history of error.
