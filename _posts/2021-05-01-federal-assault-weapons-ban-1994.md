The federal assault weapons ban answered a question that had no settled answer before Congress wrote one: which semiautomatic firearms, described by name or by a counted set of configuration features, would federal law bar from new manufacture, transfer, and possession, and which firearms already held under federal law would remain lawful to keep and to transfer. Congress legislated against a defined gap. A federal import action in 1989 had excluded listed foreign rifles under a sporting purposes standard, and domestic manufacture had remained untouched. A California statute from the same year had listed firearms by make and model under state law. Neither measure supplied a general domestic rule.

The formal identity of the measure was the Public Safety and Recreational Firearms Use Protection Act, enacted as Subtitle A of Title XI of the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, Public Law 103-322, signed by President Bill Clinton on September 13, 1994, and codified in former sections of chapter 44 of title 18. Subtitle A took effect on enactment. It amended the Gun Control Act framework rather than creating a separate firearms code, placing a definition of semiautomatic assault weapon, a definition of large capacity ammunition feeding device, two prohibitions, marking requirements, and penalty references inside the chapter that already contained the general federal rules for firearms.

The statute defined its central term for itself. Semiautomatic assault weapon carried no meaning borrowed from a military manual or an engineering standard. Section 921(a)(30) supplied the meaning through four routes: a list of named firearms and their copies or duplicates, and three feature tests for rifles, pistols, and shotguns. Every firearm in the category was semiautomatic, firing one shot for each pull of the trigger. The definition did not turn on caliber, muzzle energy, or rate of fire. Fully automatic firearms were regulated under a separate regime, and the subtitle did not address them. That starting point decided the disputes that followed, because coverage turned on the statutory words, on a name, on a copy or duplicate relationship, or on a count of two features under the test written for a particular type of firearm.

The problem of configuration produced disagreement with named holders on each side. Supporters in Congress, led by Senator Dianne Feinstein of California and Representative Charles E. Schumer of New York, and law enforcement organizations including the Fraternal Order of Police, treated the counted features as markers of the military configuration that federal regulators had identified in 1989 and as features that aided control and accessory use. Critics, including the National Rifle Association and manufacturers, argued that the counted features did not change how a firearm functioned. Colt, through its general counsel Carlton S. Chen, described a compliant version of its rifle in those terms. Congress resolved the disagreement as a drafting matter, by writing names and counted features into law and by writing a grandfather clause, exceptions, and a ten year term into the same subtitle.

That combination gave the prohibition a prospective shape from the first day. Firearms lawfully possessed on the date of enactment remained lawful to possess and to transfer under section 922(v)(2), and feeding devices received a parallel protection under section 922(w)(2). Contemporary estimates placed the grandfathered stock of covered firearms at more than 1.5 million in private hands at enactment, with no official census behind the estimate. The statute contained no registration requirement and no surrender or confiscation provision. It governed new manufacture and new transfers into the covered category, left manual action firearms, permanently inoperable firearms, and antique firearms outside the prohibition, excepted defined governmental, security, retirement, and testing uses, and set its own ending for September 13, 2004.

## Origins of the Federal Assault Weapons Ban

The Federal Assault Weapons Ban began with a problem of definition before it became a problem of prohibition. Congress confronted a group of semiautomatic firearms that legislators, regulators, and state lawmakers described through configuration rather than through caliber, muzzle energy, or rate of fire. No settled technical classification supplied a ready boundary for the category. The term that entered federal law, semiautomatic assault weapon, did not come from a military manual, an engineering standard, or a preexisting federal definition that Congress simply adopted. The statute coined and defined the term for itself. That choice shaped every part of the measure that followed, because the reach of the prohibition depended on the words Congress placed in the definition and on the routes into the category that those words created.

A definition written by a legislature differs from a classification borrowed from a technical field in an important respect. A technical classification ordinarily rests on criteria developed for a professional purpose outside the statute, and a reader can consult that outside practice to understand what belongs inside or outside the class. The 1994 statute supplied no such external reference point. It named the category, listed firearms that fell within it, and described combinations of features that brought other firearms within it. The legal question for a manufacturer, an importer, a seller, a possessor, or an enforcing agency therefore began with the statutory text itself. Whether a particular firearm fell inside the category turned on whether it matched a listed name, counted as a copy or duplicate of a listed firearm, or satisfied one of the configuration tests that the definition established.

The semiautomatic element of the term also requires precision, because it marked a boundary between the new category and firearms that federal law treated under a separate regime. Every firearm within the statutory category was semiautomatic, firing one shot for each pull of the trigger. Fully automatic firearms, described in federal law as machine guns, were regulated separately under the National Firearms Act and the 1986 prohibition, and the 1994 subtitle did not address them. The debate that produced the ban therefore concerned self loading firearms that fired once per trigger pull and that Congress sorted by name or by configuration. Statements that collapse that distinction misdescribe the statute at its starting point. Congress did not write a definition based on automatic fire, and it did not select firearms by a measure of power. It wrote a definition based on identity for some firearms and on a counted set of physical features for others.

That method did not appear without antecedents. By the time Congress took up the question in the early 1990s, a state legislature had already tried a listing method, and a federal agency had already tried a feature based method under a different and narrower authority. California used names. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms, acting on imports, used configuration. Each approach answered a part of the category problem, and each left a part unanswered. The California statute reached listed firearms under state law but rested on make and model identification. The federal import action identified features associated in the working group record with a military configuration but reached foreign rifles only and left domestic manufacture untouched. The space between those two precedents was the space in which the congressional debate occurred.

### What problem was Congress trying to solve?

Congress faced a category that had no settled technical definition, a California statute that listed firearms by make and model, and a federal import exclusion that reached foreign rifles only. The problem was to write a domestic definition that covered a described configuration without reaching firearms outside that description.

The first antecedent in the sequence was a shooting in Stockton, California, in January 1989. Five children were killed in the shooting at an elementary school. California answered during the same year with the Roberti-Roos Assault Weapons Control Act of 1989. The California act listed covered firearms by make and model. That drafting choice had practical consequences that later federal drafters had to consider. A list gives notice in a direct form. A reader can compare the marking on a firearm, or the name under which it was sold, against the names in the statute. Enforcement and compliance both begin with identification, and identification by name avoids a separate inquiry into whether an individual firearm possesses one feature or another. The statute names the item, and the inquiry follows the name.

A list also carries limits that are visible from its structure. A make and model list is only as complete as the names the legislature places in it at enactment. A firearm that presents the configuration legislators described, but that bears a different name or comes from a different maker, raises a question that a pure list answers poorly unless the statute also reaches copies or uses a broader descriptive test. The later federal definition addressed that structural problem in its own text by covering listed firearms and copies or duplicates of listed firearms and by adding configuration tests for rifles, pistols, and shotguns that were not named. The origins of that combined method lie in the antecedents. One precedent supplied the named model technique. Another precedent supplied a vocabulary of features. Congress had both techniques in the record before it wrote the federal definition.

The federal import action of 1989 supplied the feature based precedent. It arose under section 925(d)(3) of title 18, the provision that applied a sporting purposes standard to imported firearms. In March and April 1989, the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms suspended the importation of semiautomatic rifles that it considered not suitable for sporting purposes under that standard. The sequence matters. The agency first suspended importation, an interim step that halted entry while the question was examined. A working group then studied the matter and produced a report dated July 6, 1989. On the basis of that work, the exclusion became permanent for the listed types. Contemporary reporting counted 43 types within the permanent exclusion. The figure belongs to contemporary reporting of the action, and it describes types of rifles affected by an import determination rather than models named in a criminal prohibition enacted by Congress.

The working group identified a set of military configuration features. The list included detachable magazine capability, folding or telescoping stocks, separate pistol grips, bayonet capability, flash suppressors, bipods, grenade launchers, and night sights. Each item described a physical characteristic or an accessory relationship rather than a caliber or a measure of power. A detachable magazine capability meant an ability to accept a detachable magazine. A folding or telescoping stock folded or slid to shorten the overall length of a firearm or to adjust its fit. A separate pistol grip was a grip below the action, held by the firing hand. Bayonet capability rested on a mount, a lug that accepted a bayonet. A flash suppressor was a muzzle device that dispersed escaping gases to reduce visible flash. A grenade launcher fired rifle grenades. Read together, the working group list treated configuration as a pattern formed by the combination of a self loading action, magazine capability, and features drawn from a military pattern, rather than as a judgment about a single component standing alone.

That distinction between a pattern and a single component later became central to the structure of the federal tests. The working group did not treat one grip, one stock, or one muzzle device in isolation as the whole basis for its description of military configuration. It placed features in a group. The congressional definition that followed in 1994 likewise counted features for firearms reached through the rifle, pistol, and shotgun tests, and it required at least two counted features under each of those tests, subject to the conditions stated in each test. That counting structure determined whether a configuration fell inside or outside the prohibition. The origin of the structure is visible in 1989. Federal regulators had already described the relevant firearms by grouping features, and Congress legislated against the background of that administrative description.

The other central fact about the 1989 import action was its limited jurisdictional reach. The Bureau acted on importation. Its authority under the sporting purposes standard allowed it to exclude foreign rifles from entry, and the permanent action did exactly that. It did not regulate manufacture inside the United States. A rifle type excluded at the border as a foreign product could have a counterpart produced domestically without encountering the import exclusion, because the exclusion attached to importation rather than to domestic production. The import action reached foreign rifles only. Domestic manufacture was untouched. That asymmetry framed the later congressional debate.

Asymmetry of that kind creates a defined legislative question. If the policy concern, as legislators stated it, attached to a configuration rather than to a country of origin, an import only rule sorted firearms by source rather than by the configuration that had prompted the action. Two firearms with the same described features could receive different federal treatment because one arrived from abroad and the other was made in the United States. Supporters of federal legislation could point to that gap as the reason a statute was needed at all, because only Congress could write a prohibition that reached domestic manufacture, transfer, and possession within a general federal framework. Opponents of legislation could contest the underlying description of the configuration, dispute whether the counted features changed how a firearm functioned, or argue against extending a federal prohibition to domestic commerce. The record of named positions in the wider debate followed that shape. The National Rifle Association and manufacturers opposed the ban. Senator Dianne Feinstein of California and Representative Charles E. Schumer of New York supported it in Congress, and law enforcement organizations, including the Fraternal Order of Police, backed it. The asymmetry did not settle those arguments. It defined the ground on which the arguments took place.

The chronology also clarifies what the import action was not. It was not a general federal ban enacted in 1989. It was not a prohibition on possession by private persons in the United States. It was not a restriction that a domestic manufacturer had to satisfy before selling a rifle in domestic commerce. Those broader steps required legislation, and legislation required Congress to decide questions that an agency applying an import standard did not have to decide. Congress had to choose a definition. It had to decide whether to proceed by name, by features, or by both. It had to decide which transactions to prohibit, which existing firearms to leave under a grandfather clause, which governmental and other uses to except, what markings to require for items made after enactment, and what penalty to attach to a knowing violation. It also had to decide how long the measure would last, a question the enacted subtitle answered with a ten year term that ended on September 13, 2004. None of those choices followed automatically from the working group report of July 6, 1989.

Between the precedents of 1989 and the Senate debate of 1993, the subject returned to Congress in the setting of the larger crime bill. A second shooting entered the debate record during that interval. In July 1993, eight people were killed in a shooting at 101 California Street in San Francisco. The episode was cited throughout the Senate debate that autumn. The citation pattern matters for an account of origins because it shows how the debate connected an immediate episode to a category problem that legislators had been considering, in state and administrative settings, since 1989. The San Francisco shooting did not supply the definition. The definition had sources in the California list and in the federal feature list. The episode supplied part of the occasion for the Senate to take up the amendment during the crime bill debate of that autumn and part of the factual setting in which senators argued over whether a federal prohibition should be written at all.

Senator Feinstein, a California senator, offered the ban as an amendment to the Senate crime bill. She brought the amendment to the floor on November 9, 1993. The California connection in the sponsorship was more than geographical coincidence in the record assembled for the debate. California had enacted the Roberti-Roos Act in 1989 after the Stockton shooting. The 101 California Street shooting had occurred in San Francisco in July 1993. The import action of 1989 had been a federal measure, but it had left domestic manufacture outside its reach. A California senator carrying a federal amendment in November 1993 therefore brought together the state experience with a named list, the administrative experience with a feature based import exclusion, and the unresolved domestic gap that the import action had left open. The amendment form also mattered. The ban entered the Senate as an amendment to a crime bill rather than as a freestanding measure in that chamber, even though the House later considered the question first as a freestanding bill in 1994 before the combined crime bill reached enactment. Its eventual statutory home reflected that path. It became a subtitle of a larger act rather than a separate code.

The problem Congress faced can therefore be stated at three levels, each anchored in the record. At the level of language, Congress needed a term with legal content, and it created that content by definition because no technical standard supplied it. At the level of precedent, Congress had a state model based on names and a federal administrative model based on features, and neither model by itself covered domestic manufacture under a general prohibition. At the level of legislative scope, Congress had to decide whether a rule that had operated only at the border should become a rule governing manufacture, transfer, and possession inside the United States, and if so, under what exceptions, grandfather protection, markings, and penalties. Those decisions produced a statute whose structure can be read as an answer to the antecedents. The named firearms portion answered the identification problem in the manner of the California list, extended to copies and duplicates. The feature tests answered the configuration problem in the manner suggested by the working group list, translated into counted statutory criteria. The prohibitions answered the domestic gap by reaching conduct that the import action could not reach.

The California precedent requires the same caution. The Roberti-Roos Act listed firearms by make and model, and the federal statute named nineteen firearms in the conventional count used by the administration and by Congress. The resemblance in method is direct. A named list, however, operated differently inside a federal statute that also contained feature tests, an appendix of firearms expressly not covered, a grandfather clause for items lawfully possessed on the date of enactment, and exceptions for governmental use, testing, and certain retired law enforcement possession. The federal measure placed the named list inside a larger system of inclusions and exclusions. More than 650 firearms were listed by name in Appendix A as firearms not covered, and the figure most often used by the sponsors in later debate was 670. A model could therefore be named in the federal scheme in either of two opposite ways, as a covered firearm under the definition or as an excluded firearm under the appendix. The listing technique that California used to define coverage became, in federal hands, a technique used on both sides of the boundary.

Placement inside existing federal law completed the transformation from antecedent to statute. The enacted ban was the Public Safety and Recreational Firearms Use Protection Act, Subtitle A, titled Assault Weapons, of Title XI, titled Firearms, of the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, Public Law 103-322. The legislative vehicle was H.R. 3355, introduced by Representative Jack Brooks of Texas on October 26, 1993. President Bill Clinton signed the act on September 13, 1994, and the subtitle took effect on enactment under section 110105. Subtitle A contained six sections. Section 110101 supplied the short title. Section 110102 restricted manufacture, transfer, and possession of certain semiautomatic assault weapons. Section 110103 addressed large capacity ammunition feeding devices. Section 110104 directed a study by the Attorney General. Section 110105 supplied the effective date and the sunset. Section 110106 added Appendix A to section 922 of title 18.

Congress did not enact those provisions as a freestanding firearms code detached from prior law. It operated by amending chapter 44 of title 18, the framework established by the Gun Control Act. The subtitle placed new definitions at section 921(a)(30) and section 921(a)(31). It placed new prohibitions at section 922(v) and section 922(w). It added marking sentences at section 923(i), requiring the serial number of a semiautomatic assault weapon manufactured after enactment to show the date of manufacture clearly and requiring a large capacity ammunition feeding device manufactured after enactment to carry identification in the form the statute described. It added penalty cross references at section 924(a)(1)(B) and section 924(c)(1)(B)(i). Those provisions were former provisions of chapter 44, removed when the subtitle reached its sunset, but during the period from September 13, 1994 to September 13, 2004 they sat inside the same chapter that contained the general federal definitions, prohibitions, and penalties for firearms.

That placement had interpretive and administrative significance grounded in the structure of the act. A reader who opened chapter 44 encountered the new term where chapter 44 kept its definitions, the new prohibitions where chapter 44 kept its unlawful acts, the new marking rule where chapter 44 addressed identification, and the new penalties where chapter 44 addressed punishment. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms administered chapter 44 within the Department of the Treasury during the period, before the Bureau moved to the Department of Justice in January 2003 under the Homeland Security Act of 2002. An amendment built into an administered framework drew on that framework for its operative setting. It also meant that the ban was never a separate body of law that a reader could understand in isolation. Its grandfather clauses, exceptions, burden provision, and appendix all worked by reference to the prohibitions and definitions that the subtitle inserted into chapter 44.

The origins account therefore ends where the statutory text begins. A school shooting in Stockton prompted a California statute that named firearms by make and model. A federal import suspension in the spring of 1989, followed by a working group report on July 6, 1989, produced a permanent import exclusion for listed types of foreign rifles and a description of military configuration features. That exclusion left domestic manufacture untouched, and the gap between an import rule and a domestic rule framed the congressional debate. A shooting at 101 California Street in San Francisco in July 1993 was cited throughout the Senate debate that autumn, when a California senator offered the ban as an amendment to the crime bill. Congress answered the category problem by defining its own term, by combining names with counted features, and by placing the resulting prohibitions inside chapter 44 of title 18 as an amendment to the Gun Control Act framework rather than as a freestanding enactment.

## How the Ban Moved Through Congress

The ban reached the statute book through two separate congressional routes that converged in a single conference. In the Senate, the prohibition arrived as an amendment to a broader crime bill, offered by Senator Dianne Feinstein of California during the autumn of 1993. In the House of Representatives, the prohibition arrived first as a freestanding bill, H.R. 4296, carried by Representative Charles E. Schumer of New York and voted on in the spring of 1994. Each route produced its own recorded vote, and each vote carried a different margin and a different procedural meaning. The distinction mattered throughout the passage record, because a tally recorded for the freestanding House bill described the strength of the ban standing alone, while the tallies recorded for the crime bill described the strength of a combined package in which the ban formed one part among many. Keeping those votes separate is essential to reading the record accurately, and the sequence itself explains why the final months before enactment turned on conference procedure rather than on a single decisive vote in either chamber.

The congressional debate did not begin in 1993. In January 1989, five children were killed in a shooting at an elementary school in Stockton, California. California answered with the Roberti-Roos Assault Weapons Control Act of 1989, a state statute that listed covered firearms by make and model. That state response supplied one model for later federal drafting, a list of named firearms, and it placed the question of semiautomatic firearms built in a military configuration before legislators several years before the Senate took up the Feinstein amendment. The federal executive branch acted on a separate front during the same year. In March and April 1989, the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms suspended the importation of semiautomatic rifles that the agency considered not suitable for sporting purposes under section 925(d)(3). A working group report dated July 6, 1989, made the exclusion permanent for the listed types, counted as 43 types in contemporary reporting. The report identified military configuration features that included detachable magazine capability, folding or telescoping stocks, separate pistol grips, bayonet capability, flash suppressors, bipods, grenade launchers, and night sights.

The import action and the later congressional ban differed in reach, and the difference framed the debate that followed. The import action reached foreign rifles only. Domestic manufacture was untouched by that action, so a firearm of a type excluded from importation could remain in lawful domestic production. That asymmetry between imported and domestic firearms formed part of the background against which Congress considered a prohibition written into chapter 44 of title 18 itself, one that would govern manufacture, transfer, and possession without regard to the country in which a covered firearm had been made. The feature language also carried forward. The configuration features identified by federal regulators in 1989, detachable magazines, folding or telescoping stocks, pistol grips, bayonet fittings, and flash suppressors among them, reappeared in altered form in the statutory tests that Congress eventually enacted, where the presence of counted features in combination sorted a semiautomatic firearm into or out of the category the statute defined. The passage record therefore joined two strands, a named list in the manner of the California statute and a feature test in the manner of the import determination, and both strands survived into the enacted text.

A second episode entered the Senate debate directly. In July 1993, eight people were killed in a shooting at 101 California Street in San Francisco. The episode was cited throughout the Senate debate that autumn, as members argued over the amendment that Senator Feinstein prepared to offer. Its place in the record was as a cited episode, invoked during argument, rather than as a provision of the bill. By the autumn of 1993 the vehicle for the Senate action was the crime bill then before the Senate, H.R. 3355, a bill introduced in the House by Representative Jack Brooks of Texas on October 26, 1993, and taken up in the Senate in place of the Senate bill. The Feinstein amendment proposed to attach the ban to that vehicle, so the Senate vote on the amendment would decide whether the ban traveled with the crime bill into conference, while the later Senate vote on the crime bill itself would decide whether the entire package, ban included, passed the chamber.

Senator Feinstein brought the amendment to the floor on November 9, 1993. The amendment did not move to adoption without resistance. Before the Senate adopted it, the amendment survived a tabling fight, a procedural contest in which a motion to table, if carried, would have set the amendment aside without adoption. The fight over tabling therefore functioned as the first recorded test of the amendment in the Senate, and its outcome determined whether members would reach the question of adoption at all. On November 17, 1993, the Senate adopted the amendment by 56 to 43. Five Republicans joined the majority in that vote. The tally gave the ban a Senate majority as an amendment to the crime bill, recorded separately from the later vote on the bill as a whole, and it placed the prohibition inside the text that the Senate would send forward. From that point, removing the ban from the Senate position would have required a further affirmative action, because the amendment had become part of the bill the Senate was considering rather than a proposal pending before it.

The Senate completed its action on the vehicle two days after adopting the amendment. On November 19, 1993, the Senate passed the crime bill itself by 95 to 4. The contrast between the two Senate tallies belonged to the difference between the questions presented. The vote of 56 to 43 decided the contested amendment, the ban standing as the specific proposition under consideration. The vote of 95 to 4 decided the crime bill as a whole, a broad package on which members who had divided over the amendment could unite for other reasons contained in the bill, or could support despite the amendment it carried. Neither tally overstated or understated the other. Together they showed a chamber that had accepted the ban by a clear but contested margin and had then passed the larger bill by an overwhelming margin, sending the ban forward as one component of a measure that commanded far wider support than the ban had commanded on its own.

The House approached the question in a different order. Rather than beginning with an amendment to the crime bill, the House took the question first as a freestanding bill, H.R. 4296, carried by Representative Schumer. A freestanding bill presented the ban as the sole proposition, so a vote for the bill was a vote for the ban itself, unaccompanied by the other provisions of a crime package that might have drawn support from members unpersuaded by the ban, or opposition from members who supported the ban but objected to other parts of a larger measure. The choice of vehicle therefore produced the cleanest recorded measure of House support for the prohibition standing alone, and it also produced the closest vote in the entire passage record. The freestanding route had a further consequence for the sequence. Because the House acted on its own bill in May 1994, several months after the Senate had placed the ban inside the crime bill, the two chambers entered the conference stage with the ban present in both positions, but with the House having recorded an independent judgment on the ban as a separate measure before it voted on any combined package.

Support for the freestanding bill extended beyond the members who carried it. Three former presidents, Gerald Ford, Jimmy Carter, and Ronald Reagan, publicly urged House members to support it. The public urging came from three former holders of the presidency, and it was addressed to sitting House members in advance of the vote on H.R. 4296. Their intervention formed part of the public record surrounding the House vote, addressed to members who were deciding how to vote on the freestanding bill while its outcome remained undecided. The former presidents issued no votes and held no seats, so their role in the passage record was one of public advocacy directed at the chamber in the period before the vote, and the closeness of the vote that followed gave that advocacy a prominence in contemporary accounts of the division.

On May 5, 1994, the House passed the ban by 216 to 214, a margin of two votes. Congressional Quarterly accounted for the closing minutes by recording that the tally stood against passage in the closing minutes and turned when Representative Andrew Jacobs Jr. of Indiana reversed his vote. A reversed vote in the closing minutes carried the outcome because the division stood so close that a single member moving from one side to the other altered both totals at once, reducing one tally while adding to the other. The Congressional Quarterly account therefore identified the mechanism by which a bill that stood against passage near the close of voting became a bill that had passed when the vote concluded. This two vote margin belongs to the freestanding House bill, H.R. 4296, and not to the later crime bill votes that followed, and that distinction must accompany any statement of the margin. The later votes, in the House and in the Senate in August 1994, were votes on a conference report for a combined crime bill, and their margins measured support for that package rather than support for the ban presented on its own.

The passage of the freestanding bill did not enact the ban. Enactment required the two chambers to agree on a single text, and the ban formed part of the larger crime bill that went to conference between the House and the Senate. A conference report, once filed, returned to each chamber for agreement, and the procedure governing whether a chamber would take up the report at all became decisive in August 1994. The ban had survived a tabling fight in the Senate, had been adopted there by 56 to 43, and had passed the House as a freestanding measure by the narrowest margin in the record. In conference, the question changed form again. The issue was no longer the ban alone, and it was no longer an amendment that could be tabled or adopted in isolation. The issue was whether the House would consider and then agree to a combined conference report that included the ban among the provisions of a complete crime bill, after negotiators for the two chambers had settled the differences between the House and Senate positions.

### What nearly stopped the ban in conference?

The conference report itself nearly failed on August 11, 1994, when the House refused to take up the report under the proposed rule, after members of both parties objected to parts of the combined crime bill package that included the ban, before negotiators returned with a revised report.

The events of August 11, 1994, require careful statement, because the House action that day was a refusal to take up the conference report under the proposed rule, rather than a vote rejecting the conference report on its substance. A rule in the House governed the terms on which the report would be considered, and refusal of the rule prevented the report from proceeding under those terms. In conference, the combined crime bill stalled at that point. Members of both parties objected to parts of the package that included the ban, so the objections that produced the collapse were objections to a package, recorded against a procedural vehicle, and not a single chamber judgment confined to the prohibition alone. That procedural character matters for the same reason the May margin matters. A reader who treats August 11 as a House vote against the ban itself would misread the record, just as a reader who assigns the two vote margin of May 5 to the August votes would misread it. The record shows a conference report that could not be taken up under the rule proposed for it, a stall in the progress of the combined bill, and a ban whose fate depended on whether negotiators could return with a package the House would agree to consider and then approve.

What followed the collapse showed where the decision on the ban rested during the renegotiation. The President declined to drop the ban. That refusal removed one route out of the stall, the route in which negotiators would have returned without the prohibition and sought House agreement for a crime bill shorn of the provision that the Senate had adopted in November 1993 and that the House had passed as a freestanding bill in May 1994. With the ban retained, negotiators cut prevention funding and returned. The renegotiation therefore adjusted another part of the package, funding for prevention, while holding the ban in place, and the revised conference report went back to the chambers for the agreement votes that the August 11 collapse had delayed. The sequence preserved the central fact of the conference stage. The ban survived the collapse not because the House reversed its August 11 action on the same report, but because negotiators produced a further report, with the ban retained and other funding reduced, and the chambers then voted on agreement to that report.

The House agreed to the conference report on August 21, 1994, by 235 to 195, recorded as Roll no. 416. The tally differed markedly from the freestanding vote of May 5, and the difference illustrated the distinction that the passage record requires. On May 5, the House had divided 216 to 214 on the ban as the sole proposition in H.R. 4296. On August 21, the House divided 235 to 195 on agreement to a conference report for the combined crime bill, a question that joined the ban to the remainder of the package that negotiators had settled, including the prevention funding cuts made after the August 11 collapse. Members could reach different conclusions on those two questions for reasons that the tallies themselves do not disclose, and the record supplies tallies rather than motives for individual members beyond the single reversal that Congressional Quarterly recorded in May. Stated plainly, the August 21 vote carried the ban forward inside an agreed House position on the whole bill, with a margin far wider than the ban had obtained when it stood alone in the same chamber three and a half months earlier.

The Senate acted four days after the House. On August 25, 1994, the Senate agreed to the conference report by 61 to 38, after invoking cloture by the same margin. Cloture was the procedural step that allowed the Senate to bring debate on the report to a close and proceed to the agreement vote, and the identity of the two margins, 61 to 38 for cloture and 61 to 38 for agreement, showed that the division on ending debate matched the division on the report itself. The Senate tally also completed the comparison across chambers. The ban had entered the Senate crime bill by 56 to 43 in November 1993, as a contested amendment. It left the Senate in August 1994 inside a conference report agreed to by 61 to 38, after a cloture vote of the same size. Both chambers had by then agreed to the same conference text, so the legislative action required for presentation to the President was complete, and the ban stood in the text of the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994 as Subtitle A of Title XI, the Public Safety and Recreational Firearms Use Protection Act.

With agreement recorded in both chambers, the remaining step in the documented sequence was signature by the President, and the record gives that step a single date, the date written into section 110105 as the effective date for the subtitle and the amendments made by it. President Clinton signed the bill on September 13, 1994. The signing converted the conference text into Public Law 103-322, and the subtitle took effect on enactment, on that same date, under section 110105. The date closed a passage sequence that had run from the Senate floor action of November 9, 1993, when Senator Feinstein brought the amendment forward, through the Senate adoption of November 17, 1993, the freestanding House passage of May 5, 1994, the conference collapse of August 11, 1994, and the agreement votes of August 21 and August 25, 1994. Each stage had presented the ban in a different procedural form, as an amendment exposed to a tabling motion, as a freestanding bill exposed to a vote on its own merits in the House, and as a component of a conference report exposed first to a rule and then to agreement votes in both chambers. The ban survived each form, although the form determined what a vote for or against the pending question actually decided, and the tallies have meaning only when attached to the question that produced them.

The political consequences claimed for the passage formed a separate and unresolved part of the record. Claims about the November 1994 elections were attributed claims, advanced by their makers after the elections had taken place. President Clinton later wrote that the ban had contributed to the Democratic loss of the House in those elections. The claim came from the President who had declined to drop the ban during the August 1994 renegotiation, who had signed the bill on September 13, 1994, and who assessed the electoral result in writing afterward. Stated as its maker stated it, the claim held that enactment of the ban formed one contributing cause among the causes of a change in control of the House, a judgment about electoral causation that the passage record itself, consisting of dates, procedures, and vote tallies, could neither confirm nor refute.

The National Rifle Association advanced a counter claim. The association claimed credit for defeating members who had supported the ban. That claim, like the President claim, was a claim by its maker about the causes of an electoral outcome, credit claimed by an organization that had opposed the ban during its passage for a result that followed in November 1994. The two claims pointed in a compatible direction on one narrow point, each treating the ban as electorally consequential for members who had supported it, but they came from opposed positions in the passage fight, one from the President who had insisted on retaining the ban in conference and one from the organization that had sought its defeat, and neither claim was a finding by a neutral body. The record of passage therefore ends with both claims standing as attributed statements by named makers, the President claim that the ban contributed to the loss of the House and the association claim of credit for defeating supporting members, with no finding made between them and no resolution supplied by the votes, dates, and procedures that constitute the documented history of how the ban moved through Congress.

## The Statutory Definition: Named Firearms and the Rifle Test

Section 921(a)(30) of title 18 supplied the definition of the statutory term semiautomatic assault weapon. The statute defined that term for itself, and the term carried the meaning that the definition assigned to it rather than the meaning of a technical or military classification. The definition had a layered structure. It identified some firearms by name, and it identified other firearms through tests that examined the type of firearm, a threshold capability, and a count of specified characteristics. Those two methods were distinct, and the distinction mattered to the operation of the definition because a firearm could enter the category through identification by name without satisfying a feature count, while a firearm that entered through a feature test did so because its configuration met the terms of the test that applied to its type.

The definition contained four routes, designated as subsections (A), (B), (C), and (D) of section 921(a)(30). Subsection (A) was the named route, covering listed firearms and their copies or duplicates in any caliber. Subsection (B) was the rifle test, covering a semiautomatic rifle with the ability to accept a detachable magazine and at least two of five specified features. Subsection (C) was the pistol test, covering a semiautomatic pistol with the ability to accept a detachable magazine and at least two of five different specified features. Subsection (D) was the shotgun test, covering a semiautomatic shotgun with at least two of four specified features. The pistol and shotgun routes completed the architecture of the definition, although the named route and the rifle test displayed its two central methods most fully: selection by identity in subsection (A), and selection by counted configuration in subsection (B).

The architecture also contained an important asymmetry among the three feature tests. The rifle test and the pistol test each began with a precondition, the ability to accept a detachable magazine, and each then required at least two counted features in addition to that precondition. The shotgun test had no detachable magazine precondition. For a shotgun, the ability to accept a detachable magazine appeared inside the counted list itself, as one of the four features from which at least two had to be present. The same characteristic therefore occupied different positions in different tests: it was a threshold condition for rifles and pistols, and it was a counted feature for shotguns. That placement was part of the statutory text, and any account of the definition had to preserve it in order to state the tests accurately.

The four routes were routes into a single category, and each route stated a complete basis for inclusion within the terms that the route itself supplied. A firearm identified under subsection (A) entered through the named list. A rifle, pistol, or shotgun that met the elements of subsection (B), (C), or (D) entered through the feature test for its type. The routes therefore did not operate as stages of one test in which a firearm had to pass a naming stage before reaching a configuration stage. They operated as parallel statements of the category, arranged within one definition and separated by the method that each subsection used. That parallel arrangement explained why the definition had to be read subsection by subsection. Language that belonged to one route, such as the detachable magazine precondition in the rifle and pistol tests, could not be carried into another route that omitted it, such as the shotgun test, and language that belonged to the named route, such as the copies or duplicates phrase, performed work that no feature clause in subsections (B), (C), or (D) performed.

### Which firearms did the statute name?

The statute named nineteen firearms in the conventional count used by the administration and by Congress, a count reflected in the presidential letter of August 22, 1994. Its named route covered specified models from named makers, copies or duplicates of those firearms in any caliber, and revolving cylinder shotguns described by example.

The opening language of subsection (A) defined the reach of the named route before any individual name appeared. The subsection covered any of the firearms, or copies or duplicates of the firearms in any caliber, known as the firearms that the clauses then identified. The words copies or duplicates extended the route beyond an original example bearing an exact listed designation, and the words in any caliber stated that the extension operated across calibers. A copy or duplicate therefore did not leave the named route merely because it was chambered for a different caliber from another example of the listed firearm. The route turned on the identity of the firearm as one of the listed firearms or as a copy or duplicate of one of them, and the text stated that identity in caliber neutral terms.

The first clause of subsection (A) named Norinco, Mitchell, and Poly Technologies Avtomat Kalashnikovs, and it added the words all models after those names. That clause joined three maker or importer names to a firearm family name, and its all models language made the clause operate at the level of the model range associated with those names rather than at the level of a single variant. The second clause named Action Arms Israeli Military Industries UZI and Galil. That clause paired two designations, UZI and Galil, within a single clause, and it placed both under the Action Arms Israeli Military Industries identification that the statute supplied. The drafting pattern of the first two clauses differed from the pattern of a clause naming one maker and one model, because each of those clauses gathered more than one name into one clause of the definition.

The third clause named the Beretta Ar70 (SC-70), giving one designation in the main text of the clause and a second designation in parentheses. The fourth clause named the Colt AR-15, joining a maker name to a model designation in the shortest of the named clauses. The fifth clause named Fabrique National FN/FAL, FN/LAR, and FNC, and it therefore gathered three designations under one maker name within one clause. Read together, the third, fourth, and fifth clauses showed that the named list did not follow one uniform naming formula. One clause supplied an alternate designation in parentheses, one clause supplied a single maker and model pairing, and one clause supplied three designations associated with a single maker name.

The sixth clause named the SWD M-10, M-11, M-11/9, and M-12, collecting four related model designations under the SWD name. The seventh clause named the Steyr AUG, again using a maker name followed by a model designation. The eighth clause named INTRATEC TEC-9, TEC-DC9 and TEC-22, collecting three designations under the INTRATEC name. Those three clauses reinforced the pattern visible earlier in the list. The unit of the statute was the clause, but the clauses did not each correspond to a single firearm in the conventional count, because several clauses contained multiple designations and one clause described a class of shotguns by mechanism and example.

The ninth clause took a different form from every clause that preceded it. It covered revolving cylinder shotguns, such as, or similar to, the Street Sweeper and Striker 12. The clause began with a description of a mechanism and a firearm type, a shotgun whose ammunition was held in a revolving cylinder, and it then supplied two examples of that description. The words such as made the Street Sweeper and the Striker 12 examples of the described class, and the words or similar to extended the description to shotguns similar to those examples. The clause therefore operated partly by description and partly by example, whereas the eight earlier clauses operated by maker names, family names, and model designations.

Viewed as drafting, the nine clauses used several levels of identification at once. Some clauses identified a firearm through a maker name joined to a model designation, as the Beretta, Colt, and Steyr clauses did. Some clauses identified a group through a maker or importer name joined to a family name and a statement covering all models, as the opening Avtomat Kalashnikovs clause did. Some clauses gathered several model designations under one name, as the Fabrique National, SWD, and INTRATEC clauses did, and the Action Arms clause gathered two designations under a compound identification. The final clause used no maker name at all and instead identified its subject through a mechanism, a revolving cylinder, a firearm type, a shotgun, and two examples. The list therefore did not rest on a single registry of model numbers or on a single form of trade designation. It combined the names by which the listed firearms were known, in the forms that the statute supplied, and it attached the copies or duplicates language to the whole of that combined list rather than to any one clause in isolation.

The nine clauses and the conventional count of nineteen firearms measured different things. The clauses were the textual units of subsection (A). The count of nineteen firearms named in the statute, in the conventional count used by the administration and by Congress, was the count used in contemporary official descriptions of the list, including the letter that President Bill Clinton sent to Congress on August 22, 1994. A clause could contain several counted firearms, and the revolving cylinder shotgun clause described its subject through a mechanism and two examples rather than through a conventional model list alone. For that reason, counting clauses did not reproduce the conventional count, and reciting the conventional count did not reproduce the text. A faithful account of subsection (A) required both numbers to be kept in their separate roles, with nine identifying the clauses of the text and nineteen identifying the firearms in the conventional official count.

The named route also had a structural consequence that separated it from every feature test in the definition. Subsection (A) contained no feature threshold, no detachable magazine precondition, and no requirement that a listed firearm possess any particular combination of characteristics. Identification under subsection (A) rested on the name of the firearm and on the copies or duplicates language that accompanied the names. Feature removal could not cure a firearm banned by name, because the named route covered the listed models and their copies or duplicates regardless of features. The compliance path that existed under the feature tests, under which a configuration could be kept below two counted features, had no application to a firearm that entered the category through subsection (A). That separation between the routes was written into the architecture of the definition itself.

Subsection (B) stated the rifle test in a sequence of three elements. The firearm first had to be a semiautomatic rifle. The rifle then had to have an ability to accept a detachable magazine. The rifle then had to have at least two of the five features that the subsection specified. Each element performed a different function in the test. The first element fixed the type of firearm to which the test applied. The second element imposed a capability precondition that stood outside the feature count. The third element supplied the count, and the count was satisfied at two features and remained satisfied if more than two specified features were present. A semiautomatic rifle with the required magazine capability and only one specified feature did not satisfy subsection (B), and a semiautomatic rifle with two specified features but without the ability to accept a detachable magazine also did not satisfy subsection (B), because the precondition and the count were separate requirements and neither substituted for the other.

The first feature in the rifle test was a folding or telescoping stock. A stock of that kind folded or slid to shorten the overall length of the firearm or to adjust its fit. The statute joined the folding form and the telescoping form in a single feature clause, so the clause described one counted feature with two mechanical forms. The second feature was a pistol grip that protrudes conspicuously beneath the action of the weapon. A pistol grip of that description was a grip below the action that the firing hand held, and the statutory wording added two qualifications to the grip itself: the grip protruded conspicuously, and its position was beneath the action of the weapon. The third feature was a bayonet mount, a lug that accepted a bayonet. The object counted by that clause was the mount on the firearm, the fitting that made attachment possible, described in terms of the attachment that the fitting accepted.

The two forms inside the stock clause differed in mechanism while serving the functions that the definition of the feature described. A folding stock folded, shortening the overall length of the firearm. A telescoping stock slid, and that sliding movement also shortened overall length or adjusted the fit of the firearm for the shooter. The clause counted the stock as one feature in either form. The grip clause, by contrast, turned on position and projection rather than on movement. The grip sat beneath the action, and the firing hand held the grip in that position below the action. The statutory adjective conspicuously qualified the manner in which the grip protruded beneath the action, so the clause described a grip defined by its projection and its placement, in the words that the statute itself chose. The bayonet mount clause turned on a fitting rather than on the bayonet. A mount was a lug on the firearm that accepted a bayonet, and the clause counted that fitting as a feature of the rifle to which the fitting belonged.

The fourth feature in the rifle test combined two related forms in one clause: a flash suppressor or a threaded barrel designed to accommodate a flash suppressor. A flash suppressor was a muzzle device that dispersed escaping gases in order to reduce visible flash. A threaded barrel accepted screwed on muzzle devices, and the rifle test qualified the threaded barrel by its design purpose, stating that the barrel was designed to accommodate a flash suppressor. The clause therefore reached a rifle fitted with the muzzle device itself and a rifle whose barrel was threaded for that device. The fifth feature was a grenade launcher, a device that fired rifle grenades. The five feature clauses carried no ranking and no weighting within the text of the test. The statute counted features toward the figure of two, and the satisfaction of the test depended on reaching that figure after the type element and the magazine precondition had been met.

The wording of the threaded barrel language in the rifle test differed from the corresponding language in the pistol test, and the difference illustrated the precision with which each test had to be read. In subsection (B), the threaded barrel appeared inside the flash suppressor clause and was qualified by the purpose of accommodating a flash suppressor. The feature was therefore stated in two forms, the suppressor itself or a barrel threaded and designed for the suppressor, within a single clause of the rifle list. In subsection (C), by contrast, a threaded barrel stood as a counted feature in its own right and was described by a wider set of devices that the barrel was capable of accepting, a barrel extender, a flash suppressor, a forward handgrip, or a silencer. Transferring the broader pistol wording into the rifle test, or the narrower rifle qualification into the pistol test, would have misstated both tests. Each subsection defined its features for its own firearm type, and the rifle test in particular tied its threaded barrel form to the flash suppressor named in the same clause.

The placement of the detachable magazine element gave the rifle test a structure that a simple feature list would not have had. Magazine capability was not one of the five features in subsection (B), and a rifle did not move closer to satisfying the test by possessing that capability alone. The capability opened the test, and the feature count then determined whether a rifle that met the precondition also met the full definition. Conversely, features counted toward the figure of two only within a test whose other elements were present. A folding or telescoping stock, a qualifying pistol grip, a bayonet mount, a flash suppressor or qualifying threaded barrel, and a grenade launcher each had the same counting position in the clause structure, and any combination that reached two satisfied the counting element. The subsection did not require any particular pair, and it did not single out any one feature as indispensable to the rifle test.

The pistol test in subsection (C) followed the same general sequence as the rifle test while substituting a different firearm type and a different set of counted features. It applied to a semiautomatic pistol that had an ability to accept a detachable magazine and at least two of its five specified features. Those features were an ammunition magazine that attached to the pistol outside of the pistol grip, a threaded barrel capable of accepting a barrel extender, flash suppressor, forward handgrip, or silencer, a shroud that was attached to, or partially or completely encircled, the barrel and that permitted the shooter to hold the firearm with the nontrigger hand without being burned, a manufactured weight of 50 ounces or more when the pistol was unloaded, and a semiautomatic version of an automatic firearm. In functional terms, a magazine seated outside the pistol grip sat forward of the grip, as on the named pistols; a barrel shroud covered the barrel so that the support hand could be placed forward without touching a hot barrel; the 50 ounce figure operated as a manufacturing weight threshold; and a semiautomatic version of an automatic firearm was a self-loading derivative of a design that also existed in automatic form.

The shotgun test in subsection (D) applied to a semiautomatic shotgun that had at least two of four features. Those features were a folding or telescoping stock, a pistol grip that protrudes conspicuously beneath the action of the weapon, a fixed magazine capacity in excess of 5 rounds, and an ability to accept a detachable magazine. Two of those features, the stock and the pistol grip, also appeared in the rifle test, with the same functional descriptions. The fixed magazine capacity feature and the detachable magazine feature were particular to the shotgun list. Because subsection (D) had no separate precondition, a shotgun met the test through the count alone, provided that the firearm was a semiautomatic shotgun and that at least two of the four listed features were present. The four routes therefore shared a common category and a common semiautomatic boundary while distributing their conditions differently across firearm types.

The deepest structural point of section 921(a)(30) concerned the criteria that the definition did not use. The definition sorted firearms by configuration. It did not turn on caliber, on muzzle energy, or on rate of fire. The named route expressly reached copies or duplicates in any caliber, and the feature tests stated no caliber threshold, no muzzle energy threshold, and no rate of fire threshold among their conditions. Every firearm in the category was semiautomatic, firing one shot for each pull of the trigger. Fully automatic firearms were regulated separately under the National Firearms Act and the 1986 prohibition, and the statute that carried this definition did not address them. The category in section 921(a)(30) was therefore a category built from names, copies, firearm types, a magazine capability condition in two of its tests, and counts of specified configuration features, assembled inside a term that the statute had defined for its own purposes.

## The Pistol and Shotgun Tests, the Exemptions, and the Compliance Path

The pistol test and the shotgun test stood beside the rifle test in section 921(a)(30), and each worked by the same general method with a different structure. Each test first identified a class of firearm by its operating type, and each then counted configuration features from a closed statutory list. The number that mattered was two. A firearm that fell within the opening words of a test and carried at least two of the features listed for that class entered the statutory category of semiautomatic assault weapon through that test. A firearm that fell within the opening words and carried one listed feature, or none, did not enter the category through that test. The opening words decided which list applied and, in the pistol test, imposed a further condition that had to be satisfied before any feature was counted at all. The shotgun test took a different form, and the difference was structural rather than incidental. Its list was shorter, it contained one feature that appeared in no other test, and it omitted the detachable magazine precondition that the rifle and pistol tests both carried at their front.

The pistol test, at subsection (C), covered a semiautomatic pistol that had an ability to accept a detachable magazine and had at least two of five listed features. Both halves of that sentence did work. The pistol had to be semiautomatic, a term that described a firearm firing one shot for each pull of the trigger. The pistol also had to have an ability to accept a detachable magazine. That ability was a precondition and not a counted feature. A pistol that lacked the ability took no step toward coverage under subsection (C), however its other characteristics were arranged, and a pistol that had the ability satisfied only the precondition, because coverage required two counted features in addition. The five counted features were an ammunition magazine that attached to the pistol outside of the pistol grip, a threaded barrel capable of accepting a barrel extender, flash suppressor, forward handgrip, or silencer, a shroud that met the functional description the statute itself supplied, a manufactured weight of 50 ounces or more when the pistol was unloaded, and a semiautomatic version of an automatic firearm.

The first counted feature concerned the place at which the ammunition magazine attached to the pistol. The statute counted an ammunition magazine that attached to the pistol outside of the pistol grip. The pistol grip was the grip held by the firing hand, and the magazine counted under this item when it was seated outside that grip. The definition thus turned on position rather than on magazine capacity, magazine shape, or the number of rounds the magazine held. A magazine seated outside the pistol grip sat forward of the grip, as on the pistols the statute named elsewhere in subsection (A). The feature identified a feeding arrangement in which the magazine occupied its own position on the firearm instead of the position within the grip that the statutory phrase excluded by contrast. The precision of the item also mattered in the other direction. The statute did not count a magazine merely for being detachable, because detachability belonged to the precondition, and it did not count a magazine merely for being large, because no capacity figure appeared in this item. Position was the whole of the counted feature.

The second counted feature was a threaded barrel capable of accepting a barrel extender, flash suppressor, forward handgrip, or silencer. A threaded barrel accepted screwed on muzzle devices, and the statute named four attachments in connection with that capability. The barrel extender was the first named attachment. The flash suppressor was the second, and a flash suppressor was a muzzle device that dispersed escaping gases to reduce visible flash. The forward handgrip was the third named attachment. The silencer was the fourth. The item counted the threaded barrel when the barrel was capable of accepting one of the named attachments, and the wording joined the threading to the capability rather than requiring proof that a particular attachment had been fitted at a particular moment. A pistol with a threaded barrel of the described capability received one count under this item, and the count was one whether the barrel was described through one named attachment or through another, because the statute placed the four attachments inside a single feature rather than treating each attachment as a separate feature. Four named possibilities did not produce four counts. They produced alternative ways in which the single threaded barrel feature could be present.

The third counted feature was the barrel shroud, and the statute described it in functional terms of its own. The counted item was a shroud that was attached to, or partially or completely encircled, the barrel and that permitted the shooter to hold the firearm with the nontrigger hand without being burned. The description had two parts, and both parts belonged to the feature. The first part addressed the relationship between the shroud and the barrel. The shroud had to be attached to the barrel, or it had to encircle the barrel partially or completely. The second part addressed the hold that the shroud made possible. It had to permit the shooter to hold the firearm with the nontrigger hand without being burned. A barrel shroud covered the barrel so that the support hand could be placed forward without touching a hot barrel, and that functional description matched the statutory language. The statute did not state this feature as a named part detached from its function. It stated the covering, the degree of encirclement in alternative terms, and the consequence for the position and safety of the hand that did not operate the trigger.

The fourth counted feature was weight. The statute counted a manufactured weight of 50 ounces or more when the pistol was unloaded. The figure was a manufacturing weight threshold, and it was the only feature in the pistol test expressed as a number. The measurement condition was written into the item. Weight was taken when the pistol was unloaded, so the number referred to the pistol in that specified condition rather than to the pistol together with ammunition carried in it. The threshold was inclusive at its stated point, because the words were 50 ounces or more. A manufactured weight below 50 ounces did not supply the count, and a manufactured weight at the stated figure or beyond it did. Its inclusion meant that a pistol could receive one of its two required counts from a characteristic that involved no added part and no positional arrangement, and a second count had to come from one of the other four items in the list.

The fifth counted feature was a semiautomatic version of an automatic firearm. A semiautomatic version of an automatic firearm was a self-loading derivative of a design that also existed in automatic form. The item looked backward from the pistol under examination to the design from which that pistol derived. The pistol itself had to be semiautomatic to fall within the opening words of subsection (C) at all, and every firearm in the statutory category was semiautomatic, firing one shot for each pull of the trigger. The fifth feature therefore did not describe a pistol that fired automatically. It described a pistol whose design had an automatic counterpart and that existed as the semiautomatic version of that design. Fully automatic firearms were regulated separately under the National Firearms Act and the 1986 prohibition, and the 1994 statute did not address them. That separation preserved the meaning of the fifth item. Derivation supplied the count, not automatic operation.

The shotgun test, at subsection (D), covered a semiautomatic shotgun that had at least two of four listed features. The four features were a folding or telescoping stock, a pistol grip that protruded conspicuously beneath the action of the weapon, a fixed magazine capacity in excess of 5 rounds, and an ability to accept a detachable magazine. A folding or telescoping stock folded or slid to shorten the overall length of the firearm or to adjust its fit. The pistol grip in the shotgun test carried the same verbal formula the rifle test used, a pistol grip that protruded conspicuously beneath the action of the weapon, and a pistol grip was a grip below the action held by the firing hand. The fixed magazine feature counted capacity, and the line was set at more than five rounds. The detachable magazine feature counted an ability, the ability to accept a detachable magazine. The list contained no weight threshold, no threaded barrel item, no barrel shroud item, and no item based on derivation from an automatic design. Items that counted for a pistol therefore did not transfer into the shotgun test by analogy. Coverage of a shotgun depended on the four items Congress placed in subsection (D), counted under the two feature rule that subsection stated.

The most consequential structural point in subsection (D) was the absence of a precondition. The rifle test and the pistol test each opened with an ability to accept a detachable magazine as a requirement that stood before the count began. The shotgun test contained no such opening requirement. For a shotgun, the ability to accept a detachable magazine was not the gate through which the firearm had to pass before features were counted. It was one of the four features inside the count. A semiautomatic shotgun could therefore satisfy subsection (D) through two features that had nothing to do with a detachable magazine, such as a folding or telescoping stock together with a pistol grip that protruded conspicuously beneath the action. It could also satisfy the test through a fixed magazine capacity in excess of five rounds together with one other listed feature, without any ability to accept a detachable magazine entering the calculation at all. The converse also followed from the structure. An ability to accept a detachable magazine, standing alone, supplied only one count for a shotgun, where the same ability for a rifle or a pistol supplied no count and instead satisfied a precondition. Identical hardware therefore occupied different legal positions under different subsections, and an accurate reading of the definition had to begin with the class of firearm before assigning any significance to a particular characteristic.

The fixed magazine item gave subsection (D) a feature that had no counterpart in the rifle or pistol lists. The statute counted a fixed magazine capacity in excess of 5 rounds. Capacity was expressed as a property of the fixed magazine, and the threshold was crossed only when capacity exceeded five rounds. A fixed magazine capacity of five rounds or fewer did not supply the count. Capacity beyond that line supplied one count, and one further feature from the list of four was required. The third item addressed a magazine fixed to the firearm and measured by capacity, while the fourth item addressed the ability to accept a detachable magazine and contained no capacity figure at all. No item counted more heavily than another, and no item satisfied the test by itself.

Around the tests stood a set of scope exclusions that kept defined classes of firearms outside the prohibition. Firearms manually operated by bolt, pump, lever, or slide action were outside the prohibition. Firearms rendered permanently inoperable were also outside the prohibition, and the adverb carried the weight of that exclusion, because the statute described a firearm rendered permanently inoperable rather than a firearm set aside or disabled for an interval. Antique firearms were outside the prohibition as well. The three exclusions operated at the level of scope. They identified kinds of firearms to which the prohibition did not extend, and they stood apart from the grandfather clause and from the exceptions that addressed particular transfers and possessors. A firearm within an excluded class did not need to pass through the feature counting exercise in order to remain outside the ban, because the exclusions removed the class before the prohibition applied.

The appendix added by section 110106 worked in a different way again. Section 110106 added an appendix to section 922 of title 18 listing firearms that were expressly not covered. The appendix was organized by category. Its rifle listings were divided among centerfire rifles in autoloading, lever and slide, and bolt action groups. Rimfire rifles formed a further category. Shotguns formed another category, and other firearms appeared in a remaining grouping. Within those categories, protection ran model by model, through names placed on the list. The named examples included the Ruger Mini-14 Autoloading Rifle without folding stock, the Ruger Mini Thirty Rifle, the Iver Johnson M-1 Carbine, the Marlin Model 9 Camp Carbine, the Marlin Model 45 Carbine, and the Browning BAR Mark II Safari rifles. The Ruger Mini-14 entry carried its qualification inside the listing itself, in the words without folding stock, so the express protection attached to the model in the form the appendix stated. The list did not proceed by describing features that a protected firearm could have or could lack in the abstract. It proceeded by category and by name, and its protection was expressed through inclusion.

The scale of the appendix became part of the public record of the bill before enactment. In a letter to Congress dated August 22, 1994, the President described the measure as carrying specific protection for more than 650 other weapons. That figure, more than 650 firearms listed by name, was the count attached to the appendix in the administration statement made during the final passage period. The figure most often used by the sponsors of the statute in later debate was 670, and Senator Feinstein commonly said 670 in later floor statements. The function of the appendix was narrower than its length might have suggested to a reader who treated it as the boundary of the definition. The appendix removed doubt for the listed models. A firearm could also fall outside the definition without being listed, and absence from the appendix established nothing by itself about coverage. The outer boundary of the category was drawn by section 921(a)(30), through the named route and the three feature tests. The appendix confirmed that listed models stood outside that boundary. It did not draw the boundary, and it did not convert an unlisted firearm into a covered one.

### Could a banned configuration be made lawful by changing it?

For a firearm covered only through a feature test, the answer was yes, provided the finished configuration held fewer than two counted features. For a firearm named in the statute, or a copy or duplicate of a named firearm, the answer was no, because feature removal left that separate route untouched in the definition.

The route to a lawful configuration under the feature tests followed directly from the arithmetic of the definition. Because the tests counted features, a manufacturer could lawfully produce a configuration of a covered type by keeping it below two counted features. A configuration with one counted feature stood below the line, and a configuration with none stood below the line as well, provided the firearm was not covered by name or as a copy or duplicate of a named firearm. Configurations of that kind were not a theoretical construction drawn from the text after the statute ended. They were manufactured and sold throughout the period in which the prohibition operated. The lawful product was the configuration as made, measured against the same list that defined coverage.

The changes recorded in the evaluation literature were concrete and limited. Removing the bayonet mount and fitting an unthreaded muzzle took two listed rifle characteristics out of a configuration. Fitting a fixed stock in place of a folding or telescoping stock removed another counted characteristic where the stock item had supplied a count. Fitting a thumbhole stock served a comparable function in the configurations the evaluation described. Each change corresponded to an item on a statutory list, which was why each change mattered within the definition. The compliance path thus required attention to the whole configuration rather than to any single part. The maker of a configuration had to account for every item on the applicable list, including items that were easy to overlook in a description focused on attachments, such as manufactured weight in the pistol test or fixed magazine capacity in the shotgun test.

The evaluation conducted by Jeffrey A. Roth and Christopher S. Koper for the National Institute of Justice recorded how the resulting legal substitutes were presented in the market. Legal substitutes were marketed under designations such as Sporter, AB, and PCR. The evaluation expanded AB as After Ban and PCR as Politically Correct Rifle. The same evaluation recorded that substitutes differed from banned copies by lacking counted features or by minimal design modifications, such as a thumbhole drilled in a rifle stock. The record of designations and modifications belonged to the history of administration and manufacture under the statute. It showed the feature test operating as a design boundary that makers could locate and observe, with the lawful configuration defined by what the count omitted. It did not convert the substitutes into a separate statutory class. No designation, whether Sporter, AB, or PCR, carried legal effect of its own. The legal effect came from the configuration measured against section 921(a)(30), and a designation attached to a configuration that in fact carried two counted features would not have removed that configuration from the definition.

Classification questions under chapter 44 were administered during this period by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms within the Department of the Treasury. The Bureau moved to the Department of Justice in January 2003 under the Homeland Security Act of 2002. The Bureau advised licensees that component parts lawfully possessed on or before September 13, 1994 were within the grandfather clause. Its Firearms Technology Branch issued classification determinations on request. The statute itself supplied the tests, the lists, and the threshold of two that any determination had to apply.

The precision limit closed the section, and it was absolute within its terms. Feature removal could never cure a firearm banned by name. The named route in subsection (A) covered the firearms known as the listed models, and copies or duplicates of those firearms in any caliber, regardless of features. A named firearm stripped of every feature that any test counted remained a named firearm. A copy or duplicate of a named firearm remained a copy or duplicate however its stock, barrel, grip, or magazine arrangement was configured. The compliance path existed only under the feature tests, where coverage depended on a count that a change in configuration could alter. Where coverage depended on identity, on the model a firearm was or the model it copied, a change in features changed nothing that the statute made decisive. The two routes into the definition therefore demanded two different questions of any firearm under examination. The first question asked what the firearm was, by name or by derivation from a named model. The second question, reached only when the first did not settle the matter, asked how the firearm was configured, under the list written for its class. The statute kept the routes separate, and the compliance path ran through the second route alone.

## The Magazine Provision as a Separate Prohibition

Congress placed the prohibition on large capacity ammunition feeding devices in a separate section of Subtitle A of Title XI of the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, Public Law 103-322. The subtitle bore the short title Public Safety and Recreational Firearms Use Protection Act, and its sections ran in sequence from section 110101 through section 110106. Section 110101 supplied the short title. Section 110102 restricted the manufacture, transfer, and possession of certain semiautomatic assault weapons. Section 110103, under its own heading, banned large capacity ammunition feeding devices. Section 110104 directed a study by the Attorney General. Section 110105 supplied the effective date. Section 110106 added Appendix A to section 922 of title 18. The placement of the feeding device ban in section 110103, between the firearm restriction in section 110102 and the study direction in section 110104, gave the device prohibition a statutory position of its own rather than treating it as a subordinate clause of the firearm prohibition.

That separate placement matched the structure of the amendments that the subtitle made to chapter 44 of title 18. The firearm prohibition rested on a definition at section 921(a)(30), which defined the statutory term semiautomatic assault weapon, and on a prohibition at section 922(v). The feeding device prohibition rested on a different definition, at section 921(a)(31), which defined the term large capacity ammunition feeding device, and on a different prohibition, at section 922(w). Each definition described a different object. Section 921(a)(30) described complete firearms, sorted by name and by configuration under tests for rifles, pistols, and shotguns. Section 921(a)(31) described a component that fed ammunition to a firearm, and it described that component by type, by date of manufacture, and by capacity. A reader who worked through the subtitle therefore encountered two prohibitions built in parallel, each with its own defined term, its own prohibitory subsection, its own grandfather clause, and its own set of exceptions, even though both prohibitions entered the statute on the same day and operated during the same period. The Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms administered chapter 44 within the Department of the Treasury during the period, before the Bureau moved to the Department of Justice in January 2003 under the Homeland Security Act of 2002.

The subtitle took effect on enactment. President Bill Clinton signed the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994 on September 13, 1994, and section 110105 made the subtitle effective on that date. The date mattered to the magazine provision in a way that exceeded an ordinary commencement rule, because the definition at section 921(a)(31) incorporated the date of enactment into the definition itself. A device entered or escaped the defined category in part because of when it had been manufactured in relation to September 13, 1994. The grandfather clause at section 922(w)(2) used the same dividing date, expressed with the words on or before. The marking sentence that section 110103 and its companion provisions added at section 923(i) used the same dividing point again for devices manufactured after enactment. The enactment date therefore performed three functions inside the device prohibition. It started the prohibition. It sorted devices by production date. It marked the line for the grandfather clause that preserved lawful possession and transfer of the devices that fell on the earlier side of the line.

### How did the magazine provision work?

The magazine provision worked in two steps. Section 921(a)(31) defined a large capacity ammunition feeding device by type, manufacture after enactment, and capacity of more than ten rounds, and section 922(w)(1) made transfer or possession of a device within that definition unlawful, subject to a grandfather clause and exceptions.

The first element of the definition concerned the kind of object. Section 921(a)(31) named a magazine, a belt, a drum, a feed strip, or a similar device. The list began with the magazine, the object named in the ordinary description of the provision and in the heading of section 110103. It then added three further named forms, a belt, a drum, and a feed strip, before closing with the general words or similar device. The drafting therefore did not confine coverage to a single physical form that carried the name magazine. It named four concrete forms and added a general category for a similar device, and it subjected every object that answered to one of those descriptions to the remaining elements of the definition. An object outside the named forms and outside the general category for a similar device did not enter the definition at all, whatever its capacity or date, because the definition required each element to be satisfied together rather than permitting any one element to substitute for another.

The second element concerned date of manufacture, and it carried more weight in the operation of the provision than any other single phrase. The definition reached only a device manufactured after the date of enactment of the 1994 act. A device manufactured on or before September 13, 1994 did not satisfy that element and therefore did not fall within the defined term, without regard to its capacity and without regard to when it was sold, transferred, or possessed. The statute drew the line at production rather than at sale. A transfer that occurred in 1996 or in 2002 did not change the manufacture date of the device transferred, and a possession that continued across the whole period did not change that date either. The defined category consisted of newly produced devices, identified by the timing of their manufacture, and the older stock of devices stood outside the category because the first sorting question concerned when each device had been made.

That drafting choice determined how the prohibition controlled new production even though the prohibitory subsection contained no verb for manufacture. Section 922(w)(1) made it unlawful to transfer or possess a large capacity ammunition feeding device as the statute defined that term. Because the definition admitted only a device manufactured after enactment, the act of manufacturing a new device after September 13, 1994 produced an object that no person outside the exceptions could then lawfully transfer or possess. Production after the date therefore led directly into the prohibition at the points of transfer and possession. The statute accomplished through the definition what a manufacture verb in subsection (w) would have addressed directly. Writers who described section 922(w) as itself banning manufacture misstated the text, and the distinction between the two subsections preserved the accuracy of any account. Section 110102 carried manufacture in its heading and in the scheme of section 922(v), while section 110103 and section 922(w) controlled manufacture at one remove, through a definition that dated every covered device to the period after enactment.

The contrast between subsection (v) and subsection (w) was precise and textual. Subsection (v) addressed semiautomatic assault weapons and the subtitle section that enacted it spoke of restriction on manufacture, transfer, and possession. Subsection (w)(1) spoke only of transfer and possession for feeding devices. It contained no manufacture verb. The omission was not a gap that left new production unregulated, because the definition at section 921(a)(31) supplied the manufacture element as a condition of coverage. Nor was the omission cured by reading a manufacture prohibition into subsection (w) by implication. The enacted text allocated the functions differently. The definition fixed the temporal boundary of the category, and the prohibition then attached legal consequences to two acts, transfer and possession, for objects inside that category. The grandfather clause and the exceptions in subsection (w) then qualified those consequences, and the marking requirement at section 923(i) addressed devices manufactured after enactment in terms that repeated the same temporal boundary.

The third element concerned capacity. The definition covered a device that had a capacity of, or that could be readily restored or converted to accept, more than ten rounds of ammunition. The threshold was expressed as more than ten rounds, so the capacity element turned on a count above ten rather than on a count of ten itself. The first branch of the element looked to the capacity that the device had. The second branch looked beyond the configuration in hand and reached a device that could be readily restored or converted to accept more than ten rounds. Restoration language addressed a device that had once held the larger capacity and could be returned to it. Conversion language addressed a device that could be changed to accept the larger number. Both branches used the same destination figure, more than ten rounds, and both branches therefore treated the potential capacity described in the text as sufficient to satisfy the element alongside an existing capacity above ten rounds.

The exclusion at the end of the definition removed an attached tubular device designed to accept, and capable of operating only with, .22 caliber rimfire ammunition. Each word of the exclusion narrowed it. The device had to be tubular in form. It had to be attached, rather than separate. It had to have been designed to accept .22 caliber rimfire ammunition, and it had to be capable of operating only with that ammunition. A tubular device that met the design condition but was capable of operating with another kind of ammunition did not answer to the only with language. A device that operated only with .22 caliber rimfire ammunition but was not an attached tubular device did not answer to the shape and attachment conditions. The statute placed the exclusion inside the definition itself, so an object described by the exclusion never entered the defined term and never reached the prohibition at section 922(w)(1). The exclusion differed in placement from the exceptions at section 922(w)(3), which presupposed a covered device and then lifted the prohibition for specified government, security, retirement, and testing circumstances.

The prohibition itself was short. Section 922(w)(1) made it unlawful for a person to transfer or possess a large capacity ammunition feeding device. Transfer covered the movement of a covered device from one person to another, and possession covered the holding of a covered device, and the subsection attached the prohibition to both acts without adding a manufacture act of its own. The breadth of the two verbs gave the prohibition its reach across the life of a post enactment device. A covered device manufactured after September 13, 1994 could not lawfully pass to another person outside the exceptions, and it could not lawfully be held outside the exceptions and outside the grandfather clause. The prohibition did not depend on the use of the device in any particular firearm, on the commission of any other offense, or on a finding that the device had been joined to a semiautomatic assault weapon. Its trigger was the definition, and its offenses were transfer and possession of an object that met that definition.

The grandfather clause at section 922(w)(2) provided that the prohibition did not apply to the transfer or possession of a large capacity ammunition feeding device otherwise lawfully possessed under Federal law on or before the date of enactment. The wording paralleled the firearm grandfather clause at section 922(v)(2), which used the date of enactment without the additional words on or before, and the device clause made the inclusion of the enactment date itself explicit. Grandfathered devices remained lawful to possess and lawful to transfer for the whole period during which the subtitle operated. The clause therefore preserved a continuing lawful market and a continuing lawful possession in the older stock at the same time that the definition excluded new post enactment devices from lawful transfer and possession. The two mechanisms overlapped in their protection of older devices. A device manufactured before enactment fell outside the definition because of the manufacture date element, and a device lawfully possessed on or before enactment also fell within the protection of the grandfather clause, and either route led outside the prohibition.

Because coverage turned on date of manufacture rather than on date of sale, devices manufactured abroad before September 13, 1994 remained lawful and importable throughout the period. The text drew no distinction in the manufacture date element between a device made in the United States and a device made in another country. A foreign made device produced before the dividing date failed the manufactured after element in the same way as a domestic device produced before that date, and importation after that date did not alter the date on which the device had been manufactured. The consequence followed from the drafting choice that anchored coverage to production. Had the statute anchored coverage to the date of importation or the date of first sale, the foreign stock manufactured before enactment would have stood in a different position. The enacted text anchored coverage to manufacture, and the grandfather clause separately protected devices lawfully possessed on or before enactment, and together those choices left the pre enactment foreign stock inside the lawful category for the duration of the subtitle.

The marking sentence for feeding devices reinforced the same date based sorting. Section 923(i) received two sentences from the subtitle. One sentence required that the serial number of a semiautomatic assault weapon manufactured after enactment clearly show the date of manufacture. The other sentence required that a large capacity ammunition feeding device manufactured after enactment be identified by a serial number that showed the device was manufactured or imported after the effective date, together with any other identification that the Secretary prescribed by regulation. The device sentence spoke of manufacture or importation after the effective date for purposes of the marking that a post enactment device had to bear. It applied to devices manufactured after enactment, the same class that the definition captured, and it gave that class a prescribed identifying mark under the authority of the Secretary. Both sentences added at section 923(i) were among the provisions repealed when the subtitle reached its sunset.

The exceptions at section 922(w)(3) lifted the prohibition for four described circumstances. The first covered manufacture for, transfer to, or possession by the United States or a State or their departments, agencies, or political subdivisions, and it included transfer to or possession by a law enforcement officer employed by such an entity for purposes of law enforcement, on duty or off duty. The second covered transfer to a licensee under title I of the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 for an on site physical protection system and security organization required by Federal law, together with possession by its employees or contractors for those purposes. The third covered possession by an individual retired from service with a law enforcement agency, where the individual was not otherwise prohibited from receiving a firearm, of an item transferred to the individual by the agency on retirement. The fourth covered manufacture, transfer, or possession by a licensed manufacturer or licensed importer for purposes of testing or experimentation authorized by the Secretary of the Treasury. Each exception named its holder and its purpose, and each operated within subsection (w) as an exception to the device prohibition rather than as an alteration of the definition at section 921(a)(31).

The magazine provision formed part of a statute that contained no registration requirement and no surrender or confiscation provision. Its prohibitions operated through the definition, through the bar on transfer and possession, through the grandfather clause and exceptions, and through the penalty cross reference, rather than through a registry of devices or a direction that covered devices be given up. Devices that fell outside the definition because of their manufacture date remained outside that machinery altogether. Devices that fell within an exception remained lawful in the hands and for the purposes that the exception described. Devices manufactured after enactment outside the exceptions were the objects to which the bar applied, and the bar applied to their transfer and to their possession as continuing acts during the period from September 13, 1994 to September 13, 2004. Section 110105 set the repeal of the subtitle and the amendments made by it to take effect ten years after the effective date, and the prohibitions therefore ended on September 13, 2004 under the term that the statute had written for itself.

Researchers who examined the subtitle disagreed about the relative consequence of its two prohibitions. Some assessments treated the prohibition on semiautomatic assault weapons as the more consequential of the pair, and other assessments treated the prohibition on large capacity ammunition feeding devices as the more consequential, and the disagreement persisted in the literature that evaluated the statute. The statutory text supplied the ground on which that disagreement rested. One prohibition governed complete firearms defined by name and configuration, subject to a grandfather clause for a stock that contemporary estimates placed at more than 1.5 million covered firearms in private hands at enactment. The other prohibition governed feeding devices defined by type, manufacture date, and a capacity above ten rounds, subject to its own grandfather clause and to the continuing lawful status of devices manufactured before enactment at home and abroad. No resolution of that disagreement followed from the text of section 110103 alone, and the text of section 110103 made no ranking between the prohibition it enacted and the prohibition that section 110102 enacted beside it.

## Exceptions, Markings, Penalties, and Administration

The prohibitions in sections 922(v) and 922(w) of title 18 were never written as unqualified commands. The same subtitle that made it unlawful to manufacture, transfer, or possess a semiautomatic assault weapon covered by its definition, and to transfer or possess a large capacity ammunition feeding device covered by its separate definition, also wrote limits into the text. Sections 922(v)(4) and 922(w)(3) set out the channels through which manufacture, transfer, and possession remained lawful. A burden provision at the end of each prohibition allocated proof when a person charged invoked the grandfather clause or an exception. Section 923(i) received two marking sentences tied to the date that decided coverage. Section 924 supplied the penalties. Administration sat with the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms, which administered chapter 44 within the Department of the Treasury during the period. The design was single: a date line at September 13, 1994, specified functions preserved beyond it, date evidence required on items manufactured after enactment, and defined consequences for defined conduct.

### What did the exceptions allow?

Four channels kept manufacture, transfer, or possession lawful despite the prohibitions: government and law enforcement use, nuclear site security under the Atomic Energy Act, possession by a retired officer of an item transferred on retirement, and authorized testing or experimentation by licensed manufacturers or importers.

The first channel was the governmental channel, and it was the widest of the four. Under sections 922(v)(4) and 922(w)(3), the prohibitions did not apply to manufacture for, transfer to, or possession by the United States or a State, or a department, agency, or political subdivision of either. The three verbs followed an item through its whole course, from manufacture for a governmental entity to possession by that entity. The text then carried the channel from the entity to the individual officer. The prohibitions did not apply to transfer to, or possession by, a law enforcement officer employed by such an entity for purposes of law enforcement, on or off duty. Possession tied to law enforcement purposes did not end when a shift ended, because the statute expressly covered the off duty case. The channel was defined by employment and by purpose: the officer was employed by the United States, a State, or one of their departments, agencies, or political subdivisions, and the transfer or possession was for purposes of law enforcement.

The second channel ran to the security of nuclear sites, and the statute built it out of a different body of law. The prohibitions did not apply to transfer to a licensee under title I of the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 for an on-site physical protection system and security organization required by Federal law. They also did not apply to possession by the employees or contractors of such a licensee for those purposes. Every element of that sentence narrowed the channel. The recipient was not any private security employer, but a licensee under a specified title of a specified statute. The purpose was not security in general, but an on-site physical protection system and a security organization required by Federal law. The possessors were the licensee's employees and contractors, acting for those purposes. In form, the channel resembled the governmental channel, because it preserved an armed protective function that Federal law itself required at specified sites. In drafting, it stood apart: Congress did not fold nuclear site security into the governmental exception, and it did not write a general exception for private possession.

The third channel was narrower than the first two, and it was personal rather than institutional. The prohibitions did not apply to possession by an individual retired from service with a law enforcement agency, where the item possessed was an item transferred to the individual by the agency on retirement, and where the individual was not otherwise prohibited from receiving a firearm. Three conditions had to coincide. The person had retired from service with a law enforcement agency. The agency itself had transferred the item to that person on retirement, so the item reached the retiree through the agency's own act at the point of retirement rather than through a later purchase from another source. And the retiree was not otherwise prohibited from receiving a firearm, a condition that kept the exception from overriding an independent disqualification. The verb in this channel also mattered. The first channel covered manufacture, transfer, and possession. The third channel, as written, covered possession. Its function was to let a retired officer keep an item the agency had handed over at retirement, rather than to create a route by which retired officers could acquire covered items in the market.

The fourth channel preserved a controlled space for work on the items themselves. The prohibitions did not apply to manufacture, transfer, or possession by a licensed manufacturer or a licensed importer for purposes of testing or experimentation authorized by the Secretary of the Treasury. Licensure was the first condition, purpose the second, and authorization the third: the testing or experimentation was authorized by the Secretary of the Treasury. The channel therefore did not rest on a manufacturer's own judgment that experimental work was under way, and it did not open ordinary commercial production under an experimental label. Testing and experimentation were excepted as activities, not as a status held by the licensee. The placement of that authorization in the Treasury Department matched the administrative geography of the statute during most of its life, because the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms sat within that department and administered chapter 44 there until January 2003.

Set side by side, the four channels allocated the excepted activity by holder, by purpose, and by act. The governmental channel covered all three acts and ran to governments, their subdivisions, and their officers acting for law enforcement purposes. The nuclear channel covered a transfer to a defined licensee and possession by that licensee's employees and contractors for a required protective system. The retirement channel covered possession alone, by one defined individual, of one defined item, received from the agency at retirement. The testing channel covered all three acts again, but only by licensed manufacturers and importers, only for testing or experimentation, and only under authorization from the Secretary of the Treasury. None of the four channels was framed as a partial reduction of the prohibitions. Each was a statement that the prohibitions did not apply to the conduct described, and each described that conduct with a precision that let a reader test a given transaction or a given holding against its terms.

The burden provision gave those texts their procedural weight. At the end of section 922(v) and at the end of section 922(w), the subtitle placed a matching allocation of proof. Where a person charged asserted that the grandfather clause applied, or that an exception applied, the Government bore the burden of proof that the prohibition applied to that person. The grandfather clause in section 922(v)(2) excluded transfer or possession of a semiautomatic assault weapon otherwise lawfully possessed under Federal law on the date of enactment of the subsection, and the parallel clause in section 922(w)(2) excluded transfer or possession of a feeding device lawfully possessed on or before that date. An exception under sections 922(v)(4) or 922(w)(3) excluded the conduct described in the four channels. A charged person who invoked either route put the Government to its proof. The provision fitted the structure around it, because the statute created no registration requirement and no surrender or confiscation provision, and grandfathered items remained lawful to possess and to transfer for the whole period. In a system without a registry of that stock, a prosecution turned on whether the particular item and the particular defendant fell inside the prohibition.

The marking sentences in section 923(i) addressed the same date question at the factory rather than in the courtroom. The subtitle added two sentences to that section. The first concerned the firearm: the serial number of any semiautomatic assault weapon manufactured after enactment had to clearly show the date of manufacture, within the serial identification of the weapon itself rather than in records held elsewhere. The second concerned the feeding device: a large capacity ammunition feeding device manufactured after enactment had to be identified by a serial number showing that it was manufactured or imported after the effective date, plus other identification that the Secretary prescribed by regulation. The difference between the sentences tracked the two definitions. The feeding device definition itself contained the manufactured after element, defining a covered device as one manufactured after the date of enactment of the 1994 act with the specified capacity. A device manufactured abroad before September 13, 1994 therefore fell outside the definition and remained lawful and importable, a textual consequence of coverage turning on the date of manufacture rather than on the date of sale.

Markings and burden worked as a pair. A prosecution under section 922(v) or section 922(w) turned on dates and on categories: the date an item was made, the category its configuration placed it in, and the channel, if any, through which it had moved. The marking sentences required items manufactured after enactment to carry date information on the item itself, in the serial number of a firearm and in the serial identification of a feeding device. The grandfather clause protected the older stock, and contemporary estimates put that stock of covered firearms at more than 1.5 million in private hands when the statute took effect, with no official census behind the figure. When a charged person asserted that an item belonged to that lawful stock, or that an exception covered the conduct, the Government bore the burden of proving that the prohibition applied to that person. Both sentences were among the provisions repealed at the sunset, effective September 13, 2004, ten years after the effective date.

The penalty structure had two tiers, and they attached to different conduct. The first tier punished a violation of the prohibitions themselves. A knowing violation of subsection (v) or subsection (w) was punishable under section 924(a)(1)(B) by fine and by imprisonment of up to five years. The knowledge element was part of the offense as the fact of punishment was stated: the provision reached a person who knowingly violated the ban on manufacture, transfer, or possession of a covered firearm, or the ban on transfer or possession of a covered feeding device. The Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit described the exposure in those same terms in Navegar, Inc. v. United States, 103 F.3d 994 (D.C. Cir. 1997), where a manufacturer named in the statute challenged the prohibitions and the court resolved the case without invalidating them. In that litigation the court described violators as subject to fines and prison sentences of up to five years.

The second tier did not punish a violation of the prohibitions at all. Section 110102 also amended section 924(c)(1)(B)(i) so that using or carrying a semiautomatic assault weapon during a crime of violence or a drug trafficking crime carried a ten year term as enacted. The conduct described there was compound: a person used or carried the covered firearm during a separate qualifying offense. The term attached through the cross reference written into section 924(c), a provision concerned with firearms used in relation to other crimes rather than with the transactions sections 922(v) and 922(w) governed. One wrong was defiance of the prohibitions on making, moving, or holding covered items, graded at a fine and up to five years. The other was deployment of a covered firearm in the course of a further offense, graded at ten years. The later history of the ten year cross reference had a complication stated in limited terms. After the Supreme Court's 1995 decision in Bailey and Congress's 1998 rewrite of section 924(c), the later fate of that cross reference was examined in a 2009 opinion of the Office of Legal Counsel. Beyond that statement of the examination and its setting, no bottom line on the cross reference belongs in an account confined to the provisions as enacted.

Administration determined how the written categories met particular firearms, particular parts, and particular imports. During the statute's life, the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco and Firearms administered chapter 44 of title 18 within the Department of the Treasury. That placement explained several features of the text already described. The Secretary whose authorization could open the testing and experimentation channel was the Secretary of the Treasury. The Secretary who could prescribe additional identification for feeding devices by regulation acted within the same departmental structure. In January 2003, under the Homeland Security Act of 2002, the bureau moved to the Department of Justice. The move fell late in the statute's ten year span, which ran from September 13, 1994 to September 13, 2004, so administration of the prohibitions crossed a departmental boundary without any change in the prohibitions themselves. The categories, the exceptions, the markings, and the penalties remained the text Congress had enacted in 1994. What changed in January 2003 was the department inside which the administering bureau sat during the final portion of the period.

One piece of administrative guidance addressed the boundary of the grandfather clause in terms of parts. The bureau advised licensees that component parts lawfully possessed on or before September 13, 1994 were within the grandfather clause. The advice mattered because manufacture and assembly deal in parts as well as in complete firearms, and a date based exemption raised an immediate question about the status of a stock of parts held on the critical date. The guidance answered that question by treating component parts lawfully possessed on or before the date as the clause treated a complete firearm lawfully possessed on that date. Guidance of that kind did not amend the text, and its authority came from the bureau's administration of chapter 44. It told the regulated trade how the administering agency understood the clause to apply to a recurring practical case, in advance of any dispute about a particular holding.

A second administrative function addressed classification. The bureau's Firearms Technology Branch issued classification determinations on request. A determination placed a particular item in relation to the statute's categories, in a setting where coverage could turn on a name, on a copy or duplicate of a named firearm, or on the count of features under the tests for a rifle, a pistol, or a shotgun. The definition sorted by configuration, and configuration questions were questions about a specific object with specific features in a specific arrangement. A manufacturer or importer planning production, or a licensee planning a transfer, could ask the branch to determine how the statute's definitions applied to a described item, and the branch issued determinations in response to such requests. The text supplied the named list and the feature counts, and the branch's determinations applied that definitional apparatus to individual items, one request at a time.

Import administration formed a third track, older than the statute and distinct from it. In March and April 1989, five years before the subtitle was enacted, the bureau had suspended the importation of semiautomatic rifles it considered not suitable for sporting purposes under section 925(d)(3), and a working group report of July 6, 1989 made the exclusion permanent for the listed types. That action had reached foreign rifles only and had left domestic manufacture untouched, an asymmetry that framed the congressional debate from which the 1994 statute emerged. Import administration continued on that separate statutory footing while the 1994 prohibitions operated. In April 1998, the Department of the Treasury barred 56 further modified rifle models from import. The objects of that action were modified rifle models presented for import, and the action was taken within the import system rather than within sections 922(v) and 922(w). Keeping the tracks separate matters for reading the period correctly, because a rifle could be barred from import under one authority while the domestic prohibitions turned on the different tests Congress had written in 1994.

The four subjects answered four questions that a prohibition alone could not answer. The exceptions answered the question of preservation: which governmental, protective, retirement, and experimental functions continued, and on what conditions. The markings answered the question of identification: how an item manufactured after enactment was to show the fact that placed it on the covered side of the date line. The penalties answered the question of consequence, grading a knowing violation at a fine and up to five years under section 924(a)(1)(B), and the use or carrying of a covered firearm during a crime of violence or a drug trafficking crime at ten years under section 924(c)(1)(B)(i). The burden provision cut across those subjects by assigning proof to the Government when a charged person asserted the grandfather clause or an exception. Administration answered the question of application, through a bureau in the Treasury Department for most of the period and in the Department of Justice from January 2003, advising licensees on component parts, issuing classification determinations through its Firearms Technology Branch, and barring 56 further modified rifle models from import in April 1998 under separate import authority. Each answer ended with the prohibition it served on September 13, 2004, under the sunset term placed in the statute at enactment.

## The Sunset, the Extension Fight, and What Remained

The ending of the Public Safety and Recreational Firearms Use Protection Act was written into the statute on the day the statute began. Title XI of the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, Public Law 103-322, carried the prohibition in Subtitle A, and the same subtitle carried the provision that terminated the prohibition. Section 110105 performed two functions in a single section. Its first paragraph fixed the effective date, and its second paragraph fixed the repeal date. Between those two dates lay a decade in which the definitions at sections 921(a)(30) and 921(a)(31) of title 18, the prohibitions at sections 922(v) and 922(w), the marking sentences at section 923(i), and the penalty cross references at sections 924(a)(1)(B) and 924(c)(1)(B)(i) operated as federal law, and after the second date those provisions were former provisions, removed from the code by the operation of the clause that had created the timetable. The design of the prohibition, the record of the courts during the decade, the congressional fight over extension in 2004, and the body of state law that surrounded the federal statute each took shape against that deadline. The only open question throughout the decade was whether Congress would enact a new law to displace the scheduled ending, and Congress enacted no such law.

### What happened when the ten years ran out?

The subtitle reached its own expiration date on September 13, 2004. Section 110105 had provided that the subtitle and the amendments made by it were repealed effective ten years after enactment, and that provision operated without a further vote, ending the federal prohibitions on that date.

The mechanics of section 110105 deserve statement at full precision because a recurring description of the ending misstated them. Congress never voted to repeal the ban, and Congress never voted an extension into law. The termination was self executing, although the sunset clause itself was drafted in the language of repeal. The distinction was not a verbal nicety. A repeal in the ordinary legislative sense would have required a bill introduced in 2004, debated in committee and on the floor, passed by both chambers, and presented to the President, and no such measure passed. An extension would have required the same sequence in the opposite direction, and no such measure passed either. What occurred on September 13, 2004 required no action in that year at all. The repeal had been legislated in 1994, at the moment of enactment, with its effective date set ten years forward. The subtitle and the amendments made by it were, in the words of section 110105(2), repealed effective on the date that was ten years after the effective date fixed by section 110105(1). When that date arrived, the definitions, prohibitions, marking requirements, and cross references that the subtitle had inserted into chapter 44 of title 18 ceased to operate because the instrument that had inserted them had withdrawn them on a schedule fixed in advance. Accounts that described the ending as a congressional repeal compressed two distinct events, the enactment of a self repealing statute in 1994 and the arrival of its stated term in 2004, into a single decision that no chamber took in 2004.

The prospective character of the prohibition shaped everything that the sunset left behind. Section 922(v) addressed the manufacture, transfer, and possession of semiautomatic assault weapons, and section 922(w) addressed the transfer and possession of large capacity ammunition feeding devices, but neither prohibition reached backward to the stock that had been lawfully held when the statute took effect. Section 922(v)(2) provided that the prohibition did not apply to the transfer or possession of any semiautomatic assault weapon otherwise lawfully possessed under federal law on the date of enactment of the subsection. Section 922(w)(2) supplied the parallel clause for feeding devices, worded to cover a device lawfully possessed on or before that date. Grandfathered items therefore remained lawful to possess and lawful to transfer for the whole period from 1994 to 2004. Possession of a covered firearm was unlawful only where the firearm had been manufactured after the grandfather date and did not fall within an exception, and possession as such never became unlawful for the grandfathered stock. The statute contained no registration requirement and no surrender or confiscation provision. Contemporary estimates put the grandfathered stock of covered firearms at more than 1.5 million in private hands when the statute took effect, a figure used in contemporary reporting that drew on the evaluation literature, and no official census existed against which that estimate could be checked. A prohibition drawn on those terms reached new manufacture and new transfer into the covered category while the lawfully held stock of the same category, estimated at more than 1.5 million firearms, remained in circulation under the grandfather clauses, its possession and transfer lawful throughout the period.

The boundaries around the prohibition reinforced the same prospective character. The statute also left outside the prohibition firearms manually operated by bolt, pump, lever, or slide action, firearms rendered permanently inoperable, and antique firearms. Sections 922(v)(4) and 922(w)(3) carved out manufacture for, transfer to, or possession by the United States or a state or their departments, agencies, or political subdivisions, including transfer to or possession by a law enforcement officer employed by such an entity for purposes of law enforcement, on or off duty. The exceptions covered transfer to a licensee under title I of the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 for an on site physical protection system and security organization required by federal law, and possession by its employees or contractors for those purposes. The exceptions covered possession by an individual retired from service with a law enforcement agency, not otherwise prohibited from receiving a firearm, of an item transferred to the individual by the agency on retirement. The exceptions also covered manufacture, transfer, or possession by a licensed manufacturer or licensed importer for purposes of testing or experimentation authorized by the Secretary of the Treasury. In any prosecution, where a person charged asserted that the grandfather clause or an exception applied, the Government bore the burden of proof that the prohibition applied to that person. The grandfather clause and the exceptions together meant that the category the statute prohibited and the category of firearms in lawful circulation overlapped for the entire decade, and that overlap was a feature of the enacted text rather than an accident of enforcement. When the sunset arrived, therefore, the event that occurred was the removal of the federal prohibition on new manufacture and transfer, not the legalization of a stock that had been unlawful, because the grandfathered stock had been lawful at every point from enactment to expiration.

The configuration based structure of the definition produced a further body of lawful production during the decade that the sunset also left undisturbed. Because the rifle, pistol, and shotgun tests in sections 921(a)(30)(B), (C), and (D) counted features, a manufacturer could lawfully produce a configuration of a covered type by keeping the configuration below two counted features, through changes such as removing the bayonet mount, fitting an unthreaded muzzle, or fitting a fixed or thumbhole stock. Such configurations were manufactured and sold throughout the period. The evaluation prepared for the National Institute of Justice by Jeffrey A. Roth and Christopher S. Koper recorded that legal substitutes were marketed under designations such as Sporter, AB, for After Ban, and PCR, for Politically Correct Rifle, and that substitutes differed from banned copies by lacking counted features or by minimal design modifications such as a thumbhole drilled in a rifle stock. The compliance path had a stated limit in the text. Feature removal could not cure a firearm banned by name, because the named route in section 921(a)(30)(A) covered the listed models and their copies or duplicates regardless of features, and the compliance path existed only under the feature tests. At the sunset, neither the grandfathered stock nor the feature compliant configurations required any change in legal status, because neither had depended on the prohibition for its lawfulness.

The marking and penalty provisions showed the same pattern of a complete statutory apparatus built for a fixed term. Section 110102 added two sentences to section 923(i) under which the serial number of any semiautomatic assault weapon manufactured after enactment had to clearly show the date of manufacture, and a large capacity ammunition feeding device manufactured after enactment had to be identified by a serial number showing that the device was manufactured or imported after the effective date, together with other identification the Secretary prescribed by regulation. Both sentences were among the provisions repealed at the sunset. A knowing violation of section 922(v) or section 922(w) was punishable under section 924(a)(1)(B) by a fine and imprisonment of up to five years. Section 110102 also amended section 924(c)(1)(B)(i) so that using or carrying a semiautomatic assault weapon during a crime of violence or drug trafficking crime carried a ten year term as enacted, and the later fate of that cross reference, after the Supreme Court decided Bailey in 1995 and Congress rewrote section 924(c) in 1998, was examined in a 2009 opinion of the Office of Legal Counsel. Each element of that apparatus, the date markings that separated grandfathered items from post enactment items and the penalty cross references that gave the prohibitions their criminal sanction, existed to administer a prohibition whose own text fixed the date on which the apparatus would be withdrawn.

Congress accompanied the prohibition with a study mandate in section 110104, and the mandate is the whole of what the statute ordered on the question of effects. Section 110104 directed the Attorney General to study the effect of the subtitle on violent and drug trafficking crime over an eighteen month period beginning twelve months after enactment, and to report within thirty months after enactment. Measured from enactment on September 13, 1994, the study period therefore began in September 1995, ran for eighteen months, and the report fell due by March 13, 1997. The National Institute of Justice funded the evaluation, which was conducted at the Urban Institute by Jeffrey A. Roth and Christopher S. Koper, whose evaluation appeared in 1997 with updates issued after that year. The mandate specified the officer charged with the study, the subject of the study, the length of the study period, the point in the life of the statute at which the period began, and the deadline for the report, and it specified nothing else. The timetable itself reflected the prospective design described in the grandfather clauses. The placement of that window inside the first thirty months left the study as an early term measurement, taken while the grandfather clauses governed a stock that had been lawfully possessed at enactment and remained transferable under sections 922(v)(2) and 922(w)(2). The statute ordered the measurement, funded the measurement through the National Institute of Justice, and left the remainder of the decade to run without a further statutory reporting requirement of the same kind.

The courts considered the prohibitions during the decade and left both of them intact. Challenges were filed promptly after enactment. In Navegar, Inc. v. United States, 103 F.3d 994, decided by the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit in 1997, a manufacturer named in the statute challenged the prohibitions, and the District of Columbia Circuit resolved the case without invalidating them. The court in Navegar described violators of the prohibitions as subject to fines and prison sentences of up to five years, a description that matched the penalty that section 924(a)(1)(B) attached to a knowing violation. In San Diego County Gun Rights Committee v. Reno, 926 F. Supp. 1415, a district court in the Southern District of California in 1995 dismissed an early facial challenge without reaching the merits, and the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit affirmed that dismissal in 1996, at 98 F.3d 1121, on standing and ripeness grounds. Standing and ripeness are threshold doctrines that concern whether a plaintiff may bring a claim and whether a claim has matured enough for decision, and a dismissal on those grounds disposed of the case without a ruling on whether the statute was valid. The combined record across the decade was therefore a narrow one. No court struck down either prohibition, the prohibition on semiautomatic assault weapons in section 922(v) or the prohibition on large capacity ammunition feeding devices in section 922(w). The Supreme Court decided no case on the statute during its life. The prohibitions ended in 2004 with their validity unadjudicated by the Supreme Court and undisturbed by any lower court judgment of invalidation, because the challenges that reached decision ended either in resolution without invalidation, as in Navegar, or in dismissal on threshold grounds, as in the San Diego County litigation.

The fight over extension took place in the final eighteen months of the decade and ended without a law. Bills to extend the ban or to make the ban permanent were introduced from 2003 onward, among them S. 1034, which would have struck the sunset date from the statute. Striking the date was the direct legislative route to permanence, since the repeal in section 110105(2) operated by reference to that date, and a statute that removed the date before September 13, 2004 would have removed the mechanism by which the prohibitions were scheduled to end. No such bill passed. The single occasion on which an extension cleared a chamber vote came on March 2, 2004, when the Senate considered S. 1805, a bill concerning firearms industry liability. Senator Feinstein offered an amendment to that bill that would have extended the ban for ten years, and Senator John Warner of Virginia co sponsored the amendment. The Senate adopted the amendment by 52 to 47. The Senate then rejected the amended bill itself. The sequence defeated the extension as completely as a vote against the amendment would have done, but by a different route. The majority that adopted the extension amendment did not carry the underlying bill to passage, and an amendment adopted to a bill that the chamber then rejected had no legal effect, because an amendment becomes law only as part of an enacted bill. President George W. Bush had stated that he would sign an extension if Congress passed one, and that statement placed the executive position on the record during the same period. The statement changed nothing in the legislative arithmetic, because a presidential willingness to sign operates only on a bill that both chambers have passed. The House of Representatives took no vote on an extension before September 13, 2004. With no extension enacted, with the industry liability bill rejected in amended form in the Senate, and with no House vote held, the date in section 110105(2) arrived with the statutory text unaltered, and the repeal that the 1994 Congress had scheduled operated on the terms that the 1994 text had fixed.

State law formed the residue that the federal sunset did not touch, because the sunset repealed a federal subtitle and left every state statute on its own footing. Three states had banned covered firearms before Congress acted at all. California enacted the Roberti-Roos Assault Weapons Control Act in 1989, New Jersey acted in 1990, and Connecticut acted in 1993. Those three enactments supplied part of the background against which the federal debate of 1993 and 1994 proceeded, and each of them rested on state authority that the federal sunset neither granted nor withdrew. Four further states enacted provisions before the federal statute ended in 2004. Hawaii, Maryland, Massachusetts, and New York each had such provisions in place during the federal period. The District of Columbia also prohibited the covered firearms. The inventory maintained by the Law Center to Prevent Gun Violence counted the result as seven states and the District of Columbia with such laws in the years after 2004. The scope of those laws varied. Hawaii enacted a law that reached pistols rather than rifles, a scope narrower in one direction than the federal definition, which had covered rifles, pistols, and shotguns under separate tests. The federal sunset therefore changed the law of the United States Code and changed nothing in the codes of California, New Jersey, Connecticut, Hawaii, Maryland, Massachusetts, or New York, and nothing in the law of the District of Columbia. A firearm configuration that had been regulated at both levels between 1994 and 2004 was regulated after September 13, 2004 at the state level alone in those jurisdictions, under statutes whose terms, definitions, and exceptions were creatures of state law rather than copies of sections 921(a)(30) and 921(a)(31). Later decisions concerning state and District laws included Heller v. District of Columbia in the District of Columbia Circuit in 2011 and Kolbe v. Hogan in the Fourth Circuit, sitting en banc, in 2017.

After 2004, bills to reinstate a federal prohibition were introduced in later Congresses, and the proposals followed the ordinary course of introduced legislation without passage. One full chamber test of reinstatement occurred in the period that followed the sunset. On April 17, 2013, the Senate rejected a reinstatement amendment by 40 to 60. The tally recorded a chamber in which the proposal drew forty votes in favor and sixty against, short of the majority required for adoption, and the amendment failed on that vote. No reinstatement statute followed from that Congress. That vote stood as the last full chamber vote on the question in the record through May 2021, separated from the sunset itself by nearly nine years and from the March 2, 2004 extension amendment vote by a different posture, since the 2004 vote had adopted an extension that died with its underlying bill and the 2013 vote rejected reinstatement outright at the amendment stage. Between the two votes lay the whole aftermath of the federal statute. The prohibitions had operated from September 13, 1994 to September 13, 2004. The study that section 110104 ordered had been funded and conducted on the timetable the statute set. The challenges in Navegar and in the San Diego County litigation had closed without invalidation. The grandfathered stock had passed through the decade under continuous lawful status and passed out of the decade under the same status, unaffected in its lawfulness by either the enactment or the expiration. The state statutes that predated the federal act, and those enacted alongside it, continued on state authority. The federal definition at section 921(a)(30), with its named firearms, its copies and duplicates, and its three feature tests, and the feeding device definition at section 921(a)(31), with its threshold of more than ten rounds and its exclusion for an attached tubular device designed to accept only .22 caliber rimfire ammunition, survived the sunset only as former provisions, available in the Statutes at Large at 108 Stat. 1796 and in the litigation and evaluation records of the decade, and no longer operative as prohibitions in chapter 44 of title 18.

## The Statute as a Whole

Read as a single instrument, the subtitle enacted in 1994 made one central choice and then built every other provision around that choice. Congress defined a category by configuration. It named nineteen firearms in the conventional count and their copies or duplicates, and for all other firearms it counted features under tests written separately for rifles, pistols, and shotguns. Definition by configuration was the choice around which the rest of the text was built, joining a named list in the manner of the California statute to a feature test in the manner of the 1989 import determination. A pure list would have left unnamed firearms with the described configuration outside the prohibition. The combined method gave supporters a rule that reached domestic manufacture and gave critics a text with stated limits: a threshold magazine condition for rifles and pistols, a count of two features, a shotgun test without that threshold condition, and an appendix naming more than 650 firearms as expressly not covered, in the statute that carried it, the [Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994](/2017/12/01/violent-crime-control-act-1994-guide/).

The second organizing fact was prospectivity. The prohibition governed manufacture, transfer, and possession of covered firearms made after enactment, and transfer and possession of covered feeding devices manufactured after enactment, while the grandfather clauses in sections 922(v)(2) and 922(w)(2) preserved lawful possession and transfer of the stock held on or before September 13, 1994. A prospective ban on a durable good operates in that divided way by definition: the legal line falls between an existing stock that retains lawful status and a new supply that the prohibition bars. Contemporary estimates placed the grandfathered firearm stock at more than 1.5 million. No registration requirement and no surrender provision disturbed that stock. Exceptions for government and law enforcement use, nuclear site security, retired officers holding an agency transfer, and authorized testing preserved defined functions inside the same design. Compliance through feature changes belonged to the same design, available under the feature tests and unavailable for a firearm banned by name.

The third organizing fact was the term. Section 110105 set the effective date and the repeal date in one section, ten years apart. The subtitle therefore ended on September 13, 2004 by operation of its own text. Congress voted on no repeal, and Congress enacted no extension, although the Senate adopted an extension amendment on March 2, 2004 by 52 to 47 and then rejected the amended bill that carried it, and President George W. Bush stated that he would sign an extension if Congress passed one. Describing that ending as a congressional repeal misstates the record; the correction of the [repeal claim](/2021/08/15/federal-gun-law-myths/) rests on the distinction between a vote taken in 2004 and a repeal date legislated in 1994. The framework the subtitle amended, the [Gun Control Act framework in chapter 44 of title 18](/2021/04/01/gun-control-act-1968-complete-guide/), remained after the former definitions, prohibitions, markings, and penalty references were removed at the sunset, and state statutes that predated or accompanied the federal measure continued on state authority under the [state statutes](/2021/08/01/federal-vs-state-firearms-law/) that the federal sunset neither granted nor withdrew.

The statute ordered a study by the Attorney General in section 110104, funded through the National Institute of Justice and conducted at the Urban Institute by Jeffrey A. Roth and Christopher S. Koper, and the evaluators reported limited measurable effects within the study window and attributed the difficulty in part to the size of the grandfathered stock. A fuller account of the [evidence on effects](/2021/07/15/assault-weapons-ban-outcomes/) requires a separate assessment of the evaluation literature and the record after 1994. A student who holds the three organizing facts together holds the statute whole: a definition by name and counted configuration, a prohibition aimed at new manufacture and transfer while a grandfathered stock circulated under lawful status, and a self executing ten year term that ended the prohibition on a date fixed at enactment; to organize that record, [keep your statute notes, citations, and case chronologies together free on VaultBook](https://vaultbook.net/tools/legislation-study-notebook.html).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What did the assault weapons ban actually prohibit?

Section 922(v) made manufacture, transfer, and possession of a semiautomatic assault weapon unlawful, subject to a grandfather clause and exceptions. Section 922(w) made transfer or possession of a large capacity ammunition feeding device unlawful on parallel terms. The covered firearms were those named in section 921(a)(30)(A), copies or duplicates of named firearms, and rifles, pistols, and shotguns that satisfied a feature test. Covered devices were devices manufactured after September 13, 1994 with a capacity of more than 10 rounds. Possession of grandfathered firearms and devices remained lawful, and transfer of grandfathered items remained lawful for the whole period from 1994 to 2004.

### Q: What was the two feature test in the assault weapons ban?

For rifles and pistols, the test required a semiautomatic firearm with an ability to accept a detachable magazine and at least two features from the list written for that type. The rifle list contained a folding or telescoping stock, a conspicuous pistol grip, a bayonet mount, a flash suppressor or a threaded barrel designed for a flash suppressor, and a grenade launcher. One counted feature placed a firearm below the line, and two counted features placed a firearm inside the definition, provided the magazine precondition was met. The shotgun test also used a count of two, from a list of four features, without a separate magazine precondition.

### Q: Were existing guns grandfathered under the assault weapons ban?

Section 922(v)(2) provided that the prohibition did not apply to transfer or possession of any semiautomatic assault weapon otherwise lawfully possessed under federal law on the date of enactment. Section 922(w)(2) gave parallel protection to feeding devices lawfully possessed on or before that date. Grandfathered items remained lawful to possess and lawful to transfer for the whole period. Contemporary estimates placed the grandfathered stock of covered firearms at more than 1.5 million in private hands when the statute took effect, and no official census existed. The statute contained no registration requirement and no surrender or confiscation provision, and possession never became unlawful for the grandfathered stock as such.

### Q: Did the assault weapons ban limit magazine capacity?

The statute defined a large capacity ammunition feeding device in section 921(a)(31) as a magazine, belt, drum, feed strip, or similar device manufactured after enactment that had a capacity of, or could be readily restored or converted to accept, more than 10 rounds of ammunition. Section 922(w)(1) made transfer or possession of such a device unlawful, subject to a grandfather clause and exceptions. The definition excluded an attached tubular device designed to accept, and capable of operating only with, .22 caliber rimfire ammunition. Devices manufactured on or before September 13, 1994 fell outside the definition, and devices manufactured abroad before that date remained lawful and importable throughout the period.

### Q: How close was the vote on the assault weapons ban?

The closest vote in the passage record was the House vote on the freestanding bill, H.R. 4296, on May 5, 1994, which passed by 216 to 214, a margin of two votes. Congressional Quarterly recorded that the tally stood against passage in the closing minutes and turned when Representative Andrew Jacobs Jr. of Indiana reversed his vote. That two vote margin belonged to the freestanding House bill and not to the later crime bill votes. The Senate adopted the Feinstein amendment on November 17, 1993 by 56 to 43, the House agreed to the conference report on August 21, 1994 by 235 to 195, and the Senate agreed on August 25, 1994 by 61 to 38.

### Q: Did the assault weapons ban expire or get repealed?

The ban ended by expiration under a sunset clause written at enactment. Section 110105 provided that the subtitle and the amendments made by it were repealed effective on the date ten years after the effective date of September 13, 1994. The prohibitions therefore ended on September 13, 2004 by operation of that prewritten term. Congress voted on no repeal of the ban, and Congress enacted no extension into law. Bills to extend the ban were introduced from 2003, the Senate adopted an extension amendment on March 2, 2004 by 52 to 47 and then rejected the amended bill, and the House held no vote on an extension before the sunset date arrived.

### Q: How many firearms were exempted by name in the assault weapons ban?

Section 110106 added Appendix A to section 922 of title 18, listing firearms expressly not covered, organized by category. The President, in a letter to Congress dated August 22, 1994, described specific protection for more than 650 other weapons. Senator Feinstein in later floor statements commonly used the figure 670. The count stood at more than 650 firearms listed by name, with 670 as the figure most often used by the sponsors in later debate. Named examples in the appendix included the Ruger Mini-14 Autoloading Rifle without folding stock, the Ruger Mini Thirty Rifle, the Iver Johnson M-1 Carbine, and the Marlin Model 9 Camp Carbine. A firearm could also fall outside the definition without being listed.

### Q: Could manufacturers comply with the assault weapons ban by changing features?

A manufacturer could lawfully produce a configuration of a covered type under a feature test by keeping the configuration below two counted features, through changes such as removing a bayonet mount, fitting an unthreaded muzzle, or fitting a fixed or thumbhole stock. Such configurations were manufactured and sold throughout the period. The evaluation by Jeffrey A. Roth and Christopher S. Koper recorded legal substitutes marketed under designations such as Sporter, AB for After Ban, and PCR for Politically Correct Rifle. Feature removal could not cure a firearm banned by name, because the named route covered listed models and copies or duplicates regardless of features. The compliance path existed only under the feature tests.

### Q: What is a semiautomatic assault weapon under the statute's definition?

Section 921(a)(30) defined semiautomatic assault weapon through four routes. Subsection (A) covered named firearms and copies or duplicates in any caliber. Subsection (B) covered a semiautomatic rifle with an ability to accept a detachable magazine and at least two of five features. Subsection (C) covered a semiautomatic pistol with the same magazine ability and at least two of five different features. Subsection (D) covered a semiautomatic shotgun with at least two of four features. The statute defined the term for itself, rather than adopting a technical or military classification. Every firearm in the category was semiautomatic, firing one shot for each pull of the trigger, and the definition turned on configuration rather than caliber, muzzle energy, or rate of fire.

### Q: Which specific firearms were banned by name?

Subsection (A) named Norinco, Mitchell, and Poly Technologies Avtomat Kalashnikovs in all models, Action Arms Israeli Military Industries UZI and Galil, Beretta Ar70 (SC-70), Colt AR-15, Fabrique National FN/FAL, FN/LAR, and FNC, SWD M-10, M-11, M-11/9, and M-12, Steyr AUG, and INTRATEC TEC-9, TEC-DC9 and TEC-22. A ninth clause covered revolving cylinder shotguns, such as or similar to the Street Sweeper and Striker 12. Contemporary official descriptions, including the letter from President Clinton dated August 22, 1994, counted nineteen firearms named in the statute, in the conventional count used by the administration and by Congress. The nine clauses and the conventional count of nineteen measured different things, because several clauses contained multiple designations.

### Q: How did the statute treat copies or duplicates of the named firearms?

The opening language of subsection (A) covered any of the firearms, or copies or duplicates of the firearms in any caliber, known as the listed firearms. The copies or duplicates language extended the named route beyond an original example bearing an exact listed designation. The words in any caliber stated that the extension operated across calibers, so a copy or duplicate did not leave the named route because it was chambered for a different caliber. Coverage under the named route rested on identity as a listed firearm or as a copy or duplicate of a listed firearm. Feature removal could not cure a copy or duplicate, because the named route applied regardless of features and contained no feature threshold.

### Q: What did each feature in the rifle test do?

A folding or telescoping stock folded or slid to shorten the overall length of a firearm or to adjust its fit. A pistol grip that protruded conspicuously beneath the action was a grip below the action held by the firing hand. A bayonet mount was a lug that accepted a bayonet. A flash suppressor was a muzzle device that dispersed escaping gases to reduce visible flash, and a threaded barrel designed to accommodate a flash suppressor accepted screwed on muzzle devices for that purpose. A grenade launcher fired rifle grenades. The rifle test in section 921(a)(30)(B) counted each item as one feature, required at least two, and gave no feature a ranking or weighting above another feature in the list.

### Q: How did the feature test for pistols differ from the test for rifles?

The pistol test in section 921(a)(30)(C) shared the rifle test structure of a semiautomatic firearm, an ability to accept a detachable magazine as a precondition, and a count of at least two features, but the counted features differed. The pistol list counted a magazine that attached outside the pistol grip, a threaded barrel capable of accepting a barrel extender, flash suppressor, forward handgrip, or silencer, a barrel shroud that permitted the shooter to hold the firearm with the nontrigger hand without being burned, a manufactured weight of 50 ounces or more when unloaded, and a semiautomatic version of an automatic firearm. The rifle threaded barrel language was narrower, tied to a flash suppressor in the same clause.

### Q: How did the feature test for shotguns work?

The shotgun test in section 921(a)(30)(D) covered a semiautomatic shotgun with at least two of four features: a folding or telescoping stock, a pistol grip that protruded conspicuously beneath the action, a fixed magazine capacity in excess of 5 rounds, and an ability to accept a detachable magazine. The test had no detachable magazine precondition, unlike the rifle and pistol tests. For a shotgun, the ability to accept a detachable magazine was one counted feature inside the list. A shotgun could therefore satisfy the test through a folding stock and a pistol grip alone, without any detachable magazine ability. A fixed magazine capacity of five rounds or fewer did not supply a count, because the threshold was capacity in excess of five rounds.

### Q: What counted as a large capacity ammunition feeding device?

Section 921(a)(31) defined the term as a magazine, belt, drum, feed strip, or similar device manufactured after the date of enactment of the 1994 act that had a capacity of, or could be readily restored or converted to accept, more than 10 rounds of ammunition. The term excluded an attached tubular device designed to accept, and capable of operating only with, .22 caliber rimfire ammunition. Section 922(w)(1) made transfer or possession of a device within the definition unlawful. The subsection contained no manufacture verb, and manufacture was controlled through the manufactured after element of the definition, so a device manufactured on or before September 13, 1994 fell outside the defined term whatever its capacity.

### Q: Who was exempt from the prohibitions?

Sections 922(v)(4) and 922(w)(3) provided that the prohibitions did not apply to manufacture for, transfer to, or possession by the United States or a State or their departments, agencies, or political subdivisions, including transfer to or possession by a law enforcement officer employed by such an entity for purposes of law enforcement, on or off duty. The prohibitions also did not apply to transfer to a licensee under title I of the Atomic Energy Act of 1954 for an on site physical protection system required by federal law, to possession by a retired law enforcement individual of an item transferred by the agency on retirement, or to manufacture, transfer, or possession by a licensed manufacturer or importer for authorized testing or experimentation.

### Q: What penalties applied to a violation of the prohibitions?

A knowing violation of section 922(v) or section 922(w) was punishable under section 924(a)(1)(B) by a fine and imprisonment of up to five years. The Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit described the exposure in the same terms in Navegar, Inc. v. United States in 1997. Section 110102 also amended section 924(c)(1)(B)(i) so that using or carrying a semiautomatic assault weapon during a crime of violence or drug trafficking crime carried a ten year term as enacted. The two tiers attached to different conduct: the first tier punished defiance of the prohibitions on manufacture, transfer, or possession, and the second tier punished use or carrying of a covered firearm during a separate qualifying offense.

### Q: What markings did the statute require on newly made firearms and magazines?

Section 923(i) received two sentences from the subtitle. The first required that the serial number of any semiautomatic assault weapon manufactured after enactment clearly show the date of manufacture. The second required that a large capacity ammunition feeding device manufactured after enactment be identified by a serial number showing that the device was manufactured or imported after the effective date, plus other identification that the Secretary prescribed by regulation. The markings placed date evidence on the item itself rather than in records held elsewhere, in a statute where coverage and grandfather protection both turned on the date of manufacture. Both sentences were among the provisions repealed at the sunset on September 13, 2004.

### Q: Did the assault weapons ban prohibit automatic weapons?

The ban did not prohibit automatic weapons as such, and the statute did not address fully automatic firearms. Every firearm in the category defined at section 921(a)(30) was semiautomatic, firing one shot for each pull of the trigger. Fully automatic firearms, described in federal law as machine guns, were regulated separately under the National Firearms Act and the 1986 prohibition. The pistol test counted a semiautomatic version of an automatic firearm as one feature, and that item described a self loading derivative of a design that also existed in automatic form. Derivation supplied the count under that item, and automatic operation was not the basis for coverage under any route in the definition.

### Q: What should a student remember about the assault weapons ban for an exam?

A student should remember the definition, the grandfather clause, and the sunset as the three elements that explain the statute. The definition at section 921(a)(30) sorted firearms by name, by copies or duplicates, and by counted features under separate tests for rifles, pistols, and shotguns, and a separate definition at section 921(a)(31) covered feeding devices manufactured after enactment with a capacity above 10 rounds. The grandfather clauses preserved lawful possession and transfer of items held on or before September 13, 1994. Section 110105 ended the subtitle on September 13, 2004 by its own term, without a congressional repeal vote and without an extension enacted into law. The statute was Public Law 103-322, signed on September 13, 1994.
