Herbert Marcuse is usually introduced through a title he spent a decade declining. The philosopher whom newspapers of the late nineteen sixties called the father, the guru, the prophet of the New Left was born in 1898, took his doctorate in 1922, and had published three substantial books before most of the students who quoted him were born. He had been trained in phenomenology by Edmund Husserl and Martin Heidegger. He had spent the war and its aftermath inside the American government, first in its wartime intelligence agency and then in the State Department, analysing the regime he had fled and the Soviet order he would anatomise in print. And in 1965 he published an essay on tolerance whose argument is still fought over, usually by people who have read summaries of it rather than the essay itself. A profile that begins from the guru will explain none of this. This profile begins from the record, and it follows the three books through which the record becomes an argument: a defence of Hegel written as fascism rose, a fusion of Freud and Marx written in American exile, and a diagnosis of affluent society written when that exile had lasted thirty years.

The centre of the argument, and the claim this profile returns to at every stage, is what it calls the affluence thesis. Classical Marxism expected deprivation to radicalise: squeeze a population hard enough and it would eventually see through the order squeezing it. Marcuse argued that the advanced industrial order had discovered the opposite technique. A system that delivers goods, rising incomes, cheap entertainment and the tangible feeling of a improving private life can purchase the consent that repression once had to extract, and it can do so without closing the universities, banning the newspapers or filling the prisons. Opposition is not crushed. It is absorbed, offered a career, a market and a vocabulary in which its own demands come back to it as advertising copy. The result is a society that can tolerate almost any criticism because criticism has lost the dimension in which an alternative to the whole could be thought. That is a strong claim, and this profile states its weaknesses as carefully as its force. But it is the claim that makes Marcuse a philosopher rather than a mascot, and it cannot be understood apart from the life that produced it: the young revolutionary of 1918, the Heidegger student who watched his teacher embrace the regime, the refugee analyst who learned the modern state from inside its own filing cabinets.

### What is One-Dimensional Man about?

It argues that advanced industrial society absorbs opposition by delivering material satisfaction while closing off the capacity to imagine alternatives. Marcuse calls the result one-dimensional: a society whose members experience their own administration as freedom and lack the second, critical dimension in which change could be conceived.

The question that organises everything in this profile is therefore a biographical one as much as a philosophical one. How did a man formed by German philosophy, defeated revolution and American government service arrive at the conclusion that satisfaction itself had become the most elegant instrument of domination? The answer runs through three intellectual worlds that rarely share a biography. The first is the world of German academic philosophy between the wars, where Marcuse learned from Heidegger that thought could be concrete, historical and hostile to empty abstraction, and then learned from Heidegger's own conduct that a teacher's categories offered no protection against barbarism. The second is the world of the exiled Institute for Social Research, where a small group of refugees tried to understand why the working class of the most advanced capitalist countries had not behaved as theory predicted. The third is America itself: the country that sheltered him, employed him, pensioned him and then, in his seventies, made him famous for reasons he only partly accepted. Each world left a book behind it, and the books must be read as stations of one itinerary rather than as three separate performances.

## The record behind the reputation

The reputation came late and it came distorted. When One-Dimensional Man appeared in 1964 its author was sixty-five years old, a professor at Brandeis University with a reputation among specialists as a demanding Hegelian and an eccentric Freudian. The book sold respectably and was argued over in the journals. Its transformation into a campus scripture took several more years and a political temperature its author had not forecast: the escalation of the war in Vietnam, the draft, the civil rights movement's radicalisation and the eruption of student protest across the Western world in 1968. Students in Berlin, Paris and Berkeley found in the book a vocabulary for a frustration that prosperity had not dissolved, and the press, needing a face for a movement that distrusted faces, supplied his. Photographs of a white-haired professor in his seventies began to circulate as emblems of a revolt conducted by twenty-year-olds.

Marcuse's own relation to this fame was ambivalent in ways the coverage rarely reported. He was gratified that the books were read, and he said so. He travelled to speak to student audiences in Berlin and elsewhere, and he did not disown the movements that quoted him. But he refused the paternity they and their opponents assigned him, he criticised the anti-intellectual and merely destructive currents inside the protest movements with a bluntness that cost him popularity, and he insisted, sometimes wearily, that he was a philosopher whose work stood or fell by its arguments. The dispute he conducted by letter with Theodor Adorno in 1969, after Adorno had called the police to clear students from the Institute in Frankfurt, shows the real position with unusual clarity. Marcuse defended the students' motives against his old friend's severity and criticised Adorno's recourse to the authorities, while in the same correspondence faulting the students' contempt for theory and their taste for action as an end in itself. A guru does not write letters like that. A teacher does.

The distortion matters because it has governed how the books are read. Taken as a movement's scripture, One-Dimensional Man is judged by whether the movement won, and Eros and Civilization is reduced to a licence for liberation of a kind its author never issued. Taken as philosophy, the same books pose harder and more durable questions. Why should rising living standards weaken rather than strengthen opposition? What would count as evidence against a theory which holds that the evidence of contentment is itself a symptom? Who is entitled to call another person's needs false, and by what criterion? These questions did not expire with the nineteen sixties, and they are the questions this profile keeps in view. The biography is not decoration around them. The man who asked them had watched a revolution fail in 1919, had written his way through exile, and had spent eight years reading the captured documents and the intelligence estimates of two hostile states. The questions carry the weight of that experience, and they deserve to be weighed with it.

## A Berlin childhood and a Wilhelmine formation

Herbert Marcuse was born in Berlin on 19 July 1898, into a prosperous assimilated Jewish family. His father, Carl Marcuse, was a textile manufacturer whose business gave the household the solid comfort of the German industrial bourgeoisie at its most confident. The Berlin of the boy's childhood was the capital of a new empire in a hurry: a city of factories, regiments, department stores and universities, where the family's position rested on the same industrial expansion that filled its streets with the crowds the boy would later theorise. Nothing in the record suggests a childhood of deprivation or rebellion. He attended the Mommsen Gymnasium, one of the city's strong classical schools, and received the education such schools were built to deliver: Latin and Greek, the German classics, and the assumption that high culture was a possession to be absorbed rather than an object to be questioned.

That formation matters to the philosophy because it never left him. Marcuse would become the Frankfurt School's most confrontational political writer, but his prose is saturated with the classical canon his schooling installed: Goethe and Schiller, Hölderlin and Hegel, the Greek tragedians behind his reading of Freud. When One-Dimensional Man attacks the debasement of language, the standard against which the debasement is measured is the language of that education. When Eros and Civilization looks for images of a non-repressive civilisation, it finds them in Orpheus and Narcissus, figures a Gymnasium boy met early. Critics who treat his cultural pessimism as snobbery miss the mechanism. The culture he feared losing was not a class ornament. It was the medium in which he had learned that reality might be otherwise, and he watched the affluent society convert that medium into merchandise with the grief of a man watching his native language fall out of use.

The war ended the formation abruptly. Called up in 1916, the eighteen-year-old served in an army airship unit stationed in Berlin, a posting that kept him in the capital while the empire collapsed around him. He was in the city in November 1918 when the monarchy fell, the Kaiser fled, and councils of workers and soldiers briefly held power in the streets his family had walked as owners. The distance between the two Berlins of his youth, the imperial capital of the Gymnasium and the revolutionary capital of the councils, is the distance his whole work tries to think. He spent fifty years asking why the second Berlin lost.

## The soldiers' council and the failed revolution

In November 1918 Marcuse was elected to the soldiers' council of his unit in Berlin-Reinickendorf. The detail is small, and he never inflated it, but it fixes his political origin precisely. The councils of that winter were improvised organs of a collapsed state's soldiery and workforce, closer to soviets in form than to parliaments, and membership in one placed a twenty-year-old bourgeois son inside the machinery of a revolution he had not made. He joined the Social Democratic Party, the party of the council movement's cautious majority, in the same weeks. The experiment lasted months. In January 1919 the government's reliance on the Freikorps, the volunteer counter-revolutionary militias, culminated in the suppression of the Spartacus rising and the murders of Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht, the revolutionary left's two most gifted leaders, whose bodies were disposed of by their captors. Marcuse left the party in disgust at its complicity, and the exit had a permanence about it. He never again belonged to a political party.

What the winter taught him structures everything he later wrote. The German working class, the largest and best organised in Europe, had declined revolution when revolution was available, and had done so not under foreign occupation or famine but through the choices of its own institutions and leaders. A theory that explained submission by coercion alone could not digest January 1919. Neither could a theory that blamed the failure entirely on betrayal from above, since the leaders' caution found answering caution in the ranks. The problem of his career was posed there in its first form: how a social order reproduces itself inside the desires and judgements of the people it subordinates, so that its victims defend it, staff it and, at the decisive moment, rescue it from its enemies. One-Dimensional Man is the third and most famous restatement of the problem. It is recognisably the same problem a soldiers' council member carried out of Berlin in 1919.

He turned from the party to the university, and the turn was also characteristic. If the revolution had failed for want of a consciousness adequate to it, then consciousness, its formation and its deformations, became the subject. He studied German literature and philosophy at Berlin and Freiburg, and took his doctorate in 1922 with a dissertation on the German artist novel, the Künstlerroman, that genre in which the artist's formation collides with the bourgeois world that cannot house it. The choice of subject was not accidental, and he knew it. The artist novel is the story of a sensibility that cannot be integrated into the order of useful work, and its hero's failure is the order's indictment. The young scholar who wrote on that failure, in the immediate aftermath of a political failure he had witnessed from inside, was already circling the theme of his maturity: what an unreconciled inner life knows that an administered world cannot afford to hear.

## Freiburg: the student of Heidegger

After several years in Berlin, where he worked in the book trade and continued to write, Marcuse returned to Freiburg in 1928 to study with Husserl and, decisively, with Martin Heidegger, whose Being and Time had appeared the year before and had detonated inside German philosophy. The attraction is intelligible. Heidegger's book attacked the bloodless abstractions of the academic tradition and promised a philosophy of concrete historical existence: of facticity, care, resoluteness and being-toward-death, of a human being thrown into a world it did not choose and compelled to make itself within it. To a young Marxist dissatisfied with the mechanical materialism of the party textbooks, this looked like the missing anthropology. Marcuse's essays of these years, beginning with the 1928 "Contributions to a Phenomenology of Historical Materialism", attempted the fusion in print: Heidegger's analysis of existence read as the concrete ground on which historical materialism could stand. He was not alone in the attempt, and for a few years Freiburg held the improbable prospect of a phenomenology turned toward revolution.

The habilitation, the second thesis that licensed a German academic career, was written under Heidegger's direction and published in 1932 as Hegel's Ontology and the Theory of Historicity. It is a demanding, technical book, and it shows the direction of Marcuse's escape from his teacher. Where Heidegger grounded history in the structure of individual existence, Marcuse grounded it in Hegel's concept of life and labour, in the movement of a spirit that makes itself through work on a resistant world. The pupil was already relocating the philosophy of existence onto the terrain of social history, which is to say, onto Marx's terrain. Colleagues later described the parting as philosophical before it was political. Both descriptions are true, and the order matters less than the sequel.

The sequel was 1933. Heidegger joined the Nazi Party, accepted the rectorate of Freiburg University and delivered an inaugural address aligning the university with the regime. For his Jewish former students the event was not a disappointment but a severance. Marcuse left Germany that year and never studied under Heidegger again, but the relationship has one more documented episode, and it belongs in any honest profile. In 1947, visiting Germany in American service, Marcuse wrote to his former teacher asking for a public word about the regime Heidegger had served, a word Marcuse argued was owed to the victims and to the students Heidegger had taught. Heidegger's reply, when it came, evaded the request and drew an equivalence between the crimes of the regime and the expulsion of Germans from the eastern territories. Marcuse answered in April 1948 that a consciousness which could make that equivalence placed its holder outside the world of logos altogether, and the correspondence ended. The exchange is the Freiburg chapter's true conclusion. The student had spent fifteen years and an exile learning what his teacher's philosophy could not see, and the teacher, given the chance to see it, declined.

### Did Marcuse's years with Heidegger make him a Heideggerian?

No, although the training marked his method permanently. He took from the Freiburg years a distrust of abstract categories, an insistence that concepts answer to concrete historical existence, and the habit of recovering a term's buried history before using it.

He discarded the ontology that held those virtues: his habilitation already grounds historicity in labour and social life rather than in individual being-toward-death, and his mature work treats Heidegger's categories as ideology wherever they evade material interests. The pupil kept the tools and rejected the house they were built to serve.

What Freiburg gave him, besides a method and a disillusion, was Hegel. The habilitation's research had required years inside Hegel's texts, and it convinced him that the dialectic, properly read, was the most powerful instrument of critical thought the tradition possessed: a logic in which every achieved form displays the contradiction that will undo it, and in which negation is not destruction but the motor of truth. When the Nazis and their intellectual forerunners began identifying Hegel as a philosopher of the totalitarian state, Marcuse was positioned, by training and by exile, to answer. The answer became his first book in English and the foundation of the three-book arc this profile follows.

## The Institute and the road into exile

The institution that received the refugee was itself an exile. The Institute for Social Research had been founded in Frankfurt in 1923 on an endowment from the grain merchant Felix Weil, and under its second director, Max Horkheimer, it had set itself the question that the German catastrophe made urgent: why had the anticipated revolution not come, and what held the existing order together in the minds of those it injured? Marcuse joined the Institute in 1933, working first with its Geneva branch as the Frankfurt building was seized and its library confiscated, and in 1934 he followed the Institute to New York, where Columbia University gave the refugees a building and a precarious welcome. The biographical shorthand in the series' [Frankfurt School complete guide](/2015/06/01/frankfurt-school-complete-guide/) covers the institution's own history; what matters here is what the Institute gave this particular member. It gave him a salary through the worst years of exile, a journal in which to publish, colleagues of the first rank, and a research programme that matched the question he had carried since 1919.

His essays for the Institute's journal in the nineteen thirties were, on their face, contributions to the history of philosophy: on the concept of essence, on philosophy and critical theory, on hedonism. Read in sequence they are something more, the slow construction of an independent position inside the school's collective work. The essay on hedonism defended pleasure against both its bourgeois moralist critics and its ascetic socialist ones, arguing that a tradition which treats happiness as trivial has surrendered the very standard by which domination should be judged. The essay on the concept of essence insisted that the gap between what a thing is and what it might become is not a metaphysical luxury but the space in which criticism lives. A reader who knows only the late books can miss how early the signature themes arrive. The defence of happiness against its administrators, and the defence of unrealised possibility against the tyranny of fact, are both present in the exile essays of a scholar not yet forty.

The most consequential of these early works is the least known. In 1941, in the journal's English-language successor, he published "Some Social Implications of Modern Technology", an essay that contains the one-dimensional argument in embryo, twenty-three years before the famous book. Technics, he argued, is never a neutral stock of tools awaiting good or bad use. Modern technology arrives as a social process in which the apparatus of production shapes the individuals who serve it: efficiency becomes a standard that judges persons, the machine's requirements reorganise work, leisure and finally thought itself, and the individual who once might have stood against the apparatus finds his inner resistance dismantled by the comfort and rationality of the system that employs him. Readers who date Marcuse's diagnosis to the affluence of the nineteen sixties misdate it by a generation. The diagnosis was formed in the Depression, refined by the war economy, and only later fitted to the consumer society that gave it an audience.

### Why did a refugee scholar spend the nineteen thirties writing about Hegel and hedonism?

Because the Institute's method treated philosophy as evidence about society, and because Marcuse believed the coming fight would be fought over the inheritance of reason itself. The Nazis were busy claiming that German thought culminated in racial myth and the rejection of universal reason.

His essays answered by recovering the critical contents of the tradition the regime was burying: essence as unrealised possibility, pleasure as a legitimate standard of judgement, Hegel's dialectic as a logic of liberation. The scholarship was a form of custody, keeping a tradition's explosive charges intact through the years when its homeland had outlawed them.

Exile also imposed the practical education that distinguishes his biography from those of the school's purer theorists. The Institute's American members needed American incomes, American publishers and American protection, and Marcuse proved adaptable in each direction. He wrote in English, he learned the idioms of American academic life, and when war came he offered the American state the two assets a refugee scholar possessed that the state could not manufacture: a native knowledge of Germany and a trained analyst's mind. The offer was accepted. The philosopher of the coming one-dimensional society spent the decisive decade of his middle life as an employee of the society whose inner workings he was learning to describe.

## Three books, one argument

The three books table sets out the architecture this profile follows. Read across the rows, it shows a single question pursued through three different bodies of material. Reason and Revolution of 1941 asks what happened to reason, and finds the critical dialectic of Hegel buried under the positivism that succeeded it. Eros and Civilization of 1955 asks what happened to desire, and finds the instincts themselves administered by the civilisation that claims merely to restrain them. One-Dimensional Man of 1964 asks what happened to opposition, and finds it integrated into the order it was meant to negate. Reason, desire, opposition: the three dimensions in which a society might surpass itself, each shown closing under the same pressure. The table is a finding aid, not a summary, and the sections that follow take each book on its own terms before the profile draws the threads together.

One feature of the table deserves emphasis before the detail begins. None of the three books was written as a movement text. The first was an academic monograph from a university press. The second grew out of lectures to psychiatrists. The third was published by a small Boston house known for serious nonfiction and was reviewed, at first, as a work of social theory. The trajectory from the seminar room to the barricade poster was something history did to these books, not something their author designed into them, and the profile will keep returning to the gap between the writing and the reception, because the gap is where the misreadings breed.

## Reason and Revolution: Hegel defended against the fascism charge

Reason and Revolution appeared in 1941, written in English by a refugee whose adopted country was about to enter the war against his native one. Its provocation was precise. A strand of commentary, inside and outside Germany, had taken to explaining fascism as the fruit of Hegelian philosophy: the state deified, the individual dissolved, history enthroned as a tribunal that justifies whatever succeeds. Marcuse's book is a four-hundred-page refusal of that genealogy. Hegel's philosophy, he argued, is built on the category the fascist order had to destroy first, the category of negation. Reason, for Hegel, is the power to see that what exists stands condemned by what it could be, and the dialectic is the discipline of holding that tension open against every settled fact. A doctrine whose core is the unrest of the negative cannot father a politics of obedience. Fascism's intellectual parentage runs through the opposite line: through the cult of blood and soil, the celebration of instinct against intellect, the positivist worship of brute fact, everything in the nineteenth century that defined itself against Hegel's rationalism.

The argument's second half follows the inheritance downward. When Hegel's system was abandoned, Marcuse contended, European thought split into two heirs that have dominated since: a positivism that studies society as it studies weather, recording facts and renouncing judgement, and a Marxism that preserved the dialectic's critical charge by relocating it in material life. Marx appears in the book not as Hegel's undertaker but as his executor, the thinker who showed that the contradictions reason discovers in concepts are reflections of contradictions in the organisation of labour and property. The famous transition from philosophy to social theory, which the book's subtitle announces, is presented as a rescue operation. What could be saved of reason's critical function was saved by moving it out of the lecture hall and into the critique of political economy.

### Why did Marcuse write a defence of Hegel in the middle of a war against Germany?

Because the genealogy under dispute carried a practical consequence. If fascism grew from the deepest stratum of German classical thought, then that thought stood condemned with it, and the tradition that contained the tools for criticising fascism would be discarded by fascism's enemies at the moment of greatest need.

Marcuse wrote to save the weapons from the fire. He also wrote as an analyst of the enemy: a refugee demonstrating that the regime's ideology was not the fulfilment of German philosophy but its negation, a demonstration aimed at readers who would soon help decide what a defeated Germany might become.

The book's limits are the ones the table records. Its Hegel is a selectively illuminated Hegel, the dialectician of the Phenomenology and the Logic, with the philosopher of the Prussian state kept in shadow. Marcuse knew the authoritarian passages and handled them the way advocates do, by arguing that the system's method refutes its author's own political accommodations. Readers unconvinced by that manoeuvre have a real case, and the objection has never been answered so much as outlived. But the book's central contention has worn well. The intellectual historians of fascism now routinely trace its doctrines through the anti-Enlightenment and anti-rationalist currents Marcuse identified, and the notion of Hegel as fascism's godfather survives mainly as a debating point. For the series' purposes the book matters in one further way. It established the method of the two books that followed: take the concept everyone believes settled, in this case Hegel, recover the negation buried inside it, and show that the present order depends on the burial.

## The analyst: intelligence work and the State Department years

The least expected chapter of the biography is also the best documented. In 1942 Marcuse went to Washington. He worked first for the Office of War Information, the propaganda agency, and from 1943 for the Office of Strategic Services, the wartime intelligence organisation, in its Research and Analysis Branch. There, in the Central European section, he joined a remarkable company of refugee scholars: Franz Neumann, whose study Behemoth had anatomised the Nazi system, and Otto Kirchheimer, the jurist of Weimar's collapse, among others. Their task was to understand the enemy as a system: its economy, its administration, its ideology, its probable behaviour under bombing, blockade and defeat. The work was secret, collective and relentlessly empirical, thousands of reports built from intercepted documents, prisoner interrogations, the German press and the scholarly literature the refugees carried in their heads. Marcuse's assignments centred on Germany itself: the structure of the regime, the prospects for denazification, the identification of the institutions and personnel through which Nazism had organised German life.

When the war ended and the OSS was dissolved, the unit's German specialists transferred to the State Department, and Marcuse went with them. He became chief of the Central European section of the Department's intelligence research office, and his subject widened from the defeated enemy to the emerging adversary. Through the later nineteen forties he directed analyses of the Soviet Union and its satellite order: the ideology, the party apparatus, the gap between the doctrine's claims and the system's performance. He left government service in 1951, spent several years on research fellowships at the Russian institutes of Columbia and Harvard, and took up a professorship at Brandeis University in 1954. The government decade thus sits squarely in the middle of the life, between the Hegel book and the Freud book, and it is the hinge on which the profile turns.

### What did Marcuse do inside the American government?

He analysed two hostile states for the government of a third, and the surviving record shows analysis, not intrigue. For the wartime intelligence agency he worked on the internal structure of Nazi Germany and on the planning of denazification; for the State Department he directed research on the Soviet Union and communist parties in Europe.

He held no operational role, ran no agents and made no policy. His product was the written estimate, and his employers valued precisely the knowledge that exile had given him: the languages, the literatures and the inside familiarity with the doctrines his desk was charged with tracking.

The fact invites two cheap readings, and the record supports neither. The first, common among his detractors, treats the service as exposure: the revolutionary unmasked as a servant of American power, his later critique discounted as ingratitude or bad faith. The second, common among devotees, treats it as infiltration or irony, the critic gathering material inside the citadel. Both readings dissolve on contact with the file. Marcuse served a government at war with the regime that had exiled him and would have murdered him, an alignment requiring no apology and no contortion. He then served its peacetime successor through the opening of the Cold War, analysing a Soviet system he had never romanticised and would anatomise in print within the decade. What the decade gave his philosophy was not a allegiance but an education. He learned how a modern state actually administers a population: through files, categories, incentives, expertise, the quiet conversion of political questions into technical ones. When One-Dimensional Man describes a society administered so smoothly that administration is experienced as freedom, the description carries the authority of a man who had sat inside the machinery and written its memoranda. The guru of the students had been, for eight years, a bureaucrat of the national security state, and the book is unintelligible unless both halves of that sentence are held together.

A persistent item of folklore attaches the service to the wrong agency, and the profile corrects it here because the error carries an implication. Marcuse never worked for the Central Intelligence Agency. That organisation was founded in 1947, while he was a State Department analyst, and no record places him on its rolls. The distinction is not pedantry. The OSS of his service was a wartime body whose research branch was famous for employing scholars openly and publishing much of its analysis after the war; the CIA of the popular imagination is a peacetime covert-action service. Sliding the philosopher from the first employer to the second converts a documented episode of refugee service into an insinuation of clandestine work, and the insinuation is the whole payload. The record shows a desk, a section, a series of reports and a resignation. It is interesting enough without decoration.

## Soviet Marxism: the analyst's second subject

The fellowship years after 1951 converted government knowledge into scholarship, and the result, Soviet Marxism: A Critical Analysis, appeared in 1958. The book is the forgotten panel of the triptych, and its neglect has distorted the reading of the panel everyone remembers. Marcuse subjected the Soviet order to the same instrument he had applied to Hegel's reception and would apply to American affluence: recover the doctrine's own claims, then measure the system's organisation against them. His finding was double-edged in a way that guaranteed the book a cold reception on both sides of the Cold War. Against the Soviet claim, he argued that the doctrine functioned in the USSR as the legitimating ideology of a bureaucratic industrial order, its concepts of liberation administered into their opposite. Against the Western claim that this outcome refuted Marx, he argued that the Soviet system was itself a form of advanced industrial society, subject to the same technological rationality, the same productivity cult and the same administered consumption that he was beginning to describe at home. The two superpowers appeared in his analysis less as antitheses than as rival managements of one civilisation.

The Cold Warriors who had employed him found the symmetry intolerable, and the orthodox communists who might have welcomed his anti-capitalism found the anatomy of their own order unforgivable. Both reactions confirmed the book's thesis in the author's eyes. A critical theory that could be used by either camp's propaganda had already surrendered the second dimension he cared about. The episode also clarifies the government years in retrospect. He had not spent a decade inside the State Department gathering grievances. He had spent it learning that the administered society was a general form, appearing under rival flags with rival vocabularies, and that the critique of it would have to be a critique of the form itself rather than of one flag. That generality is what separates the coming book's argument from a complaint about America, and it is why the book's examples, drawn from a California he had adopted as home, were never its real boundaries.

## Eros and Civilization: the Freudian turn

The second book began as lectures. Invited to address the Washington School of Psychiatry in the early nineteen fifties, Marcuse chose the most unfashionable ground available to a Marxist of his generation: the metapsychology of Freud, the speculative late works in which the founder of psychoanalysis had built a theory of civilisation on the renunciation of instinct. Eros and Civilization, published in 1955, takes Freud's bleakest propositions with complete seriousness and then refuses their conclusion. Freud had argued that civilisation is bought with repression, that the instincts must be subdued for work, order and culture to exist, and that the discontent this produces is the permanent price of being human. Marcuse accepted the framework and disputed the accounting. The repression history actually imposes, he argued, divides into two kinds. One kind any civilisation requires if human beings are to live together at all. The other kind serves not survival but a specific organisation of domination, and it is historically variable, shrinking or swelling with the order that imposes it. A theory that lumps the two together mistakes a political arrangement for a law of nature.

The stakes of the distinction ramify through the whole book. If much of what passes for necessary renunciation is in fact imposed by a particular social order, then the order's justification collapses at its root: the scarcity that demands sacrifice can be examined, and in an age of unprecedented productivity it can be indicted. The mature industrial system, on this reading, does not merely exploit labour in the old manner. It administers instinct itself, organising sexuality, aggression and the very sense of time around the requirements of alienated work, and then sells back a managed release as proof of its generosity. Against this administration the book sets the contents that repression keeps in storage: memory, which preserves the experience of gratification the present denies; phantasy, which refuses the reality principle's jurisdiction and keeps the image of a different life; and the figures of Orpheus and Narcissus, retrieved from myth as counter-images to Prometheus the culture hero of toil, emblems of a receptivity and a joy that the performance-driven order must classify as perversion or idleness.

### What separates basic repression from surplus repression?

Basic repression names the instinctual restraint without which no durable civilisation is possible: the delay of satisfaction, the deflection of drives into work and durable bonds, the minimum of discipline that communal survival imposes at any level of development. It is the floor of renunciation, fixed by the human condition rather than by any regime.

Surplus repression names everything beyond that minimum which a specific order of domination adds for its own preservation: the renunciations demanded not by scarcity but by hierarchy, not by survival but by the organisation of labour, status and obedience that a given society happens to enforce. The distinction is the book's master key, because it converts Freud's eternal tragedy into a historical variable that politics can address.

### How does the performance principle organise a life?

The performance principle is Marcuse's name for the historically specific form the reality principle takes in his society: the standard by which a person is measured as a performer in a competitive economic order. It governs the working day first and, by extension, everything else.

Under its rule the body is desexualised into an instrument of labour for most of the waking day, time is experienced as a sequence of performances to be optimised, and worth, income and desirability all attach to output. The principle governs far beyond the workplace. It organises education as preparation for performance, leisure as recovery for performance, and even rebellion as a style of performance, so that escaping it requires more than changing jobs. It requires changing the standard by which a life is counted successful.

The book's reception replicated, in miniature, the pattern of the third book's. Specialists argued; Freudians objected that their founder's concepts had been pressed into philosophy; theologians and psychiatrists of the existential school, among them the book's early sympathetic readers, found in it a rare attempt to give the era's discontent a theory. A decade later the counterculture adopted the book as a scripture of sexual liberation, a reading its author regarded with the mixture of pleasure and alarm that became his signature response to fame. The reduction deserves the correction the brief for this profile demands. Eros and Civilization is not a summons to release the instincts in whatever form the market for release happens to offer. Its argument runs the other way: an order that has learned to sell transgression has already administered it. The liberation the book imagines is civilisational, not recreational, a transformation of work, time and need themselves, and its closing vision of a non-repressive order is offered as a possibility inscribed in the productive achievements of the existing one, not as a weekend available for purchase.

Underneath the Freud sits the anthropology the whole theory needs, and it is Marx's earliest one. The criterion by which surplus repression is recognised as surplus, the standard against which administered need is judged false, rests on a picture of the human being as a creature whose capacities exceed any use the present order makes of them, the picture the young Marx drew in the manuscripts on species being. The series' account of [Marx's species being](/2011/05/15/marx-species-being-explained/) gives that anthropology its own hearing; here it is enough to mark the dependency, because the dependency is where the critics will strike. A theory of false needs needs a theory of true ones, and Marcuse's, like Marx's, is inherited rather than demonstrated. He was more honest about the gap than most of his followers, and the objection section of this profile gives the gap its full weight.

## One-Dimensional Man: the diagnosis of the administered society

One-Dimensional Man appeared in 1964, subtitled Studies in the Ideology of Advanced Industrial Society, and its opening pages announce the paradox everything else elaborates. The society it describes is not a tyranny of the familiar kind. It delivers goods on a scale no previous order approached, it governs through a democratic machinery that functions, it permits criticism, eccentricity and protest, and it reproduces itself, the book argues, more effectively than any police state could. Its unfreedom is, in the book's celebrated phrase, "a comfortable, smooth, reasonable, democratic unfreedom", and the phrase's sting is its accuracy about the texture of the thing. Nothing in the daily experience of the integrated citizen announces domination. The demands arrive as conveniences. The controls wear the aspect of services. A critique that waits for the knock on the door will wait forever, because the mechanism the book names operates in daylight, through the satisfaction of wants the society itself has taught.

The argument's classical foil is the Marxism Marcuse had inherited. That tradition located the system's gravedigger in the class whose labour sustained it and whose interests, sharpened by deprivation, would organise the negation from inside. Marcuse's empirical claim, the scandal at the book's centre, is that this carrier has been absorbed. The working class of the advanced countries, won by rising consumption and integrated by unions, parties and media into the system's own institutions, increasingly shares the needs and aspirations the system administers. Its opposition, where it persists, negotiates the distribution of the product rather than contesting the product's world. Meanwhile the productive apparatus grows capable of pacifying discontent in advance: grievances that once matured into movements are intercepted as market signals, research topics, lifestyle options. A society that can absorb its own negation, the book concludes, has become one-dimensional, not because everyone agrees, but because disagreement circulates inside a universe of discourse whose boundaries are set by the order disagreed with.

The book therefore spends much of its length on that universe: on language, logic and the forms of thought available inside it. One-dimensional society produces one-dimensional thought, and the chapters on the closing of the universe of discourse, on operational thinking in the sciences and philosophy, and on the fate of negative concepts in an administered vocabulary are not ornaments to a political thesis. They are the thesis, stated at the level where it does its work. A population may keep every formal liberty and lose the conceptual instruments with which an alternative could be formulated; the loss would not appear in any census of opinions, because it operates before opinion, in the meanings words are permitted to carry. This is the sense in which one-dimensionality must be understood, and it corrects the laziest misreading of the book. The term does not name conformity of opinion. Opinion in the administered society is various, noisy and free. The term names the absence of a standpoint outside the society's own categories from which the whole could be negated, the flattening of thought's second dimension, in which what is might be judged by what could be.

### What does one-dimensional language do to thought?

It forecloses the concepts in which alternatives live. In the administered idiom Marcuse describes, words are fixed to operations and functions: a term means what it does, a claim is validated by the procedure that tests it, and nouns arrive fused with the attributes the system assigns them.

The tension between a thing and its unrealised possibilities is edited out of speech. Hypnotic formulas repeat until they harden into reality, abbreviations replace the concepts they once pointed to, and the syntax of public language drops the subordinate clauses in which qualification, history and doubt reside. Thought conducted in such a language can be brilliant about means. It cannot formulate an end the system has not already named.

The culture industry analysis of his Frankfurt colleagues forms one wall of this universe, and the reader who wants that mechanism in its own right should go to the series' treatment of [the culture industry](/2015/07/15/culture-industry-explained/). Marcuse's contribution is to generalise the integration beyond entertainment. Advertising speaks the one-dimensional dialect in its purest form, collapsing the distance between the product and the self that is invited to become it, and the series' study of [advertising, consumption and ideology](/2012/06/01/advertising-consumption-ideology/) pursues that collapse in detail. In the book the same closure is traced through political speech, bureaucratic euphemism and the social sciences, until the reader is meant to feel the walls of a world in which even the vocabulary of protest has been pre-formatted. The famous examples are the compound pieties of the era's official language, phrases in which a weapon's horror and its moral certificate arrive in the same breath, cleansing each other. A language that can call a bomb clean, he argued, has performed the one-dimensional operation perfectly: the word no longer points beyond the fact to a judgement of the fact.

## False needs and the happy consciousness

The doctrine of false needs is the book's most quoted and most resisted element, and it deserves a statement free of both caricatures. Marcuse distinguishes needs by their origin and their service. True needs are those whose satisfaction the individual's survival and flourishing require, and his list is austere: nourishment, clothing, lodging at the attainable level of culture. False needs are those superimposed on the individual by social powers interested in his repression: needs that perpetuate toil, aggressiveness and injustice while presenting themselves as the individual's own, whose satisfaction is experienced as pleasure and whose frustration is experienced as deprivation. The advertising system is their engine, but the engine matters less than the result. The administered individual introjects the false needs so thoroughly that they are felt, chosen and defended as personal identity. To tell such an individual that his needs are managed is to insult what he experiences as himself, and the book knows it. The difficulty is not a defect discovered by the critics. It is stated, in so many words, on the book's early pages, as the circle any critique of administered consciousness must break or confess it cannot.

Happy consciousness is the circle's subjective side. It is the belief, deeper than any argument, that the real is rational and the system delivers the goods, the settled conviction that what exists deserves to exist because it works. Under happy consciousness, facts lose their indicting power. Unemployment, war preparations, the waste of resources and the regimentation of life can all be known, documented and deplored, and the knowledge changes nothing, because the framework that would convert facts into a verdict on the whole has been retired. Marcuse calls the corresponding style of thought one-dimensional precisely here: thinking that accepts the given universe as the horizon of the possible, and confines intelligence to arranging what the universe supplies. The unhappy consciousness of earlier epochs, the awareness of the gap between the actual and the possible that drove philosophy from Plato to Hegel, appears in this light as a cognitive achievement the affluent society has unlearned, and its recovery, not its consolation, is what the book's negativity aims at.

### What did Marcuse mean by false needs?

He meant needs implanted by the society whose satisfaction binds the individual tighter to the order that implanted them: the need for the new model, the competitive display, the entertainment that reconciles, the relaxation that restores the worker for work.

Their falsity lies not in the pleasure they give, which is real, nor in the wanting, which is sincerely felt, but in their function and origin. They are heteronomous, issued by powers the individual does not control, and they perpetuate a form of life whose costs in toil and aggression their satisfaction conceals. The concept's provocation is deliberate: it denies that the consumer's own testimony settles the question of his freedom.

Against the whole construction stands a rejoinder every honest treatment must register in its strongest form, and this profile will return to it at full length. If the satisfied cannot be trusted to know their own needs, and the dissatisfied are too few or too marginal to matter, then the theory has arranged the evidence so that nothing could count against it, and the theorist's insight into other people's unfreedom rests on a criterion he cannot publicly validate. Marcuse's defenders reply that the circle is the subject, not a trick: a critique of consciousness that waited for the administered to certify it would be administered too. The exchange has never been settled, and the profile's later sections give the objection the floor before returning a verdict. What cannot be denied is the diagnosis's descriptive reach. The conversion of opposition into lifestyle, of dissent into a market segment, of radical vocabulary into advertising idiom, has been the observable routine of the decades the book has been in circulation, and it was described in 1964, before the routine had a name.

## The affluence thesis: satisfaction as an instrument of integration

The affluence thesis can now be stated in its full form, since the profile has assembled its parts. Marcuse's distinctive contribution to Marxist theory is the claim that an advanced industrial system neutralises opposition more effectively by satisfying material needs than by repressing them, and that affluence, so far from preparing the ground for liberation as the classical schema half-expected, can function as the most thorough mechanism of integration yet devised. Deprivation had always carried a subversive by-product: it taught the deprived where the system's promises ended. Satisfaction teaches the opposite lesson. Each delivered good is evidence for the defence, each improvement in private comfort a small renewal of the social contract, each new want promptly met a demonstration that the machine which defines the wants also fulfils them. The citizen of the affluent society is not deceived about his refrigerator, his car or his holiday. They are real. What the thesis claims is that their reality is the mechanism, that a domination which must be believed is weaker than a domination which can be touched, driven and eaten.

### Why does the affluence thesis invert the classical expectation?

The classical expectation ran from misery to revolt: capitalism impoverishes or frustrates its workers, and their worsening condition teaches them solidarity and negation. The affluence thesis inverts the sequence. Where the system can raise consumption while it administers the wants being consumed, improvement itself becomes the teacher, and its lesson is loyalty.

Opposition loses its material school. The thesis does not deny that pockets of deprivation persist and fester. It denies that deprivation remains the system's characteristic product, and it relocates the critical problem from the distribution of goods to the manufacture of the needs the goods satisfy, which is why its author's attention moved from the factory gate to the advertisement and the managed mind.

Three corollaries follow, and each has had a separate career. The first concerns the opposition's sociology. If integration works through consumption and identification, then the system's outside is no longer a class positioned at production's centre but the margins that consumption leaves behind, a relocation the book's famous conclusion performs and the profile examines in its own section. The second concerns the state. Affluence on this scale is not a market accident; it is organised, by military expenditure that sustains demand while disciplining politics, by public works, by the managed business cycle, so that the welfare state and the warfare state appear in the book as two faces of one administration. The third corollary concerns time. An order that must continually manufacture and satisfy new needs has mortgaged itself to perpetual expansion, and its stability is therefore dynamic and brittle at once: interrupt the delivery, and the loyalty purchased by delivery is exposed as conditional. Marcuse returned to that brittleness whenever his readers mistook the thesis for a prophecy of eternal stability. The administered society, in his telling, is not invulnerable. It is a society that has traded the old vulnerabilities for new ones, and the trade is visible in every crisis of the affluence it depends on.

The thesis also explains the framework's survival, the quality the brief for this profile singles out. Systems of thought usually expire with the conditions they described. Marcuse's described the nineteen sixties, yet the mechanism he isolated, integration through administered satisfaction, does not depend on the technologies, parties or slogans of that decade. It depends only on the conjunction of high productivity with the organised production of need, and wherever that conjunction holds, the analysis can be re-run. Whether it still holds, and with what modifications, is a judgement this profile leaves to the reader's own evidence, since the series' discipline in this article forbids the application of the framework to the reader's present. What can be said historically is narrower and stronger: of all the Frankfurt School's diagnoses, this is the one whose central mechanism was least damaged by the passing of its moment, because the moment it described was not an episode but a technique.

## Technological rationality: when reason becomes administration

Beneath the affluence stands a deeper stratum of the argument, and no profile of this thinker is complete without it. From the 1941 technology essay onward, Marcuse held that the modern order's rationality is itself a form of domination. Technological rationality, his term for the mode of thought embodied in the productive apparatus, presents itself as pure instrumentality: neutral methods, measurable results, efficiency as the universal solvent. But a rationality that knows only how, and brackets every question of what for, is not neutral in its effects. It pre-decides the range of thinkable purposes by admitting only those that the apparatus can process. Ends that cannot be quantified, scheduled or administered drop out of reason's jurisdiction and reappear as private taste, poetry or madness. The political consequence is immense and largely invisible. Decisions that are in substance choices about the form of life present themselves as technical necessities, dictated by the machine's requirements, and opposition to them is reclassified as ignorance of how things work.

This is the element of the theory that connects the profile to the school's wider project and to the volume on enlightenment's dialectic treated elsewhere in this cluster. Where his colleagues traced reason's self-destruction in myth and enlightenment, Marcuse traced it in the assembly line, the bureau and the laboratory, in practices whose rationality nobody disputes and whose cumulative effect is a society that cannot ask whether its rationality serves life or administers it. The 1941 essay already saw the individual withering inside the process: the person who once possessed an inner dimension of conscience, fantasy and refusal finds that dimension starved by a world that organises his perceptions in advance. One-Dimensional Man is that essay grown into a book, with affluence added as the mechanism the wartime version, written amid rationing and mobilisation, could only glimpse. Read together, the two texts refute the notion that the diagnosis was a mood of the affluent decade. It was a twenty-three-year investigation into what the machine age does to the mind that must live in it, and its author trusted it enough to restate it, in his final decade, against the new objection that the machine had changed its nature. He conceded the surfaces. He kept the depth.

## The tolerance essay: the argument stated accurately

In 1965 the Beacon Press published a small volume called A Critique of Pure Tolerance, containing essays by the philosopher Robert Paul Wolff, the historian Barrington Moore Jr. and Marcuse, whose contribution, "Repressive Tolerance", became the most argued-over twenty-five pages he ever wrote. The profile now states that argument with the care its brief demands, because the essay is habitually cited by parties who need it to say either much more or much less than it says. It is presented here as a text to be explained. No connection is drawn, in either direction, between the essay and any argument of the reader's own time; the profile names no institution, campaign or figure of the present, and asks the reader to meet the text in 1965, where it was written.

The argument begins from a diagnosis the reader of this profile already knows. In advanced industrial society, Marcuse holds, the background conditions of public discussion are not neutral. The means of communication are concentrated, the terms of debate are set by powers with interests in the outcome, and the consciousness of the audience has been formed by the very order the debate is meant to judge. Under such conditions, tolerance, the scrupulous equal treatment of all positions, does not function as the midwife of truth that the liberal tradition expects. It functions as a protective belt for the arrangements that dominate the background. The established order can afford tolerance because tolerance costs it nothing: the dissenting view is heard, published and refuted within a universe whose premises it is not permitted to reach, while the views that serve the order enjoy the additional tolerance of saturation. Tolerance extended equally to the policies of domination and to the resistance against them is, on this account, not impartiality but a tilt, and he gives the resulting condition the essay's title: repressive tolerance, tolerance that represses by the manner of its exercise.

The remedy he proposes is the part that must be quoted in paraphrase with total precision. He calls it liberating tolerance, and defines it as intolerance toward movements of the political Right and toleration of movements of the Left, operationalised as the withdrawal of tolerance from groups and movements that promote aggressive policies, armament, chauvinism, discrimination on grounds of race and religion, or that oppose the extension of public services and social legislation. The withdrawal, he specifies, would apply to speech and assembly as well as to action, and it would apply, in the cases he regards as clearest, before the movements in question had acted. He knows how this sounds and says so: the proposal appears radically undemocratic, and he answers that the democratic process it would suspend has already been suspended in substance by the manipulation of the conditions under which opinion forms. The criterion he offers for the asymmetry is historical rather than procedural. Movements are to be judged by their ends and their record: those that work to extend domination, aggression and privilege forfeit tolerance; those that work against them earn it. He presents the proposal as a transitional discipline for a society he diagnoses as already in a state of emergency, a democracy in name administered in fact, and he does not present it as a clause for a constitution.

Equally important is what the essay does not propose, since the commonest misreading inflates it in this direction. It is not a censorship statute and does not read like one. It drafts no law, establishes no board, specifies no penalties, and its practical register is educational and social as much as legal: a reallocation of tolerance by the institutions that confer it, universities, media and publics included, rather than a police measure. Its author's own clarifications, in the postscript he added when the essay was republished three years later, kept the same double character. He refused to soften the asymmetry, calling the withdrawal of tolerance from regressive movements a prerequisite of any tolerance worth the name, and he also insisted that the argument presupposed the emergency diagnosis, that in a society whose communications were genuinely free the case for the remedy would fall. Whether that presupposition saves the argument or damns it is the question the objections now take up.

## The objections to the tolerance essay, at full strength

The objections begin where the essay is proudest, with the criterion. The asymmetry requires a classification of movements into the regressive and the liberating, and the essay supplies the classifier's standpoint but no procedure. Whoever administers liberating tolerance must already know which movements serve domination, and the theory itself is the only instrument offered for the knowing. The objection is not that the classification might err in detail. It is that the argument licenses whoever holds it to determine which speech is protected, and licenses them in advance, since an opponent who challenges the classification can be assigned, by the classification, to the camp whose speech the theory withdraws. A doctrine that answers its critics by reclassifying them has made itself the judge of its own case, and the history of institutions suggests that such licences are exercised by the powerful against the weak more reliably than the reverse. Wolff, Marcuse's own co-author in the volume, pressed a version of this point from within the same book: the pluralist defence of tolerance rests precisely on the refusal to let any doctrine, however enlightened, appoint itself the border guard of permissible speech, because the appointment, once made, will be inherited by hands the doctrine does not choose.

The second objection is institutional. Liberating tolerance cannot remain a disposition; to withdraw speech and assembly rights it must be enforced, and enforcement requires an authority armed with exactly the concentrated power the rest of Marcuse's work identifies as the disease. The essay's defender may reply that the existing order already enforces its tilt informally, through ownership and access, and that the proposal merely makes the enforcement honest and reverses its direction. The objector answers that informal tilts can be fought, exposed and out-organised, while a formal apparatus of discriminating tolerance, once built, belongs to whoever captures it next. The argument's form, moreover, is perfectly portable. Any camp can adopt the structure, substitute its own classification of the regressive, and cite the essay's logic as its warrant, a symmetry the essay's author would have deplored and could not, within his premises, forbid. An argument that arms its enemies as reliably as its friends has a defect that no correctness in its first application can repair.

The third objection is epistemic, and it reaches the whole theory of which the essay is a fragment. The emergency diagnosis, the claim that background manipulation has already voided the democratic process, is doing the justificatory work: only if discussion is already rigged does rigging it back become liberation. But the diagnosis is itself one of the contested positions in the discussion the essay would regulate. Citizens who dispute the diagnosis, who hold that the process, for all its corruptions, remains the only instrument for correcting them, are not answered by the essay; they are diagnosed by it, their disagreement entered as evidence of the manipulation they deny. The profile has met this circularity before, at the level of needs, and it returns at the level of speech in its most dangerous form. A theory may of course be true and circular in the mouths of its opponents. The objection's force is practical rather than logical. A remedy whose justification cannot be established by the procedures it suspends must be imposed, and an imposed liberation of tolerance contradicts the freedom it invokes more nakedly than the administered tolerance it replaces.

What remains, when the objections have had their full say, is the diagnostic residue, and it is not nothing. The essay's demonstration that formal equality of treatment can entrench substantive inequality of voice, that a market of ideas with radically unequal endowments does not clear like a market in grain, and that the established order's tolerance of harmless dissent can function as a legitimating display, retains its force for readers who reject every remedy the essay proposes. The series' ruling, and this profile's practice, is to record the essay accurately, to refuse both the devotional and the prosecutorial summary, and to leave the reader in possession of the text and its strongest enemies. That is the treatment due to the most dangerous pages a serious thinker wrote, and it is the only treatment compatible with the tolerance, of the ordinary kind, that scholarship owes its subjects.

## Marcuse and the New Left: the reluctant guru

The reception that made him famous arrived through channels no publisher controls. Translations multiplied, authorised and pirated; the books appeared in student editions, in excerpts pasted into movement newspapers, in the bibliographies of courses their author never taught. In West Germany the students of the Socialist German Student League read him as a theorist of their own revolt, and in 1967 he travelled to Berlin to lecture at the Free University on the end of utopia, arguing to a hall of activists that the material preconditions for a free society had, for the first time, substantially arrived, and that the obstacle was no longer scarcity but the organisation of abundance. In France his name travelled with the events of May 1968, attached to slogans he had not written. In the United States the civil rights struggle's radical wing, the movement against the Vietnam war and the new campus left found in One-Dimensional Man an account of why prosperity had not pacified them, and in Eros and Civilization a warrant for treating their discontent as knowledge rather than maladjustment. The series' separate study of [the New Left as a formation](/2016/03/01/new-left-marxism-explained/) gives the movement its own history; this profile is concerned with the philosopher's conduct inside a fame he had not sought.

### Why did Marcuse refuse the title the movements gave him?

Partly from accuracy and partly from doctrine. The accuracy was chronological absurdity: a man approaching seventy, formed by the defeats of 1919 and 1933, could not be the father of a movement whose members were his grandchildren's age, and he told interviewers the New Left had made itself out of its own experience of war, racism and administered prosperity.

The doctrine ran deeper. A movement that accepts a father accepts an authority, and his whole theory warned against the substitution of leaders for the self-activity of the oppressed. He would be a teacher to the movement, a resource and a critic. He would not be its proprietor, and he declined the title in the same spirit in which he declined, all his life, to found a school.

His criticism of the movement, delivered while the movement was lionising him, is the strongest evidence that the refusal was sincere. He attacked the cult of action for its own sake, the contempt for theory that mistook impatience for radicalism, and the reproduction, inside liberation movements, of the aggressiveness and machismo of the order they opposed. He defended the universities against the charge that they were merely factories of the system, pointing out that they remained among the few places where the second dimension was still, however precariously, cultivated. When the Frankfurt Institute was occupied by students in 1969 and Adorno called the police, the ensuing correspondence between the two old exiles showed Marcuse threading the position he had built for himself: solidarity with the students' aims, refusal of their methods where the methods destroyed the spaces of thought, and anger at his friend for answering an occupation with state force. The movement press printed the solidarity and skipped the refusals, which is what movement presses do. The profile restores both, because the man's actual relation to his fame, grateful, critical and unpossessive, is more instructive than either the guru of the admirers or the Svengali of the detractors.

The establishment's counter-image deserves the same restoration. To the California authorities of the late nineteen sixties he appeared not as a sage but as a threat: the governor of the state publicly pressed the university over his employment, veterans' organisations campaigned against him, and he received threats that briefly required protection. He responded with a lecturer's weapons, continuing to teach, publishing a defence of academic freedom's substance against its administrators, and remarking that a society confident in its freedoms does not fear a professor of philosophy in his seventies. The episode belongs to the record because it tested the theory in the author's own person. The administered society, confronted with a critic it could not absorb by the usual methods, reached for the older ones, and found them rusting: the contract was renewed, the lectures continued, and the attempt stands as a small empirical footnote to the affluence thesis. Absorption remained the preferred technique because it usually worked. With Marcuse it had failed, and the failure is part of why the students trusted him.

## The teacher: Brandeis, San Diego and Angela Davis

The professorships came late and mattered more to him than the fame. At Brandeis from 1954 to 1965 he taught political theory and philosophy to the first generations of postwar American undergraduates, and the witness of students from those years is consistent: a lecturer of old-world formality and unexpected warmth, who treated undergraduates as capable of Hegel and expected them to prove it. Among them was Angela Davis, who arrived at Brandeis on a scholarship, studied French literature, and found in Marcuse's courses the philosophical formation she had been looking for. He recognised the ability, directed her toward graduate study, and when she went to Frankfurt to study with Adorno and then returned to California, she resumed work under his supervision at San Diego. The connection became public history in 1969, when the University of California's regents dismissed Davis from her teaching post at Los Angeles on grounds of her party membership, and her old teacher defended her in print and in person, at some cost to his own standing with the same authorities then pressing his own university. The episode is usually filed under the politics of the period. It belongs equally under the ethics of teaching: the guru of the headlines spent his capital, without hesitation, on a student.

The move to the University of California at San Diego in 1965, forced by Brandeis's retirement rule, gave him a second classroom for his last fourteen years, and a different one: a new campus, a frontier state's students, the Pacific light he professed to find suspiciously kind. He taught there through the years of fame, refusing to convert the lectureship into a pulpit, and students who expected a revolutionary elder met instead a demanding European professor who set long readings and marked hard. The testimony matters to the profile because it corrects the last of the standard misreadings on the brief's list. Marcuse was not a student radical, in temperament, generation or method. He was a scholar of the old European type who happened to write the books a radical generation needed, and the difference explains both the books' durability and his discomfort with their readers' slogans. A student radical's work dates with the movement. A philosopher's work, if it is any good, survives the movement that adopted it, and his has.

## The Great Refusal and its open end

Every reader of the late books meets the phrase, and most leave it as they found it, a stirring blank. The Great Refusal, a term Marcuse acknowledged borrowing from Alfred North Whitehead, names the protest against that which is, the refusal of the administered individual to continue performing his own unfreedom, carried in Eros and Civilization by the images of art and phantasy and in One-Dimensional Man by the bare possibility that the integrated might yet say no. Critics have treated the phrase's vagueness as a defect to be excused or a fraud to be exposed. The profile takes the position the brief directs: the vagueness is deliberate, structural, and must be explained rather than filled in. A refusal that could be specified in advance, with its agent named, its programme drafted and its tactics enumerated, would already have been absorbed into the universe it refuses, formatted as one more option on the administered menu. The refusal has to remain, in part, the name of a capacity rather than the description of a plan, because everything describable in the system's language is, by the theory's own logic, already the system's.

### Why does the Great Refusal refuse definition?

Because specification was the trap the concept was built to avoid. Marcuse had watched the twentieth century's specified alternatives congeal into new administrations, and his theory of absorption predicted that any blueprint issued in the available vocabulary would be processed by the available institutions.

What he could do, and did, was point to where refusal actually survived: in art's refusal to reconcile, in the marginal populations the integration had not reached, in the memory and phantasy that preserve a standard the present fails. The openness is the doctrine's honesty about its own limit. A theory of total administration that produced its exit on demand would refute itself.

This does not acquit the concept; it locates the charge correctly. The Great Refusal functions in the system the way the unmoved mover functions in older theologies, as the point where explanation stops and fidelity begins, and readers who require a politics will find a placeholder where the politics should be. Marcuse's later work tried to give the placeholder content without closing it: the new sensibility of An Essay on Liberation, an aesthetic and erotic re-education of perception that might precede and prepare structural change; the insistence, in Counterrevolution and Revolt, that the refusal must become organisation or remain a gesture. The tension was never resolved, and the profile presents it unresolved, as the argument's known open end. What can be said in the concept's defence is that its author knew the difference between a refusal and a programme, and refused, at some cost to his reputation for rigour, to counterfeit the second when he only had the first.

## Who can change society? The substratum beneath the integrated

One-Dimensional Man closes with the passage its author knew would be the most contested in the book. Underneath the integrated population, he wrote, there persists a substratum of those the integration never reached: the outcasts and outsiders, the exploited and persecuted of other races and other colours, the unemployed and the unemployable. Their opposition, he argued, strikes the system from outside a process they were never inside, and is therefore revolutionary in its location even where its consciousness remains undeveloped. The book's final movement joins this sociology to a sentence borrowed from Walter Benjamin, that hope is given "only for the sake of those without hope", and stops there, in a minor key its author chose deliberately. The gesture scandalised two audiences at once. Orthodox Marxists heard the abandonment of the proletariat, the theory's designated agent since 1848, in favour of a marginal fringe with no leverage at production's heart. Liberals heard a romanticisation of the margins by a professor who would not have to live in them.

The fairest reading situates the passage inside the book's own logic and inside the departure from classical Marxism that the series examines head-on in its [comparison of critical theory with the classical framework](/2015/09/01/critical-theory-vs-classical-marxism/). The passage is not a recruitment poster; it is a consistency proof. If the affluence thesis is true, and the industrial working class of the advanced countries has been integrated by the mechanism the book describes, then the standpoint outside the one-dimensional universe cannot be assigned to that class by doctrinal courtesy. It must be found where integration demonstrably failed. Marcuse's candidate locations followed from the evidence available to him in 1964 America: the racial order's victims, whose exclusion from the affluence was the affluence's own condition; the colonial world's insurgencies, then at flood tide; the permanently unemployed whom the automation debates of the decade had made visible. He offered the location as a deduction from his premises, and he hedged it with the concession his critics rarely quote: the substratum's opposition hits the system from without, but nothing guarantees that the blow becomes liberation rather than a new barbarism, and the book's last pages decline to guarantee it.

His later answers widened the cast without revising the logic. The student movement appeared to him, in the years of fame, as a catalytic stratum, not a revolutionary class: educated inside the system's own institutions, alienated by the gap between the system's promises and its wars, capable of detonating a crisis it could not itself resolve. The new working class of technicians and white-collar employees, proposed by French theorists as the successor agent, he treated with interest and reserve, noting that technical intelligence inside the apparatus was a possible carrier of refusal precisely because it knew the machine from within. Women's liberation and the ecological movement, in the essays of his final decade, he greeted as carriers of the new sensibility, movements whose demands could not be met by delivering more of the same goods. The pattern across all these judgements is the one the substratum passage established. The agent of change, for the late Marcuse, is whoever the integration failed to digest, and the identity of that agent is an empirical question each generation answers from its own exclusions, not a title conferred in advance by the theory.

## The strongest objection: a theory that satisfaction cannot refute

The objection must now be given the floor at the strength the brief requires, because a profile that protects its subject from it teaches the reader nothing. The objection has two blades. The first is falsification. A theory which holds that satisfaction is itself the mechanism of domination has arranged its terms so that no improvement in people's condition can count against it. Wages rise: integration deepens. Leisure expands: administration extends. Protest flourishes: absorption at work. Misery persists: the system's violence exposed. Every possible observation confirms the theory, and a theory that every observation confirms, the objector concludes, describes the theorist's categories rather than the world. The second blade is epistemic arrogance. The doctrine of false needs positions the theorist as uniquely able to identify needs that the people holding them cannot recognise as false, although the needs are felt, chosen and defended by their holders with every appearance of sincerity. By what instrument does the philosopher audit the desires of millions and return a verdict of falsity? If the instrument is the desires' origin in advertising and administration, the objector notes that all desires have social origins, including the theorist's own desire for liberation, and the criterion dissolves into the theorist's preferences wearing the costume of science.

Marcuse's answer, assembled from across the work, has more structure than the polemics usually allow. The basic and surplus distinction of the Freud book was designed precisely as the criterion the objection demands: a need or a renunciation is indicted not because the theorist dislikes it but because it exceeds what the attained level of productivity and knowledge requires for survival and flourishing, an excess measurable, in principle, against the material record. The civilisation that can feed everyone and shortens the working day instead chooses armaments and manufactured obsolescence has, on this criterion, an objective surplus of repression to answer for, and its members' contentment does not acquit it, because the contentment is purchased with the surplus. The answer to the first blade is parallel. The theory is not in fact compatible with every observation. It would be damaged, for instance, by an affluent order in which rising satisfaction visibly strengthened independent, system-negating movements among the satisfied themselves, or by an administered culture whose products routinely sharpened the second dimension instead of anaesthetising it. That the objector can imagine such evidence shows the thesis has empirical content; that Marcuse's defenders can dispute whether the evidence has appeared shows the content is genuinely contestable rather than empty.

The concession, and the profile makes it without flinching, is that the criterion does not close the gap the objection opens. Measuring surplus repression requires a standard of what human life, at a given level of development, could be: free of avoidable toil, of manufactured aggression, of the conversion of persons into performances. That standard is a philosophical anthropology, a picture of the human essence and its possible flourishing, inherited by Marcuse from the young Marx of the species-being manuscripts and from Freud's speculative biology, and it is nowhere demonstrated with the rigour the objection demands. The theory can show that the existing order wastes what it produces and administers what it satisfies. To call the waste domination and the satisfaction false, it needs the anthropology, and the anthropology remains an inheritance defended by intuition and tradition rather than a premise secured by argument. The same gap was identified in this series' treatment of the alienation cluster, in the young Marx himself, and it propagates faithfully into the Frankfurt descendant. The honest verdict is therefore two-storeyed. The affluence thesis, as a mechanism, survives its critics: integration through administered satisfaction is a real process, describable and observable, and naming it is a permanent contribution. The theory of needs built on the mechanism carries an unproven foundation, and readers who accept the mechanism while bracketing the foundation hold a defensible position that the theory's own author, to his credit, made it possible to state.

## What he conceded about the working class

The extra honesty the theory's critics rarely credit concerns its author's own revisions. Marcuse never withdrew the integration thesis, but across the nineteen sixties and seventies he conceded, in interviews, lectures and the essays of Counterrevolution and Revolt, that the working class of the advanced countries was a less finished case than One-Dimensional Man's bleakest pages suggested. The strikes that shook France in 1968, in which millions of workers occupied factories in a movement that overflowed the unions supposedly integrating them, he treated as evidence that integration described a tendency of the system, not a destiny of the class: the administered consciousness could crack, and when it cracked it cracked along the old seams of the labour process. He conceded likewise that the book had understated the system's continuing dependence on misery at its periphery, the colonial and postcolonial labour whose deprivation subsidised the metropolis's affluence, and that the substratum passage had gestured at a connection the argument should have developed. These concessions do not dissolve the thesis. They return it to its proper status, as the analysis of a mechanism with counter-tendencies, written by a man who revised in public when the evidence knocked. For a profile whose governing claim is that this thinker's framework survived the disappearance of its conditions, the concessions are part of the explanation: frameworks survive by having authors, and readers, willing to mark where they bend.

## The last books: liberation, revolt and the aesthetic dimension

The decade of fame produced four more books, and they show the theory being tested against its own success. An Essay on Liberation, published in 1969 and written in the heat of the movements, is the most optimistic text he ever released: its wager is that the system had begun to produce, inside itself, a new sensibility among the young, a revulsion against the performance principle's whole texture of life, in which the aesthetic and the erotic cease to be decorations and become political forces in embryo. Five Lectures and the essays gathered as Negations carried the argument into the universities. Counterrevolution and Revolt, in 1972, is the sober reckoning the title promises: it anatomises the system's counter-attack, criticises the New Left's disintegration and its flirtations with the very violence it opposed, and turns, in chapters that startled his readers, toward the ecology movement and the women's movement as the carriers of demands the affluent order cannot satisfy by delivering more of itself. Nature appears in the late work as the system's great outside, an ally with its own claim, and liberation is redescribed to include the liberation of nature from its status as mere material.

The final book, The Aesthetic Dimension of 1978, written against orthodox Marxist aesthetics and against the anti-art temper of the movements alike, returns to the profile's beginning: to the artist novel of 1922 and the question of what unreconciled form knows. Art's political power, the old man argued, lies precisely in its autonomy, in the formed work's refusal to deliver the present back to itself undistorted. In the aesthetic form, reality is indicted by the image of what it excludes, and that indictment survives every administration because it cannot be paraphrased into a message the administration could use. The book is slight in bulk and valedictory in tone, and it closes the arc the three books table opened. Reason, desire and opposition had each been shown closing under administration. Form, the shape a work gives its materials, is named at the end as the dimension that administration could not fully close, because a formed work keeps the negation in the only vault the system cannot audit: the experience of the person who stands before it and feels, without being told, that the world might be otherwise.

He died on 29 July 1979, in Starnberg, in the Bavarian countryside outside Munich, ten days after his eighty-first birthday, having travelled to Germany to lecture and to visit the intellectual world he had left forty-six years before. A stroke ended the journey the exile had begun in 1933, and the symmetry was noted by every obituarist: the refugee analyst, the American professor, the supposed guru, dying in the country that had formed and expelled him, his reputation at that hour sunk into the reaction against everything the sixties were taken to mean. The papers he left, the correspondence with Heidegger and Adorno among them, passed into archives that scholars have mined since, and the mining has consistently complicated the caricature in the direction this profile has followed: toward the Hegelian scholar, the government analyst, the Freudian speculator, the reluctant celebrity, the teacher. The books remain in print, the arguments unsettled, the questions, about satisfaction and its discontents, about who may call a need false, about tolerance and its limits, still doing their slow work in readers who have never heard the slogans of the decade that made him famous.

## Freud contested: the quarrel with the neo-Freudians

The Freud book's first serious fight was with Freud's own heirs, and the fight fixes the book's method better than any summary. Eros and Civilization closes with an epilogue attacking the neo-Freudian revisionists, the school of Erich Fromm, Karen Horney and Harry Stack Sullivan, who had softened Freud's instinct theory into a doctrine of interpersonal adjustment. Where Freud's metapsychology held that civilisation rests on the tragic repression of recalcitrant drives, the revisionists dissolved the drives into social attitudes and the tragedy into a problem of better relationships. Marcuse's charge was that the softening was an ideology in his technical sense: it removed from psychoanalysis exactly the content that made the theory dangerous to the established order, the irreconcilable remainder of instinct that no administration can fully digest. When Fromm answered in the pages of Dissent, defending the revision as humane social science against a speculative radicalism he considered empty, the exchange became the era's clearest public contest over what psychoanalysis was for. Marcuse's rejoinder insisted that Freud's most speculative constructions, the death instinct, the primal horde, the phylogenetic fantasies the clinicians were embarrassed by, were the theory's critical core, because they alone refused to treat the existing organisation of instinct as natural.

The episode matters beyond its period because it shows the theory's characteristic move in its purest arena. Confronted with a body of thought, Marcuse asks what in it cannot be integrated, and defends precisely that. In Hegel it was the negative; in Freud it was the death-haunted instinctual remainder; in the affluent society it would be the substratum and the new sensibility. The move is the same, and so is the vulnerability. A critic may always ask whether the unintegrable remainder has been discovered in the material or imported into it by a reader determined to find it, and Fromm's version of that question, that Marcuse's Freud is a philosophy wearing psychoanalysis as a costume, has never been conclusively answered. The profile records the standoff because it is the theory in miniature: a reading strong enough to reorganise a field, contestable at exactly the point where its evidence runs out and its anthropology begins.

## Memory and phantasy: the preserved dimension

Two faculties carry the second dimension inside the individual, in the Freud book's psychology, and they repay a section of their own because the later work keeps returning to them. Memory, first: psychoanalysis had taught that the individual remembers, beneath the amnesia of adjustment, the experience of gratification that preceded renunciation, and that the return of this repressed material in dreams, symptoms and art is the past's protest against the present. Marcuse enlarges the point into a philosophy of history. What memory preserves is not only private pleasure but the standing evidence that the reality principle's current form is not the only form reality has taken or could take. A society that administers memory, through the heritage industry, through the scheduling of nostalgia, through the conversion of the past into a style, is attacking the tribunal before which the present might be judged. Phantasy, second: the imagination is the one mental faculty the performance principle cannot wholly conscript, because its products are by definition not performances. Phantasy keeps the Great Refusal in storage. It insists, against the whole weight of the actual, on the image of gratification without domination, and art is phantasy given form and therefore given duration. The alliance of memory and phantasy explains a peculiarity of the late work that puzzles readers who expect a politics: the aesthetic keeps displacing the programmatic because, on this psychology, the imagination is not the politics' decoration. It is its last unconscripted organ.

The argument has a darker chamber, and honesty requires entering it. Freud's speculative history, which Marcuse adopts, reads civilisation as the return of repressed domination: the primal father's tyranny, overthrown by the sons, reinstated by the sons themselves in the form of conscience and law, so that rebellion reproduces what it rebels against. This is the deep source of the pessimism that runs beneath the affluence thesis like a ground bass. If domination is reinstalled by its victims' own psychic economy, then no merely institutional revolution escapes the pattern, and the liberation the theory wants must reach the instincts themselves, a demand so deep that its satisfaction can barely be pictured, let alone organised. The Great Refusal's vagueness, examined earlier, begins here, in a psychology that sets liberation's bar at the remaking of human nature and then declines to fake the remaking. Critics who find the late work aporetic are right, and the aporia is Freudian before it is political.

## One-dimensional thought: the quarrel with the philosophers

The famous book's least read chapters are its most philosophical, and they contain the argument that generalises the whole theory. If the administered society closes the universe of discourse, it must also close the disciplines that police discourse, and Marcuse devoted long sections to showing how the era's dominant philosophies performed the closure on themselves. His targets were operationalism in the sciences, which defines a concept by the operations that measure it and thereby expels every meaning that exceeds measurement, and the ordinary-language philosophy descended from the later Wittgenstein, which proposed to dissolve philosophical problems as confusions of grammar. Against the second he pressed the harder charge. A philosophy that takes ordinary language as its tribunal accepts, without argument, the language of the administered world as the measure of sense. The transcendent concepts, the words that point beyond the given, freedom, justice, the good life, are audited against usage and found imprecise, and their imprecision is the whole of their offence. For Marcuse those concepts are not confusions awaiting therapy. They are the second dimension's remaining embassies inside speech, and a philosophy that retires them has not cured thought of metaphysics. It has cured thought of negation.

The sociologists received the parallel treatment. The empirical social science of the period, he argued, had perfected techniques for studying the administered population's behaviour while surrendering the concepts that would let the behaviour be judged: its findings were impeccable and its universe closed, a science that could measure alienation's symptoms with increasing precision while striking the word alienation from its vocabulary as unoperational. Here the profile should register the strongest counter, since the exchange illuminates the book's genuine limits. The philosophers and sociologists replied, in reviews and in the era's journals, that Marcuse had mistaken methodological discipline for political capitulation, that a concept's vagueness is a real defect and not a certificate of depth, and that his own key terms, administered, one-dimensional, false, were never subjected to the operational scrutiny he derided others for applying. The charge lands, in part. The book's central vocabulary is evocative where its opponents' is exact, and a reader must decide whether the evocation is the price of naming something the exact vocabularies are built to miss, or the symptom of a theory that prefers resonance to testability. That decision is the book's true examination, and it cannot be taken on the author's authority.

## Automation and the obsolescence of toil

One strand of the 1964 argument has been so thoroughly vindicated in form, whatever one thinks of its politics, that it deserves separation from the rest. Marcuse observed that automation was dissolving the material basis of the performance principle. As machines absorbed the quantitative burden of necessary labour, the old justification for organising life around toil, that society's survival requires everyone's full allotment of alienated work, lost its factual ground. The apparatus could, in principle, deliver the goods with a shrinking fraction of the population's waking life, which meant that the perpetuation of full employment in increasingly make-work forms had become a political choice dressed as an economic necessity. The system, on this account, must manufacture occupations, needs and scarcities to preserve the discipline that its own technology has made obsolete, and the waste that results, the planned obsolescence, the make-work bureaucracies, the military absorption of surplus capacity, is not a malfunction. It is the price of keeping the performance principle in force after its material warrant has expired.

The strand matters for two reasons. It is, first, the most nearly testable of the book's claims, the point where the theory commits itself to a describable economic mechanism, and debates about automation's dislocations have been conducted in its shadow ever since, usually without attribution. It is, second, the point where the affluence thesis joins hands with the Freud book. Surplus repression, the renunciation beyond necessity, finds its industrial definition here: toil maintained past the point of need is surplus repression in its daily form, and the civilisation that could shorten the working day and instead lengthens the shopping list has confessed its priorities in the plainest available language. Marcuse's counter-image was never idleness as the system defines it, recovery for further performance. It was the transformation of work itself toward the character of play and experiment, Schiller's old wager restated for the automated age, and the wager's extravagance is deliberate. A theory measuring liberation by the abolition of unnecessary toil must be willing to say what the freed time is for, and his answer, the erotic and aesthetic shaping of life, is the answer of the Gymnasium boy of 1914 grown into the philosopher of 1964, unchanged in the conviction that culture is what necessity is for.

## The mirror diagnosis: the end of ideology

The famous book did not enter an empty field, and its sharpest contemporary foil clarifies it by opposition. In 1960 the sociologist Daniel Bell had announced the end of ideology in the West: the grand totalising doctrines, he argued, were exhausted, their passions spent, and the affluent democracies had settled into a pragmatic politics of adjustment in which the old revolutionary questions no longer arose. Superficially, Bell and Marcuse agreed on the phenomenon. Both saw a working class integrated, a revolutionary temperature fallen, a politics of management replacing a politics of transformation. Their interpretations were mirror images. For Bell the exhaustion of ideology was maturity, the growing-up of societies that had learned to solve problems piecemeal. For Marcuse the same exhaustion was the symptom the whole book exists to diagnose: ideology had not ended but migrated, out of the doctrines where it could be argued with and into the productive process itself, where it operated as the unexamined obviousness of the given. What Bell celebrated as the end of ideology Marcuse renamed as ideology's perfection, the stage at which it no longer needs believers because it has arranged the world its beliefs described.

An American voice closer to Marcuse's own register confirms that the diagnosis was not a European import. C. Wright Mills, the Texas sociologist whose Sociological Imagination had appeared in 1959, described the emerging type of the administered society as the cheerful robot, the man who accepts his alienation with a smile because the levers that move him have been interiorised as his own desires. Mills died in 1962, before the famous book appeared, and the two men worked the same seam from different traditions, Mills from an American radicalism of the frontier and the labour movement, Marcuse from Hegel and Freud. The convergence is evidence the profile weighs in the theory's favour. A diagnosis reached independently, from unlike premises, by observers with no school in common, is harder to dismiss as one tradition's hallucination than a doctrine manufactured inside a single institute. The end-of-ideology debate has been forgotten by most of its participants' disciplines. Read now, it looks like the moment the affluent society described itself twice, once with satisfaction and once with alarm, and the two descriptions remain the standing alternatives for judging it.

## The return that never happened: Frankfurt restored, Marcuse in America

A biographical road not taken runs beneath the American chapters. The Institute itself went home: Horkheimer and Adorno returned to Frankfurt at the decade's turn, rebuilt the school in the ruins, and became fixtures of the Federal Republic's intellectual life. Marcuse visited, lectured and remained, in the decisive sense, behind. The choice has been read as gratitude, as Americanisation, as estrangement from the old circle, and the record supports a plainer account. His working life had been rebuilt in the United States: the government decade, the fellowships, the professorships, a son educated in America, an audience forming in English. The philosophy itself had followed the biography. The books after 1941 were written in English, about materials increasingly American, for readers the exile had made his own. When the German students of 1968 claimed him, they were importing an American author whose German formation lay thirty years behind him, and the doubleness suited the theory he had built. He was the insider-outsider of both societies, the analyst who had served one state and been expelled by the other, and the one-dimensional diagnosis drew its authority from exactly that double exposure. The profile notes the road not taken because it explains the work's final shape. There is no German Marcuse period after the exile, no homecoming synthesis. There is an American philosopher with a German mind, and the friction between the two is the sound the books make.

## Feminism and the qualities the system suppresses

In 1974, at Stanford, the seventy-five-year-old philosopher delivered a lecture on Marxism and feminism that his younger admirers received as a benediction and his older critics as a puzzle. Its argument extends the affluence thesis into territory the three books had left unmapped. The qualities the performance principle must suppress to reproduce itself, he proposed, receptivity, tenderness, non-violence, the refusal to treat persons and nature as material, had been historically assigned to women and confined, with them, to the private sphere. A movement that raised those qualities from private virtues into social forces would therefore not be adding one more demand to the system's agenda. It would be contesting the anthropological basis on which the agenda rests, which is why he called the women's movement, in its radical form, potentially the most subversive of the era's movements: its satisfaction could not be delivered as a commodity. The lecture's second half kept the theory's characteristic discipline. He warned the movement against the goal of mere equality inside the performance principle, the right to compete on the terms that were themselves the problem, since integration into the administered order on equal terms would extend the order rather than negate it.

The episode belongs in the profile for what it shows about the framework's ageing. A theory is usually finished when its author stops learning from events, and the late Marcuse conspicuously did not stop. Feminism and ecology entered his work not as decorations of fashion but through the theory's own logic, as movements whose demands the affluence mechanism could not process, and he submitted his earlier categories to their correction in public, conceding for instance that the substratum of 1964 had been drawn too narrowly around the exclusions his generation could see. The concessions pattern, already noted in the case of the working class, is the same: the mechanism retained, its map of carriers revised. Readers deciding what the framework is worth should weigh this habit heavily. Doctrines that cannot revise their sociology become period pieces. This one revised its sociology three times in fifteen years while keeping its engine intact, which is either adaptability or evasion, and the profile's verdict, given in the objection section, is that the record supports adaptability in the sociology and evasion only where the anthropology is concerned.

## The critics from two flanks: MacIntyre and Mattick

The sharpest short book against him came from a philosopher who shared half his formation. Alasdair MacIntyre's monograph of 1970, an exposition and a polemic as its subtitle honestly announced, granted Marcuse the best statement of his case in English before dismantling its foundations. MacIntyre's central charge was surgical: the theory's decisive premises, that the satisfied masses' needs are false and their consciousness administered, are never argued but presupposed, and everything else in the system is commentary on the presupposition. Where the classical Marxist could at least point to the worker's experience of exploitation as the fulcrum of critique, Marcuse's premises place the fulcrum inside the theorist's own judgement, and MacIntyre pressed the biographical corollary with a polemicist's timing. A philosophy that finds the entire population of the advanced world deluded, and its own author exempt, owes an account of the exemption, and none is given. The profile has already staged this objection in its general form; MacIntyre's version earns its place as the form's most economical execution, and his monograph remains the first text a serious reader should take up after the famous book itself, because it teaches exactly where the load-bearing assertions sit.

From the opposite flank came the Marxist objection, and its ablest carrier was Paul Mattick, the council communist economist whose critique of 1972 charged Marcuse with abandoning the critique of political economy altogether. On Mattick's reading, the one-dimensional thesis mistook a long postwar boom for a permanent condition, treated ideology and consumption as autonomous forces because it had ceased to analyse capital accumulation, and dissolved the working class as an agent at the very moment when its objective position in production remained, for a materialist, decisive. Where MacIntyre found too much unargued theory, Mattick found too little economics, and the pincer is instructive. The famous book stands between a liberal objection that its premises are undemocratic and a Marxist objection that its premises are un-Marxist, and the two objections cannot both be answered by the same defence. Marcuse's replies, scattered through the essays of his last decade, conceded Mattick's point about the boom more readily than MacIntyre's about the premises, which is consistent with this profile's two-storeyed verdict. The mechanism could be dated and corrected. The anthropology could only be reaffirmed.

## The second generation answers: Habermas and the turn to communication

The most consequential response came from inside the school's own succession. Jürgen Habermas, the leading figure of the Frankfurt School's second generation, began from Marcuse's diagnosis and rebuilt its foundations in the act of answering it. His essay of 1968 on technology and science as ideology took up the technological rationality thesis directly, granting its description of a depoliticised public sphere while relocating the mechanism: where Marcuse saw reason itself become domination, Habermas distinguished kinds of action and knowledge, labour from interaction, the technical from the communicative, and argued that the pathology lay in the colonisation of one sphere by the other rather than in rationality as such. The correction preserved the critical ambition while reopening an exit Marcuse had closed. If human beings carry a second, communicative rationality that administration distorts but cannot create, then the second dimension has a home inside ordinary practice, in speech itself, and critique need not wait for a substratum or a refusal to found it.

The exchange defines the school's later history, and the profile records it without taking the second generation's side. Habermas's reconstruction gained testability and lost temperature: the erotic and aesthetic depths of the Freud book, the whole anthropology of liberation, fall away in the turn to communication, and something Marcuse knew about desire and its administration is missing from the successor theory precisely where the successor is most rigorous. Marcuse, for his part, received the correction with the ambivalence of a founder watching his problem solved by being redefined. He acknowledged the younger man's power, disputed the separation of spheres as too clean for a world in which speech itself is administered, and let the argument rest. The reader who wants the systematic comparison of the school's frameworks with the classical doctrine they revised will find it in this series' later articles; here the point is biographical and final. The theory this profile has followed was strong enough to organise the research of the generation that refuted half of it, which is the most durable form of influence philosophy knows.

## Five misreadings, corrected

The brief for this profile lists the errors that competing coverage repeats, and they can now be corrected from the record the profile has assembled. The first makes him a student radical, a young firebrand of the nineteen sixties. He was sixty-six when the famous book appeared, formed by the Kaiserreich's schools, defeated in a revolution half a century old, and he conducted himself throughout the fame as what he was, a European professor of the old school who set hard readings and disliked slogans. The second installs him as the father of the New Left with his own blessing. He rejected the title in interviews throughout the period, on the record, for reasons of chronology and doctrine alike. The third reduces the tolerance essay to a demand for censorship in the ordinary legal sense. The essay proposes no statute and no censor's office; it proposes a discriminating withdrawal of tolerance from specified movements, extending to speech and assembly, justified by an emergency diagnosis the reader is free to reject, and the profile has stated the argument and its strongest enemies in full, which is the only correction either caricature, the censor's charter or the prophet's licence, cannot survive.

The fourth misreading flattens one-dimensionality into conformity of opinion. The administered society of the book does not think alike; it thinks variously inside categories it did not choose, and its opinions are free precisely where freedom no longer reaches the foundations. The fifth makes Eros and Civilization a call for sexual liberation in the sense the counterculture practised and the market supplied. The book's argument indicts that very commerce: a liberation sold back to the liberated as lifestyle is, in its author's terms, the performance principle in carnival dress, and the liberation the book actually imagines is a transformation of work, time and need so deep that the adjective erotic describes a civilisation, not a weekend. Each correction follows the same pattern as the corrections throughout this cluster. The popular version is not false in the way a date is false. It is a true fragment, the guru's fame, the essay's asymmetry, the book's sexual candour, swollen until it hides the structure it came from, and the remedy in every case is the one this profile has applied: return the fragment to the record, and let the record argue.

## Marcuse reads the young Marx: the manuscripts intervention

One episode from the Freiburg years outranks the habilitation in its consequences, and the profile has kept it for the place where its consequences become visible. In 1932 the first complete edition of Marx's early writings published the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, the Paris notebooks in which the twenty-six-year-old Marx had worked out his account of alienated labour, private property and the human essence he called species being. Marcuse was among the very first to review them, in an essay on the new sources for the foundation of historical materialism printed that year, and his reading set the course of his life's work. The manuscripts, he argued, were not juvenilia to be indulged on the way to the mature science. They disclosed the philosophical foundation on which the whole critique of political economy rests: a conception of the human being as a creature that objectifies itself in labour, is expropriated from its objectification under private property, and can be restored to itself only by a transformation that is therefore anthropological before it is economic. Historical materialism, read through the manuscripts, is a theory of liberation founded on a picture of what human beings are.

The argument's audacity in 1932 is hard to recover. The orthodox Marxisms of both great Internationals treated the early Marx as an embarrassment or a prelude, and the dialectical materialism then being codified in Moscow had no use for an anthropology of essence. Marcuse's essay insisted on the continuity: the critique of political economy presupposes the philosophy of man, and a Marxism that forgets its foundation in the manuscripts will mistake its own categories for nature. Every later position of his is latent in that insistence. The surplus repression criterion needs the young Marx's picture of unalienated life. The doctrine of false needs needs his account of needs distorted by property. Even the tolerance essay's emergency reasoning leans on the claim that consciousness is formed by the relations it lives inside, which is the manuscripts' first lesson. The profile has marked the anthropology as the theory's unproven foundation, and the 1932 essay is where the foundation was laid, by a thirty-four-year-old Privatdozent reviewing a dead man's notebooks in the last year of the Weimar Republic. That the same manuscripts became, after the war, the charter of Marxist humanism across Europe is a reception he did more than anyone to prepare.

## Neither Washington nor Moscow: the third position

The Soviet Marxism book of 1958 had a sequel in conduct. Through the decades of the Cold War Marcuse maintained the double refusal his analyst's decade had built: the Western order was administered unfreedom with consumer goods, and the Soviet order was administered unfreedom without them, and he declined the services of both propagandas with a consistency that left him, for long stretches, without a political home. The test came in 1968, the year of his maximum fame, when the reform movement in Czechoslovakia attempted to build what its leaders called socialism with a human face. Marcuse welcomed the Prague experiment publicly and without the reservations he attached to most movements that quoted him, because it seemed to him the one political process of the era aimed at the whole of his critique: an administered society attempting, from inside its own institutions, to recover the second dimension, to subordinate the apparatus to deliberation and the performance principle to human need. The Warsaw Pact invasion that crushed the experiment in August of that year he condemned in the same breath as he condemned the war his own country was conducting in Vietnam, and the pairing was the position entire. A critic who can be conscripted by either bloc has already lost the standpoint the work exists to hold.

The stance cost him the orthodoxies in both directions and explains the shape of his political afterlife. The Communist parties never forgave the anatomy of 1958. The Western establishments that had employed the analyst never forgave the anatomy of 1964. What remained was the readership this profile has tracked throughout: students, dissidents in the East who read the critique of the West as a critique of administration as such, and the unaligned intellectuals of the West who needed a Marxism that had survived the century's evidence. He was, in the strict sense, a man of the third position in an age that kept abolishing thirds, and the profile counts the consistency among the work's credentials. A theorist of total administration who exempts one administration from the theory has refuted himself. Marcuse exempted none, including, in the end, the movements that marched under his name.

## London and the Dialectics of Liberation congress

The fame had one set-piece appearance, and it fixes the era's atmosphere better than any statistic. In July 1967 the Roundhouse in London hosted the Congress on the Dialectics of Liberation, a fortnight of lectures and happenings organised from the circle of the psychiatrist R. D. Laing, at which Marcuse appeared alongside Stokely Carmichael, Allen Ginsberg, Paul Goodman and an assembly of the decade's countercultural notables. His contribution, "Liberation from the Affluent Society", is the affluence thesis delivered as an address: the society that satisfies is the society that must be refused, and the refusal must begin in the instincts and the sensibility, because a liberation that leaves the administered needs intact will reproduce the administration under new management. The audience contained everyone the theory was about, and the transcript shows the philosopher at his most characteristic, patient with the euphoria around him, insistent that the point of the gathering was a transformation of human nature's social organisation and not a festival of its postponement.

The congress is worth its paragraph because it shows the reception at maximum concentration, and the maximum is the distortion's measure. Around his lecture swirled the era's whole theatre: guerrilla theatre, anti-psychiatry, Black Power, the poetry of the beats, and a press corps converting the fortnight into the legend of a movement with gurus. His actual text argued against the theatre's premise. Liberation, he told the hall, is not the release of what the affluent society has repressed into its subjects; the affluent society is delighted to sponsor releases, and sells the tickets. It is the building of subjects whose needs the society did not manufacture, a work of education and organisation measured in years. The students who carried his books into the streets of the following spring were carrying that argument too, whether or not the cameras noticed, and the distance between the congress's legend and its most famous lecture is the distance this whole profile exists to measure.

## The students he actually formed: Feenberg and the philosophy of technology

Against the millions who quoted him stands the small number he taught in the doctoral sense, and one of them carried the work into a field the master had only sketched. Andrew Feenberg took his doctorate under Marcuse at San Diego in 1972, worked as his assistant, and built, across the following decades, a critical theory of technology that is the most direct continuation of the 1941 essay and the famous book's central chapters. Feenberg's question is Marcuse's question with the engineering left in. If technological rationality is a form of domination embodied in design, then design is a political terrain: the artefacts and systems of an administered society encode the interests of their makers, and a democratic politics must contest the code itself rather than merely redistribute the products. The line from the teacher's desk in La Jolla to a mature philosophy of technology, taught and argued on three continents, is documented in the student's own acknowledgements and in the work's every chapter. It matters to this profile because it rebuts, with an existence proof, the charge that the famous book's concepts were too vaporous to build with. A concept that can found a research programme in another discipline has passed a test that polemics cannot set.

The pattern generalises, more loosely, across the humanities and social sciences of the following generation. The theorists of consumption who traced desire's manufacture, the critics of the administered university, the analysts of media integration who rebuilt the culture industry argument for new apparatuses, worked in a house whose frame Marcuse had raised, whether or not the debt was itemised. Influence of this diffuse kind is the hardest to audit and the easiest to exaggerate, and the profile confines itself to the auditable case. One doctoral student, one research programme, one field refounded on the teacher's premise: that is what transmission looks like when it is real, and it contrasts pointedly with the transmission the headlines recorded, in which a generation was said to have been fathered by a man who declined the paternity and spent the same years, in the same classrooms, doing the slower work of teaching a handful of people to think.

## Orpheus across the work: the singer against the order of toil

A single image threads the whole career, and tracing it binds the profile's three books into the unity the table promised. In Eros and Civilization, Marcuse sets against Prometheus, the Titan of labour, theft and productive defiance whom the performance principle claims as its culture hero, the paired figures of Orpheus and Narcissus. Orpheus the singer, whose voice orders nature without mastering it, whose love refuses the sanctioned forms and whose song is remembered as the image of a world pacified by receptivity rather than subdued by work; Narcissus, whose contemplation of his own image the tradition reads as sterile vanity and the book rereads as the emblem of a gratification that does not consume its object. The retrieval is the Freud book's boldest stroke, because it locates the counter-principle to the entire civilisation of toil inside the civilisation's own mythology, in figures every schoolboy of the author's generation had met. The performance principle did not invent its heroes. It inherited a pantheon in which its opposite had always been worshipped at the margins, and the margins, here as everywhere in this thinker, are where the theory looks for its evidence.

The image returns, transformed, at the career's end. The Aesthetic Dimension of 1978 is, beneath its polemics about art and orthodoxy, the Orpheus doctrine stated as an aesthetics: the formed work, the song, is the one product of the administered world in which the Great Refusal achieves permanence, because form preserves what the world denies without ceasing to be of the world. Between the two appearances lies the whole argument of the profile. The young reviewer of the 1844 Manuscripts had found in Marx a creature that makes itself by objectifying its essence. The old aesthetician found in art the objectification that escapes the market's and the ministry's audit. Orpheus is the point where the two findings meet: the human being as the animal whose making need not be labour, whose voice can order a world it does not dominate. A profile organised by the affluence thesis must end its thematic work here, because the thesis has a positive pole and this is it. Satisfaction administers because it offers the goods without the song. The song is what the goods are for, and a society that cannot tell the difference has stated, in the philosopher's technical sense, its one-dimensionality.

## The essayist: prose, aphorism and the refusal of the system

A word on the writing itself, since the writing is part of the evidence. Marcuse's English prose, the medium of everything after 1941, is a deliberate hybrid: Germanic in its architecture, its sentences built in long subordinated spans that hold a claim and its qualification in one breath, and American in its polemical directness at the moments of verdict. The compression this produces is the style of the theory. A Marcuse sentence typically performs the second dimension it describes, refusing to let a fact sit unjudged, embedding the negation inside the description until the reader cannot extract the fact without the judgement. This is why the books excerpt so badly into slogans and why the slogans so reliably betray them. The famous terms, one-dimensional, false needs, happy consciousness, are chapter titles of arguments, and in the prose they are never allowed to travel alone. Teachers assigning the work learn to warn their students that the quotable sentence is the one the author has surrounded with the most qualifications, and that the qualifications are the philosophy.

The shorter forms show the same discipline. The essays collected in Negations, the lectures gathered after his death, even the interviews of the famous years, keep a standard of explicitness about premises that the era's prophetic register rarely attempted. He states his anthropology, he flags his wagers, he concedes the points this profile has conceded on his behalf, usually before his critics reach them. The habit is not a decoration on the thought. It is the thought's ethics, the refusal to administer the reader that corresponds, at the level of style, to the refusal to be administered at the level of society. A profile that has spent its length correcting the guru image can rest the correction here. Gurus do not footnote their doubts. This writer built his books out of them.

## The archive and the posthumous papers

The afterlife of the work is unusually well supplied, and the supply itself is a datum. The literary estate, preserved in Frankfurt, contains the correspondence this profile has drawn on, the drafts, and a body of unpublished lectures and essays from every decade, which the philosopher Douglas Kellner spent years editing into a series of collected volumes issued from the nineteen nineties onward. The effect of the archive's opening has been the opposite of the usual posthumous deflation. The wartime memoranda confirmed the analyst's seriousness; the early essays confirmed the 1932 intervention's originality; the letters with Heidegger and with Adorno confirmed the independence of mind that the guru legend had dissolved into charisma. Each release moved the figure further from the poster and closer to the record, and the scholarship followed the releases, so that the secondary literature of the present century reads a different Marcuse from the one the obituaries buried: less a prophet of a decade than a systematic thinker whose system happened to detonate in public once.

That shift is where the profile closes its documentary work. The three books stand where the table placed them; the affluence thesis stands at their centre, its mechanism intact, its anthropology unproven, its author's concessions on record; the tolerance essay stands stated and answered, out of reach of both its devotees and its prosecutors; the life stands as the record gives it, the council member of 1918, the student of Heidegger, the analyst of two empires, the teacher of Angela Davis, the reluctant elder of a movement he criticised while it crowned him. What remains for the reader is the use of the work, and the profile's last sections are given to the three audiences every article in this series serves, after a short guide to reading the books in the order that makes them argue with each other most instructively.

## Repressive desublimation: the release that strengthens the cage

One mechanism of the famous book is so distinctive that its absence from the popular summaries counts as a sixth misreading, and the profile repairs the omission here. Repressive desublimation names the process by which an administered society permits, and commercially organises, the release of instincts it once forbade, and grows stronger by the permission. Sexuality is the paradigm. Where the older order repressed erotic life into the narrow channels of monogamous reproduction and paid for the repression in neurosis and revolt, the affluent order relaxes the prohibitions, admits eroticism into advertising, entertainment and the managed leisure of its citizens, and discovers that a desublimated population is not a liberated one. The release is real, and so is the strengthening. Instinctual energy that might have accumulated as refusal is discharged in forms the system supplies, priced and schedules, and the satisfaction functions, like every satisfaction in the theory, as a renewal of consent.

The concept's precision is worth defending against its vulgarisation. Marcuse is not mourning the prohibitions or joining the moralists who shared his decade's platforms for opposite reasons. His claim is structural: desublimation is repressive when it is administered, when the sphere of permitted release is engineered to leave the performance principle untouched, so that erotic life becomes one more zone in which the individual experiences his administration as his pleasure. The body made available as an object of display and consumption has not escaped the order of objects. It has been promoted within it. What the theory sets against the mechanism is not repression restored but the distinction the Freud book built: between release that reconciles and a gratification whose conditions would have to change for it to exist, between the sexuality the market can sell and an erotic relation to the world that the performance principle cannot survive. The distinction is demanding to the point of austerity, and it explains the otherwise puzzling severity with which this supposed prophet of liberation judged the liberation on offer around him. He had a category for it, and the category was not praise.

## The programme stated: philosophy becomes critical theory

Between the manuscripts essay and the Hegel book stands the text in which Marcuse stated, once and programmatically, what he thought he was doing. "Philosophy and Critical Theory", published in the Institute's journal in 1937 alongside Horkheimer's manifesto on traditional and critical theory, argues that philosophy's great concepts, reason, freedom, happiness, the good, were never merely descriptions of the world. They were indictments of it, preserving in ideal form the claims the actual order betrayed, and a materialist theory worthy of the inheritance must take custody of the ideals rather than sneer at them. Critical theory, on this account, is philosophy become conscious of its own social content: it refuses the division in which philosophy keeps the values and science keeps the facts, and it measures the facts by the values with a rigour the values' official guardians never applied. The essay's most quoted contention in the scholarship is its insistence that the interest guiding such theory is liberation, stated without embarrassment as a commitment the theory avows rather than a bias it conceals.

The 1937 programme illuminates the whole arc because it shows the affluence thesis's author before the affluence. Already in 1937 the enemy is the thought that accepts the given as final, whether it wears the gown of positivist science or the robes of a philosophy content to contemplate. Already the method is custody: rescue the concept from its administrators, whether the concept is Hegel, Freud or tolerance. And already the wager is the one the objection section weighed: that a theory may carry an avowed interest in human liberation without forfeiting its claim to truth, provided it states the interest and submits its descriptions to the evidence. The school's later comparative self-understanding, its quarrel with the classical Marxism from which it descended, is treated in this cluster's own place; the profile's point is narrower. When the famous books are read as episodes of a fashion, their unity disappears. Read as executions of the 1937 programme across thirty years and three bodies of material, they are among the most sustained single projects in the century's philosophy, and the programme, not the fame, is their author's signature.

## On violence: the distinction he drew and the line he held

No part of the record has been more lazily reported than his position on political violence, and the profile states it in the terms he stated it, because the era's movements and their enemies both had uses for a simpler version. Marcuse distinguished, throughout his work, between the violence of an established order, which is continuous, institutional and largely invisible to itself, and the violence of resistance, which is episodic and condemned by the standards the order's own violence escapes. The distinction is analytical before it is exculpatory: it identifies the baseline against which acts of revolt are judged, and it refuses the bookkeeping in which the first stone is the only violence in the story. Within that frame he defended, historically, the right of oppressed populations to resist, and he located the student movements' occasional eruptions inside the frame's logic rather than outside civilisation's pale.

The line he held is the part the legend omits. Against the turn, by fragments of the movements in his last decade, toward armed propaganda and individual terror in the advanced countries themselves, his judgement was unambiguous and repeated in print: such violence was strategically self-defeating, a gift to the administration's oldest reflexes, and morally continuous with the aggressiveness it claimed to oppose. Terror by the weak, in a society whose integration works by satisfaction, does not interrupt the mechanism; it supplies the mechanism with its favourite legitimation, the emergency in which administration may show its other face with public gratitude. He made the argument to audiences who had come to crown him, in the years when it cost him most, and the Counterrevolution and Revolt volume carries it as doctrine. The position will satisfy neither the prosecutor who wants a theorist of terror nor the devotee who wants a blessing on every escalation. It is, characteristically, a distinction, held against both parties at once, and the profile records it as the man's actual politics in the years when his politics were most consequential.

## The feminist reply: essentialism charged

The 1974 feminism lecture drew a reply that belongs with it, since the exchange repeats the theory's central drama in a new arena. Feminist critics of the following years charged that the lecture's architecture, masculine performance against feminine receptivity, essentialised the very qualities it praised: tenderness and non-violence are not women's nature, they argued, but the products of women's historical confinement, and a liberation that enthrones them as feminine virtues keeps the confinement's categories while reversing their signs. The charge has force, and it is the same charge, in a new key, that MacIntyre pressed against the needs doctrine: a classification supplied by the theorist, into which other people's self-understanding is required to fit. Marcuse's partial defence is visible in the lecture's own text, where the qualities are assigned historically, to the sphere a patriarchal order built, rather than biologically, and where the goal is expressly their universalisation, a society in which receptivity is no longer feminine because it is no longer assigned. Whether the defence succeeds is a judgement the profile leaves open, noting only the pattern. Three times now, on needs, on speech, on gender, the theory has been met by the objection that its emancipatory classifications are imposed from above. Three times the author's answer has been that the classifications are historical, revisable and aimed at their own abolition. The recurrence is the deepest criticism the work has attracted, and the deepest evidence of the problem it could not solve: how a critique of administered consciousness speaks without administering.

## In defence of happiness: the hedonism essay

The Institute essays contain one more signature the profile should isolate, because it explains the warmth beneath the theory's severity. The 1938 essay on hedonism took up the philosophy of pleasure at the moment when pleasure was being organised by the dictatorships as spectacle and by the democracies as consumption. Its argument refuses both available sneers. Against the idealist tradition, which treats the pursuit of happiness as a low motive unworthy of philosophy, the essay insists that hedonism preserves a materialist truth the idealists suppress: that the individual's gratification is a legitimate end, that a doctrine which sacrifices present happiness to every higher claim should be asked whose interest the sacrifice serves. Against the administered pleasures of the market, the essay draws the Freud book's future distinction inside enjoyment itself. There is a pleasure that reconciles, purchasable, scheduled, compatible with every arrangement, and there is the happiness whose conditions would require the world to change, and the first is hedonism's corruption of the second. The measure it proposes is audacious for a Marxist of the period: a society is to be judged by the happiness it makes possible, not by the virtues it enforces.

Read the late work backward from this essay and its emotional logic clarifies. The affluence thesis is frightening to its author not because people are enjoying themselves but because the enjoyment has been severed from the happiness the enjoyment advertises. The administered satisfactions are real pleasures and false happiness at once, and the distinction, pleasure as sensation against happiness as a form of life, is the 1938 essay's gift to the 1964 book. It also explains his distance from the ascetic strains of the left he otherwise marched with. A socialism that promises virtue and postpones joy had, on his account, already surrendered the standard by which domination is known, since domination's surest sign is the organisation of other people's renunciation. The priest, the commissar and the advertiser differ in everything except this, that each administers a population's pleasures in the interest of an order, and the hedonism essay is the young exile's declaration that his critique would be conducted in happiness's name or not at all.

## The warfare state: affluence's engine room

The famous book's political economy, such as it is, concentrates in one grim mechanism, and the profile isolates it because it binds the affluence to the era's wars without a conspiracy theory. The administered society's productivity, Marcuse argued, had outrun the consumption its distribution could organise: left to itself, the apparatus would choke on its own surplus. The blockage is cleared by the permanent mobilisation against an enemy. Defence expenditure absorbs the surplus in products that enter no one's standard of living, sustains the employment the automation argument shows becoming unnecessary, and supplies the permanent emergency in whose name discipline is renewed and dissent priced as treason. The enemy is therefore functional in a strict sense, not invented in a back room but required by the machine's arithmetic, and the society's foreign aggression and its domestic affluence are two departments of one administration. Peace, real peace, with the conversion of the war economy it would enforce, appears in this analysis as the system's deepest internal threat, which is why, in the book's bleakest inference, the system can survive its critics more easily than it could survive the disappearance of its enemy.

The mechanism deserves the isolation because it is the point at which the theory most plainly exceeds a complaint about consumerism. The goods that pacify are not a natural overflow; they are the civilian dividend of an economy geared to destruction, and the citizen's comfort and the distant war are entries in one ledger. Marcuse had watched the conversion from the inside, in the offices where the postwar enemy was being defined as his desk's subject changed from Berlin to Moscow, and the book's confidence on this point has the analyst's authority. Critics have objected that the mechanism overstates the military share of the affluent economies and understates the civilian sources of postwar growth, and the objection has evidence behind it. The profile's verdict follows its pattern: the mechanism is real but partial, a load-bearing wall and not the whole house, and its author's habit of promoting his true mechanisms into total explanations is the stylistic form of the overreach his critics, from Mattick onward, have always targeted.

## Two Hegelians: Lukács and the price of the party

The generation that made Hegelian Marxism produced two great careers, and their divergence is the cleanest natural experiment the tradition affords. Georg Lukács, five years Marcuse's senior in the same intellectual world, had written the founding text of Western Marxism in 1923 and then faced the choice the century imposed on its Marxist philosophers: accommodation with the party that claimed the theory, or exile from history as the party defined it. Lukács chose, again and again, the accommodation, recanting his masterwork under instruction, surviving the purges in Moscow by the disciplines of self-criticism, and returning to Hungary to serve a state whose character his own categories indicted. Marcuse chose the other road in 1919 and never retraced it: no party, no recantations, a career built in universities and, for one decade, in the service of a state he criticised before and after the employment. The experiment's result is written in the two bodies of work. Lukács's is larger, deeper in the classics, scarred by the decades of enforced conformity and by late recoveries of astonishing power. Marcuse's is narrower and unbent, the work of a man who never had to spend his genius unsaying himself.

The comparison is not entered to award a prize for purity, and the profile states the accommodation's best case before it closes. Lukács wagered that a theory severed from the organised movement becomes literature, and that the philosopher's task is to remain where history is being made even at the price of public self-abasement; the wager preserved his access to the tradition's centre and produced, in the late ontology he built from the same 1844 Manuscripts Marcuse had reviewed in 1932, a final convergence of the two roads. Marcuse's counter-wager, that the theory dies when the party owns it and that the philosopher's first duty is the unbought judgement, produced the freer books and the thinner politics this profile has weighed throughout. The reader deciding between the wagers is deciding the standing question of the whole series, whether critique survives better inside the institutions it opposes or outside them, and the two careers, read together, are the evidence. What the evidence does not show is a third road that history offered either man. That is the tragedy the comparison exists to record.

## What the theory does not contain: an honest inventory

A profile that has weighed objections piecemeal owes the reader one consolidated inventory of the theory's absences, since a framework is known by its silences as well as its claims. Marcuse's work contains no analysis of capitalist crisis: no account of the falling tendency of profit, of credit, of the business cycle's mechanics, of the contradictions this series examined in its cluster on crisis theory, and the omission is structural, not an oversight. His system's motor is administration, not accumulation, and where the classical theory expects the economy to generate its own breakdowns, his expects the apparatus to generate its own legitimations, indefinitely postponable and indefinitely renewable. The theory likewise contains no account of money or finance as independent powers, no theory of the state's form comparable to its theory of the state's functions, and a sociology of class that thins, in the late work, into the sociology of integration's successes and failures. Its geography is the North Atlantic of its author's exile; the colonial world enters as the substratum's address and the wars' location, rarely as a theorist of itself.

The inventory explains the shape of the work's reception among economists and its durability among critics of culture. Where the questions concern how the affluent order manufactures consent, organises language and administers instinct, the framework remains a working instrument with few rivals. Where the questions concern why the order periodically convulses, how its finances bind its politics, or what its crises make possible, the framework is silent, and its silence misleads readers who mistake it for a complete Marxism. Marcuse's own estimate of the matter, given across the interviews of his last years, was characteristically exact. He had written, he suggested, the critique of a specific historical configuration, the administered capitalism of the postwar decades, from the standpoint of the philosophy that configuration had formed in him. The configuration outlasted the decades. The critique did too, within the borders the inventory marks, and the profile's work is done if the reader can now patrol those borders without a guide.

## The secret reports: what the analyst actually recommended

The government decade can be brought into sharper focus, because a large body of its product survives and has been published from the archives. The reports Marcuse wrote or co-wrote for the wartime agency and the State Department, many of them with Neumann and Kirchheimer, were working documents of a high order: analyses of the Nazi Party's penetration of German institutions, estimates of German morale under bombardment, studies of the administrative personnel through whom the regime ruled, and, as the war turned, memoranda on the occupation to come. Their collective argument, where policy was concerned, was unmistakable. Nazism was not a crust of criminals on a healthy society; it was a system carried by identifiable elites in the civil service, the judiciary, industry and the professions, and an occupation that left those elites in place, in the name of administrative convenience or anti-communist reliability, would preserve the system's skeleton under new management. Denazification, to mean anything, had to break the continuity of personnel and power, and the reports supplied the maps by which it might have been done.

The sequel is a matter of record, and the profile states it without the embroidery it usually attracts. The occupation's priorities shifted within two years toward reconstruction and the new confrontation with the Soviet Union; the thorough purge the analysts had mapped was curtailed, then reversed, and the Federal Republic was built with a large measure of the personnel continuity the reports had warned against. Marcuse's later judgements on West Germany, courteous in form and cold in substance, draw on the experience directly. He had learned, at the level of the individual file, how an administered order reproduces itself through the survival of its administrators, and how the rhetoric of a fresh start can function as the mechanism of the old one's return. Readers who wonder where the affluence thesis acquired its conviction that systems absorb their negations should weigh this episode. He had watched a negation absorbed, document by document, by the government he served, in the country whose revolution had failed him in 1919. The famous book's pessimism about official anti-fascism, and its insistence that liberation cannot be delegated to administrations, were memoranda before they were philosophy.

## The optimistic wager: An Essay on Liberation

The strangest book of the fame years is the one in which the pessimist of 1964 permits himself a hope, and its argument completes the profile's picture of the theory under the stress of events. An Essay on Liberation, written through 1968 and published in 1969, wagers that the administered society had begun to generate, inside its own beneficiaries, the sensibility that could refuse it. The evidence he marshalled was the era's own: young people formed by affluence who experienced the affluence as emptiness, a revulsion against the performance principle's texture, against its wars, its ugliness, its conversion of life into career, arising not from deprivation but from satiety's discontents. The book calls this the new sensibility and gives it a precise theoretical job. Every previous revolution, on his reading, had changed the masters while leaving the subjects' needs intact, and the needs had rebuilt the mastery. A liberation that begins in the sensibility, in instincts educated to find domination intolerable and gratification without guilt possible, would be the first whose agents did not carry the old order inside them as desire.

The book's most contested chapter pushes the claim to its limit and calls the foundation of liberation biological. The term is chosen to scandalise two audiences. Against the orthodox, it asserts that socialism is not an economic arrangement to be installed on unchanged human material but a transformation that must reach the instinctual structure, the level at which the Freud book had located repression's roots. Against the biologists, it asserts that this nature is historical through and through, formed by the civilisation that represses it and therefore reformable by the civilisation that succeeds it. The profile records the chapter as the point where the theory's anthropology, elsewhere its silent foundation, steps into the open and accepts the exposure. Everything the objection section charged, that the criterion of liberation is a picture of human nature the argument cannot prove, stands confessed in this chapter and embraced. Marcuse's answer, in effect, is that every politics has such a picture and his has the virtue of declaring its own. Readers have divided on the answer ever since, and the division is the book's intended legacy: it is the one text in which the reader is not permitted to admire the diagnosis and skip the wager.

## The sober reckoning: Counterrevolution and Revolt

The sequel of 1972 is the fame's coldest book and, for the historian of the theory, among the most valuable, because it applies the framework to its own movement's defeat in something close to real time. Counterrevolution and Revolt argues that the order had learned to strike first: the counterrevolution of its title is preventive, a permanent mobilisation of law, media and managed concession against a revolution that exists mainly as potential, and its very prematurity is the affluence thesis's confirmation. A system confident in its absorptive powers does not merely wait for opposition; it budgets for it. The book's anatomy of that prevention, written as the American war wound down and the domestic movements fragmented, refuses the consolations of both camps. The movement's collapse is not blamed on repression alone, which flatters the movement, nor on the movement alone, which flatters the system. It is traced to the meeting of a strategy of prevention with a politics of gesture, and the chapters on the New Left's errors carry the authority of the letters to Adorno: the contempt for theory, the eroticisation of violence, the substitution of lifestyle for organisation, itemised by the man the newspapers called the movement's father, in a book the movement mostly declined to read.

Two chapters point beyond the reckoning. The study of nature and revolution argues that the liberation of nature is not a metaphor or a hobby of the affluent but a front of the same struggle, since a civilisation that treats the natural world as raw material will treat its own members' inner nature the same way, and the emerging ecological movement is greeted as the first politics to grasp the connection in practice. The treatment of women's liberation, developed further in the Stanford lecture the profile has examined, appears here in book form as the demand whose satisfaction requires the transformation of the sensibility itself. Read together, the two chapters show the framework in its last active service, finding its carriers where its logic said they would be: wherever a demand arises that the delivery of goods cannot meet. The book sold a fraction of its famous predecessor's copies. It is the one the profile would press on a reader who suspects the whole enterprise of being a period piece, because it shows the theory judging its own moment without mercy and surviving the judgement.

## Work as a way of being: the labour essay

The manuscripts essay had a companion, published in 1933 in a leading journal of social science, and together the two texts are the foundation documents of everything that followed. The essay on the philosophical foundation of the economic concept of labour argues that labour, in Marx and behind him in Hegel, is not one activity among others, to be priced and organised like any commodity. It is the fundamental manner in which a human being exists: the process by which a creature externalises itself in a world, recognises itself in what it has made, and becomes, through the making, what it is. An economics that treats labour as a factor of production has not made an innocent abstraction. It has taken the phenomenon in the mutilated form a specific society gives it, alienated, timed, sold, and enthroned the mutilation as the concept. The recovery of labour's full sense, as self-realising activity whose product confronts its maker as his own life, is therefore not a humanist decoration on the science. It is the science's missing foundation, and its recovery converts economics into critique.

The essay's date gives it a poignancy its author can only have felt later. It appeared in the year of the seizure of power, in a journal the new order would shortly extinguish, written by a scholar already packing for exile. The Germany that might have housed the debate it proposed ceased to exist as he published it, and the debate migrated with him into the Institute's journals and the American books. Its theme migrated too. The performance principle is the labour essay's category inverted: labour experienced purely as performance for another's measure, the self externalised into products that confront the maker as an alien administration. The automation argument is the essay's hope restated by its old age: the machine, having absorbed labour's burden, might return labour to its foundation sense, activity as the free objectification of a life. A reader who wants the whole career in one sentence can take it from 1933. Marcuse spent forty-six years asking what work does to the worker's being, and refusing every answer that began by accepting the wage relation's definition of the question.

## The other Freudo-Marxist: Wilhelm Reich and the road not taken

The Freud book is usually shelved beside one other name, and the profile should explain why its author insisted on the distance. Wilhelm Reich, the Austrian psychoanalyst whose Mass Psychology of Fascism and Sexual Revolution had pioneered the fusion of Marx and Freud a generation earlier, reached conclusions that look, from a distance, like Marcuse's: sexual repression is a political technology, the authoritarian character is manufactured in the family, liberation must reach the instincts. The distance opens on inspection. Reich's theory progressively simplified the instincts into a hydraulics of discharge, in which freedom meant the unblocking of a natural flow and politics became a branch of sexual hygiene, until the later work collapsed into the doctrine of orgone energy and the accumulator boxes that ended his American career in a federal prison. Marcuse's Freud book, written with Reich's trajectory in full view, can be read as the rescue of the Freudo-Marxist project from its founder's fate. Where Reich reduces, Marcuse dialecticises: the instincts are historical, their repression is measured against civilisation's real requirements, the death instinct is retained in all its awkwardness, and liberation is a transformation of the whole organisation of life rather than a technique of release.

The distinction mattered practically in the decade of fame, when the counterculture cheerfully conflated the two men into a single prophet of permissiveness. Marcuse's corrections, delivered in interviews with a patience that thinned visibly, restated the difference the profile has already drawn from the desublimation chapter. A society that can sell Reichian release by the unit has nothing to fear from it; the release was priced into the system before the customer arrived. What the system cannot sell, because its sale would end the seller, is the transformation of needs themselves, and that transformation, not discharge, is the Freud book's subject. The comparison closes with the symmetry the era supplied. Reich died in an American penitentiary in 1957, destroyed by the collision between his doctrine and the state his doctrine had fled to escape. Marcuse died an honoured professor in 1979, having watched his own doctrine survive collisions Reich's could not. Survival is not truth, and the profile does not argue that it is. But the divergent fates record a real difference in the doctrines: one made itself a therapy and was judged as one; the other remained a philosophy, and philosophies are harder to imprison.

## The war abroad: Vietnam and the substratum in arms

The theory's geography has been noted as a silence, and one theatre of the fame years partly fills it. Marcuse opposed his adopted country's war in Vietnam from its escalation, in print, at teach-ins and in the movement's councils, and the opposition was theoretical before it was moral. The war exhibited, for him, the warfare state's logic at full extension: the affluent society's surplus violence discharged at its periphery, its technology of administration tested on a population that had not consented to the experiment, its domestic peace purchased, in part, by the export of its destructiveness. The resistance to that war, in Vietnam itself and in the streets of the metropolis, appeared in his analysis as the substratum argument acquiring a map. The Vietnamese liberation movement was the outside the system could not integrate, a population for whom the affluence's goods were not on offer and whose refusal therefore could not be bought at the usual price; the students who filled the American streets were the inside's corresponding fracture, the beneficiaries declining the benefit. The Essay on Liberation's chapter on solidarity is built on exactly this pairing, the external and internal poles of a single refusal, and it remains the framework's most concrete attempt to name a world-historical agent after the proletariat's integration.

The profile records the position with the same care it has applied elsewhere, because the era's polemics simplified it in both directions. He was not a strategist of the Vietnamese struggle and claimed no authority over it; his subject was what the war revealed about the society waging it. Nor did his solidarity extend to the regimes that claimed the struggle's inheritance, a consistency the third-position section has already established. What the war gave the theory was a confirmation and a limit. The confirmation: an affluent society's cohesion did crack, over a war its administration had chosen, along the seams of race, generation and conscription the substratum passage had surveyed. The limit: the crack closed, the war ended by exhaustion rather than transformation, and the system that emerged absorbed the anti-war generation with the same techniques the books describe. He lived to see the absorption and said, in the interviews of his last years, that the theory had never promised anything else. A diagnosis is not a promise. The confusion of the two was the fame's final misreading, and he spent his last decade, with undiminished courtesy, declining to underwrite it.

## Administration without a tyrant: power with no address

One consequence of the theory has received less attention than the slogans, and it may be the deepest reason the books unsettle their readers. Every classical doctrine of liberation presupposes an address for power: a palace to storm, a class to expropriate, a tyrant whose fall changes the regime of daily life. Marcuse's administered society has dispersed the address. Domination in his account is exercised by no committee and intended by no villain. It is embodied in the apparatus, in the interlocking of production, administration, media and need, each part rationally pursuing its function and the whole reproducing unfreedom as an emergent property that nobody chose. The manager is as administered as the managed, the advertiser believes his copy, the official serves the file. This is why the theory's mood differs so sharply from the conspiratorial Marxisms it is confused with. There is no one home at the centre of the one-dimensional society, and a politics addressed to the centre's occupants is a letter to an empty room.

The strategic bewilderment this produces is the theory's honesty and its paralysis in a single figure. If power has no address, the Great Refusal has no destination either, and the vagueness examined earlier in this profile appears in its second form: not only must the refusal avoid specification lest it be absorbed, it lacks a recipient to refuse to. Marcuse's scattered answers, the substratum at the margins, the sensibility in formation, the formed work of art, are attempts to locate agency in a world whose centre is vacant, and none of them has the classical agent's solidity. Critics from the Marxist flank treated the vacancy as a failure of analysis, the point where a real ruling class dissolves into a fog of apparatus. The defence, which the profile finds partly persuasive, is that the fog is a finding. The postwar order his desk and his books examined really had displaced decision into process to a degree the classical vocabulary handles badly, and a theory that invents a tyrant to satisfy its rhetoric would be lying for form's sake. Between a politics with an address and no accuracy, and an accuracy with no address, he chose the accuracy, and the choice is the final reason his work reads, half a century on, less like a programme than like a description still waiting for its politics to be invented.

## The Berlin lecture: the end of utopia

In the summer of 1967, weeks before the London congress, the philosopher stood in a packed hall of the Free University of Berlin and told the students of the city he had fled that utopia was finished. The lecture, "The End of Utopia", is the most misunderstood performance of the fame years, because its title was heard as either a promise or a surrender and it was neither. His argument turned on a definition. Utopia, properly speaking, names a condition that cannot exist, a nowhere whose impossibility is built into its conception, and the concept had served for centuries as the polite dismissal of every radical proposal: admirable, no doubt, and utopian. What had changed, he argued, was the factual basis of the dismissal. The productive forces of the advanced societies, their technology, their science, their accumulated wealth, had reached a level at which the abolition of poverty, the drastic shortening of necessary labour and the pacification of existence were no longer fantasies about another world. They were unexecuted possibilities of this one, blocked not by nature or scarcity but by the organisation that profited from the blockage. The end of utopia meant the end of the excuse. What stood between the given world and a free one was not impossibility but administration.

The students heard the lecture as a warrant, and in a sense it was designed to be one, but its second movement cut against the mood of its audience as his interventions reliably did. A possibility that exists materially and is blocked politically imposes a discipline that dreaming never did: the blockage must be analysed, the forces maintaining it named, the agents of its removal organised, and none of that work is advanced by treating the lecture's thesis as a mood of inevitability. He warned the hall explicitly against the inference that the ripeness of the conditions guaranteed the outcome. History, on his showing, is littered with ripe conditions that rotted, and the administered society's absorptive power, the subject of his book of three years before, was itself the strongest counterweight to the lecture's optimism. The two texts, the diagnosis and the lecture, belong together as the systole and diastole of his late thought. The book explains why nothing changes although everything could; the lecture insists that everything could, although nothing changes. A reader who keeps only one of them has half a philosopher, and the halves have founded separate legends, the prophet of despair and the prophet of liberation, neither of which the record supports.

The Berlin visit also produced the exchange that the German movement remembered longest, and it belongs to the record of the reluctant guru. Pressed by students who wanted the theory converted into immediate tactics for their own confrontations, he declined the conversion with a teacher's formula: theory could clarify the situation, expose the false needs and the false friends, and discipline the imagination of alternatives, but it could not relieve its readers of the risk of action or the labour of organisation, and a philosophy that issued commands had become the thing his work opposed. The answer disappointed the hall and has disappointed movement readers since, in Berlin and everywhere else the books were carried into the streets. It is reproduced here because it is the authentic note, struck at the moment of maximum temptation to strike any other. The man who had analysed two states from inside and watched a third absorb its opposition was not going to pretend, for applause, that a lecture could substitute for the politics he had spent fifty years explaining the absence of.

## Schiller's wager: the play drive and the aesthetic education

Behind the Freud book's mythology stands a German poet-philosopher whose presence in the argument is easy to miss and impossible, once seen, to remove. Friedrich Schiller's Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man, written in the seventeen nineties as the French Revolution curdled into terror, proposed that the route to a free polity runs not through institutions first but through the formation of the human being who must inhabit them. Schiller divided the person between a sense drive, binding him to appetite, time and material need, and a form drive, binding him to law, reason and abstraction, and he located their reconciliation in a third: the play drive, active in aesthetic experience, in which the human being is at once fully sensuous and fully free, shaped by material he does not merely obey and by forms he does not merely suffer. Only through beauty, the Letters argue, does man make his way to freedom, because only in play is he whole enough to be trusted with it.

Marcuse's appropriation, in the closing movement of Eros and Civilization and again in the aesthetic writings of his last decade, is exact about what it takes. The play drive becomes the anthropological sketch of the non-repressive civilisation: work transfigured toward the character of play, the senses educated into organs of a gratification that does not consume, form imposed on life by the living rather than by the performance principle. Schiller supplies what Freud could not, a positive image with a philosophical pedigree, and he supplies it from inside the classical canon the Gymnasium had installed in the schoolboy of 1914, which is why the argument's texture differs so markedly from the era's other Marxisms. The liberation of the senses is, for this thinker, a Bildungsprogramm before it is a barricade, an education of perception in which art is the faculty and freedom the degree. Critics who find the late work's politics thin are usually registering this substitution without naming it. Where the classical tradition puts the seizure of the state, Marcuse, following Schiller, puts the formation of a sensibility that would no longer reproduce the state in itself, and the thinness and the depth of the position are the same property viewed from opposite sides.

The objection Schiller himself could not escape awaits his heir as well, and the profile states it in the form this series requires. Aesthetic education, in the Letters and in the lecture halls of the Federal Republic alike, presupposes the leisure and the cultivation it is meant to universalise: those most formed by the performance principle have least access to the play that would reform them, and an education dispensed by the administered university risks becoming one more administered good. Marcuse's answer, assembled from the late essays, is that the sensibility is not formed only in classrooms. It is formed wherever the system's promises fail in experience, in the revulsion against ugly cities, poisoned air, meaningless work and managed pleasure that he counted, in the movements of his last decade, as the new sensibility's raw material. Whether disgust can bear the weight Schiller assigned to beauty is a fair question, and the work leaves it open. What the wager establishes, win or lose, is the continuity this profile has traced from the start: the philosopher who began with the artist novel in 1922 ended with the aesthetic dimension in 1978, and the education of the senses is the thread on which the whole itinerary is strung.

## The quarrel with Sartre: freedom in the head

The American exile produced one more encounter that fixes his position among the century's Marxisms, and it predates the famous books. In 1948, in an American journal of philosophy, Marcuse published a long review of Sartre's Being and Nothingness, the war's most celebrated work of French philosophy, and the review is the young exile's declaration of independence from the other great temptation of his generation. Sartre had built an ontology in which consciousness is absolutely free: condemned to freedom, as the famous formula runs, choosing itself in every act, uncaused by the world it negates. Marcuse's objection was not to the book's brilliance, which he acknowledged, but to its address. A freedom that resides entirely in consciousness, untouched by the conditions in which consciousness lives, is compatible with any order whatsoever. The slave, on Sartre's premises, is as free as the master in the secrecy of his own negation, and a philosophy that cannot distinguish their situations has defined freedom so abstractly that the world need never change for everyone to possess it. What Sartre treated as the eternal structure of human reality, Marcuse argued, was the historically specific experience of the bourgeois individual, sovereign in his interior because expropriated everywhere else, and existentialism had enthroned the symptom as the essence.

The quarrel matters beyond its occasion because it marks the fork at which postwar Marxism divided. One road, Sartre's own later one, drove existential freedom toward history and eventually toward a Marxism of groups and praxis in the Critique of Dialectical Reason, a convergence Marcuse noted with respect when it came. The other road, his, insisted from the start that freedom is a property of conditions before it is a property of consciousness, and that a theory which begins with the individual's inner liberty will end by consoling him for the outer kind's absence. The administered society of the later work is the Sartrean problem photographed from the opposite side: a population whose members feel free, choose constantly and negate inwardly, while the field of the choosable is staked out in advance. Happy consciousness is Sartre's condemned freedom with the anguish administered away. Readers who set the two philosophers side by side, as the era's students insistently did, are not comparing a pessimist with an optimist. They are watching one argument conducted from its two ends, about whether the unfreedom of an administered world is located in the soul's surrender or in the world's arrangement, and Marcuse's whole career is the brief for the second location.

The biographical residue is worth a sentence, because it repeats the profile's pattern of fruitful antagonism. The two men never became allies and never ceased to be reference points for each other: the Parisian who had stayed and resisted in place, the exile who had left and analysed from safety, each supplying the other's readers with the standing alternative. When the fame of 1968 briefly made them co-icons of a single movement, the movement was sheltering, without noticing, the two incompatible accounts of its own condition that the 1948 review had already separated. That the students could march under both names is the era's eclecticism. That the names could not be reconciled is the philosophy's, and the profile leaves the quarrel, like the others it has staged, exactly where its author left it: answered on his side with a rigour his opponents acknowledged, and open on the side where the evidence runs out.

## Reading Marcuse: an order and a method

The three books reward an order different from their publication sequence, and the profile closes its documentary work with the practical guidance its brief requires. Begin with the famous book, but read it slowly and against its reputation: the first three chapters contain the whole argument, and the chapters on language and thought, usually skipped, contain its proof. Read next the 1941 technology essay, which most editions of the essays make available, to see the argument's wartime skeleton and to inoculate against the notion that affluence invented it. Then take the Freud book entire, including the epilogue against the revisionists, since the anthropology the famous book presupposes is argued there and nowhere else. Reason and Revolution comes last in this order, not because it is least but because it is the foundation rendered visible: the reader who arrives at it from the later work will recognise the method, negation recovered from inside a settled concept, operating on the largest material Marcuse ever handled. Soviet Marxism and Counterrevolution and Revolt make the essential supplements, the first for the theory's symmetry, the second for its self-correction, and the tolerance essay should be read in its original volume, between Wolff's defence of pluralism and Moore's reflections, where its argument keeps the company that disciplines it.

The method matters as much as the order. Keep the three books' arguments separate on the page, since each has its own evidence and its own failure mode, and the commonest undergraduate error is to melt them into a single denunciation of modern life. A study card for each book, holding its claim, its mechanism and its strongest enemy in three columns, is the discipline this profile recommends; the [VaultBook study notes](https://vaultbook.net/tools/marxism-study-notes.html) companion to this series is built for exactly that exercise, with the three books table at its head. Note, as you read, every place the argument asks you to distrust a satisfaction, and write beside it the criterion the passage offers, basic or surplus, administered or free. The passages that offer no criterion are the theory's open wounds, and marking them is worth more than memorising the passages that sing. Finally, read one enemy whole: MacIntyre's monograph for the liberal objection, Mattick's critique for the Marxist one. Marcuse taught by antagonism all his life, from Heidegger to Fromm to the movements that crowned him, and his books are best honoured by the treatment he gave his own teachers.

Two supplements repay the reader who finishes the main sequence. The essay collection Negations gathers the programme's middle period in short compass, the studies of authority, of the affirmative character of culture and of philosophy's fate, and it shows the method working at article length on materials the books treat in bulk; a reader pressed for time can take the whole theory's shape from it in a week. The published correspondence does different work. The letters with Heidegger document the apprenticeship and its severance in the participants' own words, and the 1969 exchange with Adorno over the occupied Institute shows the theory's two tempers, engagement and caution, arguing at full strength inside a friendship of forty years. Read the letters last, when the positions are already familiar, and they will do what letters uniquely do: they return the system's abstractions to the men who held them, at the moments when holding them cost something, which is the note on which this profile has tried to keep the entire record pitched.

A note on texts, since this thinker's corpus crosses a language border. Everything from Reason and Revolution onward was composed in English, and the English texts are the originals, not translations; readers who meet the famous books in other languages are reading at one remove from prose their author built sentence by sentence in his adopted tongue. The work before the exile, the manuscripts essay, the labour essay, the Institute pieces, was written in German and reaches most readers through the translations gathered in the essay collections, which are reliable but flatten the young philosopher's Heideggerian vocabulary into a plainer register. The distinction matters for quotation. Cite the English books from the English page, and treat every ringing sentence attributed to the early Marcuse as a translator's rendering until the German has been checked.

## For students and exam candidates

The examination version of this thinker is usually a caricature with a beard, and the marks go to the candidate who replaces it with the chronology. Anchor every answer in the three books and their dates of argument: the Hegel defence of 1941, the Freud synthesis of 1955, the affluence diagnosis of 1964, and state in each case the mechanism rather than the mood. The affluence thesis is your highest-yield formulation: opposition neutralised by satisfied, administered needs rather than by repression, with surplus repression as the criterion that keeps the claim from being a slogan. On the tolerance essay, examiners reward the candidate who can state the proposal precisely, name its criterion problem, and give the objection from within the same 1965 volume, and they penalise both the candidate who deploys the essay as a slogan and the one who dismisses it as one. ## For teachers and seminar leaders

The seminar problem with Marcuse is that every student arrives with the legend installed, so the first session is best spent on the biography as an instrument of correction: the soldiers' council of 1918, the Heidegger apprenticeship and its 1947 postscript, the analyst's decade in Washington. Students who have handled the record argue about the theory differently. Sequence the reading against the objections rather than after them: the needs chapters beside MacIntyre's monograph, the tolerance essay inside its original volume with Wolff's reply assigned in the same week, the substratum conclusion beside the comparison developed in [the set-piece comparison with classical Marxism](/2015/09/01/critical-theory-vs-classical-marxism/), which prevents the lazy conclusion that the school simply abandoned the working class. Pair the famous book with [the companion study of the culture industry](/2015/07/15/culture-industry-explained/) when the syllabus turns to media, and set the desublimation chapter against the advertising materials students bring themselves, since the concept teaches best on evidence the seminar supplies. ## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The research frontier on this figure has moved from interpretation to archive, and the profile's emphases follow the documents. The wartime and State Department reports, published from the files in recent decades, support a reassessment of the Institute's American decade as applied social research of a high order, and they complicate every account of the school as unworldly: the analyst of Germany anticipated the occupation's central failure, on paper, in advance. The correspondence with Heidegger and the 1969 exchange with Adorno are the two letter-collections through which the intellectual biography must now be written, and both are in print. Journalists reaching for the tolerance essay as a reference should be directed to this profile's statement of it, and to the rule the essay's history teaches: the text proposes a discriminating withdrawal of tolerance from specified movements under an emergency diagnosis, it is neither a censorship code nor a licence for whichever institution quotes it, and any use of it that skips the criterion problem is advocacy, not reporting. ## Frequently Asked Questions

The questions below are the ones this profile is most often built to answer; each reply stands on the record assembled in the sections above it.

### Q: Who was Herbert Marcuse?

Herbert Marcuse (1898 to 1979) was a German-American philosopher of the Frankfurt School, born in Berlin, formed by the failed German revolution of 1918 and by study under Heidegger at Freiburg, and driven into exile in 1933. He spent a decade in the American government analysing Nazi Germany and then the Soviet Union, taught at Brandeis and at the University of California in San Diego, and wrote the three books this profile follows: Reason and Revolution, Eros and Civilization and One-Dimensional Man. His fame, which arrived in his late sixties through the student movements, rested on an argument he had been building since the nineteen thirties: that advanced industrial society integrates its opposition by administering satisfaction.

### Q: What is One-Dimensional Man about?

The 1964 book argues that the affluent industrial order has learned to govern through delivery rather than denial. Rising consumption, organised by advertising and the managed economy, binds the population to the system that supplies it, while public language and the accredited forms of thought lose the concepts in which a fundamentally different order could be described. The outcome he names one-dimensional is not uniformity of opinion, which flourishes, but the disappearance of an effective standpoint outside the established universe from which the whole could be judged and refused. The book closes by locating such a standpoint among those the integration never reached, and by declining to promise that their refusal would succeed.

### Q: Did Marcuse study under Heidegger?

Yes. After a first doctorate in 1922 on the German artist novel, Marcuse returned to Freiburg in 1928 to study with Husserl and Heidegger, whose Being and Time had appeared the year before. He wrote his habilitation on Hegel's ontology under Heidegger's direction, published in 1932, and his essays of the period attempted to fuse phenomenology with historical materialism. The relationship ended with Heidegger's embrace of the Nazi regime in 1933. It has a documented postscript: in 1947 Marcuse wrote asking his former teacher for a public accounting, received an evasive reply that equated the regime's crimes with the expulsion of Germans from the east, and broke off the correspondence in 1948.

### Q: Did Marcuse work for the American government?

Yes, for a decade, and the agencies matter. He joined the Office of War Information in 1942 and moved in 1943 to the Research and Analysis Branch of the Office of Strategic Services, the wartime intelligence agency, where with Franz Neumann and Otto Kirchheimer he analysed the Nazi system and planned denazification. When the war ended he transferred with his unit to the State Department, directed its Central European research on the Soviet Union and the European parties, and left government service in 1951. He never worked for the Central Intelligence Agency, which was founded while he was a State Department analyst, and his service was analytical throughout: no operations, no agents, a desk and a series of written estimates.

### Q: What is repressive tolerance?

It is the title and the target of Marcuse's 1965 essay, his contribution to the volume A Critique of Pure Tolerance. The essay argues that tolerance exercised within a society whose communications are concentrated and whose background conditions are rigged does not function neutrally: scrupulous equal treatment protects the established arrangements, since dissent is heard inside a universe whose premises it cannot reach. His proposed remedy, which he called liberating tolerance, was the withdrawal of tolerance from movements promoting aggression, armament, chauvinism and discrimination, extending to speech and assembly, with tolerance correspondingly extended toward the oppressed. The profile states the argument and its strongest objections in full in the sections devoted to the essay, including the objection that its criterion is supplied by the theorist.

### Q: What is surplus repression?

Surplus repression is the master distinction of Eros and Civilization, Marcuse's 1955 fusion of Marx and Freud. Freud had argued that civilisation is purchased by repressing the instincts. Marcuse divided the price in two. Basic repression is the restraint any durable civilisation requires: the postponement of satisfaction, the discipline of communal survival. Surplus repression is everything beyond that minimum which a particular order of domination imposes for its own preservation, the renunciations demanded by hierarchy, by the organisation of alienated labour and by the performance expected of its members. The distinction matters because it converts repression from a law of nature into a historical quantity, measurable against what an attained level of productivity actually needs, and therefore open to political judgement.

### Q: What is the performance principle?

The performance principle is Marcuse's name, in Eros and Civilization, for the historically specific form that Freud's reality principle takes in advanced capitalist society. Under it, a person is valued as a performer in a competitive economic order: the body is organised as an instrument of labour, time is divided into performances and recoveries from them, and worth, income and desirability all attach to measurable output. The principle reaches beyond the workplace. It shapes education as preparation for performance, leisure as recuperation for it and even pleasure as a displayed achievement. Marcuse's point is that the principle is historical, not natural: other civilisations have organised the reality principle otherwise, and an automated age could organise it otherwise again.

### Q: Why was Marcuse called the father of the New Left?

The title was a journalistic convenience of the late nineteen sixties, when movements in Germany, France and the United States adopted One-Dimensional Man and Eros and Civilization as their theoretical warrant and the press required a single face for a leaderless phenomenon. Marcuse rejected the paternity consistently, in interviews and in print. His reasons were chronological, since he was born in 1898 and formed by the defeats of 1919 and 1933, and doctrinal, since a movement that accepts a father accepts an authority his theory told it to refuse. He accepted the roles of teacher, resource and critic of the movements, defended their motives against their enemies, and attacked their anti-intellectualism and their cult of action in terms that cost him their applause.

### Q: Who does Marcuse think can change society?

His answers changed as the integration he diagnosed proceeded, and the profile tracks the sequence. One-Dimensional Man closes with the substratum beneath the integrated population: the outcasts, the persecuted of other races and colours, the unemployed and unemployable, whose opposition strikes the system from a position it never absorbed. The student movement he later treated as a catalyst rather than a class, capable of detonating crises it could not resolve. In his final decade he added the women's movement and the ecological movement, whose demands, unlike demands for more goods, cannot be met by delivering more of the same. The constant is the logic: the agent is whoever the administration failed to digest, identified empirically rather than by doctrine.

### Q: Is One-Dimensional Man still worth reading?

The profile's answer is yes, with a discipline attached. The book's dated surface, its examples drawn from the America of the early nineteen sixties, its silences on crisis and finance, must be discounted as the inventory section of this profile discounts them. What repays the reading is the mechanism at its centre, integration through administered satisfaction, together with the chapters on language and thought that show the mechanism operating on the instruments of criticism themselves. Read it with one of its enemies open beside it, MacIntyre for the liberal objection or Mattick for the Marxist one, and with the criterion question kept live: each time the argument distrusts a satisfaction, ask what standard the distrust presupposes. The book survives that treatment, which is the only test of a classic that matters.

### Q: How does the affluence thesis invert the scarcity assumptions of older Marxism?

The older framework expected capitalism's failures of provision to educate its victims: immiseration, crisis and unmet need would teach the working class where its interests lay and organise the negation of the system from inside. The affluence thesis runs the film backward. Where the system can meet needs on an expanding scale while defining which needs are felt, provision itself becomes the education, and its lesson is consent. Scarcity, on this account, was never the system's weakest point; it was the point at which the system's promises visibly failed, and a system that stops failing visibly has disarmed the classical mechanism of its own overthrow. Critique must therefore move from the distribution of goods to the manufacture of the wants the goods satisfy, from the factory gate to the formation of desire, which is the relocation the whole of Marcuse's later work performs.

### Q: What should a student separate between the three books' arguments?

Three separations earn their keep. Separate the claims: the first book defends a method, the dialectical recovery of negation in Hegel; the second proposes an anthropology, the division of repression into basic and surplus; the third advances a sociology, the integration of opposition in the affluent order. Separate the evidence: textual and historical argument in the first, speculative metapsychology in the second, observation of language, media and consumption in the third, with a corresponding descent in testability that the student should be able to state. Separate the failure modes: the Hegel book can be charged with idealising its subject, the Freud book with bending its concepts, the famous book with sealing itself against refutation. A student who can run the three separations owns the material; one who melts the books into a single mood owns a poster.

### Q: Why did the great refusal stay so unspecified?

Because specification was, on the theory's own premises, the mechanism of capture. A programme for liberation written in the administered society's available vocabulary, with its agent named and its steps enumerated, would be processed by the available institutions into one more option inside the universe it was meant to refuse. Marcuse therefore kept the Great Refusal at the level of a capacity and a direction: the preserved refusal in art and phantasy, the undigested populations at the margins, the sensibility in formation among the young. The profile treats the openness as the argument's acknowledged limit rather than its secret strength. A refusal that cannot say what it wants cannot be betrayed, but neither can it be joined, and the tension between those two truths is where the theory ends.

### Q: How does one-dimensionality describe language as well as politics?

The political condition and the linguistic condition are the same closure seen at different levels. In the chapters on the closing of the universe of discourse, Marcuse argues that administered language fixes words to functions, fuses nouns with the attributes the system assigns them, and drops the constructions, the subordinate clauses, the historical qualifications, in which a thing can be distinguished from its current use. Concepts that once pointed beyond the given, freedom, justice, happiness, are audited against usage and retired as imprecise. A population may retain every formal liberty of speech inside such a language and still lack the instruments for formulating an alternative, since the alternative would have to be stated in the vocabulary that excludes it. Politics becomes one-dimensional when its language does, and the language goes first.

### Q: What did Marcuse concede to his critics about the working class?

He conceded that the integration his famous book described was a tendency, not a destiny, and he conceded it on evidence. The French strikes and factory occupations of 1968, in which millions of workers overflowed the unions that supposedly administered them, showed him the administered consciousness cracking along the old seams of the labour process, and he said so. He conceded likewise that the book had understated the system's dependence on misery at its periphery, the colonial and postcolonial labour subsidising metropolitan affluence, and that the substratum passage should have developed the connection. What he did not concede was the mechanism: the concessions mark where integration fails, and a theory of a mechanism is strengthened, not refuted, by an honest map of its failures.
