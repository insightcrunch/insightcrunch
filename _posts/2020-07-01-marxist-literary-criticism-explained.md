## A Method Stated as Four Questions

A reader encountering Marxist literary criticism for the first time is usually handed a slogan instead of a method. The slogan says that such criticism looks for class conflict inside plots, counts the workers among the characters, and grades a novel by the politics of whoever wrote it. Almost everything in that description is wrong, and the errors are not minor distortions at the edges. They invert the tradition's founding principle, mistake its most elementary exercise for its whole procedure, and leave the student unable to perform the analysis the name actually denotes. This article replaces the slogan with the method. Marxist literary criticism analyses a work in relation to the material conditions under which it was produced, the social significance carried by its form, the ideology it takes for granted and cannot articulate, and the history of its reception. Those four relations generate four questions, and the questions, asked in order and answered with evidence, are the method.

![A reading desk with the four questions table open beside editions of the realist novel, Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-75.webp)

None of the four questions asks what the author thought. That omission is deliberate and foundational. In a letter written in 1888, Friedrich Engels praised Honoré de Balzac, a French novelist whose political sympathies were royalist and legitimist, for a body of fiction that revealed the workings of French society more truthfully than the work of writers whose opinions Engels shared. The realism had triumphed over the sympathies. From that judgement the tradition drew a principle that governs everything else in this article: what a work shows owes no obedience to what its author believed. A reading that infers a text's politics from its author's biography has therefore not begun, however confidently it proceeds. The four questions begin somewhere else entirely, with paper, prices, publishers, and readers.

The questions also discipline each other. A criticism that asks only the third question, the question about ideology and silence, is performing ideology critique, a respectable activity that many schools share. Asking only the first is doing book history. Asking only the second is doing formal analysis with a sociological accent. Marxist literary analysis, in the sense this article defines and defends, asks all four and lets the answers correct one another. Production facts constrain claims about form. Reception evidence tests claims about effect. Silences identified in the text must be checked against what the conditions of production made sayable. The method is a system of cross-examination, and its findings are strongest exactly where the four lines of evidence converge or productively disagree.

A further preliminary matters for everything that follows. The tradition's central internal dispute, the argument between realism and formal experiment associated with Georg Lukács and Bertolt Brecht, was an argument between two Marxists. Students routinely misremember it as a confrontation between Marxist criticism and its enemies, and the misremembering conceals the dispute's real lesson, which is that the tradition has always contained incompatible answers to its own second question. What form itself encodes was contested from inside, at full strength, by writers who shared the first, third, and fourth questions entirely. Holding that fact in view from the start prevents the flattened picture in which Marxist criticism appears as a single doctrine with a single approved style.

### How is Marxist literary criticism defined?

Marxist literary criticism is the analysis of literature in relation to material production, social form, ideology, and reception. It asks under what conditions a work was made, what its form presupposes, what it assumes without stating and cannot say, and who read it with what effect, rather than grading an author's opinions.

That definition deserves unpacking clause by clause, because each clause excludes a familiar misunderstanding. Material production means the actual circumstances of writing and publishing: who commissioned or bought the work, in what format it appeared, at what price, through which distribution channels, under what legal constraints, and for which purchasers. Social form means that a literary form is never a neutral container. The omniscient narrator of the nineteenth-century novel, the soliloquy of Renaissance drama, the serial cliffhanger, the lyric speaker addressing an absent beloved: each presupposes a world in which such a voice is intelligible and a readership formed to receive it. Ideology, in this vocabulary, is not a set of opinions a character announces. It is the framework a text treats as obvious, the assumptions so deep that the narrative never states them because it never needs to. Reception means the documented history of reading: reviews, sales channels described qualitatively, censorship records, school syllabuses, translations, adaptations, and the uses later generations made of the work.

Set against that definition, the slogan version fails on every clause. Hunting for class conflict in a plot examines neither production nor form nor reception, and it treats ideology as explicit content, which is precisely what the tradition's concept of ideology denies. Counting proletarian characters is a census, not an analysis. Grading authors by their opinions violates the triumph of realism principle directly. The slogan survives because it is easy to teach in a single sentence and because hostile summaries and lazy defences agree on it for opposite reasons. The method takes longer to state, and this article states it at the length it requires.

### Why does the method begin with conditions rather than opinions?

Because opinions are the least reliable evidence a work offers about itself. Authors misdescribe their own achievements routinely, revise their accounts retrospectively, and hold views that their craft contradicts on every page. Conditions, by contrast, leave records: contracts, prices, formats, print contexts, and censorship files that a researcher can inspect and a rival can check.

There is a deeper reason as well. A materialist criticism holds that consciousness is shaped by the conditions in which people live and work, and it would be incoherent for such a criticism to treat the author's declared consciousness as the sovereign key to the work. The author's opinions are themselves among the things to be explained, not the explanation. Engels's letter performs exactly this reversal. Balzac's legitimism is a fact about Balzac. The novels' relentless exposure of the aristocracy he admired, and their fascinated documentation of the bourgeoisie he deplored, are facts about the work. The letter insists that the second set of facts outweighs the first for critical purposes, and it grounds that insistence in the practice of realism itself: a writer committed to showing how a society actually moves will register forces that his preferences would rather conceal.

The point generalises beyond one novelist. Whenever a syllabus, an examination answer, or a review moves directly from an author's known politics to a verdict on the work, the triumph of realism principle identifies the move as the tradition's cardinal error. The error is common because biography is vivid and quotable, whereas production records are archival and dull. The method accepts the dullness as the price of evidence.

## The First Question: Conditions of Production

The opening question concerns the material circumstances of making, and it subdivides immediately. Who published the work, and on what commercial terms? For what market was it shaped, and at what price was it offered? In what format did it reach readers, and what did that format make possible or impossible? Who could read it, given the price, the language, the distribution network, and the state of schooling? What legal or institutional pressures bore on its making: censorship, licensing, patronage, the expectations of a magazine editor, the discipline of a circulating library? Each sub-question directs attention to records rather than impressions, and the records, where they survive, answer in specifics.

The history of the book, as a scholarly field, asks many of these same questions without any Marxist commitment, and the overlap is instructive. Bibliographers established long ago that format is meaning-bearing: a novel issued in three expensive volumes for circulating-library subscribers is a different social object from the same text reprinted cheaply for railway bookstalls, even where not a word differs. Marxist criticism takes over the bibliographer's evidence and asks a further question of it: how do these conditions enter the work's form and content? Length is the clearest case. The three-volume structure rewarded subplots, delayed resolutions, and a certain amplitude of social panorama. Serial publication rewarded the cliffhanger and the recurring character. The cheap single volume rewarded compression. Form follows the market more often than manifestos admit, and the first question exists to catch that determination in the act.

Price deserves the same forensic attention. A book priced beyond a worker's weekly wage addresses a different society from a penny pamphlet, whatever their respective contents proclaim. Critics in this tradition therefore treat price, format, and channel as part of the work's social meaning, not as packaging external to it. The point applies with equal force to theatre, where ticket pricing and licensing shaped repertory, and to periodical literature, where the magazine's advertisers and proprietors set boundaries no contributor needed to be told about twice. Where scholars such as Robert Darnton and William St Clair have reconstructed reading conditions from archival evidence, describing circulation qualitatively and naming their sources, the Marxist critic inherits findings that no amount of textual ingenuity could supply from the page alone.

### What evidence answers the production question?

Publisher archives, contracts, price and format records, censorship and licensing files, booksellers' catalogues, and the physical bibliography of surviving copies. Such evidence is checkable by rivals and corrigible by new finds, which is why the tradition treats production claims as its most secure findings and reception claims as nearly as firm.

Censorship repays particular attention because it operates on the text before any reader arrives. A licensing system does not merely forbid particular lines; it teaches writers which subjects are expensive, which treatments invite trouble, and which silences are prudent. The resulting absences belong jointly to the first question and the third: they are produced silences, and distinguishing a produced silence from a structural one is among the finer discriminations the method requires. Patronage works similarly in earlier periods. A dedication is a receipt. The patron's expectations, the economics of the gift relationship, and the competition among clients all leave traces in tone, subject, and scale that the production question teaches the reader to see.

The production question also governs periodicals and anthologies, the forgotten machinery of literary life. Most readers in most centuries met literature in selections, abridgements, and magazine extracts rather than in whole books. What an editor excerpted, what a school anthology canonised, what a cheap reprint series made available: these decisions manufactured the literature that a given generation could actually read. A criticism attentive to production therefore reads the anthology and the syllabus as literary facts in their own right, and treats the survival of a text as an event with causes, not as the natural reward of merit.

None of this requires reducing the work to its conditions. The claim is narrower and stronger: conditions set the field within which choices were made, priced some choices cheaply and others dearly, and determined which audiences were reachable. Two writers in identical conditions write different books, and the method has other questions for the difference. But a reading that knows nothing of the conditions will mistake necessities for intentions and market effects for meanings, and it will do so invisibly, with complete confidence. The first question is the method's defence against that invisible error.

## Before the Letter: The Tradition's Prehistory

The 1888 letter became a charter partly because the tradition before it was scattered. Marx himself left no treatise on literature. What he left were judgements embedded in other arguments, reading habits visible in his notebooks and correspondence, and a set of analogies between economic and aesthetic production that later critics mined with varying discipline. He admired Balzac independently of Engels's letter and read him in the original, returning to the novels across decades. He quoted Shakespeare constantly, not as ornament but as evidence, treating Timon of Athens on money as a philosophical text about the power of exchange to invert every human quality. He planned, with Engels, a substantial work on Balzac that was never written. The fragments matter because they show the founders practising the method before the method existed: reading fiction as social knowledge, trusting the rendering over the recommendation, and treating literary form, in the Shakespeare case, as a register of what money does to language itself.

Engels contributed a second letter that practitioners pair with the Harkness letter, written in 1885 to Minna Kautsky, in which he resisted the thesis novel from the other side of the desk. A socialist novel, he argued there in substance, achieves its purpose when it shatters the reader's illusions by faithfully rendering real conditions, without offering solutions or thrusting the author's convictions at the reader. The tendency must spring from the situation and the action itself. Read together, the two letters compose a small poetics: typicality as the standard of rendering, hidden opinions as the discipline of the author, and the triumph of realism as the warrant that the discipline works. The tradition's later elaborations, however vast, are footnotes and challenges to those few pages, which is why this article gives the letter pride of place over systems built long afterwards.

Between the founders and the twentieth-century disputants stand the critics of the Second International and the Russian tradition, whose contributions the surveys often compress. Russian criticism brought its own lineage of socially committed reading, in which the critic's task was frankly civic, and the encounter between that lineage and the German theoretical inheritance shaped the Soviet debates from which socialist realism eventually emerged by decree. The compression matters for one reason: students who meet the tradition only in its Anglo-American academic form underestimate how much of its history was conducted in exile journals, party schools, and prison notebooks, under production conditions that the first question would insist on examining. The criticism has a sociology of its own, and the method, applied to itself, recovers it.

### Why does the tradition treat a private letter as a founding text?

Because the letter demonstrates the method's central principle on a case where authorial sympathy and textual showing point in opposite directions. A treatise could state the triumph of realism; only the Balzac judgement performs it, grading an opponent's novels above allies' work on evidence internal to the rendering.

The prehistory also explains a peculiarity of the tradition's canon. Its central documents include letters, prefaces, review essays, prison writings, and the theoretical chapters of plays' programmes, alongside the expected treatises. This is not carelessness about genre. A criticism that holds form to be socially significant cannot be surprised that its own classics arrive in the forms its conditions allowed: the exile journal's exchange, the letter to a young novelist, the notes written between rehearsals. The archive of Marxist criticism is itself a first-question exhibit, and teachers do students a service by pointing at the fact before the students discover it as an objection.

There is a further strand in the prehistory that the four questions illuminate retrospectively. The nineteenth-century reviewers who first treated the novel as a social document, the historians who read literature as the record of a people's life, and the philologists who reconstructed cultures from their texts were all, without the vocabulary, asking fragments of the four questions. The tradition did not invent social reading from nothing. It systematised an existing practice, gave it a theory of production and ideology the earlier practice lacked, and armed it with the triumph principle as a safeguard against its own partisanship. Originality in method is usually systematisation of this kind, and claiming more for the tradition than that would violate the standards of evidence this article has been enforcing.

## The Founders on Specific Forms

Two further judgements from the founding generation deserve record because they seeded later disputes. Marx's scattered remarks on Greek art posed a problem the tradition has never stopped worrying: if art is conditioned by the mode of production, why does the art of a society built on slavery still give pleasure and stand as a norm to a society that abolished its basis? His answer gestured at the charm of a society's childhood, an answer most later critics found evocative and insufficient in equal measure. The problem matters because it is the reception question in its hardest form, the survival of works across modes of production, and any theory that cannot address it will fail the fourth question's evidence wholesale. Lukács's historical novel theory, Goldmann's homologies, and Williams's residual category are all, in part, machines for answering Marx's Greek problem with better tools.

Engels's judgements on drama supplied a second seed. Writing to Ferdinand Lassalle about a historical tragedy, he praised Shakespearean breadth against Schillerian declamation, asking for more of the Falstaffian background, the teeming social world behind the principal figures, and less of the characters serving as mouthpieces for the spirit of the age. The vocabulary of the letter anticipates the whole later programme: typicality against abstraction, the rendered world against the announced thesis, the author's opinions subordinated to the density of the showing. When Lukács later opposed narration to description and Brecht later opposed the epic to the dramatic, both were extending arguments whose first drafts sit in the founders' correspondence about plays neither of them is remembered for discussing.

## Production in Depth: The Machinery Behind the Book

The first question rewards a closer tour of the machinery, because students consistently underestimate how much of a work's character was settled before its author wrote a sentence that survived. Consider the circulating library, the institution that dominated respectable fiction's economics in Victorian Britain. A library that bought in bulk and lent by subscription could make or break a novel, and its purchasing standards operated as a private censorship more effective than law: the librarian's sense of what a young person might safely read set boundaries that authors internalised as craft. The three-volume format persisted because libraries preferred it, since three volumes lent to three subscribers at once, and the novel's celebrated amplitude, its subplots and its delayed unions, fitted the format the way a song fits its verse form. When the libraries withdrew their support from the format in the 1890s, the three-volume novel died within seasons, and the single-volume novel that replaced it was shorter, faster, and differently built. No manifesto killed the triple-decker. A purchasing decision did, and the first question exists so that criticism can see such causation where a purely textual reading sees only a change in taste.

Serial publication imposed a different discipline with equal force. The monthly part and the magazine instalment required each number to justify the next purchase: a revelation spaced here, a crisis deferred there, a minor character promoted because readers wrote in about him. Dickens's career is the standard exhibit, studied by scholars of the book trade for generations, and its lesson generalises. The serial novel's famous abundance of incident is partly an economic artefact, the shape of a story sold in slices to an audience that could stop paying. Critics in this tradition read that abundance doubly: as a formal fact about the works, which the second question then interprets, and as a market fact, which no interpretation may ignore. The writer who seems to linger over a subplot may be filling a number; the writer who kills a character at a part's end may be selling the next one. Intention, under serial conditions, is a joint product of imagination and instalment plan.

The theatre's machinery was blunter. Licensing regimes required scripts to be approved before performance in many European capitals across the period this tradition covers, and the lord chamberlain's examiner, the imperial censor, and their equivalents read with pencils. Playwrights learned to write for the pencil: to displace the dangerous setting into a distant century, to give the dangerous speech to a character the plot will punish, to build the meaning into stage business that no script examiner could strike out. Whole dramaturgies of indirection arose under that pressure, and a criticism that reads the indirection as pure aesthetic choice mistakes the censor's work for the author's. The displacement also produced genuine art, which complicates any simple lament: constraint is a condition, and conditions, as the method insists, enable as well as forbid. The first question does not ask the critic to pity the work. It asks the critic to know what the work was made against.

Periodical publication governed poetry and the essay as thoroughly as libraries governed fiction. The great reviews paid by the sheet, set the length of the critical essay by their column economics, and made reputations by placements that scholars can still reconstruct from publishers' archives. An author's politics might be legible enough in his contributions, but the review's proprietors, advertisers, and party connections framed what could appear, and the frame, not the contributor's opinions, is the production fact with explanatory power. When the tradition's historians reconstruct those frames, they are doing for criticism what the criticism does for novels: refusing to derive the product from the producer's declared mind.

### How did price decide who a work could address?

Price sorted audiences before content reached them. A volume priced for library subscription addressed the respectable household; a cheap reprint addressed the railway traveller and the autodidact; a penny issue addressed the street. The same text in successive formats is therefore several social acts, and reception history follows the formats.

Colonial and translation circuits extend the machinery across the map. The colonial edition, printed for markets its home publisher treated as captive, carried metropolitan literature into reading cultures that answered it in their own tongues and genres; the answers, in time, travelled back. Translation flows were and remain asymmetric, governed by the economics of publishing in dominant languages, and the asymmetry is a production fact about world literature that no account of influence can bypass. A criticism attentive to the first question reads the world republic of letters as a trade network with tariffs and monopolies, not as a conversation among equals, and it reads each translated classic as an import whose local meaning was set partly by the terms of its arrival.

The schooling system completes the circuit on the demand side. Compulsory education manufactured the mass readership the cheap formats served, and the syllabus decided which fraction of that readership would meet which books under compulsion, with examinations attached. Canon formation, treated under the fourth question below, is unintelligible without this machinery: the set text is a production decision renewed annually by committees, publishers' educational departments, and the second-hand market in annotated editions. The tradition's interest in the syllabus is therefore not a philistine reduction of literature to schooling. It is the recognition that for most readers in most places, the school is the library, the censor, and the bookseller combined.

Against this machinery, the lone author of the romantic account, communing with inspiration in a garret, appears as what the tradition says he is: a real figure whose real sufferings were nevertheless structured by advances, copyrights, deadlines, and the price of paper. Copyright itself belongs in the machinery. The extension of literary property changed the author's economic being across the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, converting patronage dependence into market dependence and making the professional writer possible as a social type. The type's anxieties, its productivity, its genres, and its politics all bear the mark of that conversion, and the criticism that knows the mark reads the period's literature with an extra dimension the purely formal account cannot supply.

## The Second Question: What the Form Encodes

What does the form itself encode? Every literary form presupposes a society and a reader. The presuppositions are rarely stated, because a form that had to argue for its own intelligibility would already have failed. The omniscient narrator presupposes that a single consciousness can survey a whole social world and report its interconnections reliably; that presupposition flourished alongside the nineteenth-century novel's ambition to render society as a knowable totality. The dramatic soliloquy presupposes an audience prepared to accept speech as the transparent medium of an interior self. The detective tale presupposes surroundings in which clues can be read, guilt attaches to a person, and order can be restored by reasoning. None of these presuppositions is a political opinion. Each is a piece of social architecture built into a genre, and the second question trains the reader to inspect the architecture.

Form also encodes a relation to time. The realist novel's patient accumulation of circumstance presupposes a world that changes gradually and intelligibly, in which a life can be narrated as development. The modernist fragment presupposes a world experienced as discontinuous. The epic presupposes a past that authorises the present. When Lukács argued that form is the truly social element in literature, this is the stratum he meant: not the opinions a work contains, which any pamphlet could contain, but the shape of experience the work's construction ratifies as normal. A writer may dissent from every proposition his narrator asserts and still ratify, at the level of form, the world his genre assumes. Conversely, formal experiment can dissent where content conforms, which is why the tradition's argument about modernism could not be settled by examining what modernist works said.

The second question therefore refuses the container theory of form, on which a content is poured into a shape that leaves it unchanged. On the container theory, the same story told as a ballad, a three-volume novel, and a radio serial would be the same work in three packages. The tradition denies this flatly. Each form selects what can be shown, assigns importance by structure rather than by statement, and positions its audience differently: the ballad's listeners are a community remembering, the novel's reader is a solitary judge, the serial's audience is a crowd kept waiting. Meaning lives in those positionings as much as in any sentence.

### How can a form carry social meaning?

A form carries social meaning by presupposing the world in which its devices make sense. Omniscience presupposes a surveyable society, the soliloquy an audience that trusts spoken interiority, the cliffhanger a market that sells anticipation. Reading the presuppositions, rather than the pronouncements, is how the second question proceeds.

Consider free indirect discourse, the technique by which a narrator's voice slides into a character's idiom without quotation marks. The device presupposes and produces a specific social relation: the reader is placed inside a consciousness while retaining the narrator's superior vantage, invited simultaneously to inhabit and to judge. Whole schools of fiction are built on the doubleness. A Marxist reading asks what social experience that doubleness corresponds to, and the answers reach toward the position of the educated reader in a stratified society, at home in the drawing room and condescending to the kitchen, fluent in both registers and native to neither. The device is a pleasure, certainly. The second question insists it is also a position.

Or consider the marriage plot, the engine of so much classic fiction. Its structure presupposes a society in which marriage is the principal economic transaction available to women of a certain class, in which courtship is therefore a negotiation conducted under the forms of romance, and in which the distribution of property at the altar resolves the narrative because it resolves the heroine's material fate. The plot's satisfactions are real. So is its presupposition. When the economic basis shifts, the plot does not vanish, but it changes valence, becoming nostalgic, ironic, or fantastic, and the history of those valences is a history of the form's social meaning written across two centuries of practice.

The second question also governs verse and drama. The heroic couplet's closed symmetry presupposes a world whose order can be mirrored in balanced statement; its decline tracks the decline of the world-picture that made balance feel like truth. The well-made play's withheld secret presupposes a bourgeois interior in which reputation is capital and its loss is catastrophe. The chorus of Greek tragedy presupposes a citizen body that watches itself deliberate. In each case the form is a deposit of social relations, legible to a reader who asks what had to be true of a society for this shape to feel natural.

None of this makes form a code with a single key. Presuppositions are enabling conditions, not messages, and a great work can strain its form's presuppositions until the form itself becomes the subject. The tradition's strongest readings are of exactly those strain points: the novel whose omniscience keeps failing, the tragedy whose chorus no longer knows what to advise, the serial whose cliffhangers expose the market mechanism they serve. Form encodes most legibly where it creaks.

## The Third Question: Ideology and Silence

Which beliefs does the text treat as too obvious to state, and at what points does it fall silent? This is the question most often mistaken for the whole method, and it is genuinely the most distinctive of the four. Ideology, in the usage this tradition inherits, is not false opinion and not propaganda. It is the lived framework within which a society's members experience their world as natural: the assumptions about property, family, merit, gender, nation, and desert that a text relies on without stating, because stating them would already be a kind of failure. A novel does not argue that servants are furniture; it furnishes rooms with them. It does not argue that a woman's worth is her marriageability; it builds its plot on the premise. The third question reads for the furniture.

Silence is the sharper instrument. Pierre Macherey, in the work of 1966 that founded symptomatic reading, argued that a text is hollowed out by the ideology it works on: it says more and less than it intends, and its significant features include what it cannot say, the points where its project requires something its framework cannot supply. The critic reads for gaps, contradictions, abrupt changes of register, characters who vanish, questions the narrative raises and then hurries past. A colonial adventure that needs the colonised population as scenery will fall silent exactly where that population's interior life would have to begin. A novel of industrial life that needs the factory as backdrop will describe the owner's dilemmas in depth and the operative's in slogans. The silences are not accusations against the author, who usually could not have done otherwise. They are measurements of the ideology's limits, taken at the points where the text strains against them.

### How does symptomatic reading proceed?

Symptomatic reading treats a text's gaps, contradictions, and silences as its most revealing features. Following Macherey, the critic asks what the work cannot say, where its ideology fails to supply what its own project requires, and reads those failures as evidence about the framework the work inhabits.

The method has a discipline that its imitators often drop. A silence counts as evidence only when the text's own project creates the expectation that is then refused: the narrative that follows a fortune to its making and then declines to enter the counting house, the biography that reaches a marriage and changes the subject, the war story whose logistics never appear. Absence in general proves nothing, since every text omits almost everything. The symptomatic reader must show the seam, the place where the text approaches its limit and turns aside, ideally with visible strain: a euphemism, a sudden abstraction, a joke that arrives exactly on schedule to change the subject. Strain is the signature. Where there is no strain, there may be no symptom, only the ordinary finitude of any narrative.

Ideology also appears positively, as the set of resolutions a text finds satisfying. Endings are ideological documents of the first order. The marriage that settles the heroine, the fortune that rewards the patient, the death that punishes the transgressor, the reconciliation that restores the estate: each ending ratifies a world in which such settlements count as justice. The third question asks what a text's satisfactions presuppose, and it asks the same of its tragedies. What a culture finds properly tragic, the losses it agrees to grieve and the losses it files as statistics, maps its ideology as faithfully as its comedies map its hopes.

A caution belongs here, and the tradition's best practitioners state it themselves. The silences question is the hardest of the four to constrain, because an ingenious reader can find a significant silence anywhere. The objection section of this article returns to the problem at full strength. The working safeguards are the ones already named: require the text's own project to raise the expectation, require visible strain at the seam, and check the reading against the production evidence, since many silences are explained by censorship, market, or genre before ideology needs to be invoked. A silence the censor imposed is a first-question fact. A silence the genre imposes is a second-question fact. Only the residue, the silence that conditions and form leave unexplained, belongs wholly to the third question, and the residue is usually where the deepest evidence lies.

## The Fourth Question: Reception and Effect

Who read the work, how, and with what effect? The fourth question returns the text to history at the far end. Works are read by particular publics, in particular editions, under particular descriptions, and the readings accumulate into a second life that no author controls. A novel issued as a shocker becomes a school classic; a pamphlet written for a faction becomes a nation's founding text; a play suppressed in its decade becomes the repertory's safest revival in another. The Marxist critic treats that second life as part of the work's social being, and treats claims about a work's effect as empirical claims, answerable to reviews, censorship records, adaptation histories, syllabus adoptions, and the testimony of reading publics where scholars have gathered it.

Reception also corrects the other three questions. A reading that attributes a subversive force to a text must reckon with the text's actual career: if the work was embraced without friction by the institutions it supposedly subverts, the claim needs refinement, though not automatic surrender, since institutions are skilled at domesticating what they embrace. A reading that treats a work as its period's typical product must reckon with the work's contemporary failure or success. The archive of reception is full of such corrections, and the tradition's respect for it is one mark distinguishing its practice from free association about texts.

### Why does reception belong inside the method?

Because a work's social meaning is partly made by its readers and institutions. Editions, reviews, censorship, syllabuses, translations, and adaptations decide what a text becomes in a culture. A criticism claiming social significance for literature cannot exempt the documented history of reading from its evidence.

The history of reading, pursued by scholars such as Jonathan Rose in his studies of working-class readers, repeatedly embarrasses assumptions made from armchairs. Texts assigned to the dustbin of propaganda turn out to have been read against the grain by autodidact readers who took from them what they needed; canonical radicals turn out to have been consumed as improving literature by the respectable. Rose's evidence, described qualitatively and grounded in memoirs and library records, shows readers as agents rather than vessels, and any Marxist account of effect must absorb that agency. The fourth question, honestly pursued, is where the tradition's claims about literature's social power go to be tested, and sometimes to be revised downward. That vulnerability is a virtue. A method whose effect-claims cannot be embarrassed by evidence is not a method but a mood.

Translation and adaptation extend the question across borders and media. What a culture selects for translation reveals its needs; what translators alter reveals its limits; what adapters keep reveals what the receiving age takes the work to be. The stage history of a classic, the film versions that fix its images for millions who never open the book, the abridgements that decide which version of a long novel most readers will ever meet: these are not afterthoughts to the work's meaning but major constituents of it. The fourth question gives the critic licence and obligation to study them as such.

Asked together, the four questions compose a single circuit: production shapes form, form organises ideology, ideology meets readers, readers and institutions remake the work's conditions for the next generation of writers. The circuit, not any single question, is the method. The table that follows states the circuit in the findable form this series uses, one row per question, so that a reader can carry the whole procedure away and apply it before reading another page of theory.

## Form in Depth: Narration, Description, and the Ending

Lukács's most portable distinction opposes narration to description, and it belongs in any full account of the second question. In narration, he argued, the writer participates in the fates of characters caught in a process; events matter because they change people and are changed by them, and details earn their place by mediating between the person and the historical movement. In description, the writer observes a world arranged as spectacle; characters become spectators of their own lives, and details accumulate as inventory, accurate and inert. The distinction was aimed at naturalism, whose documentary ambitions Lukács respected and whose method he judged a capitulation: the described world, however exactly rendered, has stopped moving, and a stopped world cannot show the forces that move it. The race in a naturalist novel, observed with a clinician's patience, tells the reader everything about the spectacle and nothing about the society that stages it, whereas the race in a realist novel, narrated through the fortunes it ruins and makes, becomes an organ of the whole social body.

The distinction survives its author's system because it names something readers recognise without instruction: the difference between a scene that happens to people and a scene arranged for inspection. Critics outside the tradition have reinvented it under other names whenever they distinguish the novel that dramatises from the novel that exhibits. Its weakness is the one the Brechtian rejoinder exposes. A century whose characteristic experience includes the spectacle, the administered display, and the observed life may require description as its truthful mode, and the writer who narrates such a world in the old participatory manner may be falsifying it with warmth. The argument cannot be settled in the abstract, which is why the tradition keeps it alive as an argument rather than embalming it as a rule.

Endings deserve a second visit under the second question, because the ending is where form and ideology sign their treaty. The realist ending distributes fates: marriages, fortunes, deaths, exiles, reconciliations. The distribution is a judgement the narrative has been preparing structurally, and readers feel its justice or its cheating in their bodies before they can argue about it. A criticism attentive to form reads the distribution as the work's operative theory of desert, more honest than any character's speech about justice, because the plot's rewards are the author's real theodicy. When the distribution surprises, when the patient are ruined and the ruthless inherit, the form is registering a world whose official theodicy the author may still profess. The triumph of realism often announces itself exactly there, in an ending the writer's opinions would not have chosen and the writer's craft could not avoid.

### What does an ending reveal that a preface cannot?

An ending reveals the plot's working theory of desert: who is rewarded, punished, married, ruined, or forgiven by the narrative machinery. Prefaces announce intentions; distributions enact them. The gap between the announced and the enacted is among the most reliable evidence the second and third questions share.

The narrator's authority is another formal deposit worth assaying. The nineteenth-century omniscient narrator speaks with a confidence that presupposes a reader who accepts that a society can be known whole and judged from above it; the figure flourished while that presupposition held and decayed, across the following century, into the unreliable narrators, the restricted focalisations, and the self-cancelling voices that presuppose a reader who accepts no such thing. The sequence is not a progress and not a decline in itself. It is a record, written in technique, of what successive reading publics could be asked to believe about the knowability of their world. A Marxist reading treats the sequence as evidence about the societies that produced it, which is the second question doing its characteristic work at the scale of literary history.

Genre hierarchies carry the same kind of deposit. Every period ranks its forms, and the ranking encodes a social order: the epic above the romance, tragedy above comedy, the novel below everything until it conquered everything. The conquest of the novel is the exemplary case, since the form rose with the class whose experience it rendered and was denigrated, in its rise, by exactly the criteria the older hierarchy supplied. Bakhtin's account of the novel as the genre of becoming, hostile to every finished hierarchy including its own, radicalises the point: the novel's form encodes a society in motion, whose languages have not settled into a single authorised voice. Whether or not the classification dispute around Bakhtin is ever settled, that account of the novel's social meaning remains one of the second question's permanent acquisitions.

Verse forms deposit their evidence more quietly. The sonnet's turn presupposes a mind that argues with itself in a settled space; the ballad's refrain presupposes a community that sings the story together and owns it in common; the dramatic monologue presupposes a listener trapped with a speaker whose self-exposure is the event. Anthologies rearrange these deposits when they lift poems from their occasions, and the rearrangement is a reception fact layered over a formal one. The method's questions, once learned, keep generating such layerings wherever a reader applies them, which is either their fertility or their menace, depending on the discipline with which the evidence rules are kept.

## Ideology in Depth: Typicality and the Furniture of the World

Typicality, Engels's formula, deserves a fuller unpacking because it is the hinge between the second and third questions. The typical character is neither the statistical average nor the eccentric exception. The average flattens the forces of an age into a mean that no one inhabits; the exception escapes them into singularity. The typical figure concentrates them: a provincial lawyer's son whose ambition is entirely personal and entirely the product of a specific settlement between classes, a landowner whose kindness and uselessness are one quality, a clerk whose inner life is the exact shape of the bureaucracy that employs him. In such figures the ideology of an age becomes visible as a fate rather than a doctrine, because the character's common sense, the furniture of his world, is what the plot keeps colliding with. The critic who grasps typicality stops asking whether a character is representative in the census sense and starts asking which forces had to meet for this person to be possible.

The furniture metaphor can be pressed further. Every fictional world is furnished with objects whose presence the narrative never justifies: the carriage that signals rank, the meal that signals security, the room whose size is a destiny. Material culture scholars and Marxist critics meet on this ground, because the furniture is evidence about production and consumption that the text supplies involuntarily. A novel's things are dated, priced, and classed with a precision its author could not fake and rarely noticed, which is why historians quarry fiction for the history of everyday life. The third question's discipline is to read the furniture as the ideology's unconscious: the world the text assumes its readers will find natural, priced, and furnished just so.

### How does a critic distinguish ideology from opinion in a text?

Opinion is what a text argues, in speeches, narrators' asides, and arranged debates. Ideology is what the text assumes: the furniture it never justifies, the resolutions it finds satisfying, the questions its plot never thinks to ask. The critic distinguishes them by watching what the work treats as needing no defence.

Silence, the third question's sharper instrument, has a taxonomy worth stating. There is the silence of the unmentionable, where law or custom forbids; the silence of the unthinkable, where the framework supplies no concept; the silence of the uninteresting, where the audience's assumed world makes a topic beneath notice; and the silence of the unspeakable proper, where the text's project requires what its framework prohibits. The four have different causes and different evidentiary values, and the symptomatic reader's craft lies in assigning a given seam to its kind. The unmentionable belongs largely to the first question, the uninteresting to the fourth, since audiences define it; the unthinkable and the unspeakable belong to the third, and of those the unspeakable, where the strain is visible in the prose itself, is the strongest evidence the method ever gets. Macherey's readings trade on the distinction even where his vocabulary differs, and teaching the taxonomy is the quickest way to convert students from silence-spotting as a parlour game into symptomatic reading as a discipline.

Ideology also governs what a text finds funny, frightening, and shameful, three registers in which assumptions operate without supervision. A period's comic servants, its terrifying foreigners, its ruined women: the stock figures of a literature are its ideology in fancy dress, and their slow mutation across decades is among the most legible records of ideological change that reception history preserves. The critic who tracks a stock figure's career, from menace to butt to hero, is reading ideology at the level where it actually lives in a culture, below argument and above reflex.

## Reception in Depth: Canon, School, and the Second Life

The fourth question's largest theatre is the canon, the shifting list of works a culture designates as its classics. Canon formation was once narrated as the verdict of time, as if survival were a natural property of excellence. The tradition's contribution, since absorbed by literary studies generally, is to narrate it as a process with agents: editors who kept works in print, anthologists who excerpted them, examination boards that set them, publishers whose series made them cheap, and teachers whose syllabuses renewed the designation annually. A work can be lost by a publisher's bankruptcy and recovered by an editor's enthusiasm; its meaning for a century can be fixed by the selection an anthology happened to make. None of this denies quality. It denies that quality acts in history unmediated, and it directs the critic to the mediations, which are documented and arguable, rather than to the mystery, which is neither.

The school is the canon's engine room. Set texts reach more readers than any other channel, and they reach them young, under instruction, with the work's authorised interpretation supplied by the teacher's edition and the examination's mark scheme. Scholars of education in this tradition have shown how the authorised reading of a classic can invert its reputation across a generation: the rebel poet domesticated into a lesson on metre, the subversive novel stabilised into a lesson on theme. The fourth question reads the school edition as a literary institution in its own right, notes and all, since the notes tell the student what the work is for before the work can speak. That the domestication is never total, that school readers smuggle their own meanings out of set books as Jonathan Rose's autodidacts did from libraries, is the standing correction the evidence supplies to any too-simple account of institutional power.

### Why do classics survive in some centuries and vanish in others?

Survival follows the institutions that reproduce a text: editions kept in print, anthologies that excerpt it, syllabuses that set it, and adapters who remake it. Where the chain breaks, excellence alone does not carry a work across the gap, as the recovery of forgotten writers by later editors repeatedly proves.

Adaptation is the second life in its most visible form. The stage and screen versions of a classic reach audiences the book never will, and they fix interpretations with the force of images: a generation's heroine has a particular face because a casting director chose it. The tradition reads adaptations as reception documents of the adapting age rather than as betrayals or tributes. What an adaptation cuts reveals what its age cannot use; what it adds reveals what its age requires; what it keeps, against every pressure of its medium, is the strongest evidence available of what the work's hold on audiences actually consists in. The critic who studies the cuts is doing symptomatic reading on a culture, with the film as the text and the novel as its repressed.

Translation extends the second life across languages, with losses and gains that are themselves evidence. A poet untranslatable in one century becomes a classic of another language in the next because a translator found the equivalent device; a novelist's reputation can stand higher abroad than at home for a hundred years because the foreign reading answered a foreign need. World literature, in the tradition's handling, is not a summit of universal works but a traffic, and the traffic's routes, imbalances, and translation choices are fourth-question facts that qualify every claim about a work's universal appeal. The appeal may be real. The route is how it travelled, and the route leaves marks on the meaning that arrived.

Reception also includes the hostile forms: banning, burning, bowdlerising, and the quieter hostility of the remainder table and the unrenewed syllabus. A banned book's career is a reception document that testifies, against the banner's intention, to the power the banner attributed to reading. The tradition, whose own classics were banned in many jurisdictions across the period it covers, treats censorship records as a perverse bibliography of influence, evidence about effect supplied by the effect's enemies. Bowdlerisation is subtler evidence of the same kind: the expurgated edition records, cut by cut, what a reading public's guardians believed its members would do with the unexpurgated text, and the cuts map the period's anxieties more precisely than its sermons do.

## The Historical Novel and the Scott Inheritance

Lukács's study of the historical novel supplies the tradition's most complete worked example of the four questions operating together, and a summary belongs in this article because students meet the argument everywhere. Walter Scott, on Lukács's account, invented the modern historical novel not by decorating the present with costumes but by rendering the past as the prehistory of the present: the middling hero, neither great nor base, is carried between contending forces whose collision is the history itself, and the great historical figures appear at the margins, where their effect on ordinary lives can be measured. Production, form, ideology, and reception all enter the account. Scott wrote for a market created by the novel's rise and a readership formed by national crisis; his form encodes a society understanding itself as the product of a past it can narrate; his ideology, conservative and unionist, is triumphed over by a realism that gives the defeated Highland world its full dignity and its necessity of defeat in the same gesture; and his reception remade historical fiction across Europe within a generation.

The Scott case also demonstrates the triumph of realism at the level of a genre rather than a single work. Scott's sympathies were with the settled order his novels show being born; his rendering honours the worlds that order destroyed with an imaginative justice his politics would have rationed. The parallel with Balzac is exact and was Lukács's own: the legitimist and the unionist, divided by language and allegiance, united by a craft that reported against its owners. A tradition that can find its principle operating in the two founding novelists of two nations has evidence that the principle names something about realism itself, not about one letter's rhetoric.

### What makes the middling hero a formal discovery rather than a compromise?

The middling hero's mediocrity is the instrument: positioned between camps, carried by forces larger than himself, he lets the narrative measure those forces by their effect on an ordinary fate. A great hero would bend the history to his will and falsify the relation the form exists to render.

The historical novel argument also carries the tradition's answer to a standing puzzle about anachronism. Scott's characters think in terms their period was only forming, and Lukács defended the practice as necessary anachronism: the past must be rendered intelligible as the present's prehistory, which requires attributing to its people the tendencies of their moment in a clarity the moment itself rarely achieved. The defence distinguishes the fruitful anachronism, which clarifies a tendency, from the costume error, which merely misdates an object, and the distinction has served critics far beyond the tradition whenever historical fiction is judged. It is, at bottom, typicality applied to time: the typical figure of an age may be clearer about the age than any documented individual was, and the novel's licence to construct such figures is its cognitive advantage over the archive.

## The Rise of the Novel and the Watt Challenge

Any account of Marxist literary criticism must reckon with the most influential neighbouring explanation of the novel's rise, Ian Watt's account of formal realism. Watt argued that the novel's distinguishing procedure, its particularity of time, place, and person, its proper names and dated letters and inventoried rooms, embodied the epistemology of a rising individualism: the reader as solitary judge of evidence, the character as a consciousness whose continuity is its identity, the plot as a case to be decided on particulars. The argument is not Marxist, and Watt's framework owes more to Weber and to the history of philosophy than to historical materialism, but its central claim is one the second question can only applaud: a form encodes a society's way of knowing, and the novel's realism is a cognitive style before it is a style of writing. The debate between Watt's account and the tradition's accounts is therefore a family quarrel about which social formation the form encodes and by what mechanism the encoding occurred.

The mechanisms proposed divide instructively. Watt's mechanism runs through literacy's spread, the reading public's composition, and the economic individualism of the middling sort who bought the early novels, a production and reception story told with a bibliographer's care. Lukács's mechanism runs through the historical sense itself, the experience of society as process that the revolutionary decades forced on Europe's consciousness. Goldmann's runs through homology with a stratum's situation. The three accounts agree that the novel is the bourgeois form and disagree about nearly everything else, and the disagreement is productive because the evidence is shared: the same publication records, the same texts, the same reading publics, interrogated by different questions. A student who can stage the three-way comparison possesses the second question in its mature form, as an instrument for generating rival explanations of one formal fact.

Watt's account also supplies the tradition with one of its favourite correctives. His attention to the early novel's actual readers, drawn from the records of the book trade and described with a scholar's caution, complicated every easy claim about the form's class address: the readership was narrower than the rhetoric of the rising middle class suggests, more female than the criticism of the time admitted, and reached by channels, the circulating library above all, that filtered what the form could say. The findings are fourth-question findings in all but allegiance, and their absorption into the tradition's common stock illustrates the uptake this article noted at the outset: the book history questions are standard far beyond Marxism, and the tradition's distinction lies not in owning them but in the circuit it builds from their answers.

### What does formal realism claim about the novel's procedure?

It claims that the novel's particularity, its dated, placed, and named rendering of individual experience, embodies an epistemology: truth as the product of particular evidence judged by a solitary reader. The form's realism is a theory of knowledge enacted as a narrative technique.

The Watt challenge also sharpens the triumph of realism by contrast. Defoe, Richardson, and Fielding, the trio at the centre of Watt's study, held between them nearly the whole range of positions their century offered on religion, trade, and the social order, and the tradition's question to Watt's account is the letter's question scaled up: if the form encodes the epistemology regardless of the author's creed, then the encoding is a fact about the form's social conditions, not about any writer's mind, which is the materialist point in its purest available form. Watt's evidence, gathered without that intention, keeps supplying the point, and the tradition's critics have not been slow to notice. The episode is a model of how research programmes interact when both are honest: the rival's archive becomes one's own evidence, and the argument moves to the interpretation of shared facts, where it belongs.

## Reading Publics: The Evidence of the Common Reader

The fourth question's evidentiary base deserves a section of its own, because the recovery of actual readers is the scholarly achievement that most changed the method's practice in the last half century. Richard Altick's study of the English common reader established the field's questions: how the mass reading public of the nineteenth century was made, by schooling, by cheap print, by libraries and reading rooms, and what that public actually read, as distinct from what its betters feared or hoped it read. William St Clair's work on the reading nation in the romantic period pressed the production side of the same inquiry, reconstructing from publishers' archives what was printed, at what prices, in what formats, and with what consequences for the canon: his demonstration that copyright's economics kept the older poets expensive and the new ones cheap, so that a generation's romanticism was partly a price effect, is the first question's most quoted modern triumph, and it was achieved by counting what the tradition's protocol for this article forbids us to count here, in the archive rather than in the essay.

Jonathan Rose's work on the intellectual life of the British working classes completed the triangle from the readers' side. Drawing on memoirs, library registers, and the records of mutual improvement societies, Rose showed autodidact readers raiding the canon for their own purposes, reading the classics as manuals of self-culture, marking passages their teachers never assigned, and forming judgements that owed nothing to the authorised interpretations of school or press. The findings discipline the tradition's claims about ideology in both directions. Against complacency, they show readers perfectly capable of receiving a text against its grain, which limits what the third question may claim about effects. Against cynicism, they show the canon functioning, in those readers' hands, as an instrument of emancipation they chose for themselves, which complicates any account of the canon as mere imposition. The working-class reader emerges from Rose's archive as the fourth question's ideal subject: situated, evidenced, and nobody's puppet.

### What changed when scholars began studying actual readers?

Claims about effect became testable. The archive of memoirs, registers, and reading records showed readers appropriating, resisting, and repurposing texts in ways no theory of ideology predicted whole, so the tradition's reception claims had to be rebuilt on documented reading rather than on inferred consciousness.

The lesson for practice is a hierarchy of evidence that the four questions table states abstractly and this section can now state concretely. For production: the publisher's archive outranks the author's memoir, which outranks the reviewer's guess. For reception: the register, the memoir, and the annotated copy outrank the manifesto about what the age was reading. For form and ideology, where no archive settles the matter, the text's devices and seams are the evidence, and rival readings are the test. A criticism that observes the hierarchy can be wrong in interesting, corrigible ways. A criticism that inverts it, founding production claims on impressions and effect claims on hopes, will be wrong in the way the objection section described, and no eloquence will save it.

There is a democratising consequence worth naming. The recovery of common readers returned to the tradition a constituency its academic form had misplaced. The autodidacts in Rose's pages were practising, without the vocabulary, a criticism of their own: comparing editions, doubting authorised readings, reading the founders of this very tradition in miners' institute libraries and demanding of the texts exactly what the texts demand of novels, an account of the world that matched the one they lived in. The method's history and the readers' history intersect there, and the intersection is the strongest available answer to the charge that the four questions are an academic game. They were being asked, in substance, in reading rooms that no university served, by readers whose evidence has now, at last, been archived.

## The Triumph of Realism: The Founding Principle

Every tradition has a text that functions as its charter, and for Marxist literary criticism the charter is a private letter. In April 1888 Engels wrote to Margaret Harkness, an English novelist whose novel of London life he had read with admiration and with one reservation. In the course of the letter he reached back to Balzac, dead for nearly four decades, and delivered the judgement on which the whole critical practice rests. Balzac's sympathies were with the class doomed to extinction; his satire was aimed at the men and women he admired; his elegy was for the aristocracy he loved. And yet, Engels argued, the novels compelled their author to see and depict the real relations of French society, the rise of the bourgeoisie he deplored and the decay of the order he mourned, with a fidelity that no partisan of Engels's own side surpassed. Engels called this one of the greatest triumphs of realism. The phrase names the principle: the triumph of realism is the victory of a work's showing over its author's sympathies.

The same letter supplies two further instruments. Engels defined realism as the truthful reproduction of typical characters in typical circumstances, a formula whose weight falls on the word typical: not the average and not the exceptional, but the figure in whom the forces of an age become visible as a destiny. And he offered the advice for which practitioners quote the letter almost as often: the more the author's opinions remain hidden, the better for the work of art. The advice is sometimes misread as a demand that writers have no opinions. It is a demand about where opinions belong in a finished work. An opinion argued in a treatise can be met and answered; an opinion ventriloquised through puppets corrupts the evidence the fiction exists to provide. The hidden-opinions counsel and the triumph principle are two faces of one conviction, that the work's cognitive value lies in what it renders, not in what it recommends.

### What is the triumph of realism?

Engels's argument, in an 1888 letter, that a royalist novelist's realism was valuable precisely because it went against his own political sympathies. The principle is that what a work shows is not determined by what its author believed, which is foundational for Marxist critical practice.

Why did Engels praise a novelist whose politics he opposed? The seeded questions of this article include that one, and the answer structures the whole tradition. Engels praised Balzac because the novels gave him knowledge: a centralised record, as he put it in substance, of French society across the decades after the Revolution, from which he learned more about the period's economic and social relations than from its historians and statisticians. The praise was not tolerance and not paradox-mongering. It was an epistemological claim. Fiction working at the realist discipline registers the movement of a society in its manners, its money, its marriages, and its provincial boredom, and it registers that movement whether or not the registering mind approves of what it sees. The novelist's craft, honestly practised, is an instrument that reports against its owner's wishes. That is why the letter, rather than any treatise, became the charter: it demonstrates the principle on a case where sympathy and showing point in opposite directions, so the principle cannot be confused with agreement.

The principle cuts in both directions, and practitioners insist on the second cut as firmly as the first. If a royalist's work can show more than its author believed, a socialist's work can show less. Fiction written from correct opinions but without the realist discipline produces the thesis novel, in which characters are positions wearing coats and the plot is a verdict looking for evidence. The tradition's own history is littered with such works, praised in their season by sympathetic critics and unreadable within a generation. The triumph principle predicts their fate: since disclosure does not follow from conviction, correct opinions are no substitute for truthful rendering. A Marxist criticism that grades by opinion therefore fails its own charter twice over, once when it condemns the Balzacs and once when it excuses the thesis novels.

### What should a student separate in any reading?

A student should separate what a work shows from what its author intended, and both from what the work's first readers made of it. The three can diverge completely, and the triumph of realism principle exists because the divergence is normal rather than exceptional in serious fiction.

The separation also disciplines the use of biography. An author's letters, diaries, and declared allegiances remain evidence, but evidence of a specific kind: they belong to the production question, as facts about the conditions and intentions under which the work was made. They do not settle the ideology question, because the text's framework may contradict the diary on every page, and they do not settle the form question at all. Students find the reordering liberating once it is practised. The examination answer that begins with the author's party and derives the novel from it can be replaced by an answer that begins with the work's rendering of its world and uses the biography, where it is used at all, as one production fact among others. The companion procedure for performing such a reading on a specific novel is set out in the series article on [a Marxist reading of a novel](/2020/08/01/marxist-reading-of-a-novel/), which takes the four questions of this article and applies them step by step to a single text.

The principle's reach extends beyond fiction. Historians in this tradition read memoirs against their writers in the same way, trusting the incidental detail over the argued self-justification. Critics of drama apply it to playwrights whose stated morals their stagecraft refutes. Even the tradition's enemies concede the phenomenon in practice whenever they admire a work whose politics they deplore, though they rarely grant the letter its priority in naming it. What Engels named, criticism had always half known. The naming turned a critical commonplace into a methodological rule with a corollary sharp enough to grade readings by: any interpretation that moves from authorial belief to textual meaning without passing through the work's showing has broken the rule, whatever vocabulary it wears.

There is a limit, and honesty requires stating it. The principle says showing is not determined by believing; it does not say believing is irrelevant. An author's sympathies shape selection, emphasis, and the distribution of sympathy among characters, and the seams where sympathy fights the evidence are among the most instructive places in Balzac himself. The triumph is a triumph, a victory in a contest, not the absence of one. Readings that celebrate the principle by ignoring the author's pressure on the text make the opposite error to the biographical one, and the four questions, which give intention its proper place under production, exist to prevent both.

## Lukács and the Defence of Realism

The realist position received its systematic statement from Georg Lukács, the Hungarian philosopher and critic whose work on the historical novel and on the contemporary novel's fate remains the tradition's most ambitious aesthetics. Lukács held that the great realist novel achieves a grasp of social totality: it shows the connection between individual fate and social forces, not by lecturing about those forces but by constructing typical characters whose private destinies are unintelligible apart from the historical movement carrying them. The country doctor, the ambitious provincial, the ruined landowner: in the realist masterpiece each is a person first and a concentration of the age's contradictions by the same act of characterisation. Totality, in this vocabulary, does not mean completeness of coverage. It means the rendering of a world as a connected process, in which the parts explain one another.

From that standard Lukács judged the literature of his own century and found it wanting. Modernist form, in his account, reproduces the fragmentation it depicts rather than grasping it. The stream of consciousness registers a consciousness for which the social world has become opaque, and registers it from inside, so that the opacity is ratified as the truth of experience instead of being explained as its historically produced condition. Where realism narrates, modernism, on his charge, describes and juxtaposes; where realism's details are significant because they mediate between person and process, modernism's details are static, allegorical, or fetishised. The defence of nineteenth-century realism was therefore not nostalgia for upholstery and omniscient narrators. It was a claim about cognition: that certain forms grasp a society as a whole in motion and others, however brilliant, render the experience of being lost inside it.

### Why did Lukács defend realism against modernism?

Because he held that realist form grasps social totality, showing individual fate as the product of historical forces, while modernist form reproduces the fragmentation of experience without explaining it. The defence was cognitive rather than nostalgic: a claim about which forms can know a society whole.

Lukács's position has strengths its opponents concede even while rejecting its verdicts. It explains why certain nineteenth-century novels still function as social knowledge, usable by historians who share none of the novelists' views, which is the triumph of realism operating at the scale of a whole literature. It gives criticism a standard external to fashion, so that a work's reputation can be argued with rather than merely reported. And it takes form seriously as cognition, refusing to treat technique as decoration on content, which places Lukács in agreement with his modernist opponents about everything except the conclusion. The disagreement, properly seen, is about which forms know, conducted by parties who agree that form is a way of knowing. That shared premise is what makes the dispute the tradition's central one rather than a quarrel between a theorist and some novelists.

The weaknesses are equally real, and this article's neutrality requires stating them in their own strength. The standard of totality is easier to invoke than to apply, and its applications can harden into a ranking machine in which the approved century always wins. The account of modernism flattens very different writers into a single symptom. And the defence of realism was conducted, in its author's later career, under institutional pressures that compromised its independence, a production fact about the criticism itself that the first question obliges us to register. The companion article on [socialist realism explained](/2020/09/15/socialist-realism-explained/) treats the doctrine that settled the realism dispute by decree in the Soviet Union, and the contrast is instructive: a state-enforced answer to the second question is what the dispute looks like when argument is replaced by administration, a fate both Lukács's subtleties and Brecht's experiments deserved better than.

## Macherey's Workshop: Reading Verne, Reading the Seam

Macherey's theory is best understood through its most famous application, his reading of Jules Verne. Verne's novels present themselves as celebrations of science, exploration, and the bourgeois hero whose ingenuity masters nature and geography. Macherey showed that the novels are structured by what they cannot integrate: the colonial world through which the heroes travel appears as scenery, labour, and obstacle, but its peoples are granted no interiority and no history of their own, because the ideology that organises the books, the ideology of imperial progress, cannot supply them one without dissolving the hero's mission into the violence that sustains it. The books keep approaching the seam. Descriptions linger on landscapes emptied of their inhabitants; episodes require local knowledge that the narrative must obtain without acknowledging its source; the hero's triumphs depend on a world represented as vacant. The vacancy is the symptom. It is not an oversight that a better Verne could have corrected, because the project of the books, the epic of technical mastery, required the vacancy as its condition.

The reading's power lies in its explanatory direction. A message hunt would report that Verne's novels are imperialist, a verdict true enough and wholly unilluminating, since it explains neither the novels' hold on readers nor their specific construction. Macherey's account explains the construction: why the descriptions swell where they do, why certain episodes repeat with variations, why the heroes must be bachelors and engineers rather than settlers and administrators, why the maps matter so much. The ideology is not the novels' opinion about empire. It is the shape of the silence around which the novels are built, and the shape can be drawn, checked against the texts, and argued with by any reader willing to do the same work. That arguability is the method's credential, and teachers who set the Verne reading beside a conventional appreciation of Verne give students the clearest available demonstration of the difference between a verdict and an analysis.

### What does the Verne reading prove about symptomatic method?

It proves the method can explain construction, not merely allege content. The silence around the colonial world's interiority accounts for the novels' descriptive swells, repeated episodes, and vacant landscapes, features a verdict about imperialism leaves unexplained. Explanation of form is the test the method sets itself.

Althusser's contribution to the symptomatic idea, which Macherey inherited, came from reading Marx's own development: the young Marx's humanist vocabulary, on Althusser's account, is organised by a problematic it cannot escape, and the mature work becomes visible at the points where the older text strains, where a question is posed that its framework cannot answer and the strain produces a new concept in the act of failing. The transfer of that reading practice from philosophy to fiction was Macherey's act, and it carried a consequence for the critic's authority that the tradition is still absorbing. If texts do not contain their meanings as a box contains its contents, then criticism is not the extraction of a meaning but the production of a knowledge, and the critic is a producer with responsibilities, not a priest with a key. The demotion of the critic parallels the demotion of the author, and the symmetry is principled: in a production theory, everyone works on material, and no one's product is sovereign.

The symptomatic method's later career extended far beyond the tradition, as the extensions section of this article noted, and the extension brought a predictable dilution. Symptom became a compliment critics paid their own ingenuity, and silence a resource that never ran out. The corrective is the discipline stated earlier and repeated here because the dilution continues: the expectation must be the text's own, the strain must be visible, and the production and form questions must be allowed to explain the seam before ideology is credited with it. A silence that survives those three tests is evidence. A silence that skips them is a Rorschach blot, and the tradition's name is not a warranty for it.

## Lukács in Depth: Reification and the Standpoint of the Novel

Lukács's literary criticism cannot be separated from the philosophy of his History and Class Consciousness, the 1923 work whose central concept, reification, names the process by which social relations between people take on the character of things and confront their makers as an alien, law-governed second nature. The literary consequence follows directly. A society experienced as reified presents itself to consciousness as a collection of objects and facts rather than as a process people are making, and art that merely reproduces the experience ratifies the reification. Realism, in Lukács's mature aesthetics, is the counter-practice: the form that breaks the thing-like surface by narrating the relations, showing the factory as a relation among people, the fortune as a history, the character as a node in a process. Modernism's failure, on the charge already examined, is its surrender to the reified surface, its registration of fragmentation as fate. The literary argument is the philosophy applied to novels, and its power, for readers who accept none of the philosophy's other claims, is that it explains a felt difference between kinds of fiction in terms of a theory of the society that produced both.

The Balzac and Zola comparison, from Lukács's essays of the 1930s, is the argument's classic exhibit. Balzac, the legitimist, narrates: his characters are caught in the process of a society transforming itself, and his details, however lovingly accumulated, are stations in destinies. Zola, the progressive, the ally of science and the republic, describes: his characters observe a world arranged before them, and his details, however exactly researched, compose an inventory of a finished order. The paradox is the founding principle operating as a critical instrument, since the reactionary out-narrates the progressive and the explanation lies not in their opinions but in their methods' relation to a moving world. Lukács's further claim, that Zola's method reflects a later phase in which the bourgeoisie had become an observer of its own order rather than its maker, ties the formal judgement to a historical one, and the tie is the method in miniature: form read as the deposit of a class's changing relation to its world.

### How does reification connect the philosophy to the novels?

Reification names experience of social relations as things. Realist narration, on Lukács's account, dissolves the thing-like surface by rendering relations as process, while descriptive and modernist methods register the surface as fate. The literary judgement is the social theory applied to narrative technique.

Honesty about Lukács requires the production note this article has already filed and now expands. His criticism was written across a career that included political defeat, exile, recantations extracted under pressure, and a final period of relative freedom in which the aesthetics received its fullest statement. The recantations are a first-question fact about the criticism, and they complicate any simple heroisation: the defender of realism's independence spent decades negotiating the terms on which his own writing could appear. The tradition's mature judgement holds both facts together, the achievement and the compromise, and treats the tension as itself instructive about the conditions under which criticism in this lineage has had to live. A method that teaches students to read a novel's seams should not flinch from its own classic's seams, and the best editions of Lukács, with the career documented, let the student practise on the critic the reading the critic practised on the novelists.

## Brecht in Depth: The Apparatus and the Gesture

Brecht's theoretical writings extend beyond the realism exchange into a general account of the theatrical apparatus, and the extension belongs here because it is the first question raised to self-consciousness by a practitioner. In his notes on the opera Mahagonny and in the essays of the 1930s, Brecht argued that the theatre's machinery, its buildings, its economics, its habits of consumption, determines what a play can do before any playwright writes: an apparatus that sells dreams to exhausted customers will digest any content into dream, so the revolutionary task is not to supply the old apparatus with new messages but to change the apparatus itself. The radio, in his famous proposal, should be converted from a distribution system into a communication system, letting the listener speak back. The argument generalises the production question into a programme: conditions are not only to be studied but to be seized, and the artist's relation to the means of production is itself an artistic fact. Epic theatre was the programme's laboratory, an attempt to build, inside the inherited buildings, a counter-apparatus of placards, projections, direct address, and interrupted empathy that would train spectators in judgement rather than absorption.

The gestus, Brecht's central technical concept, repays the second question's attention. A gestus is a socially legible gesture, the bodily attitude that reveals a relation: the master handing over money without looking, the servant receiving it with a practised invisibility, the judge adjusting his sleeve before sentencing. Epic acting builds scenes from gestus rather than from psychology, so that the social relation becomes visible as behaviour and can be quoted, repeated, and judged. The concept is the formal counterpart of typicality. Where the realist novel concentrates an age in a character's fate, the epic scene concentrates it in a gesture's choreography, and both are methods for making relations visible that ideology renders natural. A criticism that has learned from both can move between the novel's long arc and the scene's frozen instant with the same question in hand: what relation is being shown, and what would have to change for the showing to change?

### What is the apparatus argument, and why does it matter to criticism?

The apparatus argument holds that the machinery of production and consumption, the theatre's buildings, economics, and habits, digests content to its own purposes, so changing messages without changing machinery changes nothing. Criticism inherits the lesson as the first question's strongest form: conditions govern effects.

Brecht's fate under the fourth question completes his section as the realism dispute's other party completed his. The plays conquered the world's stages in the decades after his death, and the conquest domesticated as it spread: the alienation devices became a house style, the interruptions a brand, the didactic fire a period flavour admired in revival. Reception history thus delivered on Brecht the same irony it delivers on every innovator, the conversion of an instrument into an ornament, and Brecht, who understood the apparatus better than any critic of his century, had predicted the mechanism in advance. The plays' continuing power in performance, when a production recovers their judgement-training force, is the counter-evidence that keeps the case open, and the openness is the honest state of the question: the dispute with Lukács remains unsettled because the century kept supplying both sides with evidence.

## Goldmann in Depth: The Hidden God and the Tragic Vision

Goldmann's study of Pascal and Racine, published in the 1950s, remains genetic structuralism's monument, and its argument shows the homology method at its most disciplined. The tragic vision, as Goldmann reconstructs it, is a structure of refusal: the world is experienced as degraded and godless, yet the demand for absolute value persists, addressed to a God who is present only as absence, hidden, silent, and wagered upon. Pascal's wager is the vision's philosophical form; Racine's tragedies of the Jansenist orbit are its dramatic form; and the social bearer of the vision, on Goldmann's historical argument, is the noblesse de robe, the stratum of royal officers whose function absolutism was simultaneously using and abolishing, men caught between a crown that was destroying their order's basis and a rising world in which they had no place. The homology holds at the level of structure: a stratum that can neither accept the world nor leave it corresponds to a theology that can neither find God nor renounce him, and to a drama whose heroes refuse a world they cannot escape. Each level is established from its own evidence, the stratum from administrative and social history, the theology from the texts of Port-Royal, the drama from the plays' construction, and the correspondence is then exhibited rather than assumed.

The method's subsequent career illustrates its risk as faithfully as its monument illustrates its power. Homologies proliferated in the hands of imitators who skipped the independent establishment of the group and read the structure off the work they then explained by it, a circularity Goldmann's own protocol forbids. The tradition's verdict on genetic structuralism is therefore double, and the doubleness is instructive for the whole article: a method is its protocol, not its slogan, and the same name covers the monument and the parlour game depending on whether the evidence rules are kept. Students meeting the approach should meet the protocol first, the requirement of independent evidence for the group's situation, since without it the homology is a metaphor wearing a method's clothes.

### What keeps a homology from being circular?

Independent evidence. The group's situation must be established from historical records that owe nothing to the work, and the work's structure from the text, before the correspondence between them is exhibited. Where either side is inferred from the other, the homology explains nothing.

Goldmann's later work on the novel extended the homology to the form itself, arguing that the novel's structure, the story of a problematic hero seeking authentic values in a degraded world, is homologous with the structure of life under market relations, in which use values are submerged by exchange and the quest for quality becomes the biography of a misfit. The argument, whatever its final standing, is the second question at its most ambitious: a whole genre read as the formal deposit of an economic structure, with the deposit's mechanism specified as the mediation of a world vision. Between Goldmann's ambition and Watt's empiricism the student can locate the spectrum along which formal sociology has operated, and the location exercise is worth more than a verdict, since it teaches the questions by showing how differently they can be weighted by serious practitioners who share them.

## Brecht and the Case for Experiment

The opposing position was argued by Bertolt Brecht, the playwright whose theoretical writings of the 1930s, including the exchange of 1938 conducted in the exile journal Das Wort, answered the realist orthodoxy from inside the same movement. Brecht's premise was disarmingly simple: reality changes, and the means of representing it must therefore change too. A form perfected for rendering the stable, legible world of the nineteenth-century bourgeoisie cannot be decreed adequate for a century of monopoly, mass media, world war, and revolution, in which the connections between individual fate and social force have become harder, not easier, to see. To insist on the old form is, on this account, itself a kind of unreality: it renders a vanished world convincingly and the existing one not at all.

Brecht's practice embodied the argument. The alienation effect, the interruption of identification between audience and character, the placards and songs that break the dramatic illusion: these were not decorations but instruments for making the familiar strange enough to be examined. Where the realist novel invites the reader to inhabit a consciousness and share its fate, Brecht's theatre invites the spectator to judge a situation and consider its alternatives. The epic theatre's spectator leans back, compares, and decides, and that posture, Brecht held, is the one a changing society requires from its art. Formal experiment, in this account, is not the abandonment of cognition but its renewal under new conditions: the interruption of empathy is what allows the social mechanism to become visible as a mechanism rather than being suffered as a fate.

### What was the Brecht and Lukács debate about?

The debate concerned whether inherited realist form still grasps a changed reality. Lukács held that realism renders social totality and modernism merely reproduces fragmentation. Brecht held that reality changes and representation must change with it, making experiment and interrupted identification the appropriate instruments for new conditions.

Both parties were Marxists. The sentence bears repeating because the dispute is so often misfiled. Brecht was not defending art against politics, and Lukács was not defending politics against art. Each accused the other of a failure of realism, in the root sense of fidelity to a changing reality, and each grounded the accusation in the shared framework of historical materialism. The exchange was also asymmetric in its publication history: Lukács's side appeared in print in the journals of the exile community, while Brecht's sharpest replies circulated privately and were published only later, a production fact that shaped the dispute's reception for decades. Generations of students met the debate as a verdict already delivered, because only one side had been audible. The recovery of Brecht's texts altered the debate's balance without settling it, and the honest classroom presents the exchange as what it was: unfinished business between two of the tradition's strongest minds.

The dispute's endurance comes from its structure. It is not really about two writers, or about the 1930s. It recurs wherever a movement's aesthetics are codified: in arguments about the novel after modernism, about documentary and fiction film, about the political theatre's means in every generation since. Each recurrence replays the same underlying question, which is the second of this article's four questions in its most contested form. Does a form's social meaning inhere in its structure, so that inherited structures carry an inherited world with them, or is a form an instrument whose meaning depends on the use a changed reality demands? Lukács leans to the first answer, Brecht to the second, and the tradition has never finally adjudicated between them because both answers capture something the evidence supports.

Presenting the dispute fairly requires resisting two temptations. The first is to make Brecht the hero of innovation and Lukács the policeman of tradition, a casting that flatters later taste and misrepresents both: Lukács's realism was a demanding cognitive programme, not a preference for the familiar, and Brecht's experiments were disciplined by didactic purposes as strict as any orthodoxy. The second temptation is to dissolve the disagreement into a misunderstanding, as if the two merely emphasised different aspects of one position. They did not. On the specific question of whether the modernist repertoire could render social totality, they contradicted each other, and the contradiction is the valuable thing. A tradition that contains its own strongest refutation is healthier than one that must import its critics, and the realism debate is the clearest case in this tradition of the internal standard at work.

The wider framework within which both positions sit, the Marxist account of culture as a whole rather than of literature alone, is treated in the series article on the [Marxist cultural theory guide](/2020/06/01/marxist-cultural-theory-guide/), to which readers who want the general theory behind these literary disputes should proceed after this article.

## Symptomatic Reading and the Structural Turn

The structural position, developed by Macherey in his 1966 theory of literary production, redirected the third question into a complete method. Macherey's decisive move was to treat the work as a production rather than a creation or an expression. A writer works on ideology as a craftsman works on material, and the finished text bears the marks of that labour: it does not express an ideology coherently, because the material resists, and the resistance shows as gaps, silences, displacements, and contradictions internal to the work. The critic's task is not to extract the work's message, still less to grade it, but to explain the work's structure by exhibiting the relation between what it says and what its material made unsayable. The book's title names the programme: a theory of production, in which the literary work is made, not inspired, and made from ideology the way a table is made from wood, with the grain showing.

This position radicalised the symptomatic method inherited from Louis Althusser, who had described reading Marx himself symptomatically, attending to the points where a text's visible argument strains against the problematic that organises it. Macherey applied the procedure to fiction and drew the consequence for criticism's self-image: the critic does not complete the work or judge it from above, but produces knowledge about it, a second-order production with its own rigour. The reading that results is often startling. A children's classic is shown to be structured by the colonial geography it never mentions. A realist novel's confident narration is shown to depend on exclusions its own descriptions keep approaching and refusing. The startle is the evidence that the method finds things; the discipline described earlier in this article, requiring the seam and the strain, is what keeps the findings honest.

### How does symptomatic reading differ from hunting for a message?

A message hunt assumes the work contains a meaning its author placed there, to be extracted and paraphrased. Symptomatic reading assumes the work's meaning includes its failures of coherence, so the critic explains gaps and contradictions as products of the ideology being worked on, rather than paraphrasing a content.

The structural turn also changed the status of the author within the method. Under the triumph of realism, the author's beliefs were already demoted from key to datum. Under Macherey, the author becomes a worker among conditions: skilled, situated, and no more sovereign over the product's meaning than any producer is sovereign over the social meaning of a commodity. Students sometimes receive this as hostility to writers. It is better understood as the application to literature of the tradition's general claim about production, and it has the paradoxical effect of making criticism more, not less, attentive to craft, since the seams and strains are precisely where craft met its material's resistance.

## Genetic Structuralism and the Homology Claim

The sociological position, in its most rigorous form, is Lucien Goldmann's genetic structuralism. Goldmann posited homologies between the mental structures of a social group and the structures of a work: not a resemblance between the work's content and the group's opinions, but a correspondence of structure between the world vision that a class's situation makes available and the fictional universe a great work constructs. His celebrated study of Pascal and Racine argued that the tragic structure of their work, its pattern of refusal and hiddenness, corresponded to the situation of a specific stratum of the French nobility under absolutism, caught between a crown it served and a world that was abolishing its function. The correspondence is structural: the same pattern of a God who is present as absence organises the theology, the drama, and the stratum's political predicament.

### How does genetic structuralism explain a work?

Genetic structuralism, Goldmann's method, explains a work by the homology between its structure and the mental structures of the social group whose world vision it expresses. Genesis supplies the group's historical situation; structure supplies the correspondence, which holds between patterns rather than between opinions and contents.

The method's elegance is its danger. A homology, once the critic is free to choose the level of abstraction, can be found between almost any structure and almost any group, and Goldmann's critics pressed exactly there: the correspondence risks being an artefact of the description's flexibility. Goldmann's defence was to demand that the homology be demonstrated at multiple levels at once, in the work's smallest devices and its largest architecture, and that the group's situation be established independently from historical evidence rather than inferred from the work. Under those constraints the method produced some of the tradition's most admired studies. Without them it degenerates into pattern-matching. The lesson generalises across this article's approaches: each position in the tradition carries a characteristic failure mode, and knowing the failure mode is part of knowing the position. For symptomatic reading the failure mode is the unconstrained silence; for genetic structuralism it is the unconstrained homology; for the realist position it is the ranking machine; for the Brechtian position it is the assumption that novelty of means guarantees truth of rendering.

## Bakhtin and the Contested Inheritance

A body of work on the novel as the form in which social languages contend has been enormously influential in literary studies, and its principal figure, Mikhail Bakhtin, poses the tradition a genuine problem of classification. The concepts are famous: dialogism, the claim that the novel orchestrates a plurality of social voices without reducing them to one; heteroglossia, the stratification of language into the dialects of class, profession, generation, and occasion; carnival, the licensed inversion in which hierarchy is suspended and the world turned upside down. These concepts entered Marxist criticism and stayed, because they answer the second question with unmatched precision: the novel's form, on Bakhtin's account, encodes a society as a contest of languages, and its meanings live in the friction between voices rather than in any single voice's doctrine.

Whether Bakhtin is a Marxist critic is, however, genuinely disputed, and the dispute has two independent grounds. The first is doctrinal: Bakhtin's work lacks the framework's characteristic commitments in any explicit form, draws on sources the tradition does not claim, and has been appropriated by schools with no materialist commitments whatever. The second is textual and stranger: several of the books published under the names of Bakhtin's associates, including works on Marxism and the philosophy of language and on the formal method in literary scholarship, have been attributed in whole or in part to Bakhtin himself, and the attribution remains contested among scholars. A criticism that cannot be certain who wrote its candidate texts, or whether their author held its commitments, has an unusual classification problem, and this article states the dispute as unresolved, as the brief for the article requires and as the evidence warrants.

### Can Bakhtin be claimed for the tradition?

The honest answer is that the classification is disputed on two grounds: his work's explicit commitments are not the tradition's, and the authorship of several key texts published under his associates' names is contested. His concepts are used throughout Marxist criticism whatever the verdict on the man.

The practical consequence for students is a citation discipline. Bakhtin's concepts may be used, with attribution to the texts that present them, and the disputed authorship flagged where the argument depends on it. What may not be done is the quiet annexation practised by survey courses, in which dialogism appears as a settled Marxist doctrine with a settled author. The tradition's own standards, the ones this article has applied to Balzac and to the realism dispute, require the uncertainty to be carried in the open. A method that reads other texts for their seams should not conceal its own.

## Working the Circuit: A Generic Illustration

Abstract method persuades few readers; the circuit is better shown in motion. Take a generic case, deliberately stripped of names so that no contemporary work is enlisted and no real text is traduced: a long novel, issued serially in a respectable magazine across the middle of the nineteenth century, whose plot follows a clerk's rise, a fortune's making in colonial trade, a marriage that joins the fortune to an old family, and a fall when the trade collapses. Generations of school readers met it in an abridged edition as a tale of ambition punished. The four questions reopen every part of that settled summary.

Production first. The magazine's proprietors served a readership of the professional classes, and the serial's terms are recoverable: the instalment length that shaped its chapters, the editorial interventions visible in the correspondence, the illustrations commissioned to fix the characters' faces for the first readers, and the volume edition's price, which placed the finished book in the library market rather than the railway stall. The colonial trade at the plot's centre was chosen under constraints the correspondence reveals: the magazine would not serialise a narrative that examined the trade's sources too closely in the years when its advertisers included the trade's firms. That single production fact reorders the reading. The novel's celebrated vagueness about where the fortune comes from is not, or not only, an ideological symptom; it is partly an editorial condition, priced into the contract before the symptom could form.

Form next. The novel narrates through a confidant figure, a friend of the clerk who tells the story in retrospect with a warmth that keeps qualifying itself. The device presupposes a reader who trusts the clubman's judgement and enjoys the qualifications as irony. Structurally, the marriage occupies the exact centre, and the fall is compressed into the final sixth, a distribution that makes the rise feel like the story and the ruin like its moral. The ending restores the old family, chastened, to a reduced estate, and marries the clerk's daughter within the class the clerk spent his life entering. The distribution of fates is legible as a theodicy: ambition is punished, endurance is preserved, and the social order survives its own speculation by digesting the speculator's child.

Ideology third, its turn now governed by the answers already banked. The fortune's source is the famous silence, but the seam taxonomy assigns it jointly: the unmentionable, given the editorial condition, and the unspeakable, since the narrative's own project, following money through three generations, keeps approaching the counting house and the dock and turning, each time with a joke or a landscape, aside. The jokes are the strain made audible. What the text takes for granted is richer than its silence: that a daughter's marriage is the natural instrument for repairing a fortune, that servants observe everything and count for nothing in the moral arithmetic, that the old family's code, however empty its purse, remains the standard by which the clerk's money is judged vulgar. Those assumptions are never argued. They are the furniture, and the plot's satisfactions depend on the reader's finding the furniture natural.

Reception last, and the settled summary dissolves. The first reviewers were divided, with the radical press praising the exposure of speculation and the respectable quarterlies deploring the sympathy extended to the clerk. The school abridgement, which cut the colonial chapters to a preface and the fall to a chapter, manufactured the ambition-punished tale the twentieth century inherited, and the examination questions set on it for decades asked candidates to discuss the hero's flaw, a question the unabridged text, with its structural account of the trade's collapse, does not invite. A stage adaptation in the following century restored the counting house scenes the magazine had softened, and the restoration read, to its first audiences, as a revelation about the novel rather than about the adaptation. Each stage of the career is documented, and the documentation, not ingenuity, is what allows the reading to claim that the school tale and the serial are two different social objects sharing a text.

### What does the worked example show that definitions cannot?

It shows the questions correcting one another: production evidence reassigning a famous silence, form analysis exposing a theodicy the preface disowns, reception history dissolving a settled summary into successive different works. The circuit's value is the corrections, which no single question generates alone.

The example also shows the method's economy. Nothing in the reading required a theory of the author's soul, a verdict on the novel's politics, or a census of its class conflicts, though class saturates every finding. The findings are about how the work was made, built, furnished, and remade, and each is stated in a form a rival can test against correspondence, editions, and reviews. That testability is the article's recurring standard, and the worked example is where the standard stops being a promise and becomes a procedure a student can imitate on the next unfamiliar text.

## The Critic's Own Conditions

A method that asks production questions of every text will eventually be asked them about itself, and the tradition's honest practitioners welcome the turn. Marxist criticism has been produced in universities, in exile, in party schools, in prison, and in the little magazines of capitals and provinces, and its emphases track those conditions in ways its historians can document. The academic form of the method, dominant in the Anglophone world since the 1970s, asks the four questions with archival rigour and publishes the answers in journals its subjects never read; the exile form of the 1930s wrote for a movement readership under the pressure of events; the prison form, of which Gramsci's notebooks on literature and national culture are the supreme case, read the popular novel and the serial as evidence about a people's common sense because the prisoner's Italy could be studied through its reading. None of these conditions invalidates its product. Each explains a selection: which texts mattered, which questions led, which audiences were imagined.

Gramsci's literary writings deserve their sentence here because they extend the fourth question into a programme. His interest in the serial novel, in the popular success of foreign adventure fiction in Italy, and in the absence of a national-popular literature was an interest in what a people's reading reveals about its formation and what a different literature would have to do to form it differently. The critic appears in that programme not as a judge above the circuit but as a worker inside it, one of the agents by whom reception is made. Cultural materialism inherited the stance most directly, with its insistence that the critic's present use of a text is part of the text's career and should be owned rather than disguised.

### Does the method apply to its own practitioners?

Yes, and the application is a consistency test rather than a refutation. The criticism's emphases vary with its conditions of production, in exile, prison, party schools, and universities, and its historians document the variation. A method embarrassed by its own first question would forfeit the right to ask it of novels.

The self-application has a practical consequence for readers of this article. The article itself is a product: written for a study series, organised around a findable table, routed to companion tools, and composed under rules about evidence and neutrality that its production conditions supplied. Its four questions can be asked of it, and its silences found, by any reader who has learned the method well enough to turn it on the teacher. The tradition would count that outcome a success rather than an embarrassment, since a method's proof is its portability, and the reader who audits the article with its own table has understood the article better than the reader who merely agrees with it.

## Teaching the Method

The four questions teach well because they convert a doctrine into a sequence of tasks, and the conversion is worth describing for the teachers among this article's readers. A seminar that begins with the definition and the letter can put a short unfamiliar text in students' hands within the hour and set the questions loose in order, with the evidence rules stated as the game's constitution: production claims need records, form claims need devices, silence claims need seams, reception claims need documents. The first attempts will be uneven, and the unevenness is instructive, because each failure localises itself under one question and can be corrected there without condemning the whole reading. The student whose silence lacks a seam has a third-question problem; the student whose effect lacks a document has a fourth-question problem; the taxonomy of errors is the taxonomy of the method, which is what makes the method teachable at all.

Assessment rewards the same structure. The examination answer that proceeds question by question, carrying its evidence rules with it, outperforms the answer that performs an attitude toward the text, and examiners' reports across the decades in which this material has been set return to the same discriminations: candidates who separate the author's beliefs from the work's showing, who can state both sides of the realism dispute in their own terms, who distinguish cultural materialism from new historicism without conflation, and who know what would count against their own reading. The defeater habit, required by this run of articles for every verdict, is an examination virtue before it is a scholarly one, since it demonstrates that the candidate's judgement is a judgement, held against evidence, rather than a loyalty performed for marks.

### Which text should a beginner open first?

Begin with Engels’s letter of 1888, a brief text that contains the principle whole, then read one classic application at length before any survey of the schools. Practise the four questions on a familiar novel in order, with the evidence rules kept, and let the disputes come after the method is in the hands.

The reading route matters because the tradition's secondary literature is vast and uneven, and the surveys, taken first, deliver the slogan version in academic dress. The letter first gives the student the charter in the founders' prose. One substantial application, a Lukács chapter, a Macherey reading, a Williams essay, shows the method at working length, with its evidence habits visible. The disputes then become intelligible as disputes, because the student knows what the disputants share. The profiles of individual critics in this series, and the exam guide that closes the cluster, assume exactly that order, and the study notes linked earlier in this article are arranged to support it, with the letter's principle at the head and the questions in their sequence beneath.

## Adorno's Counterweight and the Aesthetic Theory Problem

No full account of the tradition's aesthetics can omit the counterweight that Theodor W. Adorno supplied, even though his position fits none of the schools surveyed so far and was held, deliberately, against most of them. Adorno argued that art's social truth lies in its autonomy: the work that refuses to be useful, that declines to communicate a message or serve a movement, preserves in its formal difficulty a negation of the administered world that committed art, however correct its commitments, surrenders when it makes itself legible and serviceable. The argument reverses the usual valences. For Adorno, the overtly political work risks becoming propaganda and thereby affirming the order of things it protests, while the hermetic work, the lyric that withdraws, the music that refuses melody's comforts, keeps faith with suffering by refusing to reconcile it in form. His exchanges and non-exchanges with the realist orthodoxy were conducted at a distance and partly through intermediaries, and his judgement on Brecht's didactic theatre was as severe as his judgement on the doctrine that opposed Brecht, a symmetry that placed him outside the dispute this article has treated as central and made him the tradition's internal exile on aesthetic questions.

Adorno's presence in the account matters for the second question. His claim is a form claim: that the social meaning of a work in a administered society is carried by its refusal of the forms communication takes there, so that difficulty itself becomes evidence and accessibility itself becomes suspect. A student does not have to accept the claim to be improved by meeting it, because it prevents the realism debate from hardening into a two-party system. Between Lukács's totality and Brecht's interruption stands Adorno's negation, and the three positions, mapped together, show the tradition arguing about form with a seriousness that the slogan version of Marxist criticism, hunting messages in plots, never suggests. The Frankfurt inheritance also complicates the reception question, since Adorno's account of the culture industry treated mass reception as manufactured consent, a claim that the later history of reading, with its evidence of resistant and appropriating readers, has qualified without wholly refuting. The qualification is itself a fourth-question finding, and the interplay, claim and archival correction, is the method working as this article has described it throughout.

### How does Adorno's position disturb the realism dispute?

By denying the premise both parties shared, that art serves truth by engaging its society's content. Adorno locates truth in autonomous form's refusal of service, making difficulty the evidence and commitment the risk. The dispute becomes three-sided, and its map of possible positions enlarges accordingly.

## Trotsky and the Question of Party Direction

A further position, historically consequential and intellectually distinct, is Leon Trotsky's in Literature and Revolution, written in the early 1920s when the question of the party's authority over art was still open in the Soviet Union. Trotsky argued that art must be judged by its own laws, that the party should not command artistic creation, and that the notion of a specifically proletarian culture, to be manufactured in the transition period, misconceived both culture's timescales and the transition's tasks. The book defended fellow travellers, writers not of the movement whose work the movement should nevertheless protect and learn from, and it treated the formalists with a respect that the coming orthodoxy would withdraw. The position matters to this article because it is the triumph of realism translated into cultural policy: the refusal to grade art by the artist's allegiance, stated by a revolutionary leader at the moment when grading by allegiance was becoming available as an instrument of rule. The subsequent settlement by decree, treated in the socialist realism article already linked, is the counterfactual made history, the record of what the refusal was refusing.

The episode also supplies the tradition's clearest case of the first question applied to criticism itself. Trotsky wrote as a leader with a state to build and a factional struggle to win, and his aesthetic liberalism was entangled with both; his opponents wrote from institutions that were accumulating the power to settle arguments administratively. The texts' afterlives complete the lesson. Literature and Revolution was suppressed in the country whose revolution it named, circulated in exile editions, and recovered by later generations as evidence that the settlement had been a choice rather than a necessity. Reception history, pursued honestly, keeps such alternatives visible, which is one of the fourth question's quieter services to the tradition's self-knowledge.

## The Formalist Neighbour

Marxist criticism developed in argument with Russian formalism, and the argument's residue is worth a section because the comparison article in this series treats the two methods side by side and the present article should state what the quarrel was about. The formalists proposed to study literariness itself: the devices by which language is made strange, the plot's retardations and defamiliarisations, the work as a sum of techniques whose evolution could be charted without reference to biography or society. The Marxist objection, in its serious form, was not that devices do not exist but that a history of devices cannot explain its own sequence: why this device exhausts itself, why that one arrives, why the strange becomes familiar on a schedule that tracks the society outside the text. The formalist rejoinder, also serious, held that the social explanation, invoked at that altitude, explains everything in general and nothing in particular, and that the poem on the desk still waits for someone to say how it works.

The quarrel moderated, as quarrels between serious methods do, into borrowings. The later formalists themselves admitted social series into their model of literary evolution; the Marxist tradition absorbed the device as a unit of formal analysis and has used defamiliarisation ever since, usually without remembering to be grateful. Bakhtin's circle conducted the most sustained negotiation between the two, criticising the formal method for severing the utterance from its social situation while learning from its precision about construction. The negotiation is a model of how the tradition at its best treats a rival: as a partial method whose findings need a wider frame, not as an error to be denounced. Students who stage the quarrel fairly, in an examination or a seminar, demonstrate the comparative habit that the whole cluster of articles in this series is designed to teach.

### What did the formalist quarrel leave to Marxist criticism?

A unit of analysis and a standing warning. The unit is the device, the identifiable technique whose presuppositions the second question reads. The warning is that social explanation at high altitude explains nothing in particular, so every claim must descend to the device, the document, or the seam.

## Feminist and Postcolonial Extensions

The four questions proved portable beyond the tradition that formulated them, and two extensions deserve record as receptions of the method itself. Feminist criticism took the third question and turned it on the canon the Marxists had inherited, asking what the great realists took for granted about women's labour, women's speech, and the marriage market their plots administered, and it took the fourth question to the recovery of women writers whom the canon-forming institutions had let fall out of print. The extension was not always friendly: the tradition was charged, with evidence, with its own silences about domestic labour and its own furniture of assumptions, and the charge was a symptomatic reading of Marxist criticism by its own method, which the method's logic obliged it to absorb. The absorption changed the practice permanently, so that a contemporary application of the four questions that did not interrogate a text's gendered furniture would be judged incomplete by the tradition's own standards.

Postcolonial criticism performed the parallel operation on the literature of empire. The colonial adventure's silences, the metropolitan novel's offstage fortunes, the geography of the classic text in which the colony supplies wealth and never interiority: these became the most widely taught symptomatic readings of the late twentieth century, practised by critics whose relation to Marxism ranged from affiliation to declared independence. The method's portability raises the classification question in a new form, since a symptomatic reading of empire by a critic who rejects the framework entire is either evidence that the method exceeds the doctrine, which the tradition can welcome, or evidence that the method was never exclusively its property, which the prehistory section of this article already conceded. Either way, the extensions belong in the account, because the fourth question, applied to the method itself, records them as its most consequential receptions.

## Williams in Depth: Structures of Feeling and the Long Revolution

Raymond Williams occupies a singular place in this tradition because he rebuilt its literary criticism from the side of experience. Trained in the Cambridge of close reading and formed by adult education teaching in the years after the Second World War, Williams spent a career arguing that the tradition's inherited vocabulary, base and superstructure above all, misdescribed the process it named when it was applied mechanically to culture. Culture, in his counter-formula, is ordinary: a whole way of life, not a garnish on the real business of production, and the arts are among the ways a society thinks and feels its life rather than reflections struck off after the thinking is done elsewhere. The correction was delivered from inside the tradition, with its commitments intact, and it changed what the four questions could find, especially the third and fourth.

Structures of feeling is the instrument for which Williams is most cited, and it deserves precise statement. At any period, alongside the articulated doctrines and institutions that historians can document, there exists a lived sense of the period's quality, present in manners, tones, styles, and choices before it is present in propositions: the feeling of a world, as it is being lived, at the edge of semantic availability. Literature registers that stratum earlier and more finely than any other record, because literature works in the very medium, language as lived, in which the feeling subsists. The critic who reads for structures of feeling is therefore reading the present tense of a past society, the moment at which experience was still in solution, before the precipitate of doctrine formed. The concept gives the third question a temporal refinement: ideology is not only what a text assumes but when, in the long process by which assumptions form, the text is standing.

The triad of dominant, residual, and emergent refines the same insight for cultural history. No period's culture is a single system. The dominant formation organises the central meanings and values; the residual, formed in an earlier order, persists as practice, loyalty, and institution, and may be merely nostalgic or genuinely alternative; the emergent announces meanings and practices that the dominant cannot yet recognise as its successors and often cannot recognise at all. A novel of any richness contains all three in negotiation, and a reading that assigns the whole text to one formation has flattened the evidence. Williams's own readings, of the country house poem, of the industrial novel, of the drama of his own century, proceed by exactly that discrimination, and they model the patience the method requires when it is practised at full strength rather than recited.

### What does a structure of feeling let a critic see that ideology misses?

It lets the critic see experience before it hardens into doctrine: the lived quality of a period as registered in tone, style, and choice, at the edge of what the period could say about itself. Ideology names the settled assumptions; the structure of feeling names their formation in progress.

Williams's account of the industrial novel illustrates the instruments working together. The novels of the 1840s that took the new industrial order as their subject were written, with few exceptions, from outside the world they depicted, by observers whose sympathy was real and whose knowledge was a visitor's. Williams read their structures of feeling with unmatched delicacy: the fear beneath the sympathy, the conversion of a social relation into a personal misunderstanding so that the ending can reconcile persons where it cannot reconcile classes, the good mill owner and the misled worker arranged to make a structural conflict narratable as a failure of communication. The readings neither condemn nor excuse the novels. They show the ideology at work as a set of narrative solutions to a problem the novelists' position made insoluble, which is symptomatic reading conducted with a historian's tact and a teacher's patience, and they remain the standard against which later applications of the third question are measured.

His work on drama extended the same attention to form's history. The argument that each period's dramatic structure embodies its structure of feeling, that the private room and the public square are not settings but propositions about where life happens, gave theatre studies a materialist poetics that Brecht's practice had anticipated from the stage side. Williams and Brecht, the theorist of the study and the practitioner of the rehearsal room, converge on the second question from opposite directions, and the convergence is one of the tradition's reassuring moments: the form that a society finds natural is, in both accounts, the deposit of its way of experiencing itself, legible to whoever learns to read deposits.

## Eagleton and the Synthetic Textbook

Terry Eagleton's role in the tradition's modern form is double, and both halves belong in the record. As a theorist, his early work attempted the most systematic statement of a Marxist literary science in English, proposing categories for the relations among a text, its author, its ideology, and its moment that were meant to give the method the rigour of a discipline. As a teacher, his introduction to literary theory carried the whole field, Marxist criticism included, to a student audience of a size no previous work in the tradition had reached, and its wit performed a reception feat in its own right: it made the method's questions feel like common property rather than party doctrine. The profile article in this series treats his career at length; here the point is structural. A tradition survives in classrooms through its textbooks as much as through its classics, and the fourth question, turned on the tradition itself, records the textbook as a canon-forming institution of the first order.

Eagleton's synthetic work also illustrates a tension the method never finally resolves, between criticism as science and criticism as judgement. The scientific ambition wants categories, mechanisms, and findings that rivals can verify; the judicial inheritance wants verdicts on works, rankings, and the defence of the best against the merely fluent. Eagleton's career moves between the poles, and his later writing defends the judicial function with increasing openness, arguing in substance that a criticism too fastidious to say what is good has surrendered its public reason for existing. The four questions, as this article has stated them, lean scientific: they generate findings. But the tradition's readers have always wanted the findings to add up to judgements, and the verdict with its defeater, required throughout this run of articles, is the compromise form: a judgement, stated, with the evidence that would reverse it named in the same breath.

### Why does the tradition need both findings and verdicts?

Findings without verdicts surrender criticism's public function, the guidance of readers among works; verdicts without findings surrender its discipline, becoming taste with a vocabulary. The defeater form joins them: a verdict is issued, and the conditions of its own reversal are published with it, keeping the judgement corrigible.

The textbook's reception also demonstrates a hazard the tradition should record against itself. A method reduced to a chapter in a survey, between chapters on rival methods, risks being learned as a posture: the student acquires the accent of the four questions without the evidence rules that make them a method, and produces the slogan version with better footnotes. Teachers in the tradition have complained of exactly this outcome for decades, and the complaint is a fourth-question finding about the tradition's own most successful book. The remedy proposed throughout this article is unfashionable and simple: the charter text first, one long application second, the survey last, so that the method is met as a practice before it is memorised as a position.

## Drama and Poetry Under the Four Questions

The method's exhibits have been novels, because the tradition's classics are novel-centred, but the four questions are genre-blind and their application to drama and poetry corrects the novel-centrism that students absorb from the surveys. Drama is the most obviously social of the forms: it exists only in performance, before an assembled public, under licensing, in buildings whose architecture sorts the audience by price into boxes, stalls, and gallery, so that the production and reception questions are not research projects but the art's daily conditions. The Renaissance public theatre, with its mixed audience standing and seated in one room, made a social fact of every performance that the later proscenium house, with its darkened auditorium and separated classes of seat, unmade. A Marxist reading of a play therefore begins where a reading of a novel cannot: with the room, the prices, the licence, and the crowd, and the text's meanings are pursued as events among those conditions rather than as properties of a script. Cultural materialism's celebrated readings of Renaissance drama proceed exactly so, asking what the play did in its theatre and what it does in ours, and treating the distance between those doings as the criticism's subject.

Poetry poses the opposite challenge: the lyric is the form that seems most private, a voice overheard, and the tradition's handling of it tests whether the method can respect an art of inwardness without dissolving it. The handling proceeds by the same questions in a lower key. Production: the little magazine, the patron, the anthology, the schoolroom where most poems are in fact met. Form: the presuppositions of the lyric speaker, the fiction that a voice can be at once singular and universal, which is itself a social proposition about the self. Ideology: the furniture of the pastoral, the country house, the war poem, the love lyric, each genre carrying its settled assumptions about land, nation, and desire. Reception: the anthology wars, the set poem, the quoted line that detaches itself and lives in speeches its poet never imagined. Adorno's defence of the lyric, the argument that the poem's withdrawal from society is its social truth, stands as the tradition's own warning against flattening this application, and the warning is incorporated rather than rebutted: the lyric's privacy is a formal fact with social meaning, which is the second question's finding, not an obstacle to it.

### How do the four questions change when the text is a play?

The conditions become visible. Performance assembles a priced and licensed public in a sorted room, so production and reception are the art's daily facts rather than archival reconstructions, and the script's meanings are chased as happenings inside those conditions.

The country house poem, a small genre with an outsized critical literature, can serve as the poetry case in miniature. The poems that celebrate a great house and its estate in the seventeenth century perform, in Williams's classic reading, a delicate operation: the estate's order is praised as natural bounty, the labour that produces the bounty is conjured away, and the house's hospitality figures a social order in which exploitation has been edited out of the picture by the picture's own conventions. The reading is symptomatic before the term, locating the silence, the absent labourers, at the exact point where the genre's project, the praise of abundance, required their absence. Later scholarship has complicated the picture with estate records and a fuller sense of the genre's range, which is the method's normal fate and no scandal: the reading made claims the archive could test, and the archive, in testing, refined them. The episode belongs in teaching because it shows the whole circuit, claim, evidence, correction, on a canvas small enough to see at once.

## Gramsci and the National-Popular

Gramsci's literary writings, composed in prison and published posthumously, extend the method toward the question of what a people's literature is and does. His starting point was an empirical embarrassment: the Italian reading public consumed foreign serial fiction in vast quantities while Italian writers produced little that the public wanted, a reception fact that Italian criticism preferred to deplore rather than explain. Gramsci explained it by the divorce between Italy's intellectuals and its people: a literature that does not arise from the people's own experience and sentiments cannot become national-popular, cannot perform the educative and formative work by which a class's world view becomes a culture. The serial novel, the feuilleton, the adventure story despised by the academies were therefore documents of the first importance, evidence of the common sense of the nation as it actually existed, and the critic who scorned them scorned his evidence.

The concept of common sense that Gramsci built on this foundation is the third question's popular counterpart. Common sense is the folklore of philosophy, the sedimented assumptions a society lives by, contradictory, fragmentary, and powerful precisely because unexamined; literature is one of its principal carriers and one of the few instruments by which it can be examined in the act of transmission. The critic's task, in the Gramscian version, is neither to celebrate the sediment nor to scold it but to inventory it, to distinguish its healthy nucleus of practical insight from its encrustations, and to understand the literary forms by which each is carried. The inventory is symptomatic reading aimed at a nation's whole reading diet, and its scale explains why Gramsci's notes range from Dante's structure to the detective story's popularity without any sense of incongruity: the questions are the same at every altitude.

### What does the national-popular add to the four questions?

A formative ambition. Gramsci asks not only what a people's reading shows but what a literature arising from the people's experience would have to become to educate a new common sense, making the critic a worker inside the circuit of reception rather than its observer.

Gramsci also supplies the tradition's most searching treatment of the critic's own position, since his notes on intellectuals argue that every social group creates its own intellectuals organically, and that the literary critic is one such functionary whether he knows it or not. The claim dissolves the fantasy of the unattached judge. The critic serves a formation's self-understanding by the questions he asks and the texts he canonises, and the honest response is not neutrality, which the framework holds to be unavailable, but consciousness: to know whose common sense one's criticism is inventorying and to what end. This article's neutrality rules, steelmanning before verdict and the defeater attached to every judgement, are a procedural answer to the Gramscian challenge rather than a denial of it: partisanship is disciplined by evidence rules precisely because it cannot be abolished by declaration.

## National Strands: One Method, Several Accents

The tradition speaks with national accents, and the differences are production facts about criticism itself. The German strand, formed in exile and in the universities of two postwar states, kept the closest ties to philosophy: its questions were the system's questions, its classics the aesthetic treatises and the Lukácsian essay, its characteristic product the theory of the novel or of art's autonomy argued at the level of the concept. The French strand, formed around the philosophical schools and the great publishing houses of Paris, produced the structural turn: Althusser's seminar, Macherey's production theory, and a criticism whose characteristic product was the reading as theoretical event, dense with a vocabulary that travelled badly and influenced everything. The Russian strand, oldest in its civic criticism and most tragic in its fate, contributed the sociological school's early rigour and the formalist interlocutor, then was administratively frozen at the moment of its greatest fertility, so that its classics reached the rest of the world late and out of order. The British strand, formed in adult education, the provincial university, and the little magazine, produced the empirical and pedagogical accent: Williams's patience, the history of reading, the textbook as an instrument of democratisation. The American strand arrived through the universities' expansion and the theory decades, blending the French vocabulary with the German system and domesticating both into a professional method whose characteristic product is the article on the early modern text, which is new historicism's home ground.

The accents matter to the student because the same term does different work in different strands. Realism in the German strand is a cognitive category with a Hegelian pedigree; in the British strand it shades toward the documentary and the working-class novel; in the French strand of the structural years it was nearly a term of abuse, the name of the illusion that language copies a world. Ideology in the French strand is a theoretical concept with a precise address in the theory of interpellation; in the British it remains closer to lived assumption, Williams's sense rather than Althusser's. A reading of the secondary literature that ignores the accents will manufacture contradictions the tradition does not contain and miss agreements it does. The four questions travel across the strands intact, which is further evidence for the verdict this article has defended: the circuit is the shared inheritance, the accents are its local productions.

### Why do the same terms shift meaning across national strands?

Because each strand produced the vocabulary under different institutional conditions, in exile philosophy, Parisian seminars, British adult education, or American departments, and the conditions bent the terms toward local uses. The questions travelled intact; the terminology bears the marks of its journeys.

The translation history of the tradition's own texts enacts the point. Lukács's major aesthetics reached English decades after their composition, in selections whose order was set by publishers' judgements about a market; Brecht's theoretical writings arrived piecemeal, the sharpest interventions last, which is why the realism debate was misread for a generation as a verdict; Macherey's book waited years for its English edition and arrived into a critical scene already changed by its rumoured contents. A criticism that teaches the fourth question cannot exempt its own classics from it: the tradition English-speaking students inherit is a translated, selected, and sequenced tradition, and the sequencing has arguments embedded in it. The recovery projects of later scholarship, the complete editions and the restored contexts, are fourth-question work performed on the tradition itself, and they have repeatedly changed what the tradition is taken to be.

## The Condition-of-England Novel as a Test Bench

The cluster of novels that took the industrial transformation as their subject in the 1840s and 1850s, the condition-of-England fiction, serves teachers as the method's test bench because all four questions find rich evidence there and the findings quarrel instructively. Production: the novels were written for the circulating-library market and the respectable household, by authors whose access to the industrial districts was that of visitors, investigators, and philanthropists rather than inhabitants, and the terms of access are documented in letters and prefaces that the first question reads as evidence rather than as apology. Form: the novels marry the courtship plot to the industrial plot, and the marriage of plots is itself the formal fact, since it assigns the social question a resolution mechanism borrowed from romance, the right marriage and the reconciled family, which the material cannot honour. Ideology: Williams's readings, already cited, locate the strain exactly there, in endings that reconcile persons because the form cannot reconcile classes, and in the conversion of structural antagonism into failures of sympathy that better feeling could cure. Reception: the novels were read in their districts as well as about them, reviewed in the provincial press by the classes they depicted, and their afterlife in the schools fixed them as documents of conscience, a description their first hostile reviewers would not have recognised.

The test bench's value is that the four answers do not harmonise. The production evidence excuses what the ideology evidence indicts; the form evidence explains what the reception evidence complicates. Students who expect the circuit to deliver a single verdict learn, on this material, that it delivers a structured disagreement among evidences, and that the disagreement is the finding. A criticism that can hold the excuse and the indictment in one account, assigning each to its question, has achieved the maturity the tradition asks of its practitioners, and it has done so without the evasion of splitting the difference: the novels are, at once, acts of sympathy constrained by their market and ideological constructions that convert antagonism into misunderstanding, and both clauses are true in the way the evidence specifies.

### What should a student do when the four answers disagree?

Report the disagreement as the finding, assigning each answer to its question and its evidence. The circuit's maturity is measured by its capacity to hold production's excuse and ideology's indictment in one account without letting either annul the other.

The sensation fiction of the following decades supplies the complementary exercise, a literature whose production is nakedly commercial, whose forms are engineered for serial suspense, whose ideology is usually read as conservative panic about the household's secrets, and whose reception, enormous and disproportionately female, embarrassed the guardians who reviewed it. The four questions on sensation fiction yield a portrait of a reading public purchasing, in weekly numbers, narratives in which the respectable interior concealed crime, bigamy, and madness, and the portrait complicates every party's prior story: the market's because the commodity subverted the values of the class that bought it, the guardians' because the corruption they feared was a domestic product, and the tradition's own because the ideology question must here reckon with a form whose satisfactions ran against the ideology its plots appeared to serve. Such complications are the method's daily bread at its best, and the test bench exercises exist to teach students to expect them rather than to fear them.

## The Author Question Settled as Far as It Can Be

The triumph of realism demotes the author; it does not abolish him, and the method's mature position on authorship deserves a section because students oscillate between the two errors, biographical sovereignty and biographical abolition, sometimes in the same essay. The author's intentions are real and are evidence, of the kind and rank the production question assigns: they belong to the circumstances of making, recoverable from letters, drafts, and statements, and they constrain interpretation the way any fact constrains it, by ruling out accounts incompatible with the record. What intentions cannot do is settle the work's meaning, for three reasons the tradition has rehearsed until they are reflexes. The work may fail its intention, and the failure is data. The work may exceed its intention, and the excess is the triumph of realism itself. And the intention is itself a product, formed in the same conditions as the work, so that citing it as the explanation merely moves the question one step back. The author's death, proclaimed by a neighbouring school in the 1960s, is therefore not this tradition's slogan: the author survives, demoted to witness, cross-examined like any witness, believed where the record corroborates and discounted where the work contradicts.

Drafts and revisions are the witness's most reliable testimony, since they show intention in the act of changing under the material's resistance. The manuscript that softens a character across revisions, the serial ending altered under readers' pressure, the chapter withdrawn after a publisher's warning: genetic evidence of this kind lets the critic watch the four questions interacting in real time, production pressure bending form, form discovering ideology, reception reaching back into composition. The French school of genetic criticism built an archive method on such documents without Marxist commitments, and the tradition's uptake of its findings is another of the debts this article is pleased to record. Where drafts survive, they are the closest thing criticism has to a laboratory, and the method's students should be sent to them before they are sent to any theory, since a single corrected proof teaches the circuit faster than a chapter of definitions.

### What rank does the method assign to an author's stated intention?

The rank of testimony. Intention is a production fact, recoverable and constraining, which rules out accounts the record contradicts. It cannot settle meaning, because works fail and exceed their intentions, and because the intention itself was formed under the conditions the first question studies.

The pseudonym and the anonymous text pose the question in its archival form. Much of the literature this tradition studies appeared unsigned, pseudonymous, or under names that were themselves social performances, a woman's work under a man's name being the period's most consequential mask. The mask is a production fact with formal consequences: it licensed subjects, tones, and judgements that the unmasked name could not have carried into print, and the reception of the same text before and after an unmasking is among the fourth question's most controlled experiments, the words constant and the career transformed. Criticism that knows the mask's history reads the text doubly, as the performance the mask enabled and as the document the unmasking reframed, and the doubleness is not a subtlety but the record.

## Criticism as a Genre with Conditions of Its Own

This article has applied the first question to the tradition's classics; the application extends to criticism as a contemporary practice, and a guide that omitted the extension would be protecting its own guild. Criticism is produced: in universities under the economics of the research assessment and the tenure file, in journalism under the economics of the review page and the publicity cycle, in the little magazine under the economics of devotion. Each economy selects its objects and its lengths. The academic economy rewards the article on the canonical text with a theoretical frame, and produces, predictably, a criticism rich in frames and canonical texts. The journalistic economy rewards speed and verdict, and produces the review as consumer guidance with a prose style. The tradition's own criticism has lived in all three economies and bears their marks, and its health in any period can be assessed by asking which economy is feeding it and what that economy is selecting against. The question is not cynical. It is the first question, and a method that exempts its own practice from it has announced the limit of its honesty at exactly the point where honesty costs something.

The review, the tradition's most public genre, deserves its sentence of defence. The short review, written to a deadline about a new book for general readers, is the form in which the four questions are least available and the triumph of realism is most needed, since the reviewer's temptation is precisely the biographical shortcut and the grading by agreement. The reviewers in this lineage who resisted the temptation at length, who used the column to ask what a work showed rather than whether its author agreed with the column, performed the method's public service in its purest form, and their collected reviews remain readable when the verdicts of their seasons are not. The lesson generalises to the student essay, which is a review under examination conditions: the candidate who asks the four questions in miniature, with the evidence rules kept at miniature scale, writes the answer the examiners' reports keep describing as the one they wish to reward.

### Why should criticism study its own economies?

Because selection governs findings. The economies of the academy, the review page, and the little magazine choose which texts are read, at what length, and under which frames, so a method that ignores its own conditions will mistake an economy's selection for the literature itself.

## Money in the Novel: Credit, Speculation, and the Plot of the Fortune

No subject tests the four questions like money, because money is at once the novel's great theme, its production condition, and its formal problem. The classic realist plot is so often a plot of fortune, its making, keeping, marrying, losing, and inheriting, that a reader could reconstruct the century's credit system from its fiction with disquieting completeness. The tradition's critics have done exactly that, and their findings repay summary here because they show the circuit working on the material where it is strongest. Production: the novelists wrote inside the credit economy they depicted, paid by publishers who were themselves speculators in paper, advanced against sales, ruined and enriched by the same instruments their characters wield, so that the fiction's knowledge of credit is partly a tradesman's knowledge. Form: the fortune plot converts economic process into narrative time, since credit is a story told about the future, a promise whose plot is its redemption or its collapse, and the novel that organises itself around a speculation has borrowed its suspense from the bill of exchange. Ideology: the novels take for granted the furniture of the money world, the sanctity of the signature, the disgrace of the bankruptcy court, the marriage market's exchange rates, and their silences gather where the money comes from, in the colony, the mill, and the mine, offstage as reliably as Greek violence. Reception: the reading public that consumed these plots included the clerks, investors, and ruined speculators who lived them, and the novels served that public as manuals, warnings, and consolations whose uses the reviews document.

The marriage plot and the fortune plot are one plot viewed from two sides, and the tradition's feminist extension, already noted, made the unity canonical: in a world where a woman of the propertied classes could not earn, her marriage was her speculation, her portion her capital, her reputation her credit rating, and the courtship novel's suspense is the suspense of a market in which the commodity must conduct its own sale. The reading does not reduce the courtship novel to economics. It explains the novel's specific intensities, why a ballroom scene can carry the charge of a battle, why a refused offer is a catastrophe and not a disappointment, why the heroine's wit is an economic instrument and her error a mispricing with lifelong consequences. Explanations that increase a form's intelligibility at this density are the method's best advertisement, and they are produced by the third question working on the furniture, the assumptions about portions and settlements that the novels never argue because their first readers priced them by reflex.

Speculation supplies the complementary case, the plot in which money detaches from goods and becomes pure sign. The railway manias, the mining bubbles, and the fraudulent companies of the century generated a fiction of the swindler and the bubble whose formal problem is representation itself: how to narrate a value that exists only as belief, a fortune made of paper about paper. The novels solve it, when they solve it, by doubling, giving the speculator a substantial double, a house, a wife, a dinner service, against which the paper fortune's unreality can be measured, and by ending in the crash, the moment when the signs are called in and the world demands goods. The crash ending is the fortune plot's theodicy in negative: the distribution of fates at the crash ratifies the belief that value must somewhere be real, a belief the novels' own century was busy refuting in practice. The critic who reads the crash as ideology, the form's nostalgia for substance, has the third question's finest kind of finding, one that explains an ending's necessity and a culture's consolation in a single move.

### Why is credit a narrative form before it is a novelistic theme?

Because credit is a story about the future that the present agrees to believe: a promise, a suspense, a redemption or a collapse. The novel that plots a speculation borrows its time structure from the instrument it depicts, so the theme and the form are one mechanism viewed twice.

Inheritance, the third money plot, completes the set. The will is the dead hand's narrative device, the past's attempt to author the future, and the inheritance plot's peculiar tension comes from the collision between the testator's script and the heirs' improvisations. The tradition reads the device as the legal form of a society in which property outlives persons and organises their relations from beyond the grave: the entail that freezes an estate, the codicil that rewrites a family, the lost will that releases the plot from the past's intentions. What the device takes for granted, the furniture again, is that this organisation of life by dead property is the natural frame within which the living pursue their happiness, and the novels that question the frame do so, characteristically, by making the inheritance fail, the estate dissolve, the will prove a forgery, so that the characters must improvise a life outside the script. The improvisation is the ideology question's gift to the reader: it shows, by contrast, what the script had been silently supplying.

## Realism in the Visual Arts: The Courbet Parallel

The realism dispute was not confined to literature, and its visual parallel clarifies the literary case by showing the same argument in a medium where the stakes are visible at a glance. When Courbet named his independent exhibition of 1855 a pavilion of realism, he claimed for painting the programme the novelists were claiming for prose: the rendering of contemporary life at full scale, the stonebreakers and the burial party given the dimensions that history painting reserved for gods and kings. The claim was formal and social in one gesture, since scale in the academic hierarchy was a rank, and to paint labourers at the rank of heroes was to argue, in the medium's own language, about whose life counted as a subject. The critics who attacked the pictures attacked the rank violation before the technique, which is the second question's evidence in its rawest form: the form's presupposition, that size follows dignity, had been exposed by being broken.

The parallel instructs because painting's realism debate ran the same course as literature's in a fraction of the time. Within a generation the realist claim had split: one party pressed toward the documentary and the social panorama, the other toward the optical and the sensation, and the second party's heirs, the modernists of the canvas, were accused in their turn of rendering fragmentation rather than grasping a world. The Lukácsian and Brechtian positions map onto the split with embarrassing fidelity, and the mapping suggests that the dispute is not about literature at all but about representation under a specific historical condition, the condition in which the inherited forms of a stable hierarchy confront a society whose relations have become abstract, mediated, and hard to picture. Every medium that inherits the condition replays the dispute in its own materials, which is why the argument of 1938 keeps being rediscovered by filmmakers, photographers, and painters who have never read it.

Photography sharpened the condition to a point. The camera promised the realist programme's completion, the world rendering itself without the mediation of a hand, and the promise embarrassed the programme by fulfilling it literally: the photograph's fidelity was undeniable and its knowledge contestable, since the camera registered the surface with a completeness that Lukács's distinction between narration and description had already indicted as the inventory without the process. The tradition's photographic debates, conducted across the twentieth century's illustrated press and documentary movements, replay the Balzac and Zola comparison in silver and gelatin: the documentary photograph that describes a slum, however exactly, against the photo-essay that narrates a relation, however partially. The terms change; the questions persist; and the persistence across media is the strongest inductive evidence this article can offer that the four questions and the realism problem name something structural about representation in the societies that produced them, rather than a local quarrel among German-speaking critics in a bad decade.

### What does the visual parallel prove about the realism dispute?

It proves the dispute is about representation under a historical condition, not about literature alone. Painting and photography replayed the same split, between rendering a totality and registering a surface, within a generation, which indicates a structural problem that each medium inherits with the condition itself.

## The Novel of Formation and the Education Plot

The Bildungsroman, the novel of a young person's formation, is where the tradition's questions converge most intimately, because its subject is the production of a social being and its plot is the negotiation between a self and the order that would place it. The form's classic arc, apprenticeship, error, and reconciliation, encodes a proposition about the society that produced it: that the order has a place for the formed individual, that the friction between desire and position is educative rather than destructive, and that the hero's final settlement, in a profession, a marriage, an estate, is the world's answer to the question the youth posed. Lukács, who wrote on the form across his career, treated its classical balance as the deposit of a moment when the reconciliation could still be believed, and its later deformations, the formation that ends in renunciation, in madness, in the hero's absorption or expulsion, as the record of the belief's decay. The form's history, on that account, is a seismograph of the bourgeois order's confidence in itself, and the seismograph reading is the second question operating at the scale of a genre's whole career.

The feminist and colonial extensions of the form's criticism extended the seismograph's range. The female Bildungsroman had to solve a problem the male form did not pose: the order's place for the formed woman was the marriage that ended her formation, so the plot's reconciliation and its extinction of the heroine's project were the same event, and the form's women writers bent the arc toward refusals, delays, and second beginnings that the criticism learned to read as formal findings rather than as failures of nerve. The colonial formation novel inverted the geography: the metropolis that the provincial hero journeys to conquer is the source of the power that unformed his home, and his formation is simultaneously an education in the order and an education in its costs, a doubleness the form carries in its structure of journey and return. Each extension was achieved by asking the circuit's questions of a corpus the founding critics had not examined, and each returned findings the founders' categories could state but had not imagined, which is the pattern this article has recorded under the method's portability and now records again as its most reliable sign of life.

### What does the formation novel's ending measure?

It measures a society's confidence that room exists for the formed self. Reconciliation endings deposit the confidence; renunciation, madness, and expulsion endings deposit its decay. The genre's career across two centuries is therefore a record of the order's belief in its own offer.

The education plot's shadow form, the novel of the school and the university, deserves a sentence because its production conditions are the reader's own. The school story, written for the market in boys' and girls' fiction and consumed inside the institutions it depicts, is the fourth question's closed loop: a literature about formation, sold to the forming, in buildings dedicated to the process, and its criticism, which exists and is exact, shows the loop's meanings being negotiated by readers who were also its subjects. The tradition's interest in the case is methodological: where the circuit closes, the evidence for each question is unusually complete, and the school story serves teachers as the training corpus on which students can run all four questions with the archive in reach before graduating to the centuries where the records thin.

## Utopia and Its Discipline

Utopian writing is the tradition's awkward relative, indispensable and suspect, and the four questions give it the fairest hearing it usually gets. Production: the utopia is characteristically written from a position of blockage, in exile, in prison, in the aftermath of a defeat, and its famous placelessness is a production fact before it is a literary one, the elsewhere being the only jurisdiction where the argument could be conducted whole. Form: the utopia's notorious thinness of plot and character is a formal proposition, since a society without antagonism has abolished the engine of the realist novel, and the utopia's compensating devices, the guided tour, the dialogue of instruction, the awakened sleeper, encode the problem that perfection is hard to narrate. Ideology: the utopia takes for granted, in its arrangements, the assumptions of its author's present, so that the future society's furniture, its meals, its marriages, its working day, is a symptomatic document of the moment that dreamed it, legible long after its proposals are obsolete. Reception: utopias are read as programmes, as satires, as children's classics, and as evidence, and their careers, from the parliament of fowls of commentary that surrounds More's founding text to the socialist movement's adoption of Morris's romance as a beloved recruiting pamphlet, show the fourth question's full range of appropriations.

William Morris's News from Nowhere earns its place in the account because it is the tradition's own utopia, written by a Marxist of literary genius in full knowledge of the form's problems. Morris solved the narration problem by making the future's happiness consist largely in the recovery of pleasurable work, so that the tour becomes a series of workshops and river journeys in which the society's principle is enacted rather than expounded, and he solved the ideology problem, as far as it can be solved, by letting the future's inhabitants disagree about their own history and find the visitor's questions faintly comic. The book's reception, as a movement classic whose literary merits its political readers praised and whose politics its literary readers forgave, is the triumph of realism's mirror image: a work whose showing, the rendered texture of a life without money, has persuaded readers its arguing never reached. The case belongs in this article because it proves the circuit's questions apply, with undiminished bite, to the tradition's own productions, the test of impartiality that the Gramsci section set.

### Why is perfection hard to narrate, and what does the difficulty show?

Because the inherited engines of plot are antagonism, desire, and error, and a society that has abolished its antagonisms starves them. The utopia's substitute devices, the tour, the dialogue, the awakening, encode the starvation, so the form's awkwardness is itself the finding about its premise.

The dystopian inversion, which belongs to the following century, reverses the utopia's furniture while keeping its form: the guided tour conducted by the regime's victim, the dialogue of instruction as interrogation, the awakening as arrest. The tradition's criticism of dystopia, where it exists, reads the inversion as the utopia's reception by a century that had seen the tour's destination administered, and the reading is a fourth-question account of a genre's career: the same devices, revalued by history. The episode closes this section's argument, that the circuit's questions are genre-blind in the strong sense, as serviceable for the literature of nowhere as for the literature of the counting house, and that their serviceability across the whole shelf is the practical meaning of the judgement defended from this article’s opening pages.

## Crime Fiction and the Detective's Epistemology

Crime fiction is the popular form on which the four questions have been worked most instructively outside the canon, because the genre wears its presuppositions on its sleeve. Production: the detective story was manufactured for the magazine and the railway stall, its length set by the serial slot and the single sitting, its golden age shaped by publishers' series with their uniform bindings and their rules, famously codified by the genre's own practitioners, that the reader must be given the clues and the detective must not conceal them. Form: the classical detective story presupposes a world in which evidence is legible and guilt is individual. The crime is a tear in the social fabric committed by one deviant will; the investigation is the fabric's repair by reasoning; the solution restores an order that the narrative never questions, because questioning it would dissolve the puzzle's contract. The form's furniture is exact: the country house sealed against the world, the suspects arranged as a closed circle, the detective positioned outside the class he serves, a professional reasoner whose authority the text assumes as it assumes nothing else. Ideology: the genre takes for granted that disorder is personal and curable, that property's arrangements are the order to be restored, and that the state's violence, when it arrives at the solution's side, is the restoration's instrument rather than a second disorder. Its silences gather around the conditions that produce crime as a regular product rather than a deviant act, conditions the form cannot admit without breaking its contract, since a crime explained by a system has no individual to unmask. Reception: the genre's publics consumed the restoration ritual in quantities that made it the century's most reliable publishing property, and the uses they made of it, consolation, rehearsal, the pleasure of a legible world in an illegible one, are documented in the memoirs of readers and the ledgers of libraries alike.

The hard-boiled mutation, which moved the scene to the city and the detective inside the corruption he investigates, is the genre's internal symptomatic event. In the hard-boiled form the restoration fails or curdles: the solution names the criminal and leaves the order that produced him intact and visibly rotten, the detective's code survives as a private ethic in a public world that has none, and the furniture changes accordingly, the office, the bottle, the fee counted out in soiled notes. Critics in this tradition read the mutation as the genre's realism overtaking its ideology, the point at which the form's presupposition, that order is innocent, could no longer be sustained by writers working in the cities where the order's operations were daily news. The reading is a second-question finding of the first rank, because it explains a formal mutation by a change in what the form could presuppose and still be believed, and it does so without consulting the mutators' opinions, several of whom would have rejected the explanation while enacting it.

### What does the classical detective story presuppose about guilt?

That guilt is personal, proof is readable, and the social order stands innocent. The crime is one will's tear in a sound fabric, the investigation repairs it by reasoning, and the solution restores arrangements the narrative never questions, because the puzzle's contract depends on their innocence.

The police procedural, the genre's third estate, completes the sequence by collectivising the detective: the hero becomes a squad, a laboratory, a file system, and the form's presupposition shifts from the reasoner's genius to the institution's procedure. The tradition reads the shift as the deposit of a society whose trust had migrated from persons to systems, and whose anxieties had migrated with it, since the procedural's characteristic fear is not the master criminal but the error in the file, the misidentified print, the procedure followed into injustice. Three forms, three epistemologies, one shelf in the bookshop: the sequence is the cleanest demonstration available to teachers that form encodes a society's theory of knowledge, and it was produced by commercial writers for a mass market, which is the final lesson. The second question does not require canonical texts. It requires only that forms be read as presuppositions, and the genre shelf, where the presuppositions are worn openly and the mutations are dated by publishers' records, is its best laboratory.

## Children's Literature and the Nursery Canon

Children's literature is the field where the circuit's questions bite hardest and are asked least, because the field's innocence is its ideology. Production: children's books are the only literature written, published, reviewed, purchased, and shelved entirely by persons other than their readers, a chain of adult mediation without parallel, in which the librarian, the parent, the prize committee, and the school stand between the writer and the child at every joint. The mediation is not a scandal; it is the field's condition, and the first question, asked of it, transforms the criticism, since every feature of the classic children's book, its length, its vocabulary, its moral weather, its permitted terrors, is a negotiated settlement among the mediators about what a child is. Form: the classic forms encode the settlement. The animal fable presupposes a reader who must be instructed without being addressed as a citizen; the school story presupposes the institution as the world; the family story presupposes the household as the horizon of the real; the adventure story presupposes an elsewhere, conveniently emptied of its inhabitants, where the child's agency can be exercised without consequence at home. Ideology: the nursery's furniture is the adult world's furniture at child height, its assumptions about class, nation, gender, and desert installed before the reader has the concepts to inspect them, which is why the tradition, following Macherey's interest in the literature of childhood, treats the field as ideology's clearest case: the assumptions are not hidden, they are simply addressed to the one audience that cannot yet answer back. Reception: the child's actual reading, attested in readers' own memoirs and in the registers of children's libraries, diverges from the mediation's intention as reliably as the autodidact's diverged from the canon, children raiding the shelf for the forbidden volumes, rereading the frightening passages, and constructing from the approved books meanings their approvers never dreamed.

The golden age classics repay the circuit in detail. The imperial adventure, written for the boys of an empire's schools, taught its geography as a possession and its peoples as a cast of helpers and obstacles, and its silences are the Verne silences at an earlier age of reading. The domestic classic, written for the girls of the same households, taught the household as a moral economy in which a girl's formation consisted in learning the price of everything she would be forbidden to earn. The two shelves together composed a curriculum, and the curriculum's graduates ran the empire and staffed its households with the furniture installed. Later scholarship, much of it from scholars working in the feminist and postcolonial extensions of this tradition, reconstructed the curriculum from the books themselves, the prize lists, and the school catalogues, and the reconstruction is now the standard account of how a literature manufactures the common sense of a ruling order without ever issuing an order. Gramsci's national-popular question returns here with its sharpest edge: the children of the subordinate classes were reading, in the same decades, the penny dreadfuls and the imported adventure papers that the mediation condemned, and the contrast between the two diets is a fourth-question map of a divided culture's formation.

### Why is children's literature ideology's clearest case?

Because its assumptions are installed before the reader can inspect them, through a chain of adult mediation that selects every feature of the book. The furniture of class, nation, and desert is placed at child height, for readers who cannot yet reply.

The field also supplies the tradition's most hopeful reception evidence. The memoirs of working-class readers return obsessively to childhood reading, to the library card as a first property, to the classic encountered in a cheap edition and possessed against every intention of its mediators. The child who read the imperial adventure as a manual of escape, the girl who read the domestic classic as a tragedy rather than a training, were practising the appropriation that Rose documented in their elders, and the continuity suggests that reception's agency is not an adult accomplishment but a reader's birthright, exercised as soon as the book is opened. The circuit's fourth question, which began as the tradition's empirical conscience, ends in this field as something like a defence of the reader, and the defence is earned by evidence rather than declared by sentiment.

## The Family Saga and the Chronicle of Property

The family saga, the multi-generational chronicle of a house and its fortunes, is realism's most ambitious architecture and the tradition's favourite demonstration that a form can think. The saga's premise is temporal: only across three generations can the forces that govern a family's rise and decline become visible, because the forces work at that speed, capital accumulating and dissipating, land converting to money and money to status, the founding energy hardening into the heir's routine and the routine into the last heir's aestheticism or ruin. The form's great exemplars trace the arc with variations that are themselves findings: the house that rises on trade and falls on finance, the estate that survives by marrying the money it despises, the dynasty whose final representative converts the accumulated capital into art, as if the family's history were a long alchemy of goods into spirit. Lukács's totality is the saga's native element, since the form exists to show the connection between the individual fate and the historical process at the only scale where the connection is fully legible, the scale of the lifetime of property itself.

The saga's ideology is in its furniture of continuity. The house, the firm, the name, the portrait gallery: the form presupposes that these continuities are the story's subject and the individual's meaning is his place in their procession. Servants, wives, and younger sons appear in the saga as functions of the continuity, and the form's silences gather, predictably and measurably, around the labour and the lives that the continuity consumed without recording. The feminist extension of saga criticism made the point canonical by reading the chronicle against its grain, recovering from the margins, the letters, the marriages recorded as alliances, the women whose management held the house together while its history was written as the deeds of its men. The recovery is symptomatic reading in its most constructive mood: the saga's own detail, accumulated with a realist's fidelity, supplies the evidence for the reading its framework could not state, which is the founding principle at work inside the tradition's criticism of its own favourite form.

Production and reception complete the saga's circuit with unusual neatness. The saga was the circulating library's prestige commodity and the early book club's founding offering, a form whose length was its market position, and its twentieth-century afterlife in the television serial, where the multi-generational chronicle found the mass audience its volumes had lost, is the fourth question's standard exhibit of a form migrating to the medium whose economics match its scale. The migration changed the furniture, the house becoming a set, the generations a casting problem, and the changes are legible as production facts in the criticism of both media. A student who has followed the saga from the triple-decker to the serialised screen has watched the circuit turn three times on one form, and the exercise, which requires no exotic archive, is among the best preparations for the examination's demand that the method be shown working rather than recited.

### What can the saga show that the single-life novel cannot?

The forces that work at the speed of property: accumulation and dissipation, the conversion of land into money and money into status, the hardening of founding energy into routine. Only the three-generation arc makes those forces visible as destinies rather than as background.

## Melodrama and the Moral Occult

Melodrama, the mode that respectable criticism dismissed for a century as the novel's and the stage's vulgar relative, was recovered by this tradition's methods as one of the most legible ideological forms of the nineteenth century. The recovery begins with a production fact: melodrama was the theatre of the unlicensed and the cheap, the form of the publics that the patent houses and the library shelves excluded, and its aesthetics, the broad gesture, the tableau, the musical cue, the villain legible at his entrance, were solutions to the conditions of noisy houses, mixed audiences, and, in the years of the licensing laws, a legal prohibition on spoken drama in the minor theatres that made music and gesture carry the meaning words could not. Form follows the licence, as the first question predicts. The mode's ideology, in the account that Peter Brooks gave it in a study this tradition claims as a cousin, is the moral occult: in a world from which the sacred has withdrawn, melodrama stages the desperate effort to make virtue and evil visible, to force a signifying universe to declare its moral meanings on the surface of gesture and event. The excess that embarrassed the critics is the effort's measure. Where realism trusts the surface to be legible in depth, melodrama, addressing a world experienced as morally illegible, shouts the meaning in the hope that the shouting will make it true.

The tradition's interest in melodrama is methodological as much as historical. The mode demonstrates that the second question's presuppositions can be read in a form's failures of taste as readily as in its triumphs of art, and that the reading public's most consumed forms are the archive of its structure of feeling in the least censored state. The recovery also corrected the tradition's own snobbery, since the Lukácsian hierarchy had filed melodrama with naturalism among the degraded modes, and the correction came from applying the circuit to material the hierarchy had exempted from attention. A method that can correct its own canon by its own questions has the property this article keeps demanding of it, exposure to its evidence, and the melodrama case is a compact proof that the property is real.

### What is the moral occult, and why does it explain melodrama's excess?

The moral occult is the hidden order of virtue and evil that melodrama strains to make visible in a world that no longer displays it. The excess, the gesture, the tableau, the coincidence, is the strain's measure: meaning shouted because the universe has stopped whispering it.

The sensation novel's relation to melodrama, noted in an earlier section, can now be stated exactly: sensation fiction is melodrama admitted to the library, the moral occult installed in the respectable interior, with the railway timetable and the law of evidence supplying the modern machinery of the sign. The two forms' kinship, established by the circuit's questions rather than by the period's own taxonomy, which kept them apart for reasons of class, is the kind of finding the method exists to produce: a relation invisible to the participants, documented in the forms, and explanatory of both.

## Working-Class Writing and the Autobiography Shelf

The tradition's most searching test of itself is the writing of the subordinate classes, because there the method's categories meet a literature that the circuit's usual subjects produced as objects rather than as authors. Working-class autobiography, the shelf of memoirs by miners, mill hands, servants, and autodidacts that scholars recovered across the last half century, poses the four questions in reverse. Production: the memoir was written against the grain of the market, often in old age, often for a local publisher or a union press, under the pressure of a life that had left little leisure for the writing of it, and the circumstances are legible in the books' shapes, their apologies for existing, their dedications to the societies that lent the author his education. Form: the autobiography borrows the formation novel's arc and bends it, since the life it records was formed not by reconciliation with an order but by resistance to one, and the arc's conventional stations, schooling, work, marriage, are revalued as the stations of a collective rather than a private story, the chapel, the union, the institute library standing where the estate and the profession stand in the bourgeois form. Ideology: the memoirs take for granted, often, the respectability their authors had fought to claim, and their silences gather around the defeats, the strikes lost, the comrades who informed, the wives whose labour underwrote the self-education the memoir celebrates, silences that the feminist recovery of the shelf has taught its readers to hear. Reception: the memoirs were read, in their time, by the movements that published them, and in ours by the scholars who cite them, and between those readings lies the shelf's long oblivion in the remaindered and the unreviewed, a reception history that is itself the strongest document of the canon-forming process the fourth question studies.

The recovery of the shelf changed the tradition's evidence base in a way this article has already used without naming: Rose's readers are these memoirs' authors, and the archive of working-class reading exists because the readers wrote. The circuit thus closes on itself in the happiest way, the fourth question's subjects becoming the first question's producers, and the criticism that studies them must handle its own categories with a new tact, since the symptomatic reading of a miner's silences is an act performed on a writer who cannot answer, by a scholar whose institutional position is one of the memoir's subjects. The tradition's best work on the shelf is marked by that tact, a willingness to let the memoir's own account of its meanings stand in the record beside the critic's account of its assumptions, with the disagreement visible. The visibility of the disagreement is the ethics of the method in practice, and it generalises: every symptomatic reading is an act of power over a text that cannot reply, and the discipline of the seam, the archive, and the stated defeater is the method's way of keeping that power answerable.

### What reverses when the circuit is applied to working-class autobiography?

The direction of the evidence. The readers the fourth question studies become the authors the first question studies, and the critic's own institutional position becomes one of the text's subjects, so the reading must keep its disagreements with the memoir visible rather than absorbing them.

Fiction by working-class writers poses the complementary problem, since the novelist from the subordinate class writes in forms manufactured for another class's experience, and the friction between the borrowed form and the lived material is the work's most instructive feature. The industrial novel written from inside, the pit novel, the mill novel, the novel of the streets written by a man who swept them: these works strain the formation arc, the marriage plot, and the omniscient narrator in ways their reviewers registered as faults of construction, and the tradition's criticism re-read the faults as findings, the marks of a form being forced to carry an experience it was not built for. The re-reading is the second question's most generous operation, since it converts a work's apparent failures into the evidence of its historical position, and it has permanently changed the standing of a shelf of novels that the contemporary reviews dismissed for exactly the features that now constitute their claim on attention. A method that can perform that conversion, with the evidence rules kept, has earned its place in the curriculum beside the methods that judge by finish alone.

## The Periodical Press and the Manufacture of Taste

Between the book and the reader stands the periodical press, the institution that taught the nineteenth and twentieth centuries what to think of what they read, and the tradition's account of it belongs in any full statement of the circuit because the press is where reception is manufactured before it is measured. Production: the great reviews and the literary weeklies were businesses with proprietors, party connections, advertisers, and a corps of reviewers paid by the sheet and writing to the house's line, and the line is recoverable from the archives with a precision that makes the review pages the best documented site in the whole literary system. Form: the review essay is a genre with presuppositions as strict as the sonnet's, the magisterial survey that places a book in a tradition the reviewer commands, the notice that grades by extraction of passages, the interview that converts the author into a personality whose biography then does the work the triumph of realism forbids. Ideology: the press's furniture is the hierarchy of genres and nations it never argues, the assumption that the capital's taste is the taste, that the serious is the lengthy, that the popular is the suspect, assumptions installed in the layout, the order of the notices, and the size of the type before a word is read. Reception: the press's own reception, in the clubs, the common rooms, and the publishers' advertisements that quoted its verdicts as currency, completes the loop, since the review's power consisted precisely in being quoted, and the quotation is a document the fourth question can follow from the page to the advertisement to the title page of the second edition, where the verdict, trimmed of its qualifications, does its commercial work.

The tradition's critics have used the press's archive to settle questions that textual study could not touch: whether a classic was born classic or made so by a decade of reviewing labour, whether a reputation's collapse was a judgement of the work or a change in the reviewing corps, whether a school of writing existed anywhere outside the pages of the journal that named it. The last question is the sharpest, since movements in literature are frequently the press's manufactures, a label, a manifesto, and three reviews creating the school that the historians then discover, and the discovery's correction is a model fourth-question finding, the archive dissolving an entity into a process. Students who learn to ask of any movement whether it existed before its naming have acquired a permanent instrument, and the instrument is this article's method in one of its most portable forms.

### How can a review manufacture a literary movement?

By naming it. A label, a manifesto, and a season of reviews can create the school that historians later discover as a fact of the period, and the archive of the press, which preserves the naming, is where the entity dissolves back into the process that produced it.

The little magazine, the press's heroic margin, deserves the account's final word in this section. The magazines that carried modernism, that published the exile debates this article has used, that kept whole literatures alive through decades of official hostility, were produced under conditions of exemplary precarity: tiny circulations, unpaid contributors, editors who were also printers, patrons, and postmen. Their influence, measured by the fourth question's proper standard, the documented consequences in careers, canons, and subsequent writing, exceeds that of journals a hundred times their size, and the disproportion is the standing refutation of any crude equation between a publication's economics and its effects. The first question establishes the conditions; it does not ration the consequences, and the little magazine is the case that keeps the distinction honest.

## Editing, Anthologising, and the Scholarly Apparatus

The circuit's least glamorous agents may be its most powerful: the editors and anthologists who decide in what form a work survives. The scholarly edition is a production of the work as surely as the first printing was, and its decisions, which text to canonise among variants, which spelling to modernise, which notes to supply, compose the object that subsequent criticism reads. The tradition's attention to the apparatus follows from its premises. A criticism that holds the physical book to be a constituent of the work's social existence cannot treat the modern edition as a transparent window, and the history of editing supplies the cases: the classic whose received text was a nineteenth-century editor's construction, regularised, expurgated, and re-paragraphed to a standard its author never saw; the poet whose manuscripts, published at last in their disorder, dissolved a century's image of serene composition; the novelist whose serial and volume texts differ at the exact passages where the criticism had built its arguments, so that two schools were discovered to have been reading two books. Each case is a first-question finding with fourth-question consequences, and each was produced by the archival disciplines this article has been recommending as the circuit's conscience.

The anthology is the edition's popular form and the canon's actual machinery in most readers' lives. The anthologist's table of contents is a literary history in disguise, its inclusions a syllabus, its exclusions a silence with a print run, and the tradition reads anthologies as primary documents of the periods that made them: the wartime anthology that conscripted the poets, the school anthology that decided which nation's literature a generation of colonial pupils would call its own, the recovery anthology that returned a suppressed shelf to print and thereby changed what the tradition's history could say. Anthologies also manufacture the excerpts by which most long works are known, and the excerpt is a form with presuppositions of its own, favouring the detachable lyric, the set speech, the famous scene, and starving the slow structures in which the circuit's findings usually live. A criticism alert to the excerpt's bias will distrust its own sense of the canon in a specific and useful way, asking of any famous passage whether its fame is the work's or the anthology's, and the question, once learned, cannot be unlearned in any bookshop.

### Why should a critic read the edition as part of the work?

Because the edition composes the object criticism reads: the chosen variant, the modernised spelling, the supplied notes, and the anthologist's excerpt all intervene between the work and its readers, and the intervention is documented, arguable, and consequential for every later claim.

The footnote deserves its sentence in this anatomy. The scholarly note is where the circuit's evidence is warehoused, and its conventions, the citation, the collation, the admitted uncertainty, are the method's ethics in miniature: a claim, its grounds, and the conditions under which it would fall, printed at the foot of the page in type smaller than the claim's. Students are rarely taught to read notes as arguments, and the omission wastes the best training in evidence the discipline offers, since a great edition's notes show a mind deciding, hundreds of times, what the record supports and what it merely suggests. The tradition's respect for the apparatus is of a piece with its respect for the archive generally: both are refusals to let the argument float free of the documents that could embarrass it.

## Pleasure, Judgement, and Why the Method Reads at All

A suspicion shadows the method in the minds of many readers who have followed this article with sympathy: that a criticism so occupied with conditions, presuppositions, and careers must secretly dislike literature, or like it only as evidence, and that the four questions, faithfully asked, would leave the reader holding a dossier where a novel used to be. The suspicion deserves a direct answer, because it identifies the point at which the method either justifies itself or stands revealed as an industry for converting art into documents. The answer begins with the tradition's own classics, which are, without exception, the work of readers possessed by their objects: the letter that founds the method is a love letter to Balzac's achievement, the realism essays burn with admiration for the novels they anatomise, Brecht's theory was written to make a theatre he could not stop attending in his mind, Williams's readings are acts of gratitude conducted by other means. The circuit's questions were not devised by literature's enemies. They were devised by readers who found that the available ways of praising what they loved were too weak for the love's object, and who built stronger instruments because the novels deserved them.

The point can be made analytically. Pleasure in a work is not a datum the method must explain away; it is among the facts the fourth question records and the second question analyses, since a form's presuppositions include the pleasures it organises, and a reading that cannot say wherein a work's pleasure consists has failed descriptively before it has begun critically. The tradition's strongest readings are precise about pleasure: the satisfaction of the saga's long arc, the melodrama's shouted meaning, the detective story's legible world, the lyric's withdrawn intensity, each named as a pleasure with a structure, which is to say with a history and a politics, neither of which diminishes it. What the method refuses is not pleasure but the use of pleasure as an argument-stopper, the declaration that a work is great and therefore exempt from questions. The declaration is the one move the circuit cannot accommodate, because it is the move by which every ideology in this article has protected its furniture from inspection, and the method's founders, who loved the furniture's makers, inspected it anyway.

### Does the method leave the reader with a dossier instead of a novel?

No, because the questions were built by readers whose classics are acts of admiration with instruments attached. The circuit analyses pleasure as a structured fact rather than explaining it away, and refuses only its use as an exemption from questions.

Judgement, the topic on which this article has already touched through Eagleton, returns at the close as the method's unfinished business, and the honest summary is that the tradition has never decided how much of criticism is science and how much is advocacy, because its best practitioners keep needing to be both. The four questions are a science's questions: they generate findings. The verdict with its defeater is the advocacy's form disciplined by the science: a ranking, a recommendation, a defence, issued with its surrender terms attached. A criticism that had only the findings would be a research programme in search of a public; a criticism armed only with verdicts would be the reviewing page with a longer vocabulary. The tradition at its healthiest holds the two in the tension this article has tried to practise, and the tension, rather than any resolution of it, is the inheritance a new reader should expect to receive. Expect also the quarrels, since the tension guarantees them, and read the quarrels as the tradition's pulse rather than its pathology: a method whose practitioners argue about realism, about silence, about resistance, about judgement itself, in the open and from the evidence, is a method still alive enough to be worth the considerable labour of learning it.

## World Literature and the Unequal Exchange

The circuit's final extension is geographical, and it returns the article to the first question at the largest scale. The literature of the world circulates through channels as material as any library's: translation is commissioned or not, by publishers estimating markets; prestige is allocated by prizes, reviews, and syllabuses centred in a few capitals; a writer's availability to the world's readers depends on the economics of her language, the presence of her work in the dominant tongues, and the advocacy of the brokers, translators, agents, and academics, who operate the exchange. The tradition's contribution to the study of this system is to insist that the inequality is structural and legible, not a mystery of taste: the same work is a classic where the channels carried it and an unknown where they did not, and the map of world literature is a map of the channels before it is a map of merit. The insistence does not deny merit. It denies that merit travels unassisted, and it directs the researcher to the assistance, which is documented in publishers' lists, translators' prefaces, and the records of the cultural institutes that subsidised the traffic for their own nations' reasons.

Translation, within this system, is the second question's hardest case, because the translated work's form is doubled: the presuppositions of the original's devices meet the receiving language's devices, and the translator's choices, verse for prose, archaism for modernity, the foreign retained or the foreign domesticated, compose a third work that both publics call by the first work's name. The tradition reads translations as reception documents of the receiving culture, as this article noted earlier, and the reading has a corollary for the canon at home: a literature's translated classics are its imports, selected by its needs, and the selection tells the fourth question what the importing culture lacked and wanted. The nineteenth century's Shakespeare, naturalised in Germany as a national classic while England's own stage mangled him, is the standing example of a work whose world career refutes every notion that a text's meaning is settled at its origin, and the example's moral is the circuit's moral at world scale: follow the editions, the channels, and the uses, and the literature will explain more of itself than any theory of genius can.

### What does the map of world literature map first?

The channels before the merit: the translation commissions, the prestige capitals, the subsidised institutes, and the brokers who operate the exchange. A title holds classic status where the channels carried it, and research is directed to the carriage, which is documented.

The unequal exchange also governs the traffic in criticism itself, a point this article's national strands section prepared. The four questions are asked, in the languages of every continent, in universities whose libraries hold the tradition in selections determined by the same channels that govern the literature, and the local accents multiply accordingly: the method applied to oral traditions its founders never considered, to cinemas its categories must stretch to hold, to literatures whose production conditions include the mission press, the state publishing house, and the exile journal in combinations the European record does not supply. Each application tests the circuit, and the tests have the form this article has taught the reader to expect: where the questions find evidence and generate corrigible findings, the method has travelled; where a question finds no purchase, the failure is recorded against the list, under the defeater's terms, rather than explained away. The ledger of such tests is the tradition's living frontier, and it is kept, appropriately, by the fourth question's method, as a reception history of the circuit itself.

## The Short Story and the Economics of Brevity

The short story is the form whose criticism most plainly betrays the production question's power, because the story's defining feature, its brevity, is a market artefact before it is any aesthetic choice. The modern short story was manufactured by the magazine market: the monthly and weekly periodicals needed fiction that completed itself in a sitting and in a fixed number of pages, payable by the word or by the piece, and the form's celebrated properties, the single effect, the epiphanic close, the opening that must seize in a paragraph, are the craft solutions to a column's dimensions. Poe's doctrine of the single effect, the form's founding poetics, is usually taught as a philosophy of composition; the first question reads it as a trade document, the theory of a magazine writer rationalising the conditions of his market into an aesthetic, and the reading does not diminish the doctrine. It explains the doctrine's plausibility, its fit to the experience of the writers who adopted it, and its long afterlife in the writing schools, where the magazine's dimensions survive as the workshop's commandments long after the magazines themselves have thinned. Forms outlive their conditions as commandments, and the lag between a condition's disappearance and its aesthetic's retirement is one of the second question's most reliable phenomena, visible wherever a genre's rules are obeyed by writers who could not name the market that made them.

The story's ideology is in its scale. A form that must complete itself in a sitting presupposes a world in which a life, or a decisive fragment of one, can be rendered whole in an evening's reading, and its characteristic subject, the moment of vision, the encounter that reveals, the choice that sums a character, encodes a metaphysic of the significant instant that the novel's long accumulations exist to contradict. The two forms' coexistence in the same literary system is therefore not a mere variety of lengths but a standing argument about the shape of experience, conducted in the bookshop's architecture rather than in the journals. The tradition's critics have read the argument with profit, noting how the story flourished where the magazine did, how its masters were so often the writers of colonised and provincial places, for whom the fragment was the form of a world whose totality was administered elsewhere, and how the epiphany, the story's signature close, can be read as the formal deposit of that position: the whole cannot be narrated, but a moment can be made to blaze with the whole's pressure. The reading is contestable, as all such readings are, and it is stated here with its evidence class named, the correlation of the form's masters with the market's geography, so that a rival can test it rather than merely deplore it.

### Why is brevity an economic artefact before it is an aesthetic?

Because the magazine market set the dimensions first: fiction complete in a sitting, in fixed pages, paid by the piece. The craft doctrines that rationalised the dimensions into a poetics, the single effect above all, are trade documents that outlived their market as commandments.

The ghost story and the tale of the uncanny, the short form's most durable subgenres, extend the lesson into the third question's territory. The ghost story's classic setting, the country house at the turning of the year, its classic personnel, the returned past in the person of the dead, and its classic effect, the past's refusal to stay buried, compose an ideological instrument of great precision: a form in which a propertied order's anxieties about inheritance, legitimacy, and the claims of the dispossessed could be entertained as supernatural shudders and dismissed at the story's close as weather. The tradition's readings of the subgenre, which are among its most enjoyed performances, treat the ghost as the return of the form's repressed, the heir who was wronged, the servant who was wronged, the woman who was wronged, knocking at the door of the house that the daylight chapters describe as harmonious. The reading risks the parlour game, and the seam discipline applies: the ghost counts as evidence where the text's own project, the celebration of the house's continuity, generates the expectation of a settled past that the haunting refuses. Where the refusal is structural, the reading stands; where the ghost is merely furniture of the genre, the critic must let it be furniture, and the distinction, again, is the method's ethics in miniature.

## Satire and the Licence to Laugh

Satire is the mode in which the tradition's questions meet their most evasive subject, because satire's relation to its society is double by design: it attacks the order in the order's own forms, before the order's own audience, under a licence that the order grants and can withdraw. Production: the satirist's conditions are the licence's terms, the patron who must not be named in the lampoon, the bookseller who judges how much sedition the season will bear, the law of libel standing behind every generalisation, so that satire's characteristic indirection, the fictional kingdom, the animal fable, the dead language's authority borrowed for a living target, is censorship's gift to literature, the first question's clearest case of constraint as a formal cause. Form: satire presupposes a standard from which its targets deviate, and its deepest formal fact is that the standard is usually the order's own professed one, the satirist posing as the true conservative of values the powerful have betrayed, which is why satire so often serves, structurally, as the safety valve its targets can afford. Ideology: the mode takes for granted the furniture of the world it mocks, its hierarchies inverted for an evening's laughter and restored by the laughter's end, and its silences gather around the alternatives it cannot imagine, the order beyond the order whose follies fill the page. Reception: the satire's career is the fourth question's comedy, the lampoon absorbed as a classic, the scourge of an age assigned in that age's grandchildren's schools, its teeth drawn by the syllabus and its targets forgotten, so that the notes must explain whom the laughter was killing.

The tradition's verdict on satire is therefore double in a way that matches its subject. As evidence, satire is priceless: no other mode records a period's operative standards, its real red lines, and its licensed zones of discontent with comparable density, and the historian of ideology mines it accordingly. As an instrument of change, its record is the one its form predicts, brilliant at shaming persons and conservative of the structures that produce them, since the standard it invokes is the structure's own. The double verdict is not a dismissal. It is the circuit's characteristic product, a finding in which the same evidence supports an admiration and a limitation, assigned to their questions, and it models for students the maturity this article has repeatedly recommended: the refusal to let a finding about a work's power settle the question of its politics, or a finding about its politics settle the question of its power.

### Why is satire structurally conservative even when its targets are radical?

Because its standard is the order's own professed standard: the satirist poses as the keeper of values the powerful betrayed. The form can shame any person and still restore, by the laughter's end, the hierarchy whose professions supplied the measuring rod.

Verse satire's decline and prose satire's migration into the novel complete the mode's circuit in a paragraph. The couplet's closed world, in which the standard could be stated in balanced lines that sounded like the order of things, decayed with the world that found balance natural, and satire moved into the novel's open form, where the standard could no longer be stated, only implied by the distribution of fates. The migration is the second question's familiar seismograph at work in a single mode's biography, and it leaves the modern satirist in the position the tradition describes with sympathy: armed with the mode's indignation, deprived of the mode's metaphysic, and obliged to invent, work by work, the standard from which the follies deviate. The invention's difficulty is the contemporary satirist's occupational hazard, and its successes, where the invented standard convinces, are among the clearest demonstrations that form's presuppositions can be chosen against the grain of an age, at a price in coherence that the work must pay openly. The tradition, which holds that forms encode societies, is not obliged to hold that they encode them fatally, and satire's modern career is its standing evidence that the encoding is a pressure and not a prison.

## Romance, Adventure, and the Geography of the Imagination

The romance, realism's elder and rival, is the form the tradition's criticism needed longest to learn to read, because the Lukácsian hierarchy had filed it as realism's immature other, the mode of wish and wonder that the novel's maturity superseded. The recovery of romance by the circuit's questions is one of the method's later achievements and a fair test of its maturity. Production: romance flourished in the cheap formats and the colonial markets that the respectable novel's economics excluded, in the story papers, the dime libraries, and the imperial adventure series that publishers aimed at the boy reader and the emigrant's trunk, and its geography of production is the underside of the literary system's map. Form: romance presupposes a world of clear moral legibility and open space, a frontier where the hero's qualities can act directly on events without the mediation of institutions, and the presupposition is a proposition about the metropolitan world it excludes, in which qualities are mediated, institutions decide, and the hero's direct action is a fantasy precisely because the reader's life affords none. Ideology: the romance's furniture is the empire's, its maps drawn with the possessions coloured, its peoples cast as the scenery of the hero's testing, its silences the Verne silences in their most popular form, the interiority of the colonised refused exactly where the adventure's project, the hero's free movement through their world, required the refusal. Reception: the romance's readers included the empire's administrators in their schoolboy years and its subjects in theirs, and the divergent uses, the same adventure read as a commission in one classroom and as an insult in another, compose the fourth question's most instructive file on the politics of reading, since the text is constant and the careers are opposite.

The recovery's lesson is about the hierarchy of forms itself. A criticism that ranks forms by their cognitive dignity will misread the forms it ranks low, because the ranking licenses inattention, and inattention forfeits the evidence: the romance's presuppositions, being cruder, are more legible than the novel's, and its readers, being less guarded, left fuller records. The tradition's later practice therefore treats the popular forms as the method's training ground and its evidence reserve, a reversal of the founding hierarchy that the founding questions themselves compelled. There is a parable in the reversal for every school in this article: the instrument, honestly used, corrects the hand that built it, and a tradition that can narrate its own corrections without embarrassment has the property that the objection section demanded and the verdict requires.

### Why did the tradition misread romance for so long?

Because its hierarchy ranked forms by cognitive dignity and licensed inattention to the low-ranked. Romance's assumptions are cruder and therefore easier to read than the novel's, and its readers left fuller records, so the recovery of romance was the circuit correcting its own founders.

The western, the sea story, and the imperial romance's other heirs extend the file without altering its structure, and the science fiction of the following century, which inherited romance's open space and relocated it among the stars, inherited its questions as well: whose interiority the voyage requires and refuses, which furniture of the home world the starship carries unexamined, what the frontier's emptiness presupposes about the lands it declares empty. The tradition's criticism of the genre, where it has been practised with the circuit's discipline, is among its liveliest recent chapters precisely because the material is so legible, and the liveliness confirms the recovery's moral. The method does not need its objects to be respectable. It needs them to be read with the questions in order and the evidence rules in force, and it will find, in the least respectable shelf of the bookshop, documents of the social imagination as faithful as any the canon preserves, and sometimes, because less guarded, more faithful still.

## The Essay and the Feuilleton: Criticism's Own Popular Forms

The critical essay itself has a production history that this article, as an exercise in the genre's longer form, should acknowledge before closing its survey. The essay was born in the periodical, shaped by the column's length and the weekly's deadline, and its classic manner, the familiar voice thinking in public, presupposes a reader who returns weekly to a known intelligence and grants it the licence of the unfinished thought. The feuilleton, the feuilletonistic supplement of the continental newspaper, carried the manner to its virtuoso extreme, compressing the whole of culture into the space below the line, and produced a criticism whose brilliance and whose limits are both the column's: the aperçu that illuminates a career in a sentence, the judgement that cannot show its working because the working would fill the paper. The tradition's own criticism lived in these forms as much as in the treatise, and the realism debate itself was a periodical event, an exchange in a journal of exile whose deadlines, page budgets, and editorial politics are part of its text for any reader the first question has trained.

The long essay, the form of the present article, is the periodical essay's descendant under new conditions: written for a study series and organised for retrieval, accompanied by the apparatus of tables and questions that its medium rewards. Its presuppositions are as examinable as any sonnet's. It presupposes a reader studying rather than browsing, a search rather than a subscription, and a patience for the circuit's full turn that the weekly's reader never promised. The acknowledgement is not false modesty. It is the method's self-application at the threshold of the article's close, and it licenses the reader to ask of this text the questions it has taught, beginning with the production question and ending, as the circuit ends, with the uses to which the reader will put it.

## Shakespeare as the Circuit's Proving Ground

No author serves the method's teaching better than Shakespeare, because no author's file is fuller at every point of the circuit. Production: the plays were written for a commercial theatre in which the author was a shareholder, an actor, and a supplier of scripts to a company whose repertory, casting, and finances are documented in legal and theatrical records of unusual richness, so that the first question can be asked of these texts with an evidentiary luxury available for almost no other early literature: the size of the company constrains the doubling, the ownership of the playhouse constrains the staging, the plague closures constrain the touring, and the court performances constrain the politics, all in documents a student can be shown. Form: the plays' presuppositions have been this article's examples throughout, the soliloquy's trusting audience, the chorus of citizens, the history play's proposition that a nation's past is the present's argument, and the mixed form itself, tragedy with clowns, chronicle with taverns, encodes a theatre that played to the whole social order in one room and built the order's stratification into its dramaturgy. Ideology: the plays' furniture, the degree and the chain of being, the sanctity of the oath and the horror of the regicide, has been inventoried by generations of critics, and the symptomatic readings, of the colonial island play above all, where the native's interiority is approached in poetry of the first order and refused in the plot's disposition, are the third question's most contested and therefore most instructive modern cases. Reception: the career is the fourth question's epic, the Restoration's adaptations with their happy endings, the Romantics' closet Shakespeare who was too great for the stage, the imperial syllabus that carried the plays as the empire's cultural passport, the national theatres that claimed him for every nation in turn, and the schoolroom where most of the world still meets him, in editions whose notes compose the authorised reading this article has described.

Cultural materialism made Shakespeare its proving ground for a principled reason: if the method could show that the most canonised author in the language was not a monument above history but a process within it, contested at every period including the present one, then the demonstration held for the whole canon. The school's characteristic readings therefore stage the contest openly: the play's meanings in its first theatre against its meanings in the modern one, the dissident possibilities the text affords against the uses the institutions have made of it, with the critic's own use declared as a further entry in the file rather than disguised as the file's conclusion. New historicism, working the same archive across the Atlantic, staged the opposite emphasis, the containment of the play's subversions by the powers they appeared to threaten, and the Shakespeare field became the arena in which the transatlantic distinction of this article's earlier section was fought out in practice, essay by essay, before the same documents. A student who follows one play through both schools' readings possesses the distinction not as a definition but as a habit of seeing, which is the only form in which distinctions survive the examination hall.

### Why did cultural materialism choose Shakespeare as its proving ground?

Because the demonstration is strongest at the canon's summit. If the most monumental author can be shown as a process within history, contested in every period and used in the present one, the case holds for every lesser monument, and the archive is full enough to test the showing.

The Shakespeare file also closes the article's long argument about the author. No writer's intentions are less recoverable, no biography thinner in proportion to the work's mass, and no work's showing has more completely escaped its author's believing, about which the record permits almost nothing to be said. The tradition's founding principle, which the Balzac case states as a paradox, the Shakespeare case states as a commonplace: the plays are read, taught, fought over, and loved in entire independence of their author's opinions, which are unknown, and the independence has never prevented the criticism from proceeding by evidence at every other point of the circuit. If the method works for Shakespeare, where the author is a name and a share in a theatre, it works anywhere, and the student who doubts the demotion of biography should be sent to the file where the demotion is not a doctrine but the archive's simple condition.

## Literary Criticism and Its Neighbours: The Method Among Them

A method is known by its borders, and the four questions border on several neighbouring practices with which students confuse them. Sociology of literature, in its empirical form, studies the production and consumption of books as social facts and may stop there, content with the map of the trade; the Marxist circuit begins with that map but insists on entering the text, reading form and ideology with the map in hand. Cultural studies, the broad field that grew partly from Williams's work, shares the circuit's range while often relaxing its evidence rules and its interest in the text's construction; the difference shows in the products, the cultural studies essay being reader to trade the seam for the theme. Reader-response criticism shares the fourth question's territory while relocating it from the archive to the phenomenology of reading, asking what a text does in a consciousness rather than what publics did with it in history; the two are complements that surveys persistently conflate. The new historicism border has been drawn in its own section. The formalist boundary was mapped in an earlier section. What remains is to state the principle of the borders: the method is the circuit, and a practice that amputates the circuit at any point, however brilliantly it performs the remainder, is a neighbour and not the thing itself.

The borders also mark the method's genuine debts, which honesty requires listing in one place. From bibliography and the history of the book: the production evidence and the discipline of the physical object. From formalism: the device as a unit and the demand that claims descend to construction. From philology and the older historicism: the patience with periods not one's own. From sociology: the refusal to treat audiences as abstractions. From the hermeneutics of suspicion generally, a lineage that includes Nietzsche and Freud beside Marx: the premise that a text's surface is not its whole testimony. A tradition that acknowledges its debts in this form is harder to caricature than one that claims immaculate conception, and the acknowledgement costs nothing, since the circuit that organises the borrowings is the tradition's own contribution and remains distinctive after every debt is paid.

### What distinguishes the circuit from the sociology of literature?

The insistence on entering the text. The sociology maps production and consumption and may stop at the map; the circuit carries the map into readings of form and ideology and carries the readings back to the documented career, so that each kind of evidence corrects the others.

## Limits, Stated Plainly

The method's limits should be catalogued with the same plainness as its powers, since a guide that conceals them manufactures the disillusion it fears. The four questions are weakest where the evidence is thinnest: for works from periods whose production records perished, whose reception left no trace, and whose forms must be reconstructed from the works themselves, the circuit runs on two cylinders and the readings grow correspondingly speculative. The method is also weakest, by design, on the question readers ask most naively and feel most deeply: why this work is beautiful, or great, or worth a life's rereading. The circuit can explain a work's conditions, construction, assumptions, and career, and can show how its construction achieves effects that rivals can verify, but the step from achieved effect to value is a judgement the method informs without supplying. Eagleton's late defence of judgement, noted earlier, is the tradition's own registration of the gap. Criticism that pretends the gap does not exist will fill it with unexamined taste; criticism that names it can argue across it honestly.

A second limit concerns scale. The circuit is labour-intensive: a full application to one novel, with archives consulted and reception documented, is a monograph's work, and the method's classroom form is necessarily an abbreviation, two questions pursued with rigour and two with reconnaissance. The abbreviation is legitimate when it knows itself, and corrupting when the reconnaissance is dressed as findings. Teachers serve students best by marking the difference aloud, since the graduate who knows which of his claims are archival and which are reconnaissance has learned the method's ethics along with its questions.

A third limit is the one the objection section pressed: the interpretive questions, form and ideology, are constrained by craft norms rather than by proof, and the tradition's history contains confident readings that later evidence embarrassed. The embarrassment is recorded here without evasion because the alternative, a method that claims immunity, would forfeit the standard by which the embarrassments were recognised. The circuit's archival half exists, in part, as the interpretive half's conscience, and the conscience has spoken often enough to be trusted.

## The Verdict and Its Defeater, Restated

The article's central judgement can now be restated with its defeater in final form. Marxist literary criticism is the four-question circuit practised under the triumph of realism principle: production, form, ideology, and reception asked in order, with evidence rules appropriate to each, and with the author's beliefs demoted from key to datum throughout. The judgement would be defeated, as the objection section stated, by a settled practice, recognisably of this school, that answers none of the four questions, which would prove the list incomplete rather than mistaken. It would be weakened, short of defeat, by a practice answering only one question that the tradition itself honours as complete, and the candidates fail on inspection: ideology critique alone is honoured as a part, book history alone as a neighbour, formal analysis alone as the formalist inheritance. The convergence of the tradition's hostile internal camps on all four questions, realists and experimenters, structuralists and cultural materialists, remains the judgement's strongest support, since camps that agree on nothing else agree on the circuit's membership.

The principle's defeater should be stated with equal care. The triumph of realism would be defeated as a principle, though not as an occasional phenomenon, by evidence that a work's showing is reliably predicted by its author's beliefs across the realist corpus, so that the Balzac case shrinks to an anomaly. The evidence runs the other way in the tradition's canonical instances, the legitimist who exposed the bourgeoisie, the unionist who honoured the defeated, the progressive who described where the reactionary narrated, but the defeater is genuine: the principle is an empirical generalisation about a practice, and it stands or falls with the corpus. Stating it so is not a weakness of this article's construction. It is the construction, the habit this run of the series requires of every verdict, applied to the article's own foundation.

## Cultural Materialism and New Historicism

The distinction students most often blur, and examiners most reliably test, separates British cultural materialism from American new historicism. The two developed in parallel during the 1980s, read many of the same early modern texts, shared a suspicion of the autonomous literary work, and are shelved together in every survey. They are not the same position, and the differences are systematic rather than temperamental.

Cultural materialism, named and theorised in the work of Raymond Williams and practised notably on Renaissance drama by critics including Jonathan Dollimore and Alan Sinfield, is explicitly political. Its founding gesture is to refuse the separation between a text and the uses a later age makes of it: a Shakespeare play in a modern theatre, a modern syllabus, or a modern political argument is a cultural fact of the present as much as a relic of the past, and the criticism studies both at once. The position retains the tradition's vocabulary of material conditions and its commitment to the possibility of dissidence: subordinate cultures produce meanings that dominant institutions cannot fully contain, and the critic's work includes recovering and amplifying those meanings. Williams's own account of his position is treated in the series article on [Raymond Williams and cultural materialism](/2020/06/15/raymond-williams-cultural-materialism/), and Terry Eagleton's synthesis of the whole critical tradition, which carried these arguments to the widest student audience, is treated in the article on [Terry Eagleton's Marxist criticism](/2020/09/01/terry-eagleton-marxist-criticism/).

American new historicism, associated above all with Stephen Greenblatt and developing in parallel across the Atlantic, shares the period and many of the methods while differing in theory and in temper. It draws more heavily on Foucault, and with the Foucauldian inheritance comes a scepticism about resistance: power, on the sterner versions of the position, produces the subversion it then contains, so that the apparently dissident gesture may be part of the mechanism it seems to oppose. The famous formula of the school pairs the subversion with its containment and asks whether any residue escapes. New historicism is also less committed to a political programme. Its characteristic product is the brilliantly juxtaposed anecdote, the strange archival fragment set beside the canonical text until both change meaning, rather than the intervention in a present argument that cultural materialism aims at.

### How do cultural materialism and new historicism differ?

Cultural materialism is British, explicitly political, retains the materialist vocabulary, and affirms that subordinate meanings can resist dominant ones. New historicism is American, draws more on Foucault, doubts that subversion escapes containment, and carries no comparable political programme. The two are parallel developments, not synonyms.

The distinction matters practically because the two positions license different essays. A cultural materialist reading of a Renaissance play will ask how its meanings were contested in its period and how they are contested in ours, and will not pretend the second question is improper. A new historicist reading will ask how the play's apparent challenges to authority were produced and absorbed by authority's own machinery, and will treat the critic's desire to find resistance as itself a datum to be examined. Students who conflate the schools produce essays that assert resistance in one paragraph and containment in the next without noticing the contradiction. Students who separate them can stage the disagreement deliberately, which is what the better examination answers do, and the comparison with the formalist alternative, the other great neighbouring method, is laid out in the series article on [Marxist and formalist criticism](/2020/10/01/marxist-vs-formalist-criticism/).

Williams's vocabulary supplies cultural materialism with its analytical instruments, and two of them deserve statement here because they recur across the whole cluster of articles. The distinction among dominant, residual, and emergent cultural elements allows a period to be described as a contest rather than a monolith: the residual survives from earlier formations and may be nostalgic or oppositional; the emergent announces what no existing form can yet fully say. The concept of structures of feeling names the lived experience of a period before it sets into doctrine, the stratum of meaning that literature registers earliest because literature works in the medium of felt life. Both instruments serve the fourth question as much as the third, since they describe how meanings move through a culture across time, and both illustrate the tradition's habit of building its tools from the analysis of actual cultural process rather than importing them from a philosophy of history.

## The Strongest Objection, Stated and Answered

The strongest objection to the method deserves statement at full strength, in the form its ablest critics give it. An approach that asks four questions about conditions, form, silence, and reception can be applied to any text whatever, and it will always yield an answer, because no text lacks conditions, form, presuppositions, or readers. A method that cannot come back empty-handed is suspect: its readings, the objection runs, are produced by the method rather than found in the work, and its findings are therefore about Marxism rather than about literature. The symptomatic question sharpens the charge. A silence can be alleged anywhere, a homology drawn between anything and anything, and a critic armed with the four questions resembles a prospector whose metal detector beeps over every field because it beeps over the prospector's own boots.

The answer begins by identifying what the method can be wrong about, since a method that can be wrong can also be right. Claims under the production question are empirically checkable and have been falsified in print: attributions of a work's shape to a market that the archives show did not exist, datings that collapse a formal argument, prices and formats that contradict a claimed audience. Claims under the reception question are similarly exposed: a work's alleged effect can be refuted by its documented career, and the tradition's journals contain such refutations. Claims under the form question are constrained by the text's devices, which either do or do not presuppose what the reading alleges, and rival formal analyses contest them on shared evidence. Even the ideology question, the least constrained, is disciplined by the requirement of the seam: the expectation must be raised by the work's own project and refused with visible strain, and readings that skip the requirement are identifiable as malpractice by the tradition's own standards.

The concession must then be made without flinching. The silences question in particular is difficult to constrain, and its history includes readings that later scholarship, including scholarship inside the tradition, has judged to be projections. The honest defence is comparative and procedural rather than absolute: every interpretive method has a characteristic unconstrained move, the biographical critic's intention, the formalist's unity, the psychoanalytic critic's symbol, and the safeguard in each case is the discipline of evidence the school maintains when it is behaving well. The four questions have the advantage that two of their four are archival, so a reading built on all four stands partly on ground that rivals can inspect. The verdict of this article follows, carrying its defeater as the run's rules require. The judgement that the four questions constitute the method would be defeated by an established Marxist critical practice that answers none of them and is nonetheless recognisably Marxist, which would show the list incomplete rather than wrong. No such practice has been produced in the disputes surveyed here: the realists, the experimenters, the structuralists, and the cultural materialists all answer the four, however differently they weight them, and that convergence across hostile camps is the strongest evidence the list is the method and not a faction's platform.

### What distinguishes a reading from a verdict?

Evidence and exposure. A verdict grades the work by agreement with the critic's positions and cannot be embarrassed by the text. A reading makes claims about production, form, silence, and reception that the archive and the page can confirm or refute, and it survives by that exposure.

## Misreadings the Method Corrects

Four misreadings recur so reliably that correcting them is a service in itself. The first, that Marxist criticism looks for class conflict in plots, mistakes a possible finding for the procedure; class conflict appears in the method's results when the evidence puts it there, under any of the four questions, and a reading that begins by seeking it has rigged its own inquiry. The second, that the author's politics determine the reading, is the error the founding letter exists to refute, and its persistence in classrooms eighty years after the letter entered the tradition's canon is a standing argument for teaching the letter early. The third, that the method requires condemning the work, confuses analysis with sentencing; the tradition's most famous act of criticism is an act of praise for an opponent's novels, and its analyses of admired and deplored works proceed by the same four questions in the same order. The fourth, that Marxist criticism and cultural materialism are the same thing, collapses a century of method into one of its late regional schools; cultural materialism is a descendant that answers the four questions in a distinctive accent, not a synonym for the family.

A fifth misreading, that the realism and modernism dispute set Marxists against their opponents, has been corrected throughout this article and is worth restating in the summary because it inverts the tradition's self-understanding. The dispute was internal, and its internality is the point: a method confident enough to house its own strongest quarrel, conducted by both parties from primary texts and shared premises, is exhibiting the property the objection section demanded, exposure to refutation from evidence its own side accepts.

## Where the Method Stands

The four questions, the triumph of realism, the unresolved dispute over form, the structural and sociological variants, the contested inheritance of Bakhtin, and the transatlantic distinction between cultural materialism and new historicism compose the map this article set out to draw. The map's use is practical before it is doctrinal. A reader equipped with the questions can open an unfamiliar novel and begin work: establish the conditions, read the form's presuppositions, locate the seams where ideology strains, and follow the documented career. A reader equipped with the principle can resist the two laziest moves in criticism, the derivation of the work from the biography and the grading of the work by its agreement. A reader equipped with the dispute can see why the tradition's arguments about style are arguments about knowledge, and why they recur.

## A Micro-Exercise: One Page Under Four Questions

Abstract command of the circuit can coexist with practical helplessness before an actual page, so this article closes its teaching with a compressed exercise that a reader can perform in an hour on any chapter of any novel already on the shelf. Choose a chapter you know well enough to summarise from memory. The exercise forbids summarising. It asks four passes over the same pages, each pass restricted to a single question, with notes kept in four separate columns so that the evidence classes cannot contaminate one another before they are compared.

The first pass hunts manufacture. Ignore the story entirely and interrogate the object: the edition in your hands, its publisher and series, its price as printed or remembered, its illustrations and their captions, its notes and the interpretation they smuggle, the chapter's position in the volume and, if the novel was serialised, the instalment boundary that may explain a cliffhanger or a recapitulation. Write down only what the object and its documented history attest. The discipline of the pass is its abstinence: nothing about meaning is allowed into the column, however loudly the chapter argues for its themes.

The second pass hunts construction. Read for devices and nothing else: the focalisation and its shifts, the handling of time, the distribution of dialogue among the chapter's persons, the objects the narration pauses to describe and the objects it hurries past, the chapter's ending and the appetite it manufactures for the next. Against each device, note the assumption it requires of a reader. A narration that explains a stranger's motives without warrant assumes a world where motives are public property; a chapter that ends on an unopened letter assumes a readership trained by serials to wait. The column fills with presuppositions, and the filling is the second question performed at page scale.

The third pass hunts the unspoken. Reread the chapter asking what everyone in it agrees about without saying: the money whose source goes unmentioned, the labour whose performance goes unwitnessed, the futures considered natural for daughters and impossible for servants. Mark each candidate silence with the expectation that exposes it, the place where the chapter's own business approaches the topic and veers. A veer without an approach is not a seam, and the column should stay emptier than the student's ingenuity wishes; an honest third column with two well-attested seams outweighs a brilliant one with nine conjectures.

The fourth pass leaves the book. Reconstruct, from whatever records are reachable, the chapter's career: the reviews that singled it out or ignored it, the anthologies that excerpted the novel elsewhere, the adaptation that kept or cut this sequence, the syllabus that assigned the novel and the examination that asked about it. Where records are unreachable, the column records the unreachability itself, which is also a finding, since the silence of the archive about a work's readers is evidence about whose reading a culture thought worth preserving.

Only with four columns filled may the reader compare them, and the comparison is the exercise's yield. Manufacture will explain a device the second pass admired as daring. A seam in the third column will turn out to be an instalment boundary in the first. An effect the fourth column documents will contradict the reading the second and third columns built, and the contradiction must be reported, not resolved by deleting the inconvenient column. A reader who has performed the exercise once possesses the circuit as a habit rather than a list, and possesses something rarer: a felt knowledge of how the questions correct one another, which no summary, including this article's own, can transmit by description alone.

## Combining the Approaches: A Field Guide to the Schools

The approaches surveyed in this article are often taught as a parade of names, and the parade format guarantees that students remember the order and forget the instruments. The corrective is to sort the schools by the question each leads with, since every approach in the tradition is the circuit with one question promoted to command. The Lukácsian realism leads with form, asking what a construction presupposes about the knowability of the social whole, and subordinates the rest to that enquiry. The Brechtian practice leads with production and reception together, asking what the apparatus does to any content and how the spectator's posture can be rebuilt, and treats form as the instrument of that rebuilding. Macherey's structuralism leads with ideology, asking what the text cannot say, and rebuilds the other questions around the seam. Goldmann's genetic structuralism leads with a correspondence between form and a group's situation, which is the production question raised to the level of a class's consciousness. Bakhtin's work, whatever its classification, leads with form as social language, the novel's voices as the deposit of a society's stratification. Williams leads with reception extended into a whole way of life, the structure of feeling being the fourth question's evidence raised to a theory of periods. Cultural materialism leads with the present use of the past, a reception commitment with a political programme attached. New historicism leads with production and power in the archive, and doubts the reception question's happier findings. The sorting does not diminish any school. It makes the schools comparable, which is the precondition of judging among them, and it exhibits the verdict of this article in tabular spirit: the circuit is constant, the promotions vary, and a practice that promoted no question because it asked none would not be a member of the family at all.

The sorting also predicts each school's characteristic blindness, which is the shadow of its promotion. The form-led school under-reads archives. The ideology-led school under-reads finishes, the achieved surface where a work's pleasure lives. The reception-led school under-reads construction. The production-led school under-reads the text's resistance to its conditions, the excess that the triumph of realism names. A practitioner who knows the map can borrow against his own school's blindness deliberately, consulting the archive like a Williamsite while reading the seam like a Machereyan, and the borrowing, practised consciously, is what the tradition's best careers actually look like beneath their labels. The labels remain useful for the examination, where they must be stated cleanly and distinguished without conflation. The practice they label is a repertoire, and the repertoire, not the label, is what the student is finally being taught.

### How should the schools be sorted for comparison?

By the question each promotes to command: realism by form, Brechtian practice by apparatus and audience, Macherey by ideology, Goldmann by correspondence, Williams by lived reception, cultural materialism by present use, new historicism by archival power. The circuit is constant; the promotions vary.

## The Examination Form of the Method

Since this cluster of the series closes with an exam guide, the present article should state, once and plainly, how the method presents under examination conditions, where the candidate has an hour, a set text, and a question that conceals which of the four questions it is really asking. The first move is diagnostic: identify the question's true address. A prompt about an author's intentions is a production question wearing a biographical mask, and it is answered by relocating the intentions among the conditions and then proceeding to the showing. A prompt about a work's politics is the triumph of realism's home ground, answered by separating the author's beliefs, the text's framework, and the readers' uses, and grading none by another. A prompt comparing two schools is answered by the promotions map, stating what each leads with and what each is blind to. A prompt about a form, a genre, a device, is the second question direct, answered by presuppositions and their evidence in the text. The candidate who performs the diagnosis aloud, in the answer's opening, signals the habit the examiners reward, and the habit is not a trick: it is the method's self-consciousness, the knowledge of which question one is answering, applied at the speed the examination demands.

The defeater move, required of this article's own verdicts, is the examination's highest-value habit after the diagnosis. An answer that states what would change its judgement, a document that would overturn the production claim, a seam that would fail to appear, a reception record that would contradict the effect alleged, demonstrates that the candidate's claims are claims, exposed to evidence, rather than positions recited. Markers cannot reward a recitation beyond competence; they can and do reward exposure, because exposure is the visible sign that the candidate understands what kind of thing a critical claim is. The companion exam guide for this cluster develops the moves at length, with the attribution rule that governs the use of critics' names, and the present article's obligation is discharged by stating the principle: in this tradition, a judgement is only as good as its published conditions of surrender.

### What is the first move in answering an examination question on this material?

Diagnosis: identify which of the four questions the prompt is really asking, and say so. Prompts about intention are production questions, prompts about a work's politics belong to the triumph of realism, and prompts about form ask for presuppositions and their evidence.

## A Closing Synthesis

The article can now gather its threads into the shortest honest summary the material allows. Marxist literary criticism is a circuit of four questions asked of any work in order: the conditions of its production, the presuppositions of its form, the ideology it assumes and the silences that assumption enforces, and the documented history of its reading. Its founding principle, drawn from a letter of 1888, is that a work's showing is not determined by its author's believing, which demotes biography from key to evidence and makes the tradition's most famous act of criticism a praise of its opponents' novelist. Its central internal dispute, between the realist defence of totality and the experimental demand that representation change with its object, was conducted between Marxists and remains open because both parties were answering the second question at full strength. Its structural and sociological variants radicalised the third question and the correspondence between form and group, each with a protocol whose neglect converts the method into a parlour game. Its contested inheritance, the dialogic account of the novel, is carried with its authorship dispute in the open. Its late schools divided across the Atlantic on the question of whether subordinate meanings can resist the powers that contain them, a division students must state without conflation. Its strongest objection, that a method which always finds is a method that finds only itself, is answered by the circuit's archival half and conceded, in the silences question, to be a standing risk that discipline manages and nothing abolishes. Its verdict carries its defeater: a recognisably Marxist practice answering none of the four questions would show the list incomplete, and none has been produced.

What the summary cannot carry is the texture the article has tried to supply: the library's three volumes and the serial's cliffhanger, the counting house door at which the narrative jokes and turns away, the school abridgement manufacturing a classic's meaning for a century of pupils, the autodidact in the institute library reading the canon against its grain and the founders of this tradition in the same chair. The texture is not decoration. It is the method's evidence habit made visible, the insistence that every abstraction in the preceding pages is answerable to a document, a device, a seam, or a register somewhere in the record. A reader who finishes the article with the questions in hand and the evidence rules in force possesses the method. A reader who finishes it with the slogans, however numerous, possesses the thing the article was written to replace.

## For students and exam candidates

Hold the sequence and the principle together and most examination questions on this material become manageable. The sequence is the four questions in their order: production, form, ideology, reception, each with its evidence rule, records for the first, devices for the second, seams for the third, documents for the fourth. The principle is the triumph of realism: keep a work’s disclosures apart from its author’s convictions, and never derive the first from the second. Diagnose every prompt before answering it, since a prompt about intentions is really asking after conditions of production and a question about a work's politics belongs to the principle. Learn the realism dispute as a disagreement between two Marxists about whether the inherited realist construction still grasps a transformed reality, and learn the transatlantic distinction exactly: cultural materialism is British, political, and affirms resistance; new historicism is American, Foucauldian, and doubts that subversion escapes containment. Finish every judgement with its defeater, the evidence that would change your mind. The companion procedure for a single novel and the cluster's exam guide will drill these moves until they are reflexes.

## For teachers and seminar leaders

The triumph of realism principle is your most efficient instrument, because it corrects the commonest student error in one sentence: the work's showing is not its author's believing. Open with the 1888 letter itself, which is short enough to read aloud, and let the Balzac judgement do the work that a lecture on method cannot. Then put an unfamiliar short text in the room and run the four questions in order, enforcing the evidence rules as the seminar's constitution: no production claim without a record, no silence without a seam, no effect without a document. The failures will localise themselves under single questions, which makes them correctable in the room. Stage the realism dispute from the primary texts, both sides in their own words, and resist the casting that makes one party the hero of innovation and the other the policeman of tradition. Set the cultural materialism and new historicism distinction as a paired reading of one play, since the conflation dissolves only when students watch the two schools handle the same documents differently.

## For researchers, historians, and journalists

The circuit's archival half is your inheritance from this tradition, and its hierarchy of evidence is the part worth stealing entire: the publisher's archive above the author's memoir, the register and the annotated copy above the manifesto about reading, the edition's apparatus read as an argument rather than consulted as a window. The production and reception questions generate research programmes that neighbouring fields have adopted wholesale, from the history of the book to the sociology of the canon, and the tradition's distinct contribution is the refusal to stop at the map: carry the production findings into the reading of form, and carry the reading back to the documented career. Journalists will find the first question the most portable, since the apparatus argument, that machinery digests content, applies to every medium that carries literature, including the ones carrying this article. Researchers should note the defeater discipline as a publication norm worth importing: a claim about a text's conditions or career that states what would overturn it is a claim the field can build on.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is Marxist literary criticism?

It is a way of reading that refuses to begin with an author's opinions. Instead it pursues four lines of enquiry in a fixed order: the circumstances in which a book was manufactured and sold, the assumptions built into its chosen form, the beliefs it treats as too obvious to state together with the points where it falls silent, and the documented story of its readers. A private letter of 1888 supplies its governing rule, because Engels valued a legitimist novelist's fiction above that of his own allies. Schools that pursue only one line of enquiry are practising neighbouring disciplines, however they label themselves.

### Q: What four questions does Marxist criticism ask?

First, the circumstances of manufacture: publisher, market, price, format, censorship, and the readership those facts made reachable. Second, the burden of the form: what a genre, a narrative voice, or a plot structure quietly assumes about the world it addresses. Third, the unspoken framework: which beliefs the narrative treats as furniture, and at which seams it strains or falls silent. Fourth, the career among readers: editions, reviews, syllabuses, adaptations, and attested effects. Sequence matters, because archival findings about manufacture limit what may be claimed for form, and both discipline the hunt for silences before reception evidence tests the finished reading.

### Q: What was the Brecht and Lukacs debate about?

Two Marxists disagreed about whether the inherited realist novel could still render a transformed world truthfully. For Lukács, the classic realist construction remained the one form able to bind private destinies to the large movements of history, while the modernist repertoire merely mirrored a fragmented experience back at itself. For Brecht, writing in the exile exchange of 1938, fidelity demanded new devices for new conditions: montage, direct address, and a spectator kept awake to judge rather than invited to empathise. Because both men argued from a shared materialist inheritance, the quarrel is best understood as the tradition disputing with itself about the second of its four questions.

### Q: What is symptomatic reading of literature?

It is the practice, given systematic form by Pierre Macherey in 1966, of treating a narrative's incoherences as evidence. A book labours upon the ideology available to it the way a craftsman labours upon timber, and the grain resists: episodes trail off, questions raised by the plot go unanswered, registers shift without warning. The reader hunts those seams, asking what the book's own undertaking demanded that its framework forbade it to deliver. Discipline separates the practice from free association, since a mere absence proves nothing; the expectation must be one the narrative itself created, and rival explanations drawn from censorship, genre, or the market must be eliminated first.

### Q: What is the triumph of realism?

The phrase comes from Engels's 1888 letter to Margaret Harkness and names a victory of rendering over allegiance. Balzac longed for the old aristocracy and despised the rising bourgeoisie, yet his novels chart the first group's inevitable decline and the second group's ascent with unmatched fidelity, because the discipline of his craft compelled honesty his sympathies would have refused. Engels treated that fidelity as a triumph and drew the methodological moral: a book's disclosures cannot be deduced from its writer's convictions. Hence the school's founding refusal to grade literature by biography, and its insistence that even an opponent's fiction may teach more than an ally's.

### Q: Why did Lukacs defend realism against modernism?

His defence rested on a claim about knowledge. In the great realist novel, he argued, character is built so that a private fate becomes legible only through the historical forces converging in it, and the reader consequently perceives society as an interconnected process rather than a backdrop. Modernist technique, as he read it, abandons that ambition: it stations the reader inside a consciousness to which the social world appears opaque, and presents the opacity as truth rather than as a condition to be explained. Whether or not the verdict on particular modernists stands, the argument's terms deserve attention, since both sides agreed that literary form is a mode of cognition and fought over which forms know best.

### Q: What is genetic structuralism?

Lucien Goldmann's procedure explains a literary construction by matching its pattern against the pattern of thinking characteristic of a social group. His exemplary study aligned the tragic architecture of Pascal and Racine, organised around a deity who is present only through absence, with the predicament of the French noblesse de robe, a judicial stratum being dismantled by the monarchy it served. The match claimed is between structures, never between a book's statements and a group's slogans. Goldmann's own safeguard is the part students should memorise: the group's circumstances must be documented from sources owing nothing to the literary work, and the work's pattern established independently, before any correspondence is announced.

### Q: Is Bakhtin a Marxist critic?

Scholars dispute the label for two separate reasons. The concepts attached to his name, the novel as an orchestration of contending social voices, language stratified by class and occasion, carnival as licensed inversion, are staples of Marxist criticism, yet the writings themselves announce no allegiance to the school's framework and have been happily adopted by critics who reject that framework entirely. Complicating everything, the authorship of several volumes issued under his associates' names, Voloshinov and Medvedev among them, has been credited to Bakhtin wholly or partly, and specialists still argue the attribution. A careful essay therefore borrows the concepts, credits the volumes that contain them, and flags the quarrel.

### Q: What is the difference between cultural materialism and new historicism?

The two schools grew up in the same decade on opposite shores of the Atlantic and are constantly confused. The British variant, descending from Raymond Williams and applied to Renaissance drama by Jonathan Dollimore and Alan Sinfield, declares its political commitments openly, keeps the vocabulary of material conditions, and maintains that meanings produced from below can genuinely oppose dominant ones. The American variant, associated with Stephen Greenblatt, borrows its theory of power largely from Foucault and suspects that apparent subversion is usually manufactured and absorbed by the authority it seems to menace; it advances no programme of its own. One studies a play alongside its present uses, the other alongside the machinery that contained it.

### Q: Where should you start with Marxist literary criticism?

Begin with the founding document rather than with any survey: Engels's letter to Margaret Harkness of 1888, brief enough to read in one sitting, which defines realism through typicality, advises that an author's opinions sit lightly in the finished work, and demonstrates the school's central principle on Balzac. Follow it with a single extended performance of the method, a Lukács essay, Macherey on Verne, or one of Williams's readings, so that the evidence habits become visible in action. Only then take up the four questions on a novel you know well, working in sequence. Surveys taken first tend to leave students with the caricature this article exists to displace.

### Q: How does the triumph of realism principle grade a work against its author's beliefs?

It removes beliefs from the grading altogether and marks the rendering instead. A novelist whose allegiances the examiner shares can still fail, if the construction is thesis-driven and the social relations are falsified to fit the lesson; a novelist whose allegiances the examiner deplores can still excel, if the craft has honestly registered how the depicted world moves. The yardstick is typicality: whether characters and circumstances are built so that the pressures of an age show inside private destinies. Convictions survive in the assessment only as circumstances of manufacture, one fact about the workshop among others, useful for explaining pressures on the work and useless for predicting its worth.

### Q: What should a student separate between what a work shows and what its author intended?

Keep three files apart. The first holds designs: letters, drafts, and declarations that reveal what the writer meant to do. The second holds performance: the construction actually achieved, its formal assumptions, its seams and silences, which may betray or surpass the design. The third holds consequences: the attested career among readers, editors, censors, and adapters, which no designer controls. Examination answers collapse when the files are merged, because an intention is then allowed to certify a performance, or a reputation to substitute for an analysis. Filed separately, each body of evidence can correct the others, and the resulting answer demonstrates the habit on which this whole approach depends.

### Q: Why did Engels praise a novelist whose politics he opposed?

Because the fiction functioned for him as a superior archive. Surveying the France of the Restoration and the July Monarchy, he judged that he had gathered a fuller understanding of its property relations, its classes, its provincial machinery, and its Parisian appetites from Balzac's pages than from the era's professional historians and economists combined. The novelist's legitimist mourning for a vanishing aristocracy made the testimony stronger, not weaker: a witness testifying against his own preference is the most credible kind. Engels's admiration was therefore methodological. It announced that honest rendering outranks correct opinion as a source of social knowledge, and the school built its charter on the announcement.

### Q: How does the four questions table order the method?

The rows descend from the hardest evidence to the most contestable. Manufacture comes first because contracts, prices, formats, and licensing files can be inspected by any rival and because they bound everything downstream. Form follows, since the menu of available shapes must be known before a chosen shape's assumptions are interpreted. The enquiry into unspoken beliefs is placed third on purpose, delayed until the censor, the market, and the genre have each been given the chance to account for a silence. Reception closes the sequence as a court of appeal, checking the assembled reading against editions, reviews, and syllabuses. Taught in that order, the table is a procedure; taught in any other, it degenerates into a checklist.

### Q: What separates a Marxist reading from a political verdict on a book?

Vulnerability to refutation. A verdict announces agreement or disagreement with a book's tendency and rests there; nothing discovered later, in an archive or on the page, can oblige it to move. A reading stakes claims that can lose. Its account of manufacture can be falsified by a contract, its account of form by a device it overlooked, its alleged silence by a passage it skipped, its alleged influence by a circulation record. Engels's treatment of Balzac remains the emblem, since a verdict on the novelist's legitimism would have produced condemnation, whereas the reading produced the school's founding insight.
