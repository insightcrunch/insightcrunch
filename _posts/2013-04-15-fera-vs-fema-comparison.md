The FERA vs FEMA comparison is usually forced on a reader by a practical difficulty rather than by curiosity. A family discovers that a parent who worked abroad for thirty years left accounts and a small flat in the country of employment, and the family needs to know what the law makes of its silence. A finance head finds an old remittance that travelled outside the authorised channel and needs to know whether the company faces a prosecution or a penalty notice. A student preparing for an examination meets two confident sentences in two different books, one saying that India liberalised foreign exchange in 2000 and the other saying that it merely relabelled the controls, and needs to know which sentence to write. Each of them has run into the same unresolved argument, and the argument has lasted because both sides of it are describing something real. This article settles it in the only way it can be settled: dimension by dimension, provision by provision, against the texts of the Foreign Exchange Regulation Act 1973 and the Foreign Exchange Management Act 1999, with every change classified and the count allowed to deliver the verdict.

The liberalisation account runs as follows. In 2000 India abolished a criminal code under which a person who broke the exchange rules could be imprisoned for years, replaced it with a civil statute under which the same act attracts a money penalty, freed the ordinary transactions of life, travel, education, medical care, family maintenance, from a regime of prior permission, and closed down the old prosecutions with a statutory deadline. On that account the change was one of the genuine emancipations of Indian economic law. The relabelling account runs in the opposite direction. The state, it says, surrendered nothing it valued: dealings in foreign exchange must still pass through an authorised channel, a resident still may not hold assets abroad except as permitted, capital movements across the border remain as controlled as they ever were, the operative rules sit in regulations that the Reserve Bank rewrites at will, and the enforcing agency is the same directorate with the same habits, later rearmed with a power to seize Indian assets against foreign holdings. On that account the change altered the vocabulary of control and little else.

![FERA vs FEMA comparison, the shift from a criminal foreign exchange regime to a civil one in India - Insight Crunch](/assets/images/blog/blog-82.webp)

Both accounts fail as complete descriptions, and they fail in a pattern that gives the game away. The liberalisation account is accurate about everything that concerns the consequence of breaking the rules and vague about everything that concerns the extent of the rules. The relabelling account is accurate about the extent of the rules and silent about the consequence. The deciding factor in the whole comparison is therefore a choice of measurement: whether a legal regime is characterised by what it forbids or by what it does to the person who forbids himself against it. Measured by restrictions, the change of 2000 was modest, in places purely formal, and in one large territory, the capital account, no change at all. Measured by consequences, it was total: the same act that had been a crime carrying a presumption against the accused became a civil default carrying a price. The verdict defended through this article is that India liberalised its enforcement rather than its exchange controls, that decriminalisation is the correct name for what happened and deregulation is the incorrect one, and that a person who reads the change as permission misreads it in the one direction that creates liability. The narrative of how the replacement was reached, the crisis of 1991, the drafting, the passage, belongs to [the account of the FERA to FEMA transition](/2013/02/01/fera-to-fema-transition/), which owns that story; what belongs here is the head-to-head measurement of the two regimes and the practical consequence that falls out of it.

## Two statutes, two economic worlds

A comparison is only as honest as its statement of what each side was built to do, and the two statutes were built for different countries. The Foreign Exchange Regulation Act 1973, Act 46 of 1973, passed in September 1973 and brought into force on 1 January 1974, was written for an economy in which foreign exchange was a rationed national asset. Its long title announced the conservation of the foreign exchange resources of the country and their proper utilisation in the interests of economic development, and its scheme matched the title: dealings in foreign exchange were lawful only through authorised persons and only as permitted, payments to persons outside India were restricted, exporters were bound to realise their proceeds and bring them home, residents were forbidden to acquire or hold immovable property abroad without permission, and the Reserve Bank's permission structure reached into corporate ownership itself, directing foreign-controlled companies to dilute their shareholding, a direction that drove IBM and Coca-Cola out of India in 1977 when they refused it. The context was a thin reserve, food and oil imports financed from scarcity, and, within weeks of the Act's commencement, the first oil shock, which converted a chronic shortage into a standing emergency. A statute drafted for conservation found its decade ready-made.

The Foreign Exchange Management Act 1999, Act 42 of 1999, was written for the country that the crisis of 1991 created. In that year India's reserves fell to a level covering a few weeks of imports, gold was pledged abroad for emergency credit, and the government of P. V. Narasimha Rao, with Manmohan Singh as Finance Minister, began dismantling the licensing structure of which exchange control had been the external wall. In August 1994 India accepted the obligations of Article VIII of the Articles of Agreement of the International Monetary Fund, undertaking not to restrict payments for current international transactions, a commitment that sat awkwardly beside a domestic statute whose grammar was prohibition. The old Act was first softened from inside, by the Foreign Exchange Regulation (Amendment) Act 1993, Act 29 of 1993, brought into force on 8 January 1993, which cut back restrictions across the text without touching its criminal character. A replacement Bill followed in 1998, was examined by the Standing Committee on Finance, and was enacted in December 1999, receiving the assent of the President on 29 December 1999 and commencing on 1 June 2000. Its long title reverses its predecessor word by word: an Act to consolidate and amend the law relating to foreign exchange with the objective of facilitating external trade and payments and for promoting the orderly development and maintenance of the foreign exchange market in India. Conservation has become facilitation, and a ration has become a market.

Between the two enactments sits the event that explains the successor's deepest design choice. The Asian financial crisis of 1997 damaged economies that had opened their capital accounts faster than their banking systems could bear, and India's relative escape was widely credited, by the Reserve Bank and by outside observers, partly to the controls that reform had marked for demolition. The committee chaired by S. S. Tarapore, which reported in 1997 on a phased move towards capital account convertibility, had itself made the opening conditional on inflation, the fiscal deficit, the soundness of banks and the adequacy of reserves. The drafters of FEMA therefore kept the capital gate with their eyes open while removing the prison, and any comparison that treats the surviving controls as leftover residue misunderstands the document. The replacement was also built differently as an instrument. FERA aspired to be a code that contained its subject. FEMA is a skeleton: its operative content lives in rules made by the Central Government and regulations made by the Reserve Bank under sections 46 and 47 of the Act, notified in time for the commencement date and amended ever since at administrative speed. That formal difference, a code against a framework, recurs through every dimension of the comparison that follows, because it decides where a citizen must look for the law and how quickly the law can move.

## FERA vs FEMA on the eight dimensions

The comparison is organised around the eight dimensions that decide a person's practical position under an exchange regime: the scheme of the statute and its default rule, the nature of liability, the burden and standard of proof, the sanction, the forum, the reach of the statute over the person, the treatment of capital account transactions, and the enforcement powers. The table states the contrast in one view and classifies each change as substantive liberalisation, a formal change with the same practical effect, or continuation. The classification is the argument, and the prose that follows the table defends each entry in turn, because a table can assert what only analysis can earn.

| Dimension | FERA 1973 | FEMA 1999 | Classification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scheme and default rule | Prohibitory: a dealing in foreign exchange was unlawful unless permitted | Current account dealings permitted unless restricted; capital account dealings permitted within specified classes and limits | Substantive liberalisation |
| Nature of liability | Criminal offence, with a parallel departmental penalty available for the same act | Civil contravention attracting a penalty | Substantive liberalisation |
| Burden and standard of proof | Proof beyond reasonable doubt, with a statutory presumption of culpable mental state that the accused had to displace to the same standard | Preponderance of probabilities before an Adjudicating Authority, with no presumption of a guilty mind | Substantive liberalisation |
| Sanction | Imprisonment of six months to seven years for offences involving more than one lakh rupees, alongside an adjudication penalty of up to five times the sum involved | Penalty of up to three times the sum involved; imprisonment only as civil imprisonment for default in paying the penalty | Substantive liberalisation |
| Forum and appeals | A criminal court for prosecution; departmental adjudication with an appeal to the Appellate Board | Adjudicating Authority, appeal to the Special Director (Appeals), then to the Appellate Tribunal, then to the High Court on a question of law | Substantive liberalisation |
| Reach over the person | Residents, and Indian citizens wherever they lived, together with the branches abroad of Indian companies | Persons resident in India, tested by presence and intention, together with the establishments abroad of residents | Substantive liberalisation |
| Capital account transactions | Controlled case by case through the permission structure | Controlled through classes of permissible transactions and limits set by regulation | Continuation |
| Enforcement powers | Summons, search, seizure and arrest in the hands of the Directorate of Enforcement | Summons, search and seizure retained; arrest removed; seizure of Indian assets of equivalent value added in 2015 | Formal change, same practical effect |

The count in the final column is the verdict in miniature, and it should be read before the detail: six of the eight dimensions record substantive liberalisation, one records a formal change laid over a continuing enforcement toolkit, and one, the largest single territory of control, records outright continuation. The pattern is not random. Every dimension that concerns what the state does to a person who breaks the rules moved decisively. Every dimension that concerns which transactions the state reserves to its control stayed where it was. The two halves of the table are the two accounts of the introduction, each vindicated on its own ground and each refuted as a description of the whole.

Begin with the scheme, because it is the dimension on which the relabelling account scores its most quoted point and on which it is also most subtly wrong. FERA's prohibitions were general and its permissions were exceptions, so the legal question in any transaction was whether a permission existed that covered it, and silence in the rulebook meant prohibition. FEMA reverses the default for one class of transactions and keeps the old posture for the other. Current account transactions, defined in section 2(j) to cover payments due in the ordinary course of trade and business, interest and income from investment, remittances for the living expenses of family abroad, and expenditure on travel, education and medical care, are set free by section 5: a person may sell or draw foreign exchange from an authorised person for such a transaction, subject to reasonable restrictions imposed by the Central Government in the public interest in consultation with the Reserve Bank. Capital account transactions, defined in section 2(e) as transactions that alter the assets or liabilities, including contingent liabilities, of residents outside India or of non-residents in India, remain under section 6, where the Reserve Bank, in consultation with the Central Government, specifies the classes that are permissible and the limits of each. The reversal of the default is a substantive change in daily life, felt at every bank counter where a student's fees or a patient's treatment abroad is released against documents rather than licensed against discretion. It is nonetheless a reversal confined to the current account, and the honest classification of the scheme row depends on holding both halves of that sentence together: the default changed, the boundary of the controlled territory did not.

The liability row needs no such qualification. Under FERA the same act could be, and routinely was, pursued twice: departmental adjudication under sections 50 and 51, where a penalty of up to five times the amount or value involved, or five thousand rupees, whichever was greater, could be imposed, and criminal prosecution under section 56, which opened by declaring itself without prejudice to the penalty, so that the state's two proceedings were cumulative rather than alternative. Under FEMA there is one proceeding for the generality of conduct, an adjudication under section 13, and the vocabulary of the statute has been rebuilt to match: contravention for offence, noticee for accused, penalty for sentence. A change that abolishes a parallel criminal track is not a relabelling by any test that words can bear, and the dimensions that follow, burden, sanction and forum, are best understood as the working out of this single decision through the machinery of a case.

## The burden and the standard: where a case is actually decided

Practitioners who worked under both statutes locate the real transition neither in the preamble nor in the penalty clause but in the hearing room, and the dimension they name is the burden of proof. Under FEMA, a contravention is established before the Adjudicating Authority on the preponderance of probabilities, the civil standard: the authority weighs the department's account of the transaction against the noticee's, on the material each side has produced, and asks which is more probable. The Supreme Court's general adjudication jurisprudence, developed in the customs context in Collector of Customs v D. Bhoormull, had long treated departmental adjudication as a proceeding of this civil character, in which the strict rules of criminal evidence do not apply and the deciding authority acts on the probabilities the record discloses. FEMA aligned exchange law with that model expressly and completely. There is no charge to frame, no plea to take, and no presumption waiting at the centre of the case for the noticee to displace.

Under FERA the formal standard was the criminal one, proof beyond reasonable doubt, but the machinery arranged around it was anything except neutral. Section 59 of the Act directed the court, in any prosecution for an offence requiring a culpable mental state, to presume that the accused had that mental state, and cast on the accused the burden of proving its absence. The section then fixed the measure of that proof in terms a defence lawyer of the era could recite from memory: a fact was to be treated as proved only when the court believed it to exist beyond reasonable doubt, and not merely on a preponderance of probability. The resulting inversion is the sharpest single contrast in the whole comparison. The prosecution enjoyed a presumption on the very question, intention, that ordinarily decides a criminal case, while the accused, in displacing it, was held to the standard ordinarily demanded of the prosecution itself. Section 59 travelled, with the necessary changes, into adjudication proceedings as well, so the tilt operated on both of the old statute's tracks, and a person proceeded against under FERA defended uphill in whichever forum the state chose.

The evidentiary texture of the two proceedings differs as much as the formal standard. A FERA trial was characteristically built on statements recorded by enforcement officers from persons summoned before them, on the retractions of those statements, and on argument about the circumstances in which each was made. The Supreme Court examined the status of a person summoned to give evidence under the Act in K.T.M.S. Mohd. v Union of India, and in Vinod Solanki v Union of India it held that a retracted confessional statement could not, without corroboration, carry the weight the prosecution asked it to bear. A FEMA adjudication is built on documents: bank records, the authorised dealer's reports, the noticee's own filings, invoices, contracts and correspondence. The noticee who cannot produce the paper trail of a transaction carries a practical disadvantage that functions as an evidential burden without being named one, because an authority deciding on probabilities will count an unexplained gap against the side that should have filled it. Practitioners describe the consequence as a change in the discipline of defence, from a jurisprudence of contested statements to a jurisprudence of records, and the comparison article that ignores it has measured the statutes and missed the proceedings.

One refinement completes the dimension, because it corrects a misunderstanding that the civil label invites. A penalty under section 13 is a civil liability, and the Supreme Court's jurisprudence on regulatory penalties, stated for the securities laws in Securities and Exchange Board of India v Shriram Mutual Fund, treats such a penalty as a civil obligation for breach of a civil duty, for which a guilty mind is not an essential ingredient in the way it is for a crime. FEMA adjudication has worked on the same understanding: the department must establish the contravention, the doing of the prohibited act or the omission of the required one, and the noticee's state of mind becomes material chiefly to the quantum of penalty, where good faith, voluntary disclosure and cooperation do real work, rather than to liability itself. The reversal from FERA is complete and mildly paradoxical. The criminal statute made intention the centre of the case and then presumed it against the citizen. The civil statute makes intention peripheral to liability and lets it dominate the argument about price. This is also the dimension on which the answer to which statute is stricter reverses depending on the question asked: FERA was stricter in what it presumed, FEMA is stricter in what it does not require the state to prove, and a careful comparison keeps both truths in view rather than trading one for the other.

## The sanction: from a prison term to a price

The sanction row of the table compresses the most visible change of 2000, and it rewards slow reading because its edges are where the folklore goes wrong. Under FERA, section 56 graded the punishment by the size of the offence. For an offence involving an amount or value exceeding one lakh rupees, conviction carried imprisonment for a term of not less than six months and extending to seven years, together with fine. For other offences the term could extend to three years, or the court could impose fine, or both. Alongside the prosecution, and cumulative with it, the adjudication penalty under sections 50 and 51 could reach five times the amount or value involved in the contravention. A separate offence under section 57 punished failure to pay a penalty or to obey an order with imprisonment extending to two years. Corporate accused received no shelter from the mandatory terms: in Standard Chartered Bank v Directorate of Enforcement, decided in 2005 on prosecutions launched in the FERA years, the Supreme Court held that a company could be prosecuted for an offence carrying a mandatory term of imprisonment, with fine imposed in place of the imprisonment a company cannot serve.

Under FEMA, section 13 fixes the penalty on adjudication at up to three times the sum involved in the contravention where that sum is quantifiable, up to two lakh rupees where it is not, and a further penalty of up to five thousand rupees for every day during which a continuing contravention persists after the first. The multiple fell, from five to three, a small numerical confession that the old figure had been set by the deterrent logic of criminal law rather than by the logic of a regulatory charge. The adjudicating authority may also direct confiscation of the currency, security or property in respect of which the contravention has taken place, and may direct that foreign exchange holdings be brought back into India. Imprisonment survives in exactly one general place, and it arrives indirectly: under section 14, a person who fails to pay the penalty in full within ninety days of being called upon to do so is liable to civil imprisonment, the coercive process used against a defaulting debtor, not the punishment of an offender. The prison was not abolished from the statute book. It was moved, from the contravention itself to the refusal to pay for the contravention, and that relocation is the precise measure of the sanction change.

Two counterweights keep the classification honest, and both belong in any comparison that a practitioner would recognise. The first is that a FEMA penalty can be heavier in money than the fines criminal courts habitually imposed under FERA. Three times a large sum is a very large sum, and the confiscation and repatriation directions add a proprietary consequence that a fine does not capture. The civil regime is gentler in kind and capable of being heavier in amount, and the tribunal and High Court jurisprudence that reads the section 13 ceilings as maxima for the worst cases rather than tariffs for all cases, pressing the figure down for voluntary disclosure, cooperation and absence of loss, exists precisely because the unmediated text would otherwise punish paperwork defaults at the scale of frauds. The second counterweight is compounding, introduced by section 15, under which most contraventions may be settled by payment of a sum to the compounding authority, closing the matter without adjudication at all. FERA had no equivalent statutory route; settlement under the old regime was an exercise of discretion, not a procedure with published terms. Compounding converts the sanction from a lottery between prosecution and impunity into a priced exit for the generality of defaults, and its heavy use, by multinational companies regularising reporting delays and by individuals regularising inherited accounts alike, is the standing evidence of the gap between the system's strictness on paper and the innocence of most of the conduct it catches. A comparison that counts only the maxima will call FEMA milder in this dimension and be right; a comparison that counts the machinery of exit will call it milder still, and be righter.

One feature of the old two-track system is usually mistold, and correcting it sharpens rather than softens the contrast. In the working life of FERA, most matters never reached a criminal court. The adjudication track absorbed the bulk of the caseload, and prosecution was a selection, made by the directorate, from the files it judged serious, symbolic or winnable. Defenders of the old regime cite the pattern as evidence of its moderation; the prosecution, they say, was the exception and the penalty the rule, so that FEMA merely generalised the track that already did the work. The observation is accurate and the inference is wrong, because a track's practical weight is set not by how often it is used but by the fact that it can be. Every adjudication under FERA was conducted in the shadow of a prosecution that the same act could attract without prejudice to the penalty, with a presumption waiting inside it and a prison term at its end, and the shadow priced every settlement, silenced every negotiation and followed every noticee who considered whether to contest the department's account of his own transaction. FEMA did not invent the civil proceeding. It abolished the shadow, and the abolition is the transition's operative event, from which the burden, sanction and forum contrasts all descend. A comparison that weighs only the proceedings each statute ran will call the change modest. A comparison that weighs the proceedings each statute threatened will call it what it was.

## Compounding: the exit only one statute built

Compounding deserves a section of its own in any honest comparison, because it is the dimension on which the two statutes differ not in degree but in kind, and because it is the route by which the largest number of real cases under the new regime actually end. Section 15 of FEMA empowers the compounding authority, on an application by the person who has contravened, to compound the contravention, which is to say to close the matter on payment of a sum determined under the Foreign Exchange (Compounding Proceedings) Rules, without an adjudication, without a finding paraded through the appeal ladder, and without the contravention ripening into anything larger. The design divides the work by subject. The Reserve Bank compounds the generality of contraventions, those arising under the investment, borrowing, deposit, property and reporting regulations that constitute the working law. The Directorate of Enforcement compounds the single class reserved to it, contraventions of section 3(a), the dealing in foreign exchange otherwise than through an authorised person, which is the statutory description of the hawala transaction. Within each authority the file travels to an officer of a seniority graded to the amount involved, the amount must be quantifiable before compounding is available at all, and the rules refuse a second compounding of a similar contravention within three years of the first, so that the exit is priced as a settlement for the inadvertent and the first-time defaulter rather than as a subscription for the repeat player. The rules themselves have been recast, most comprehensively in 2024, to widen the monetary limits of the compounding officers and to digitise the process, a history of administrative revision that illustrates in miniature the framework character of the whole statute.

FERA contained no such mechanism, and the absence is diagnostic. A criminal code can offer prosecutorial discretion, and the directorate under FERA exercised plenty of it, deciding which files ripened into complaints and which rested in the adjudication track. But discretion is not a procedure: it has no published terms, no entitlement to apply, no graded scale of officers, no duty to decide within a structure the citizen can inspect in advance. The FERA subject who wanted out of the machine petitioned for grace. The FEMA subject applies for a settlement as a matter of course, on a form, against criteria, with the compounding amount computed by reference to the sum involved, the duration of the default, the gain made or loss avoided and the conduct of the applicant, under a published matrix of guidance that the Reserve Bank maintains for the purpose. The difference is the difference between a pardon system and a settlement system, and it is the institutional signature of the change from crime to default. A regime that expects most of its subjects to be careless rather than wicked builds a priced door for carelessness. A regime that presumes wickedness builds a courtroom.

Compounding also reframes the sanction comparison, because it means that the penalty maxima of section 13 are, for the generality of cases, opening positions in a negotiation the statute itself structures, rather than outcomes. The multinational that discovers a decade of late filings on its overseas subsidiary, the family that discovers the inherited account, the exporter whose proceeds arrived by the wrong route: each faces, in the first instance, not three times the sum involved but an application, a computation and a closure, with adjudication waiting only for those who decline the exit or fall outside its terms. The relabelling account has no answer to compounding, because a relabelled regime would have had no reason to invent it; the liberalisation account understates it, because compounding is not a freedom but a piece of machinery, valuable precisely because the controls it settles breaches of remain in force. It belongs, in the classification of this article, to the consequence layer, and it is the consequence layer's most-used component.

## The forum: from the magistrate's court to the adjudicating authority

Forum is the dimension in which the change of regime is most easily felt and most easily misdescribed, because both statutes offered a departmental proceeding and the difference lies in what stood beside it. Under FERA a person faced two forums in sequence or in parallel: the adjudicating officer within the enforcement directorate, whose penalty could be appealed to the Foreign Exchange Regulation Appellate Board and onward to the High Court on a question of law, and the criminal court, where the prosecution ran with the full apparatus of a trial, charge, evidence, cross-examination, and the possibility of acquittal or imprisonment at the end of years of hearings. The criminal forum dominated the experience even when it was never used, because every negotiation with the department took place in its shadow.

Under FEMA the ladder is single and administrative. An Adjudicating Authority appointed under section 16 holds the inquiry under section 13. An appeal lies to the Special Director (Appeals), a second departmental tier. A further appeal lies to the Appellate Tribunal for Foreign Exchange, and from the tribunal an appeal lies to the High Court under section 35 on a question of law. The design trades the trial's armour for speed, specialisation and privacy, and the trade is genuinely double-edged. The citizen loses the magistrate's independence at the first stage, since the adjudicating authority is an officer of the enforcing establishment's own world, and must rely on the tribunal ladder and the High Court to supply judicial supervision. The citizen gains a proceeding that ends, a penalty that if set aside on appeal is refundable in a way that years of a criminal trial are not, and a record that carries no conviction. The classification of the forum row as substantive liberalisation rests on that balance of losses and gains, and on the observable fact that the first-stage authority, deciding most cases in practice, works under appellate doctrines of hearing, reasons and proportion that the criminal process never needed because its safeguards were built into the trial itself.

### Is there a time limit for starting a FEMA adjudication?

FEMA prescribes no limitation period for commencing adjudication proceedings, a silence that contrasts with the two-year deadline the same Act imposed on proceedings under the repealed statute. Notices have been issued many years after the transactions they examine, and the appellate courts have disciplined stale proceedings case by case, testing the delay and the prejudice it causes the noticee.

The absence recorded in that answer is the forum dimension's unfinished business, and it belongs in the comparison because it shows the legislature's discipline applied asymmetrically: the old regime's proceedings were given a fuse, examined in the sunset section below, while the new regime's proceedings were given none. In practice the authorised dealer's records, on which adjudication depends, thin with time, the personnel who handled a transaction scatter, and the noticee's ability to reconstruct a decade-old remittance is not what it was. The courts' case-by-case correction is workable and untidy in equal measure. For the comparison, the point is classification discipline: a defect in the new regime's design is not evidence that the old regime was better, only that the liberalisation of the forum, though substantive, was drafted less carefully than the liberalisation of liability.

## Adjudication in practice: the doctrines the new forum built

The forum comparison would be incomplete if it stopped at the ladder of appeals, because a forum is known by the doctrines it generates, and the FEMA forum has spent its life generating a doctrine of proportion that the statutory text only implies. The raw material is the structure of section 13: a ceiling of three times the sum involved, expressed as a maximum, with no floor, no tariff and no guidance in the Act about where within the range a given contravention should land. Left unmediated, that structure would punish the reporting delay and the deliberate hawala run by the same arithmetic. The Appellate Tribunal and the High Courts, hearing the appeals the ladder provides, have mediated it in a consistent direction: the ceiling is for the worst cases, the quantum must be reasoned against the gravity of the contravention, the gain made or loss avoided by the contravener, the loss caused to the exchequer, and the conduct of the noticee, and voluntary disclosure, cooperation and the absence of loss press the figure downward, in some lines of cases to a token. None of this is in the Act. All of it is in the working law, and a comparison written from the two bare texts will understate the new regime's moderation exactly as it understates the old regime's, whose formal maxima were likewise mediated in practice, by prosecutorial selection and by the courts' insistence, in cases such as Vinod Solanki, on discipline within the presumption-laden machinery.

Two further doctrines mark the forum's character. The first is the hearing doctrine: because the first stage sits inside the enforcing establishment, the appellate courts have required of adjudicating authorities the visible apparatus of fairness, notice that discloses the case to be met, a hearing genuinely granted where facts are contested, and a reasoned order that engages with the noticee's documents rather than reciting the department's. Orders that fail the requirement are set aside and remanded, and the remand rate is itself a datum in the comparison, the price the design pays for its speed. The second is the documentary doctrine, the working rule, described in the burden section, that the case is won in the file. Together the two doctrines describe a forum whose fairness is achieved procedurally and retrospectively, through appeal and remand, where the criminal forum's fairness was achieved structurally and in advance, through the trial. Whether that exchange serves the small noticee, who cannot fund an appeal, as well as it serves the corporate one, who can, is the standing criticism of the design, and the honest comparison records it as the forum row's unresolved debit. It does not reverse the classification. It qualifies the classification's beneficiary, which is a different and more useful thing to know.

## The corporate accused: companies and the people who run them

A comparison conducted only at the level of the individual misses the population against which exchange law is most often deployed, the company and the officers who sign for it, and on this population the two statutes converge in structure while diverging completely in consequence. FERA's section 68 provided that where an offence had been committed by a company, every person who was in charge of and responsible to the company for the conduct of its business at the time, as well as the company itself, was deemed guilty of the offence, unless that person proved the offence was committed without his knowledge or that he had exercised all due diligence to prevent it. FEMA's section 42 reproduces the architecture almost exactly for contraventions: the company and every person in charge of and responsible for the conduct of its business at the time are deemed liable to the penalty, with the same defence of absence of knowledge or of all due diligence, and with liability extended to directors, managers, secretaries and other officers where the contravention occurred with their consent or connivance or is attributable to their neglect. The attribution rule survived the repeal intact, because the problem it solves, the diffusion of a decision across a boardroom until no one has decided it, is a problem of corporate organisation and not of penal policy.

What changed is the currency in which the attributed liability is paid. Under FERA the officer in charge faced prosecution, and the company faced a prosecution it could not answer with the usual plea that a corporation cannot be imprisoned, a plea the Supreme Court disposed of in Standard Chartered Bank v Directorate of Enforcement by holding the company prosecutable for an offence carrying mandatory imprisonment, with fine substituted for the term it could not serve. Under FEMA the same officer faces a penalty, assessed on the same deemed-liability rule, escapable on the same proof of diligence, and the company faces the same exposure in money. Boardroom behaviour has adjusted to the new currency without relaxing: FEMA compliance sits on audit committee agendas, exchange filings are made with a diligence that the possibility of personal penalty explains, and the due diligence defence is prepared in advance, in minutes and compliance calendars, by officers who understand that the defence must be proved with records rather than asserted with indignation. The comparison here is a useful corrective to the crude version of the liberalisation thesis. Decriminalisation did not de-responsibilise anyone. It changed what responsible persons risk, from liberty to money and reputation, and for a class of defendants who can insure against money and cannot insure against imprisonment, the practical deterrent may in some boardrooms run deeper in the civil regime than it did in the criminal one, where prosecutions were rare, slow and frequently defeated by their own evidentiary machinery.

## Who is inside the regime: citizenship against residence

Every exchange statute must first answer a jurisdictional question, whose transactions it governs, and the two statutes answer it in ways that reveal their philosophies more plainly than any penalty clause. FERA's answer was a mixture of residence and citizenship. By section 1 the Act applied to all citizens of India outside India and to branches and agencies outside India of companies incorporated in India, so the passport itself carried the statute across borders. A citizen who had settled abroad permanently, built a working life in another tax system and another currency, remained a person whose ordinary banking arrangements the Indian state could in principle reach. The claim had a fiscal logic in an era when the emigrant's earnings were a resource the home state hoped to attract back, and when the line between the settled emigrant and the resident's agent abroad was hard to police. It also generated the statute's most resented encounters: the returning emigrant questioned about accounts lawfully opened and lawfully taxed where they were held, the student treated as a conduit, the seafarer and the air crew member whose working lives were spent in foreign currency.

FEMA answers with residence alone, and then defines residence as a compound of arithmetic and intention. Under section 2(v), a person resident in India is, in the individual case, a person residing in India for more than one hundred and eighty-two days during the course of the preceding financial year. Two intention-based limbs then override the count at the moment of movement and in both directions. A person who has gone out of India for employment abroad, for carrying on a business or vocation abroad, or in circumstances indicating an intention to stay outside India for an uncertain period, is not resident, whatever the day count says. A person who has come to India for employment, for business, or in circumstances indicating an intention to stay for an uncertain period, is resident from arrival, again whatever the count says. The practical consequence is that FEMA status changes on the day of the journey rather than at the end of a counting period: the professional who leaves to take up employment abroad becomes a person resident outside India on departure, entitled from that day to hold what the regulations allow a non-resident and obliged from that day to redesignate the resident accounts left behind, while the same professional returning for good becomes resident on arrival, and the foreign accounts that were lawful the day before must be fitted within the permissions for residents and the facilities built for returning emigrants.

The establishment limbs of the definition carry the same idea into business life. An office, branch or agency in India owned or controlled by a person resident outside India is treated as a person resident in India, and an office, branch or agency outside India owned or controlled by a person resident in India is treated the same way, so the statute reaches the Indian establishment of the foreign company and the foreign establishment of the Indian company through the single concept of residence and control. Set against FERA's citizenship limb, the redesign is principled and practical at once. It accepts that an emigrant's economic centre of gravity can genuinely move, which the old Act never accepted, and it confines the state's claim to persons and establishments the banking system of the territory can actually administer. The classification of this row as substantive liberalisation records the emigrant's exit from the regime, the largest single release of persons that the change of 2000 effected.

The comparison has a trap built into it, and it is the costliest practical mistake in the whole field: the assumption that residence means the same thing across Indian statutes. It does not, and the two tests produce different answers for the same person in the same year as a matter of routine. Section 6 of the Income-tax Act 1961, the subject of [the complete guide to the Income-tax Act 1961](/2012/07/01/income-tax-act-1961-complete-guide/), tests residence by presence in India during the previous year itself, one hundred and eighty-two days, or, in the alternative combination, sixty days in that year together with three hundred and sixty-five days across the four preceding years, and it asks nothing about the traveller's purpose. FEMA tests presence during the preceding financial year and then overrides the count with intention at the moment of movement. The executive who returns to India in November to settle is resident under FEMA from November and may be non-resident under the income-tax test for that entire financial year, with account designations, holding permissions and disclosure duties pulling in opposite directions under the two codes. Neither answer is an error. Each statute drafted its test for its own purpose, the tax statute to locate the right to tax worldwide income, the exchange statute to locate the duty to route transactions through the authorised channel, and the person caught between them must run both tests, separately, every time status matters. A comparison that reports the FEMA test as the Indian residence test has not simplified the law; it has manufactured a default.

## The definitions that do the sorting

One formal contrast between the statutes explains more of their practical difference than the celebrated change of title. FERA prohibited first and defined as it went; its operative sections were addressed to acts, payments, holdings, transfers, and the citizen's task was to find, somewhere in the Act or in the Reserve Bank's permissions, an exception that covered the act in contemplation. FEMA defines first and prohibits through its definitions. Three definitions carry the structure. A current account transaction, under section 2(j), is defined by a list oriented to ordinary life, payments due in connection with foreign trade and ordinary business, interest and net income from investment, remittances for the living expenses of parents, spouse and children living abroad, and expenses in connection with foreign travel, education and medical care. A capital account transaction, under section 2(e), is defined by its effect on a balance sheet, any transaction which alters the assets or liabilities, including contingent liabilities, outside India of a person resident in India, or the assets or liabilities in India of a person resident outside India. A person resident in India, under section 2(v), is defined by presence and then corrected by intention, as the residence section of this article sets out. Feed a transaction into the three definitions and the statute has sorted it before any prohibition is consulted: current or capital, resident or non-resident, free unless restricted or controlled within specified classes.

The definitional method is a substantive feature of the liberalisation and not a drafting taste, for two reasons. The first is that a sorting rule stated in the Act itself gives the citizen a question that can be answered without an intermediary: whether a transaction alters assets or liabilities across the border is a matter of accounting character, knowable in advance, where the FERA question, whether a permission existed, was a matter of administrative record, knowable only by asking. The second is that the definitions decide where the burden of the system's strictness falls. Because the capital definition is drawn by effect rather than by list, it captures transactions the drafters never imagined, financial instruments and digital arrangements that alter cross-border balance sheets in novel ways, and delivers them into the controlled classes automatically. The same breadth that makes the current account freedom workable makes the capital account control future-proof, and the single definitional choice serves both halves of the reform at once. A comparison that confines itself to liability and penalties will miss this layer entirely, yet it is the layer a practitioner consults first, and it is the layer on which the framework's celebrated adjustability depends: regulations can reclassify the treatment of a transaction only because the Act's definitions have already told everyone which shelf the transaction sits on.

The sorting has a contested middle, and its existence qualifies the elegance of the design in a way the comparison should record. Some transactions resist the two definitions: a payment that is partly the price of a service and partly the purchase of an asset, a remittance whose purpose straddles the schedules, an arrangement under which value moves at once and an asset materialises later. Under FERA a doubtful case was resolved by the permission structure, in the citizen's disfavour by default, since silence meant prohibition. Under FEMA a doubtful case is resolved by characterisation, and characterisation is performed in the first instance by the authorised dealer, advised where necessary by the Reserve Bank's clarifications and master directions, which have accumulated into a body of rulings on the borderline: which receipts are current and which capital, which holdings are deposits and which are investments, which transfers are gifts and which are settlements of liability. The citizen's position in a doubtful case is better than it was, because the doubt concerns classification rather than permission, and classification can be argued from the definitions in the Act. It is not costless, because the dealer's caution, examined elsewhere in this article, resolves institutional doubt against the customer, and because a transaction characterised wrongly at the counter carries its contravention with it until an adjudicating authority or a compounding officer recharacterises it. The definitional republic has a border, the border has guards, and the guards are banks.

## The current account in daily life: where the liberalisation is actually felt

The dimension of the comparison with the largest population is the one with the smallest legal drama, the current account, because it is the territory of the transactions ordinary people actually perform. Under FERA, the traveller, the student, the patient and the emigrant's family lived inside the permission structure at its most granular. Foreign exchange for travel was released against entitlement categories and documentary proof; exchange for a child's education abroad was an application; a family's maintenance remittance moved through forms whose categories had been written for a rationing state; and the general permissions and manual directions that softened the edges across the nineteen eighties were revocable grace, administered at a bank counter by an officer whose safe course was always refusal where the manual was silent. The statute's defenders pointed, correctly, to the liberal administration of the later years. The citizen's memory, also correctly, recorded the structure: every outward payment was a request.

FEMA's section 5 inverted the experience before it changed any number. The current account transaction is permitted, and the Central Government's Current Account Transactions Rules then sort the exceptions into three schedules, a short prohibited list, covering items such as remittances for the purchase of lottery tickets and the proceeds of lotteries and racing, a schedule of transactions requiring the approval of the Central Government, and a schedule requiring the approval of the Reserve Bank beyond stated limits. The mass of ordinary life sits in none of the schedules, and is therefore free, a default experienced at the counter as the difference between producing documents that evidence a transaction and filing an application that begs a discretion. The authorised dealer still examines, still classifies by purpose code, still reports; the citizen still fills forms; the paperwork did not retire in 2000. What retired was the legal character of the paperwork, from petition to record. Set beside the FERA baseline honestly measured, against the manual-administered system of 1999 rather than the raw text of 1974, the change in daily life is real but incremental in volume and categorical in kind: the same counter, the same dealer, the same documents, and a different answer to the question of who must persuade whom. That is the precise size of the liberalisation for the largest class of persons the regime touches, and a comparison that inflates it into open borders, or deflates it into nothing, has stopped measuring.

## What did not change: the channel, the capital account and the rulebook

Turn from the dimensions that moved to the two that did not, and the relabelling account has its day. The first continuity is the channel. FERA forbade dealing in foreign exchange outside the authorised persons, and FEMA, by section 3, forbids it still: no person may deal in or transfer foreign exchange to a person other than an authorised person, and section 4 restates the core asset prohibition in modern dress, providing that no person resident in India shall acquire, hold, own, possess or transfer any foreign exchange, foreign security or immovable property situated outside India except as provided under the Act. Everything a resident lawfully holds abroad is held through a permission structure, and the structure did not dissolve in 2000. It was re-papered.

### What is the authorised channel, and why did FEMA keep it?

The authorised channel is the requirement that dealings in foreign exchange pass through persons authorised under section 10 of FEMA, principally banks as authorised dealers. FEMA kept it because a regime that polices transactions through the banking system needs a gatekeeper at every transaction, and the authorised dealer, which checks permissibility before executing, is that gatekeeper.

The dealer's role shows what the continuity means in practice. Under FERA the dealer was a licensee inside a rationing system, dispensing permissions framed elsewhere and sheltering behind literal compliance with the Reserve Bank's Exchange Control Manual; its institutional instinct, where the manual was silent, was refusal. Under FEMA the dealer is a regulated examiner with judgement to exercise: it must satisfy itself that a transaction is permissible before executing it, perform the diligence that identifies the customer and the purpose, allot the purpose codes under which cross-border flows are classified, and report the transaction into the Reserve Bank's monitoring systems. A dealer that executes a prohibited transaction exposes its own authorisation. The criminal law of FERA policed transactions after they happened, with the apparatus of prosecution. The civil law of FEMA polices them before they happen, with the apparatus of banking compliance, and the second apparatus touches immeasurably more transactions than the first ever did. A citizen's effective law is applied, in the first instance, by a bank's compliance department, whose caution frequently makes the operative rule stricter than the written one and whose refusal is, in practice, answerable only by taking the business elsewhere or by seeking the Reserve Bank's clarification.

### What makes a transaction a capital account transaction rather than a current account one?

A capital account transaction, under section 2(e), is one that alters the assets or liabilities, including contingent liabilities, of a person resident in India outside India, or of a non-resident in India: buying property abroad, investing in a foreign company, borrowing across the border. A current account transaction leaves assets and liabilities unchanged and settles the flows of ordinary life.

The second continuity is the capital account itself, the single row of the table classified as continuation outright. The permissible capital transactions of a resident, investment in a joint venture or wholly owned subsidiary abroad within limits expressed against net worth, borrowing under the external commercial borrowing framework with its eligible borrower and lender categories, maturity floors, cost ceilings and end-use restrictions, acquisition of immovable property abroad, are specified by regulation, class by class and limit by limit, and the classes and limits are rewritten as policy moves between encouragement and restraint. What the Act contributes is the insistence that there shall be a framework and that the Reserve Bank, in consultation with the Central Government, shall keep it; the politics of the period decide what the framework says. The Liberalised Remittance Scheme is the cleanest exhibit of the philosophy at the individual level. Introduced in 2004, it allows a resident individual to remit up to two hundred and fifty thousand US dollars per financial year for permitted current and capital purposes, including opening accounts abroad, acquiring shares and buying property, a ceiling reached in stages from an opening figure of twenty-five thousand dollars as the reserves position strengthened. The scheme is a general permission, generous by FERA standards, bounded by a ceiling, routed through an authorised dealer, and revocable in design.

The revocability is not theoretical. In the currency turbulence of 2013, when the rupee fell sharply, the Reserve Bank cut the scheme's ceiling from two hundred thousand dollars to seventy-five thousand dollars and tightened the overseas investment limits for companies, closing the gate within days through the delegated machinery, and restored the ceiling in stages, through one hundred and twenty-five thousand dollars to two hundred and fifty thousand, as reserves recovered. A Parliament working with a FERA-style code would have had to amend the Act, in session and in public, to achieve the same movement. The episode is the strongest practical evidence for the proposition that the transition replaced a fixed code with an adjustable instrument, and it cuts in both directions: the adjustability that protects the external position in a crisis is the same adjustability that leaves the resident's permissions permanently provisional. Beneath the scheme sits the wider delegation on which the whole regime runs, the rules of the Central Government sorting current account dealings into prohibited, approval and limit-bound schedules, and the regulations, master directions and circulars of [the Reserve Bank of India](/2012/12/01/rbi-act-1934-explained/), whose governing statute charges it with the currency and the external value of the rupee, amending the working catalogue scores of times in a busy year. FERA, for all its faults, printed its prohibitions in an Act of Parliament that a citizen could read from beginning to end. FEMA prints a frame and delegates the picture, and the distance between the Act's permissive vocabulary and the system's operative strictness is filled entirely by instruments most citizens will never open.

### What happens to a bank that executes a transaction the regulations prohibit?

An authorised dealer that executes a prohibited transaction acts against the conditions of its authorisation under section 10 of FEMA and exposes itself to the Reserve Bank's supervisory consequences, including action against the authorisation and penalties of its own. The exposure is deliberate design: it converts the bank from the customer's counterparty into the state's first examiner of the transaction.

## Hawala: the shadow system both statutes were written against

No comparison of the two regimes is complete without the parallel system that shadowed both, because hawala was simultaneously FERA's paradigmatic crime and the standing proof of its channel's failure. The network's mechanics were simple: a broker in India accepted rupees and instructed a counterpart abroad to pay the equivalent in foreign currency to the beneficiary, balances being settled through trade, couriers and reverse flows, without money crossing the border through any bank. Its customers were never a single criminal class. They were the emigrant worker whose family needed money faster and at a better rate than the official channel offered, the small importer whose licence or permission covered part of an order, the family paying a foreign university through an intermediary because the authorised paperwork had failed them, and, at the other end of the scale, the smuggler and the tax evader for whom invisibility was the product being bought. Where a state fixes the price of foreign currency below its market level, rations access to it and criminalises the alternatives, an unofficial market is not a pathology but a prediction, and FERA's enforcement decades were spent prosecuting the predicted.

The old statute's answer to hawala was the full weight of its design: the channel offences under its prohibitions, the presumption of section 59, prosecution under section 56, and behind them preventive detention under the 1974 detention statute for those the state wished to remove from circulation without a trial. The design's brittleness appeared at the highest level of public attention in the Jain hawala affair of the nineteen nineties, in which diaries seized from two brokers recorded payments attributed by initials to a roll of senior politicians and officials, and in which the FERA prosecutions, after the Supreme Court's intervention in Vineet Narain v Union of India had restructured the supervision of the investigating agencies, collapsed in the trial court for want of admissible evidence connecting the diaries to the payments alleged. The episode displayed the criminal track's characteristic weakness: transactions engineered to leave no trace are the hardest to prove beyond reasonable doubt even with a presumption assisting, and the cases the regime most wanted to win were the cases its machinery was least able to carry.

FEMA's answer relocates hawala within the legal map rather than pretending to abolish it. The dealing itself is a contravention of section 3, the channel prohibition, and it is the one class of contravention whose compounding is reserved to the Directorate of Enforcement rather than the Reserve Bank, a reservation that marks the legislature's judgment that the channel offence is the regime's serious core. Adjudication prices the dealing; the criminal consequences that hawala attracts in the modern order arrive from outside the exchange statute, from the money laundering statute where the funds are proceeds of a scheduled offence, from the customs law where goods move with the money, and from the tax law where the income was never declared. The hawala operator of 1985 was the central criminal of the exchange regime, prosecuted under its own statute. The hawala operator of the FEMA era is a contravenor under the exchange statute and a criminal, if at all, under other people's statutes. That redistribution of criminality, out of the exchange code and into the specialised codes built for its components, is the transition's least celebrated and most characteristic move, and a comparison that looks for the crime of hawala inside FEMA and reports its absence as deregulation has looked in the one place the reform deliberately emptied.

## The exporter's duty: the obligation that never changed

If a single provision had to be chosen to stand for the continuity layer, it would be the exporter's duty to bring the money home, because the duty was the working heart of the old regime's trade enforcement and it crossed the repeal in nearly identical substance. Under FERA, section 18 required the exporter to furnish a declaration that the full export value of the goods would be realised, and cast on the exporter the duty to do all that was necessary to ensure that the proceeds were realised and repatriated within the prescribed period. Behind the duty stood the balance-of-payments logic of the entire code: an export was not a private sale but a national earning event, and the exporter's freedom to leave the price abroad, to under-invoice the consignment, or to delay repatriation indefinitely was a leak in the collective pool. Under-invoicing of exports and over-invoicing of imports were accordingly the classic FERA offences of the trading class, and the enforcement directorate's largest cases of the era were built on valuation, on proving that the paper price and the real price of a consignment had parted company.

Under FEMA the duty stands in sections 7 and 8 with its structure unaltered. The exporter must furnish to the Reserve Bank, or to the prescribed authority, a declaration of the full export value of the goods or services, and must do all that is necessary to realise and repatriate to India the amount representing that full value, within the period and in the manner the Reserve Bank's regulations specify. The service exporter, invisible to the drafting of 1973, is gathered into the same duty in the modern text, and the freelancer who leaves earnings from foreign clients sitting indefinitely in an overseas payment account is in breach of a duty whose lineage runs unbroken to the scarcity state. What changed for the exporter was never the obligation but the file's destination and its price. Under FERA the file could travel both tracks at once, a fivefold penalty in adjudication and a prosecution in which section 59 tilted the question of intent, the trial measured in years and the firm's credit lines hostage to the outcome. Under FEMA the file travels one track, an adjudication on probabilities in which the tribunal-built doctrines of gravity, gain and disclosure calibrate the figure, with compounding available for the defaults that are really about delay and documentation. The exporter's experience of the transition is therefore the article's thesis in occupational form: the conversion of an existential legal risk into a financial and reputational one, for a duty identical in both worlds. A reform that had genuinely deregulated would have abolished the duty. The reform of 2000 kept the duty and repriced its breach, and the choice tells a reader more about the reform's intentions than any preamble.

## The sunset: how the old regime was wound down

A comparison of the two regimes must account for the years in which they overlapped, because the manner of the old regime's ending is itself evidence in the argument. Section 49 of FEMA performs the repeal and the savings together, and its structure is more aggressive than the conventional formula. The first sub-section repeals the Act of 1973 outright. The fourth performs the ordinary saving: anything done under the repealed Act continues to be governed by it, so offences committed before 1 June 2000 remained punishable under FERA and proceedings for them could continue as though the repeal had not happened. Standing alone, that saving would have given the criminal regime an afterlife measured in decades. The third sub-section cut the afterlife short. It provided that, notwithstanding anything contained in any other law, no court shall take cognizance of an offence under the repealed Act, and no adjudicating officer shall take notice of a contravention under the old adjudication provision, after the expiry of two years from the commencement of FEMA. The window closed on 31 May 2002. A prosecution filed by then could run its course, and many did, some for years, as the Standard Chartered litigation illustrates. A prosecution not filed by then could never be filed at all. The section's later sub-sections preserved the general application of section 6 of the General Clauses Act 1897 to the effects of the repeal and transferred appeals pending before FERA's Appellate Board to the new Appellate Tribunal, so that the regulatory and appellate content of the old regime carried forward seamlessly while its punitive content was given two years to live.

### Which statute governs a foreign exchange transaction from 1999 that comes to light years later?

The transaction is governed by FERA, because section 49 of FEMA saves acts done under the repealed Act, but punishment is governed by the sunset: a prosecution or adjudication notice for it had to begin within two years of 1 June 2000. Discovered after the window closed, the FERA-era act carries no FERA penalty, whatever its merits.

The device deserves its place in the comparison because a legislature that merely wished to relabel a regime would not have bothered to bury its prosecutions. An amnesty would have forgiven the past offences, and Parliament did not forgive them. A simple repeal with a full savings clause would have preserved them indefinitely, and Parliament did not preserve them. Instead it handed the enforcement agency a deadline to convert its files into complaints or lose them, and in Dropti Devi v Union of India the Supreme Court, describing the new order in the course of upholding a preventive detention, quoted the sunset clause as part of its account of a legislature that had made foreign exchange violations civil and compoundable. The honest comparison records the cost of the device alongside its meaning. Outcomes turned on dates rather than merits: the trader whose file the directorate completed in 2001 faced a criminal code with a presumption against him, and the trader whose identical file was completed in 2003 faced nothing under the old law. Limitation always draws such lines, and the two-year fuse was defensible as the price of finality for an entire class of disputes arising from a disowned economic era, but the closing months of the window saw a filing rush and a generation of litigation over whether particular complaints had been taken cognizance of in time. The criminal regime ended as it had operated, in procedural contest, and the last FERA arguments heard in Indian courts concerned not whether the accused had broken the rules of a closed economy but whether the state had beaten a clock of its own legislature's making.

## The enforcement continuity and the hardening of 2015

The enforcement row of the table is classified as a formal change over a continuing toolkit, and the classification has to survive the strongest fact available to the relabelling account: the agency did not change. Section 36 of FEMA established a Directorate of Enforcement under the new Act, continuous in personnel, method and institutional memory with the directorate that had policed FERA, and section 37 armed it with investigative powers modelled on those of the income tax authorities, summons, inquiry, search and seizure, while withholding the old power to arrest for the contravention itself. Practitioners who worked under both statutes make the observation that the investigative culture crossed the repeal intact, that the same summons, the same recorded statement and the same file-building method await the noticee of the civil regime that awaited the accused of the criminal one, and the observation is a serious one that this comparison presents as it is made rather than endorsing or dismissing it. Its force is real but bounded: culture is not jurisdiction, and the powers the culture works with were redrawn in ways the liability, burden and sanction sections have measured. What the continuity of the agency proves is that decriminalisation was a change in the law administered, not a change in the administrator, and a verdict that needs the administrator to have changed is a verdict about a different reform.

One survival sharpens the point. The Conservation of Foreign Exchange and Prevention of Smuggling Activities Act 1974, which empowers preventive detention, without charge or trial, of persons acting in a manner prejudicial to the conservation or augmentation of foreign exchange resources, was not repealed in 2000 and was not repealed with FEMA's decriminalisation. The exchange offence became civil while the detention power built for the exchange offence remained on the book, a juxtaposition the Supreme Court itself recorded in Dropti Devi v Union of India, where the detention under challenge was described against the background of a legislature that had made the underlying violations compoundable civil defaults. The survival does not convert FEMA back into a criminal statute, since detention under the 1974 Act is a separate power with its own safeguards and its own jurisprudence, aimed at the smuggling end of the spectrum. It does mean that the map of coercive law around foreign exchange was redrawn in 2000 rather than erased, and the comparison that stops at the two exchange statutes will misstate the state's full hand.

The second act of the story complicates the classification further. In 2015, by the Finance Act of that year, Parliament inserted section 37A into FEMA, in force from September 2015, to answer a jurisdictional embarrassment: the assets whose holding section 4 prohibits sit abroad, beyond the physical reach of Indian process, so a resident could hold an unauthorised foreign account or property in defiance of the Act while the agency stood at the border with an unenforceable finding. Section 37A substitutes the asset. Where an authorised officer has reason to believe, recorded in writing, that foreign exchange, a foreign security or an immovable property situated outside India is held in contravention of section 4, the officer may seize value equivalent, situated within India, of the foreign holding. Seizure is barred where the aggregate value of the foreign holding falls below one crore rupees. The order must be placed before a Competent Authority within thirty days, the authority must decide within one hundred and eighty days, and an appeal lies to the Appellate Tribunal constituted under the Smugglers and Foreign Exchange Manipulators (Forfeiture of Property) Act 1976, a forum whose name alone announces the lineage of the power. The same amending package recast the consequences for the foreign asset class within section 13, adding confiscation of the equivalent value in India and a prosecution route, filtered through a written recommendation of the Adjudicating Authority and the recorded satisfaction of the Director of Enforcement, carrying imprisonment of up to five years for the acquisition of foreign assets beyond the threshold.

The jurisprudence on the equivalent value power is where the new regime approaches the severity of the old, and it must be described as contested because it is. The Appellate Tribunal, in the case of Devendra N. Desai, lifted a seizure after the foreign exchange had been repatriated and disclosed, reasoning that the provision's object had been fulfilled; in the matter arising from the estate of Anil Salgaocar, the tribunal held that assets acquired abroad before section 37A existed could still found a seizure where they continued to be held abroad after its commencement, rejecting the charge of retrospectivity. Critics of the provision argue that a seizure founded on an officer's reason to believe, inside a regime whose premise is that these are civil matters, reproduces the criminal law's sequence of coercion first and adjudication afterwards, and that a one crore threshold is low enough to catch the careless alongside the deliberate concealer. Defenders answer that without an interim instrument the adjudication would be pointless in the paradigm case, since the asset abroad is beyond reach and the asset in India can be sold in an afternoon, and that the recorded reasons, the confirming authority, the statutory clock and the repatriation exit are exactly the safeguards a civil system should substitute for the trial it renounced. Both positions are live in the tribunal litigation, and the same holdings attract the separate and heavier regime of [the Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Act 2015](/2012/09/01/black-money-act-2015-explained/), enacted in the same season and aimed at the same conduct.

The parallel statute completes the practical picture, because a citizen's exposure is set not by FEMA alone but by FEMA in combination. The directorate that administers the exchange statute also administers the Prevention of Money-Laundering Act 2002, in force from 1 July 2005, and a FEMA contravention without more is not money laundering, since the laundering offence requires proceeds of a scheduled criminal offence projected as untainted. The factual patterns that attract the directorate's attention, however, characteristically inhabit both worlds at once: the remittance routed through a hawala broker may be a contravention in itself and, if the money moved is the proceeds of a scheduled offence, the raw material of a laundering prosecution. The reader who needs the architecture of that second statute should go to [the complete guide to the Prevention of Money-Laundering Act 2002](/2013/03/01/pmla-2002-complete-guide/), which owns it; what belongs to this comparison is the structural consequence. The same facts are examined in parallel proceedings under different statutes, in different forums, under different standards of proof, with the directorate's power of arrest, upheld under the laundering statute, available in the second proceeding and unavailable in the first, and with statements made in the civil investigation capable of acquiring a second life in the criminal one. The transition of 2000 separated liability from criminality inside the exchange statute. The enactment of the laundering statute five years later reconnected them at the level of the enforcing institution, and any verdict on the post-2000 order that ignores the second statute understates the coercive law a foreign exchange irregularity can still attract.

## Three errors the comparison attracts

Because the comparison is searched constantly by people who need a practical answer, the errors in circulation are worth naming, since each of them manufactures liability in the person who believes it. The first error is to describe capital account transactions as liberalised, usually by sliding from the true proposition that current account transactions were freed. The slide is understandable: the same statute book, the same commencement date, the same speeches. It is also the costliest of the three, because the reader who acts on it acquires the foreign property, the foreign shares or the foreign borrowing first and discovers the classes and limits of section 6 afterwards, in a notice. The capital account was not liberalised in 2000; it was re-housed, from case-by-case permission to specified classes under regulation, and its subsequent history, ceilings raised in calm years and cut within days in the turbulence of 2013, is the history of a control being adjusted, not dismantled.

The second error is to state the FEMA penalty as a fixed figure, as though the statute prescribed a fine of a stated number of rupees for a stated default. Section 13 prescribes a multiple, up to three times the sum involved where the sum is quantifiable, and the fixed figures in the section are the ceiling of two lakh rupees for unquantifiable sums and the five thousand rupees a day for a continuing contravention. Pages that quote a single penalty number have mistaken the margin of the section for its rule, and the mistake matters in both directions: it frightens the small defaulter, whose realistic exposure sits far below the ceiling under the proportionality doctrine and the compounding matrix, and it reassures the large one, whose exposure is a multiple of a number he may prefer not to compute. The third error, examined in the residence section and repeated here because it is the one with the longest tail, is to treat residence under the exchange statute as identical to residence under the Income-tax Act. The two tests differ in the year they count, in the days they demand and in the intention they admit, and they return different answers for the same person in the same year as a matter of design. The reader who runs one test and files the answer under both statutes has not made an arithmetical slip but a categorical one, and the correction, run both tests separately every time status matters, is the cheapest sentence in this article.

Behind the three errors sits a single habit, the habit of reading a change of label as a change of substance in whichever direction the reader prefers. The reform's celebrants read management as freedom; its detractors read it as the old regulation in disguise; and both readings relieve their holders of the work the statute actually requires, which is to ask, transaction by transaction, which class it falls in, which schedule or regulation governs it, which consequence follows breach, and which exit, compounding or adjudication, prices the breach most cheaply. The comparison defended here is unfashionable precisely because it is two-handed. It asks the reader to hold that the state surrendered its prison and kept its gate, and to plan accordingly.

## The verdict: decriminalisation, not deregulation

The evidence assembled across the eight dimensions supports a verdict, and the discipline of a comparison article is to state it without trimming. India, in 2000, liberalised its enforcement of exchange control and did not liberalise exchange control itself to anything like the same degree. Six of the eight dimensions, the scheme's default for the transactions of ordinary life, the nature of liability, the burden and standard of proof, the sanction, the forum, and the reach of the statute over the person, record substantive liberalisation, and every one of them concerns the position of the citizen against the state. Two dimensions qualify the picture: enforcement powers, where the investigative toolkit survived the repeal with its arrest power surrendered and a seizure power later restored in a new form, and the capital account, where control continued outright, administered through regulations rather than permissions but unbroken in substance. The deciding factor, named at the outset and earned by the count, is the choice between measuring a regime by its restrictions and measuring it by its consequences. The restrictions shrank at the margin of ordinary life and held everywhere the state feared instability. The consequences were transformed in kind.

Stated at full strength, the liberalisation account is right about the thing that matters most to the person inside a proceeding. A statute that presumed the accused's guilty mind and held his disproof to the criminal standard, that prosecuted exporters for realising their proceeds late, that followed the emigrant's passport around the world, was an instrument of fear out of proportion to the reserves it protected, and the removal of the presumption, the arrest power and the prison sentence was a civil liberties gain of the first order, not a change of stationery. Stated at full strength, the relabelling account is right about the thing that matters most to the person planning a transaction. The state surrendered no control it valued, the channel remained compulsory, the realisation duties remained, the detail migrated into subordinate instruments that change faster than any Act, and in 2015 the state bolted a seizure power and a filtered prosecution back onto the civil frame for the foreign assets it most wanted to reach. The two accounts are not rivals describing one fact differently. They are accurate reports from two different layers of the same reform, and the work of a comparison is to keep the layers separate and to say which layer governs which question. For the question what may I do, the continuity layer governs, and the answer is: very much what the regulations permit, as under FERA, though reached by a general permission instead of a licence. For the question what happens to me if I get it wrong, the liberalisation layer governs, and the answer is: a priced penalty after a documentary hearing, in place of a prosecution under a presumption. The folklore error is to answer the second question and believe one has answered the first.

The word relabelling deserves a direct answer before the verdict rests, because it is the strongest word the continuity account owns and it is, strictly, the wrong word. A relabelled thing is the same thing under a new name, as a renamed tax remains the same tax. What happened in 2000 fails that test at the level of the individual case: the exporter proceeded against in 1999 and the exporter proceeded against in 2001 committed the same act, owed the same duty and faced the same agency, and yet the second was never in danger of prison, never defended against a presumption, never stood in a dock, and could end the matter by application and payment. If those differences are labels, then the distinction between a crime and a debt is a label, and the vocabulary of criticism has dissolved the very distinctions it exists to police. The accurate criticism of the reform is not that it relabelled the old regime but that it liberalised selectively, consequence first and coverage barely, and selective liberalisation is a policy that can be argued with on its merits, which is more than can be said for a slogan.

The bargain, so described, was also a defensible one, and a comparison article may say so. India in 2000 was not ready, on any serious view of its reserves, its banking system or its fiscal position, to surrender the capital account, and the Tarapore framework had made convertibility conditional and phased; a reform that had pretended otherwise invited reversal at the first external shock, and the decriminalisation would have been reversed with it, as the region's experience in 1997 warned. Nothing in the external position, by contrast, ever required the imprisonment of the late-realising exporter or the presumption against the traveller's state of mind. The reform kept the part of the old regime that necessity defended and discarded the part that only habit defended, and then, in the sunset clause, it buried the discarded part on a deadline rather than letting it litigate itself out over decades. That is what a successful repeal and replacement looks like, and it is why the verdict of this article is a verdict for the reform as well as a verdict about its name.

## What the verdict means, by reader

A comparison earns its place by issuing different instructions to different readers, because the same verdict that liberates one reader's understanding can mislead another's conduct. The non-resident and the holder of overseas assets should take the continuity layer as the operative one. Residence, not the passport and not the family's sense of where home is, decides which side of the regime a person stands on, status changes on the day of the journey that changes it, and assets acquired in one status must be actively fitted into the permissions of the next: accounts redesignated, holdings disclosed, inherited property brought within the retention rules rather than left to accumulate silence. The practical error this reader makes is the folklore error, treating the civil character of FEMA as a general permission to hold abroad what the regulations have not granted. The advocate should take the liberalisation layer as the operative one and litigate accordingly: the case is won on documents and probabilities, intention is an argument about quantum rather than about liability, compounding is a priced exit to be evaluated before adjudication hardens positions, and the tribunal's doctrines of hearing, reasons and proportion are the working safeguards of a first stage conducted inside the enforcing establishment. The business reader, the exporter, the company with a subsidiary abroad, should plan on the basis that the framework is adjustable: ceilings move in crises, classes of permissible transactions are rewritten by regulation, and compliance is a standing function audited by the authorised dealer at every transaction, not a clearance obtained once.

The historian should take the comparison as a case study in how Indian economic statutes actually change: the practice liberalises first, an amending Act ratifies the practice, the replacement statute arrives last to give the new practice a legal form, and the consequence structure is renegotiated a decade and a half later when the state's anxieties return, as they did in 2015. Read in that sequence, the transition is neither the rupture of the celebrants nor the continuity of the detractors, but a layered settlement whose layers were laid down in different decades for different reasons.

The student and the examination candidate should hold the verdict in its named form, because it is the form in which the material is tested and the form that survives cross-questioning: decriminalisation, not deregulation, with the deciding factor stated. Candidates who write that FEMA liberalised foreign exchange without the qualifier will be contradicted by their own examples the moment they reach the capital account, and candidates who write that nothing changed will be contradicted the moment they reach section 59 of the old Act. For revision of the comparison in structured form, [reviewing the eight dimensions as a slide deck in the ReportMedic presentation viewer](https://reportmedic.org/tools/ppt-viewer.html) is a serviceable way to keep the rows, and their classifications, in order. One sentence of discipline belongs to every practical reader rather than to any class: the general position described in an article of this kind is information about a regime, not advice on an individual case, and compounding windows, limitation arguments and the consequences of delay are unforgiving in ways that only advice taken on the actual facts can answer.

## Where the verdict does not hold

A verdict that admits no exceptions has usually been reached by ignoring the hard cases, and three territories qualify this one. The first is the foreign asset class after 2015. For the resident who acquires or holds undisclosed foreign exchange, foreign securities or immovable property abroad beyond the one crore threshold, the post-2015 statute reassembles much of the old severity inside the civil frame: seizure of equivalent value in India on an officer's recorded belief before liability is established, confiscation of that value on adjudication, a filtered prosecution carrying imprisonment of up to five years, and, in the same season's legislation, an entire separate statute for undisclosed foreign income and assets. In this territory the relabelling account is not a cynical misreading but a fair description of a deliberate re-hardening, and the verdict of decriminalisation must be read as subject to the exception the state carved out for the conduct it cared most about.

The second territory is the parallel proceeding. Where the facts of an exchange irregularity also constitute, or touch, the proceeds of a scheduled offence, the money laundering statute supplies the criminal track that FEMA renounced, complete with arrest and attachment, and the citizen experiences the two statutes as one long proceeding administered by one directorate. The separation that the verdict celebrates is a separation inside the exchange statute, and it holds only for so long as the facts stay inside that statute's territory. The third territory is the capital account in daily operation. For the resident whose life project is a capital transaction, a property abroad beyond the remittance ceiling, an investment beyond the automatic route's limits, a borrowing outside the borrowing framework, the practical difference between a permission refused under a prohibitory code and a class not specified under a permissive frame can shrink to the width of the counter at which the refusal is delivered. In that territory the change of 2000 altered the rhetoric of refusal more than its incidence, and honesty requires the comparison to say so. None of the three territories overturns the verdict, because the verdict was always about the generality of conduct and the generality of consequences. Together they mark the frontier on which the meaning of the transition continues to be litigated, in the tribunal's seizure cases, in the parallel-proceeding challenges, and in every future crisis in which the adjustable instrument is adjusted sharply against the resident.

The closing sentence of a comparison should return the reader to the decision the article opened with. The family with the inherited flat, the finance head with the old remittance, the candidate with the two contradictory books are each asking, in their own idiom, whether the India of the exchange statutes is a freer country than it was. The answer the eight dimensions give is precise and twofold. It is a country in which the state kept nearly every control it had and surrendered nearly every cruelty it had used to enforce them, in which breaking the rules stopped being a crime and remained a liability, and in which the citizen's task is no longer to obtain permission to live an international life but to document one. Decriminalisation, not deregulation: the restrictions mostly stayed, the consequences wholly changed, and the person who confuses the second proposition with the repeal of the first will learn the difference from an adjudicating authority, at three times the sum involved.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: What is the difference between FERA and FEMA?

The Foreign Exchange Regulation Act 1973 was a prohibitory criminal code: dealings in foreign exchange were unlawful unless permitted, breach was an offence punishable with imprisonment, and the accused defended against a statutory presumption of guilty intent. The Foreign Exchange Management Act 1999, in force from 1 June 2000, is a civil framework: current account transactions are permitted unless restricted, capital account transactions are allowed within classes and limits set by regulation, and breach is a contravention punishable with a money penalty after adjudication on the balance of probabilities. The channel through authorised dealers and the control over capital movements survived the change. The consequence, the burden of proof, the forum and the reach over citizens abroad did not.

### Q: Why is FEMA described as a civil law?

Because everything about its proceeding is civil in character. The wrong it defines is called a contravention rather than an offence, the person proceeded against is a noticee rather than an accused, liability is decided by an Adjudicating Authority on the preponderance of probabilities rather than by a criminal court beyond reasonable doubt, and the outcome is a penalty, confiscation of the property involved, or a compounded settlement, never a sentence or a criminal record. The statute even replaces imprisonment with civil imprisonment, the debtor's process, reserved for a person who refuses to pay a penalty. Two qualifications keep the description accurate: a filtered prosecution for large undisclosed foreign asset holdings was added in 2015, and separate statutes such as the money laundering law can criminalise the same underlying facts.

### Q: Is a foreign exchange contravention a crime in India?

For the generality of conduct, no. A contravention of FEMA is a civil default that attracts a penalty of up to three times the sum involved after adjudication, and it can usually be compounded on payment of a sum. Three qualifications complete the answer. A person who fails to pay an imposed penalty within ninety days faces civil imprisonment under section 14, which is the coercion of a debtor rather than the punishment of an offender. The acquisition or holding of foreign assets beyond the threshold of one crore rupees in contravention of section 4 can, through a filtered route requiring a written recommendation and a recorded satisfaction, lead to prosecution with imprisonment of up to five years. And the same facts may constitute crimes under other statutes, the money laundering law, the Black Money Act 2015 or the customs law, even though the exchange statute itself treats them civilly.

### Q: Can a person be arrested for a FEMA contravention?

No. FEMA gives the Directorate of Enforcement powers of investigation modelled on the income tax authorities, summons, inquiry, search and seizure, but it withholds the power of arrest for the contravention itself, which is the clearest single break with FERA, under which an enforcement officer could arrest a person believed to have committed an offence under the Act. Arrest enters the picture only from outside the exchange statute. The money laundering law carries a power of arrest for the laundering offence, and the preventive detention statute of 1974, which survived the decriminalisation, allows detention without trial of persons acting prejudicially to the conservation of foreign exchange. A person facing only a FEMA adjudication faces a notice, a hearing and a penalty, not a cell.

### Q: Which is stricter on the burden of proof, FERA or FEMA?

FERA, by a margin that decided cases. In a FERA prosecution the court was directed by section 59 to presume that the accused possessed the culpable mental state the offence required, and the accused could displace that presumption only by proving the absence of the mental state beyond reasonable doubt, the very standard the prosecution ordinarily bears. FEMA abolished the presumption altogether. In a FEMA adjudication the department must establish the contravention, and it does so on the preponderance of probabilities, the civil standard, before an Adjudicating Authority. The comparison has one refinement: FEMA is lighter in what the state must prove about intention, since a guilty mind is not an ingredient of the civil liability at all, but stricter in the sense that probability, not certainty, is enough to fix liability and price it.

### Q: Does the department have to prove a guilty mind in a FEMA adjudication?

No. A penalty under section 13 of FEMA is a civil liability for breach of a civil duty, and the reasoning the Supreme Court applied to regulatory penalties in the securities context, in Securities and Exchange Board of India v Shriram Mutual Fund, treats a guilty mind as no essential ingredient of such a liability. The department must establish the contravention itself, that a prohibited act was done or a required act omitted, on the preponderance of probabilities. The noticee's state of mind then becomes important at a different stage: good faith, voluntary disclosure, cooperation and the absence of gain or loss are the factors that press the penalty down from the statutory ceiling, and concealment and repetition press it up. Intention under FEMA is an argument about price, not about liability.

### Q: What penalties can be imposed under FEMA?

Section 13 of FEMA fixes the scale. On adjudication, a contravention attracts a penalty of up to three times the sum involved where that sum is quantifiable, up to two lakh rupees where it is not, and a further penalty of up to five thousand rupees for each day a continuing contravention persists. The Adjudicating Authority may also direct confiscation of the currency, security or property in respect of which the contravention occurred, and direct that foreign exchange holdings be repatriated to India. In practice, tribunals treat the multiples as ceilings for the worst cases and calibrate the figure to gravity, gain and conduct, and most contraventions can instead be compounded under section 15 on payment of a computed sum. For the foreign asset class beyond one crore rupees, confiscation of equivalent value in India and a filtered prosecution are added by the amendments of 2015.

### Q: Can a FEMA penalty exceed the fine a criminal court would have imposed under FERA?

In money, yes, and the possibility is built into the design. Criminal fines under FERA accompanied imprisonment and were set by courts with a sentencing instinct formed on individual offenders; they were frequently modest beside the value of the transactions involved. The FEMA ceiling is a multiple, three times the sum involved in the contravention, so the penalty scales with the transaction in a way a fine rarely did, and confiscation of the property involved can be added to it. The civil regime is gentler in kind, because it takes money and not liberty and leaves no conviction behind, but it is not necessarily gentler in amount. Two mechanisms moderate the ceiling in practice: the tribunal doctrine that reads the multiple as a maximum for the worst cases rather than a tariff, and compounding, which settles most defaults at a computed figure well below the maximum.

### Q: Where does a case begin under FEMA compared with where it began under FERA?

Under FERA a case could begin in two places at once: before a departmental adjudicating officer, on the penalty track, and before a criminal court, on the complaint of an authorised officer, on the prosecution track, with an appeal from adjudication lying to the Foreign Exchange Regulation Appellate Board. Under FEMA a case begins in one place, before the Adjudicating Authority appointed under section 16, which holds the inquiry and decides penalty. The appeal ladder then runs to the Special Director (Appeals), to the Appellate Tribunal for Foreign Exchange, and to the High Court on a question of law. The practical difference is that the FEMA proceeding starts and usually ends inside an administrative structure, faster and in private, while the FERA proceeding could start in a courtroom, in public, with liberty at stake from the first day.

### Q: Did FEMA make the rupee fully convertible?

No. FEMA made the rupee convertible on the current account in substance, completing the commitment India had accepted in 1994 under Article VIII of the Articles of Agreement of the International Monetary Fund not to restrict payments for current international transactions. The capital account was deliberately kept controlled: section 6 of FEMA empowers the Reserve Bank, in consultation with the Central Government, to specify which classes of capital account transactions are permissible and within what limits, and that control has been exercised continuously through regulations on investment, borrowing and property. Full capital account convertibility was the destination the Tarapore Committee mapped in 1997 on stated conditions of fiscal deficit, inflation, banking soundness and reserves. It was never enacted, and instruments such as the Liberalised Remittance Scheme are ceilings-bound permissions inside the control, not the abolition of it.

### Q: Why did India keep control over capital account transactions in FEMA?

Because capital flows differ from trade flows in the one respect that decides policy: they can reverse. A current account payment settles a transaction that has happened; a capital flow is a stock that can leave, and the crises that formed the drafters were crises of departure, the emergency of 1991 at home and the Asian financial crisis of 1997 in the neighbourhood, from which India's relative insulation was widely credited to the controls reform had marked for demolition. The Tarapore Committee had made opening conditional on the fiscal deficit, inflation, the soundness of banks and the adequacy of reserves, and the conditions were treated as unmet. Control through regulation also offered the state an adjustable instrument: classes and limits could be widened in calm years and narrowed within days in a currency crisis, as happened in 2013, without returning to Parliament.

### Q: Does FEMA apply to non-residents?

Yes, within defined limits, though differently from FERA. FEMA's rules bind persons resident outside India in their dealings on the Indian side of the border: a capital account transaction is defined to include transactions altering the assets or liabilities in India of a person resident outside India, an office or branch in India owned or controlled by a non-resident is itself treated as a person resident in India, and the deposit and investment regulations govern the accounts, including the non-resident external and non-resident ordinary accounts, through which non-residents bank and invest in India. What disappeared in 2000 was the citizenship limb: FERA had applied to Indian citizens wherever on earth they lived, so a settled emigrant's foreign banking remained within the Indian statute. FEMA follows residence, and the emigrant who has genuinely settled abroad passes out of the regime for dealings with no Indian side to them.

### Q: What changed for students, travellers and families when FEMA replaced FERA?

The legal character of ordinary life changed. Under FERA, exchange for foreign travel, for a child's education abroad, for medical treatment or for the maintenance of family overseas was released inside a permission structure built for rationing, softened over the years by general permissions and the Reserve Bank's manual but structured as an application on which silence meant refusal. Under FEMA those transactions are named in the definition of a current account transaction and freed by section 5, subject only to the Central Government's schedules of prohibited items, government approvals and Reserve Bank approvals beyond limits. The authorised dealer still examines documents, classifies the purpose and reports the flow, so the paperwork survived. What changed is that the paperwork evidences a transaction the citizen is entitled to make, instead of petitioning for one the state may allow.

### Q: Can the Reserve Bank change the FEMA rules without going back to Parliament?

For the operative detail, yes, and that is the framework design working as intended. Sections 46 and 47 of FEMA empower the Central Government to make rules and the Reserve Bank to make regulations, and the working law of foreign exchange, the permissible classes of capital transaction, the borrowing framework, the property rules, the remittance ceilings, sits in those instruments and in the master directions and circulars beneath them. The Liberalised Remittance Scheme ceiling was cut in the currency turbulence of 2013 and restored in stages afterwards, entirely by notification. What the Reserve Bank cannot change without Parliament is the architecture: the civil character of liability, the penalty multiples in section 13, the compounding power, the appeal ladder. When the state wanted those changed in 2015, it needed a Finance Act, which is the boundary between administration and legislation in this field.

### Q: How were companies and their officers treated under FERA compared with FEMA?

The attribution rule is nearly identical and the consequence is wholly different. FERA's section 68 deemed every person in charge of and responsible for the conduct of a company's business, and the company itself, guilty of the company's offence, unless that person proved absence of knowledge or the exercise of all due diligence. FEMA's section 42 repeats the structure for contraventions, extending it to directors, managers and officers whose consent, connivance or neglect contributed. Under FERA the deemed liability was criminal, and the Supreme Court held in Standard Chartered Bank v Directorate of Enforcement that a company could be prosecuted even for an offence carrying mandatory imprisonment, with a fine substituted for the term. Under FEMA the same deeming produces a penalty. The officer's escape route, proved diligence, is unchanged, which is why compliance records are kept with a care the folklore of deregulation would never predict.

### Q: Did the Enforcement Directorate itself change when FERA was replaced?

Institutionally, very little, and the continuity is one of the comparison's load-bearing facts. Section 36 of FEMA established a Directorate of Enforcement under the new Act, and the directorate that staffs it is continuous in personnel, method and institutional memory with the directorate that policed FERA: the same summons, the same recorded statements, the same file-building craft. What changed was the legal instrument in its hands. The power to arrest for the contravention itself was withheld, the presumption of the old Act was gone, and the directorate's work product became a penalty case or a compounding file rather than a prosecution, until the money laundering statute of 2002, in force from 2005, gave the same directorate a criminal statute to administer in parallel. Practitioners who describe an unchanged enforcement culture are describing the agency accurately. They are not describing the law it enforces.

### Q: Is hawala still unlawful after FEMA replaced FERA?

Yes. Dealing in foreign exchange outside the authorised channel is the core contravention of section 3 of FEMA, and hawala, the settlement of cross-border value through brokers without money moving through the banking system, is precisely such a dealing. Its legal character changed rather than its legality. Under FERA it was the regime's paradigmatic crime, prosecuted under a presumption of guilty intent, with preventive detention behind it. Under FEMA it is a civil contravention attracting adjudication and penalty, with the telling exception that its compounding is reserved to the Directorate of Enforcement rather than the Reserve Bank. The criminal consequences in the modern order arrive from neighbouring statutes: the money laundering law where the funds are proceeds of a scheduled offence, the customs law where goods move with the money, and the tax law where the income was concealed.

### Q: Can the same transaction lead to both a FEMA proceeding and a money laundering prosecution?

Yes, and the possibility is structural rather than accidental, because the same Directorate of Enforcement administers both statutes. A FEMA contravention by itself is not money laundering: the laundering offence under the Prevention of Money-Laundering Act 2002 requires proceeds of a scheduled criminal offence, projected or concealed as untainted property. But the factual patterns that attract the directorate's attention often satisfy both descriptions at once, an outward remittance routed through an unauthorised channel that is a FEMA contravention in itself and, if the money is the proceeds of a scheduled offence, the raw material of a laundering prosecution. The two proceedings then run in parallel, in different forums and under different standards: adjudication on probabilities under FEMA, trial beyond reasonable doubt under the laundering law, with attachment and arrest available in the second and unavailable in the first, and with statements recorded in the civil investigation capable of mattering in the criminal one.

### Q: What should a person do after discovering a foreign account held for years without disclosure?

The first rule is negative: do not move the funds, close the account quietly, or bring the money home by an informal route, since each converts a regularisable default into conduct that looks like concealment. The position turns on facts that must be reconstructed before anything is filed: when and in what residence status the account was opened and funded, whether the holdings were lawful in that status, what the tax filings said, and whether the aggregate crosses the one crore threshold that engages the seizure and prosecution provisions added in 2015. The regularisation routes are then the ones the statutes provide: disclosure and payment under the tax law and the Black Money Act framework where it applies, and a compounding application under section 15 of FEMA for the exchange contravention, which closes the matter on a computed sum. Because the characterisation decides which regime governs and the amounts decide the exposure, the reconstruction should be done with professional advice on the actual documents, not on assumptions.

### Q: Could India return to a FERA-style regime?

Nothing in the Constitution prevents Parliament from re-enacting criminal exchange offences, and the state's freedom of movement is wider than nostalgia in either direction usually admits. Controls can be tightened substantially without any new Act, because FEMA's classes, schedules and ceilings are set by regulation and can be narrowed in a crisis, as they were in 2013. A return to criminalisation, by contrast, would require legislation repealing the civil architecture, and it would run against the commitment on current account payments India holds under Article VIII of the Articles of Agreement of the International Monetary Fund and against the credibility on which foreign investment pricing depends. A partial return has already occurred once: the foreign asset provisions of 2015 rebuilt seizure and a filtered prosecution inside the civil statute. The realistic spectrum is therefore not FERA or FEMA as museum pieces, but a civil framework whose dials, including its criminal edge for chosen classes of conduct, Parliament and the Reserve Bank continue to turn.
