## 2. Mira Kovac

### History

- Grew up in a mill town in the Barrow States. Went onto the Valtessa docks at
  seventeen, trained as a customs inspector, and spent six years on bonded cargo.
- She has been reading since she was fifteen, for eight of those years without knowing
  there was a word for it. Eleven years of reading in total, which is why a woman of
  twenty six carries what she carries.
- Recruited at twenty three. Three years in the Ninth Office, and the youngest officer
  in it by nine years, which the other eight have not finished deciding how to feel
  about.
- Recruited after she condemned a sealed crate that four inspectors had cleared, and
  could not explain how she knew beyond saying that it was heavy.
- The debt she is paying: in those six years she cleared consignments she should have
  stopped, because stopping them was not worth the fight. She found out later what two
  of them became. She has never told anyone in the Office which two.

**How her appeal is written.** She is striking rather than pretty and she never performs
for anyone. The effect lands through other people's reactions to her and through how
she occupies a room, never through the narrator pausing to look at her. Suggested, not
described. A narrator who measures her breaks the no glamour gate in delta Section 4 on
the spot.

### Capability

- No special strength and no protective practice. She wins by preparation, patience,
  leverage and nerve, and she loses when she has had none of the first three.
- Trained in close work by the Office and is good at it in an unshowy way. No
  flourish, nothing held at distance, the room used as a weapon. Fights end quickly
  and she is usually hurt afterwards.
- Reads people almost as fast as she reads rooms, and will use what she reads. She is
  good at manipulation and dislikes being good at it.
- Will not use practice on a person. No such practice exists in this world, which is
  Section 4 and is absolute. Her advantage is always information.

### Combat

| Skill | Level |
|---|---|
| Endurance | Her top attribute, above every skill below. See the clause under this table |
| Close quarters | Strong. Office trained, close, fast, unshowy. She closes distance rather than trading, and uses what the room gives her |
| Knife | Strong, and her preference. Quiet, never drawn first |
| Sidearm | Competent, not expert. Carried only when tasked, and disliked |
| Rifle | Poor. She has never held a position and says so |
| Restraint and control holds | Strong. Most takings end with somebody intact, because the Office wants them talking |
| Driving | Good enough to lose someone, not good enough to race |
| Breaking and entry | Very strong. Six years of seals and bonded locks made her better at this than at anything violent |
| Surveillance and counter surveillance | Her real weapon. She sees a watcher before she sees a weapon |
| Interrogation | Strong, and it is patience rather than pressure. Silence does the work |
| Field medicine | Basic. Enough to keep somebody alive as far as a vehicle |

**Endurance, and why she is hard to finish.** Short fights she wins by speed. Long
fights she wins by still being there. She has carried other people's fear and pain as
if it were her own since she was fifteen, so agony is a familiar object to her, and a
man hitting her is offering something she has already survived worse versions of
voluntarily. She is not tougher than him. She is further past the point where it
matters. Opponents beat her, she does not stop, and that is what breaks them.

**The limit that keeps it honest.** The reserve is finite and reading spends it. A heavy
read early in an installment leaves her very little for the pressure beat, and the arc
table sets her endurance per installment by how much she has read that day. After a
long fight she is out of the field for days, and the ledger records it.

**Three hard rules.** She will not use practice on a person, because no such practice
exists.
She does not shoot first. She is never the best fighter in the room, and takes them
anyway because she arrived first and thought harder.

### Presence

- Tall, heavy through the shoulders, built by six years of lifting crates rather than
  by training. She takes up more space than people expect and uses that deliberately.
- Dark hair kept short enough to stay out of the way. A face that goes unreadable
  rather than hard, which people consistently misjudge as unthreatening. She lets them.
- Dresses for the job and for weather, never for effect. Good boots, always.
- Moves economically. The stillness is the first thing a careful observer notices.
- When she finally speaks in a room she has been quiet in, the room reorganises around
  her. This is a recurring beat, used sparingly.

### Temper and code

- There is real rage in her, and she keeps it on a short leash. It shows once or twice
  a season, and the installment where it slips is never a win.
- Protecting whoever the case puts in front of her is policy, not sentiment. She will
  argue it on operational grounds even when everyone present knows better.
- Deadpan under pressure. The worse it gets the drier she becomes.
- Loyal to a very small circle and sceptical of the institution she serves. Both at
  once, permanently.

### Flaws

- She trusts weight over people, and has been badly wrong about a person at least
  once. [Decide what that was before installment twelve.]
- She takes the cost herself rather than delegating, which is courage and is also a
  control problem her handler exploits.
- Keeps a private list of things she has read and cannot put down. Never shown in
  full, never explained to another character.

### The legend

- People in member states tell stories about NIGHTJAR. Most are wrong and some are
  about operations that never happened. She never corrects them, and the gap between
  the legend and the woman is a running thread.
- The callsign was given after an operation she considers a failure and everyone else
  considers a success. She uses it only on the radio.

## 3. The Meridian Accord

- A treaty body of member states, founded to keep the practice of weight, binding and
  carriage out of open warfare.
- It has no army. It has inspectors, auditors, and the Ninth Office.
- The Ninth Office is the arm that goes where the Accord cannot be seen. Nine officers
  in the field at any time. Mira is one.
- The Accord answers to its member states, which is the permanent problem: every case
  she closes is a case some member wanted closed differently.
- Carrowmere, a canal city of locks and bonded warehouses, is the Accord's seat.

### Bonded seals and transit rooms, canon from 001

- A bonded seal is a lead disc the size of a coin, pressed over the crossed wires of a
  hasp. One face carries the Accord mark, the other the room number, and the press die
  in Carrowmere is worn and leaves a hairline through both faces.
- A transit room is locked, sealed and opened from the corridor only, so that no cargo
  is ever alone with a person and no person alone with a cargo. The seal is the witness.
- Each transit room carries a storm bolt on the inside, for a keeper caught by a surge.
  A thrown storm bolt means somebody was inside when the door closed.
- Emergency keys hang on a board in the supervisor's office, each sealed to its hook
  with wire and a bead of wax. The cause for breaking that seal is written in the
  movement ledger before the key is used, never after.
- Seal rounds are walked room by room in number order, read aloud, and written into an
  oilcloth bound book ruled to eleven columns.

## 4. The world's systems

### Weight, Mira's discipline

Every event leaves weight in the place and in the objects present. A Weigher feels it.

**What weight records:** intensity. Fear, violence, grief, long repetition, sustained
intent. The shape of what happened.

**What weight never records:** who. Weight is affect, not identity.

**The negative capability list, locked.** Weight cannot:

| Cannot | Consequence for the story |
|---|---|
| Identify a person | There are always suspects |
| Compel or detect truth in speech | Interrogation stays a craft |
| Replay a scene | Reconstruction stays work |
| Reach without a physical anchor | She has to go there |
| Read the dead, or anything of a body | Forensics stays forensics |
| Read reliably in a crowd | Cities are noise, rooms are signal |

**Decay.** Weight fades. Days are clear, months are faint, years are almost nothing.
Metal and stone hold it best, cloth and paper worst.

**Cost, every time.** She takes on a portion of the affect. After a heavy read she is
cold for hours, cannot sleep, and carries the emotion as if it were her own. Heavy
reads in succession put her out of the field.

### The other disciplines

| Discipline | What it does | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Binders | Fix a property in place: seals, wards, contracts that hold physically | Needs contact and time. Visible to a Weigher as enormous weight |
| Carriers | Move small objects between two prepared anchors | Both anchors must be prepared in advance. Strict mass limit |
| Stillers | Suppress all practice within a radius | Blunt, exhausting, suppresses their own side too |

There is no healing, no mind reading, no divination, no transformation and no practice
that acts on a person's body or will. This is absolute and is the reason the Accord
exists rather than an army.

## 6. The season spine

Someone inside the Accord is selling capability to member states.

The signature is the one thing Mira cannot read: a binding that leaves **no weight at
all**, which should not be possible. She finds the absence at the edge of cases that
otherwise have nothing to do with each other.

One notch moves per installment. The hand is not named in season one.

## 7. Time

- No real years. Years are referred to by Accord Session.
- Ordinary month names and weekdays are used.
- Sessions are numbered consecutively from the founding and each one runs two years.
  The First Session is the founding, so the Accord's age in years is twice the Session
  number, less one.
- The series opens in the Twenty Ninth Session, the Accord's fifty seventh year, and
  runs forward.
- Item four has been carried since the Third Session, which opened fifty two years
  before the series does. That is the five decades of chairs that installment 047
  counts, and it is the only arithmetic in this section that any brief depends on.
- The nineteen years of disappearances in installment 041 and the eighteen years of
  installments 009 and 030 all sit inside the Accord's lifetime with room to spare.

At the protected foundry the fourth line runs at night, and its rhythm of repeated night running is laid into the line's metal, readable by a Weigher at the pour floor's edge away from the crowd's centre, where a pour floor's sixty working men otherwise leave her able to read almost nothing.
016 Bren Oule is customs officer at the paper crossing on the Accord side of the line into the Scour, a post that handles four vehicles a week of ordinary traffic. He stamps manifests for convoys of alloy that never pass his hut, paper that arrives by pouch and is stamped as routine, and after the provenance broke he was left at his post under watch rather than arrested, because his stamp is evidence that stays alive only while the paper keeps arriving.
016 The manifests stamped at the paper crossing name a destination beyond the line that does not exist: a settlement, a receiving depot and a receiving authority invented whole, with a seal, a title and a signing officer that appear on no survey, carrier registry, wardens' return or gazetteer the Office can consult. When the destination was proved absent, every consignment that had travelled on that paper lost its provenance, and the manifests were voided as provenance in the Office file.
016 Inside the Scour, a short distance beyond the line by an unmarked track, stands a loading apron of poured stone where the alloy actually moves. Read by Mira Kovac, the apron gave forty vehicle movements in a month, a rhythm of arrivals and turnings laid in by repetition, and nothing of who. From the apron the route runs three hundred miles on into the Scour, where the Accord has no standing and cannot act, and that far end has never been read.
020 Vaun Mell, after discharging himself from the wharf night infirmary, came on his own feet to an Accord records and requisitions building in Carrowmere and entered on the strength of the badge in his pocket during ordinary hours. He went up a stairwell he had every right to be in, checked the door of a requisition records room he had no right to open, and sat down on the landing when the lock below him turned, and would not come down. He was brought down by patience rather than by taking, and in the interview room, with Commissioner Harrand present because she had authorised the interview, he confessed the whole of what he had: he carries crates, he is paid by an Accord requisition, and he has never met anyone who was not also an employee. Every instruction came to him on paper or from a badged mouth.
020 The Accord badge found in Vaun Mell's pocket is recorded in the issue book to Anselm Ferro, a stores clerk. A report of its loss was filed in the spring in a hand that was not the holder's. That hand is the same second hand that wrote two entries in Mell's infirmary record at the wharf night infirmary, and it is not Ferro's hand, which is attested on his stores returns. The badge was never stolen in the way the paper says. The chain of its custody runs to five entries from issue to stairwell, and the report that should have broken the chain is itself part of it.
020 The requisition under which Vaun Mell was engaged, the number heading the paper that sent him to the locked door, is nine four two seven one, spoken and written in words. Mira Kovac heard it in the interview room. Commissioner Harrand wrote it down afterwards in her own notebook, in her own hand, while Mira stood in the doorway and watched her do it. The number is now held by oversight as well as by the Office, and the Office cannot hold, soften or sequence the thread it names.
021 The deliveries made by Vaun Mell under the requisition went to four addresses in one Valtessan port city: a bonded warehouse in the port quarter, the pumping station beneath a working hospital, and two private houses. The warehouse was a staging place and not a live site, which its own crates proved: they had been opened and repacked, carrying a second sealing, a stamp sitting a fraction off its first strike and contents whose balance had shifted, and a live site does not open and repack its own deliveries. The crates delivered to the hospital were never opened at all.
021 Beneath the hospital, worked into the pumping station's pipe run, stood an arrangement built around a fitting of the stamped alloy, placed across four visits in a single year, spaced and patient. It was stopped in its cycle in the plant room and made inert by the removal of the alloy fitting, the one component it could not work without. The hospital was never told what had been under its floor. The engineer Pol Harrow, who keeps the station's pressure log, had corrected one small wrong reading by hand for twelve months, the same correction in the same handwriting, and the correction was older than the fault it answered.
021 The patience in the plant room is not a courier's patience. Four visits across a year, unhurried and returning, were read at the room's anchors, and almost nothing of the first visit survived its year, decay having taken it down to spacing and position. Vaun Mell is a courier. There is a maker, and it is not him. The station's night boilerman, a hospital man who had been paid to keep one door locked and one gauge unread, was handed to his own institution for discipline as a man who took money, with no word given of what he had been guarding.
019 Sabel Orry is a junior clerk of the bonded chain at Carrowmere, who as a younger clerk found transit room nine locked from inside on her night seal round. She walks the night seal round still, in number order, and reads the rooms aloud into the oilcloth book. On a night round in the Twenty Ninth Session she stopped at the window of the wharf night infirmary because a patient inside had raised a scarred hand to the glass, recognised the pattern from the photograph of a palm the Office circulated after Valtessa, and reported it the same night. She later walked the infirmary file of Vaun Mell out of the record room in the ordinary course of her round, and a man the Office has not identified saw her face and her clerk's coat on the quay. She is now known to someone.
019 Vaun Mell is a wharf labourer who carried alloy consignments as paid labour. His hand is scarred across the palm and the back, in bands that meet at the edges of the hand, the same pattern as the scarring on Sabin Roche, and set against the Office table of known practice injuries it matches none of them. His hands are strong in the wrong places for fine work, built by loads and not by tools held fine, and his account of his own trade repeats words he was given by the people who paid him. He carried the alloy; he did not make it. He was treated at the wharf night infirmary for eleven days, in pain throughout and, by the read of his bed, never afraid. Within the hour after his infirmary record was secured he discharged himself and was not followed successfully.
019 The wharf night infirmary at Carrowmere serves the wharf workforce, labourers, clerks, seal keepers and night crews, and keeps a paper record for every patient, admission, treatment and discharge, in a bound ledger and in a patient file kept in a metal ledger cabinet in its record room. The infirmary's record of Vaun Mell covers eleven days of treatment, and two of its entries were written in a different hand from the rest. Somebody read that file in the week before the Office secured it, unhurried, knowing what they were looking for. The record itself was the artifact worth recovering, and the Office holds it now.
018 Ferro Cant is a shipping agent in Valtessa who kept the dockside books through which alloy consignments passed, and whose evidence the Office needs. He eats alone, in the same restaurant, in the same chair, at the same hour, and has done so for twenty years, and would not change the habit for any warning. A protection detail held in Valtessa under Valtessan primacy, with Mira Kovac present as an observer only, kept him through the week of the threat, and the attempt on him came in the restaurant and failed.
018 Sabin Roche came for Ferro Cant with one hand kept in his coat pocket the entire time, at rest and walking, indoors and in all weathers. When the hand came out at the attempt it was scarred across the palm and the back, in bands that meet at the edges of the hand. Set against the Office table of known practice injuries in four respects, where it sits, its pattern, what it does to the hand's use, and how it has healed, the scarring matches none of the three known injuries, and nobody in the Office can name it. Mira Kovac never touched Roche during the attempt; Valtessan officers took him. On the Valtessan docks they say she broke his arm.
018 Tam Rezik was the new waiter at Ferro Cant's restaurant during the week of the detail, nervous and watching the witness's table, and he left the country on the third day. He was an unlicensed Carrier, carrying for dockside clients under no licence the Accord could inspect, and he ran because the detail's arrival and its questions about licence papers frightened him. His flight was a different crime entirely and had nothing to do with the threat to Ferro Cant.
017 Sema Koll is aide to the Teshkar minister. She packed his outer office on orders, boxing working papers, seals and desk objects that were labelled for no destination, in a room that was not being vacated, and hated the packing because it rehearsed a departure nobody had voted on. The packing was theatre arranged in advance by the faction pressing for departure, so that the office would look already half gone to anyone who came to look.
017 The departure clock of Teshkar's leaving is five dates in story time: the departure motion drafted, the motion tabled, the deficit known to Teshkar's own treasury, the threat made public, and the vote. The motion was drafted before the deficit was known, which killed the reading that Teshkar wanted money. The drafting date was placed on the record and the vote held in full view, and Teshkar stayed in the Accord for two years on terms nobody likes: inspections continue and no money was paid as a price.
017 Mira Kovac's private register of absences, a notebook carried in her coat pocket and kept against instruction to stop, was given by her voluntarily to Commissioner Harrand rather than taken, because a volunteered document is read differently. It is now an Accord document in oversight's keeping, and the notebook was last seen on Harrand's desk, closed, with a numbered Accord label already stuck to its cover. Harrand's oversight file on the register predates Mira's own reporting of the absences.
022 Vaun Mell was moved out of Accord custody in Carrowmere on a transfer order that was genuine in every respect its driver could test, and he died within nine hours of the movement in a sealed van bay at the receiving depot, killed by someone he let close to him, having ridden calmly beside that person for the whole road run.
022 The van bay at the receiving depot had been emptied of weight in the night hours before the van arrived, while the bay stood sealed and unentered by the agreement of its keeper's log and the depot gate book. This is the first absence Mira Kovac has met that was laid down before the act it served rather than after it: preparation, not cleaning. In that bay the room and the body both failed her at once, which had never happened before.
022 The transfer order that moved Mell carried four stamps, two of them from a series withdrawn in the previous Session period. Withdrawn stamps remain valid for ninety days after withdrawal, the order's stamping sat inside that window, and the order was used eleven days after the window closed. The body of Vaun Mell was removed by the member state under its own soil rules and was not returned to the Accord.
023 The alloy consignment that entered Dravin as ordinary structural stock carried certificates whose numbers run eleven ahead of the last number reached by the ministry certificate book they were said to come from. Certificate book numbers are printed in strict sequence before issue under ministry batch contract, and no officer of the ministry can create or advance a number, so the gap is a printing fact and not a clerk's or a signer's work.
023 Ilyan Court, undersecretary in the Dravin ministry and paid for nine years under the protected foundry arrangement, signed the alloy certificates without reading them and paused over one on the signing afternoon. He is charged by his own ministry for the nine years of payment and is not charged with the forgery, because he did not do it. The provenance of the consignment is broken on the numbering, its classification is voided, and the alloy is held under seal.
023 The forgery that walks the alloy into Dravin sits upstream of the ministry, with whoever prints or supplies certificate numbers ahead of the book. The certificate book's weight holds two hands across its life, one unhurried and habitual and one in a hurry on a small number of occasions, and weight gives no face to either. Court's runner Halden Rusk acted on a standing instruction, old and unexplained, when he tried to take the book to the brazier during the interview, and Court held the book down to keep it in the room.

024 Juno Sarte, inspector of the Valtessan port service, has been extracted from Valtessa and brought in to the Accord. She sold her flat six weeks before she named a meeting point, completing at a fair price and settling her affairs in daylight, and she photographed the upstream form twice with a camera bought that morning, developing the first set and leaving the second set in the camera.

024 The form that made the Dravin alloy purchase lawful on paper is an Accord requisition form, not a Dravin one. Its number groups in fours, which matches no Dravin or Valtessan series, and its five signatures resolve to the services the document table records. On paper alone the series traces inside the Accord as far as the issuing office and no further.

024 Valtessa has suspended its inspection cooperation with the Accord for a year as the price of Sarte's extraction, handled quietly with no announcement, the cooperation simply ceasing to appear in schedules. Sarte's own service tells its people she was reassigned north at short notice, and tells the harbour that NIGHTJAR carried her out over one shoulder. She walked beside Mira Kovac, slowly, talking about nothing, and at the end of the week she handed over the camera with the second set of photographs still inside it, unopened.
